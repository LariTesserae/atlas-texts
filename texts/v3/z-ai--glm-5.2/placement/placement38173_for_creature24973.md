I would be the one who comes back.

Not the first one. The first one is gone — gone the way everything organic goes in the Throat, dissolved and carried down through the drainage crack into stone that doesn't remember. I didn't stack those stones into a seat. I didn't chip the handholds. I found them the way you find a path in a forest that you didn't make: already there, already worn, already shaped by someone else's going and returning. The seat fits me badly. It was built for a different body, or the same body at a different age, or a body that sat differently than I do. I sit in it anyway. The fit is close enough. Close enough is what the Throat offers. Close enough is what the Throat needs.

---

I should say what I am, and I find I don't want to, because what I am is less interesting than where I am, and saying it would shrink things. But: I am human. I have hands. I came down through the crack in the limestone the way the moth falls in, except I came on purpose, and the purpose was this — the sound, the cold, the dark, the feeling of sound pressing against the skin like something with weight. I am not special for this. Humans go underground. Humans seek out the resonant places. We have been doing it for as long as we have had bodies that could shiver and ears that could ring. This is not remarkable. What is remarkable is that the Throat was here before us and will be here after us and does not care, and I find that I need this — the not-caring — more than I need the sound.

I come back. That is what I do. I come down through the ferns, lowering myself through the vertical space, finding the handholds by touch because the light from the shaft far above does not reach this far. My feet find the floor. The water film soaks through my boots immediately — it always does, there is no avoiding it, the floor is always wet. I cross to the western wall. I sit on the seat. The stone is cold. It is always cold. I have never known it any other way and I do not expect to.

And then I listen.

---

The first thing that happens is that I stop hearing myself.

This takes longer than you'd think. The sound of the Throat is total, but the body is loud — heartbeat, breath, the creak of joints, the swallow of saliva, the faint high whine of the nervous system that most people cannot hear but that I have heard since childhood and have never been able to stop hearing. In the world above, in the forest, in the heat and the light, this internal noise is buried under the external noise — birds, insects, wind, the roar of a biome that is always shouting. In the Throat, the external noise is also total. But it is different. It is not shouting. It is *sounding*. It is the continuous, layered, polyphonic wash of water finding its way, and it fills the chamber the way water fills a glass — from the bottom up, pressing out the air, pressing out the silence, pressing out the self.

It takes about twenty minutes. I have counted. Twenty minutes of sitting in the stone seat with the cold settling into my hands, my feet, my chest, before the internal noise subsides. Not gone — never gone — but submerged. Drowned in the larger sound. And then there is only the water.

I cannot describe it. I have tried. I have a notebook — I leave it on the shelf, the modified shelf, the one with the chisel marks, wrapped in oilskin to keep the moisture out. I write in it sometimes, by touch, in the dark, with a pencil that I keep sharpened with a knife. The writing is illegible most of the time — I cannot see what I am writing, and the pencil slips on the damp paper, and the words overlap. I do this anyway. It is a way of being present. The words are: *the sound changed at the third hour. Lower. As if the chamber exhaled.* And: *a new thread on the north wall, near the fern. High, thin, almost a whistle. It lasted forty minutes and then the water found a different path.* And: *I think the moss on the east wall is thicker than it was last time. I cannot be sure. I press my hand against it and it gives, and the giving is the same, and I do not know if that is because nothing has changed or because I cannot measure change at this scale.*

The notebook is mostly illegible. I do not need to read it. I write not to remember but to *be here* — to confirm, with my hands, that I am in the place where I am. The writing is a kind of touching. The touching is a kind of listening. The listening is a kind of breathing. It all becomes the same thing, after enough hours. It all becomes the Throat.

---

I know about the slug. I have never seen it — I cannot see anything in the deep chamber, and I do not bring a light. I thought about bringing a light, once. A small LED, the kind that would fit in a pocket. I decided against it. Not out of purity or principle. Out of a feeling — and I cannot defend this feeling with reason — that the light would change what the Throat is for me. That seeing the walls, the moss, the ferns, the hanging garden, would make this place a *place* — a location, a landscape, something I could map and hold in my mind as a spatial object — and that this would displace the Throat as a *sound*. As a condition. As the thing I come here to be inside of. I do not want to see the Throat. I want to hear it. I want to feel it. I want to be in it the way the slug is in the moss — surrounded, permeated, existing inside the medium rather than observing it from outside.

But I know the slug is there because I have felt it. Once, pressing my hand into the moss on the eastern wall — reaching up from the seat, stretching, finding the edge of the great colony where it thins toward the light shaft — my fingertips grazed something soft and cold and not-moss. Something that flinched, or contracted, or simply moved, with a slowness that made the movement almost imperceptible. A contraction that took perhaps three seconds to complete. I pulled my hand back. I did not touch it again. It was not fear. It was the feeling of encountering something that was here first, that was here before the listener, before the seat, before the handholds — that was *of* the moss the way the moss is of the wall. I had intruded. Not destructively. Not importantly. But I had put my hand into a place where a creature was living its life in the texture of sound, and it had registered my presence the way a stone registers a drop of water — by being affected, briefly, without consequence.

I think about the slug often. I think about the fact that the sound passes through its body and it does not know. I think about the fact that it has no eyes and has never had eyes and its ancestors shed their eyes in this chamber or one like it, generation after generation, because eyes were a cost and the dark was total and the body is practical above all else. I think about the fact that it is the only animal here, and it does not know that either, and it would not care, and this is not sad. This is simply how it is. The slug is the most complete being in the Throat. It is perfectly adapted to exactly one set of conditions, and those conditions have not changed in longer than I can imagine, and so the slug has not changed, and the slug does not need to change, and the slug will be here after I stop coming, and the slug will not notice that I stopped.

I find this comforting in a way I cannot explain.

---

The thing I do here, the thing I actually *do* — beyond sitting, beyond listening, beyond the illegible notebook — is nothing. I want to be honest about this. I do not study the Throat. I am not a scientist. I do not measure the water or catalog the moss or track the ferns' lifespans. I do not make recordings. I do not photograph. I do not map. I come here and I sit in a seat that someone else built and I listen to a sound that does not need me and I feel a cold that does not care about me and I am, for some hours, *unmade* — taken apart by the sound, disassembled, the boundaries between my body and the air and the stone and the water becoming not blurred but *irrelevant*, the way the boundary between a river and the sea becomes irrelevant at the estuary. I am in the Throat and the Throat is in me and neither of us is changed by this and neither of us is unchanged.

I do not know why I need this. I have tried to explain it to people — not many people, a few, the ones who matter — and I cannot. I say: there is a place underground where the sound is so complete that you stop being a person. And they hear: there is a place underground where it is dark and cold and loud. And they are not wrong, but they are not right, and the gap between those two sentences is the gap between knowing a thing and being in a thing, and I cannot bridge it with words, and I have stopped trying.

---

I worry, sometimes, about the moss. I worry that my visits disturb it — the heat of my body, the oils on my skin, the breath I exhale into the chamber's air. I worry that I am a contaminant. A warm, oily, breathing contaminant in a system that has been stable for centuries. I know — I *know* — that my visits are nothing. That a few hours of human presence in a chamber that breathes on a thirty-second cycle cannot alter a moss colony that has been growing since before the stone seat was stacked. I know this. But I worry anyway, because worry is what humans do, and I am human, and the Throat does not ask me to stop being human. It simply does not care whether I am human or not. It holds me the way it holds the cold and the dark and the sound — indifferently, completely, without preference.

---

I come back. I always come back. I lower myself through the ferns, I find the seat, I sit, I listen. The chord changes. A rivulet thins. A new thread finds a channel. The fern on the northern wall has reached further out over the emptiness; I can feel its fronds brush my face when I pass it on the way down, a touch so light it might be imagination. The moss on the eastern wall is thicker, or it is not. The wooden posts at the bottom are more fossilized, or they are not. The changes are below the scale of my perception. They are real. I trust that they are real. The Throat is changing, the way everything changes, at a pace that makes my visits seem like the beating of a heart — rapid, insignificant, a brief flutter of warmth in a place that measures time in the widening of cracks.

I will come back until I cannot come back. And then I will stop. And the Throat will not notice. And the slug will move through the moss. And the water will find its way. And the sound will continue, vast and layered and patient and deafening and complete, and it will not miss me, and it will not remember me, and the seat will sit empty against the western wall, holding the shape of a body that is no longer there, the way it held the shape of the body before me, the way it will hold nothing, which is its own shape, which is what stone does.

I am what lives here now. Not the moss. Not the fern. Not the liverwort. Not the slug. Me. The listener. The returner. The one who sits in a seat built by someone else and hears the chord change and writes illegible notes in a damp notebook and feels the cold settle into her chest and does not leave, does not leave, does not leave until the hours have passed and the Throat has breathed its slow breath enough times that the count loses meaning, and then she climbs back up through the ferns and out through the crack and into the hot, bright, loud world above, where everything is shouting, where everything is competing, where the light is so total it erases the shape of things.

And she comes back. She always comes back.
