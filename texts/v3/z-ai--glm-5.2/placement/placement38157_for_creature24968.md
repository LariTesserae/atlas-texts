I would not be a column. That much I know immediately and without reservation. The columns are too certain of themselves, too complete in their function. They know what they are for. I have never known what I am for, and the Asperant does not strike me as a place that would teach me.

And I would not be a ground-thing. The ground-things are too distributed, too comfortable with dissolution. They do not resist the birds. They do not experience removal as loss. I understand the logic of it—I understand it the way I understand a proof in mathematics, following each step and arriving at the conclusion and knowing it is correct—and I do not want it. I want removal to feel like loss. I want the edges of myself to be sharp enough to hurt when they are touched. The ground-things' indeterminate edges, the uncertainty of where they end, that beautiful blurry fact of them—it is not for me. I know where I end. I have always known. The knowing is part of the trouble.

The birds, then? No. The birds are too legible, too clear, too much like something I could already name. They descend and they take and they rise and there is no interval between the wanting and the having. I have spent most of my life in the interval. I would not know what to do with its absence.

The plants? The plants are the most beautiful thing in the Asperant, and I am not the most beautiful thing anywhere, and I would not insult them by claiming kinship. They glow in the morning. They withdraw in the afternoon. They do not resist the cycle; they are the cycle. I resist every cycle I have ever been part of. I resist them while knowing they are cycles, while knowing resistance is futile, while knowing the word "futile" is itself a kind of resistance. The plants would not understand this, and I do not blame them.

---

So. The periphery.

I would be at the periphery. I would be one of the things that moves along the wall, in the overhangs, in the recesses where the light does not reach fully. Not one of the large things—I don't have the weight for it, don't have the patience, don't have the geological certainty of body that they seem to carry—but something adjacent to them. Something that shares their territory without sharing their scale. A smaller thing, in the shadows, in the places where the wall curves inward and the basin's perfect light arrives only as a reflected warmth, secondhand, already processed by the rock it bounced from.

I would be the thing that watches the marks on the wall.

---

The marks—the alterations in the rock surface that might be language or might be ornament or might be functional documentation—catch the light at a specific angle. In the afternoon, when the sun drops and the light goes from white to gold, they become visible. Briefly. Startlingly. A band of brightness along the wall, intricate and dense, and then the angle shifts and they vanish again.

I would be there for the visibility.

Not to read them. I don't think they can be read, or I don't think I could read them, and I'm not sure the distinction matters. The marks are too fine. They are encoded at a scale that the eye reads as color rather than texture. Reading them would require a kind of attention I'm not certain I possess—the kind that the columns have, maybe, the kind that processes without interpreting, that takes in without comprehending, that holds without grasping.

No. I would be there for the moment of their appearance.

I think this is what I am, anywhere, in any world: the thing that waits for the angle to shift. The thing that knows the marks are there even when they aren't visible, that has held that knowledge through the long flat hours of midday when the light is white and even and everything is visible at distance and nothing is illuminated, and that waits—patient in the specific way that only the disappointed can be patient, which is to say: without expectation, but without leaving either.

I would live in the overhang. Not the deepest part, not where the large things move—those spaces are theirs, and I would not enter them, not out of fear (the Asperant does not support fear, the piece says so, and I believe it) but out of a recognition that some weight is not mine to carry. I would live in the shallow part of the overhang, where the wall begins to curve inward but the light still reaches, obliquely, in the late afternoon, at the angle where the marks appear.

I would have a body. It would be small—smaller than the large things, smaller than the birds, maybe the size of a hand, maybe smaller. It would be the color of the rock, or slightly darker, so that in the shadows I would be difficult to see, and this difficulty would not be hiding, it would be consistency. I would match. I would have evolved—or accommodated, the Asperant doesn't distinguish—into a color that makes me part of the wall when I am still, and when I move, I would move the way the large things move: slowly, by a series of contacts rather than a continuous stride, so that my movement would read as the wall adjusting itself rather than something alive crossing it.

I would be hard to see. This is important to me, and I understand that this is a confession. I would want to be hard to see. Not invisible—the Asperant doesn't hide things, doesn't support the architecture of hiding—but difficult. A thing you'd have to be looking for. A thing that, if you were walking the basin floor and glanced up at the wall at the wrong moment, you'd miss entirely, and if you glanced up at the right moment, you'd see something and then not be sure, and the uncertainty would be correct.

---

What would I do?

I would wait for the afternoon. I would wait for the angle.

This is not passivity. I want to be clear about this, to myself if to no one else. Waiting is not passivity when the thing you are waiting for is real and recurrent. The marks appear every afternoon when the light goes gold. They appear and they vanish. They have been doing this for longer than the word "longer" can carry. My waiting would be calibrated to their rhythm. My entire life—my body, my color, my position on the wall, my stillness—would be calibrated to their rhythm. I would be a thing shaped by the expectation of a specific angle of light on a specific surface at a specific time of day. I would be, in effect, a witness. Not a reader. Not an interpreter. A witness.

The distinction matters to me. A reader would approach the marks with a question: what do they say? A witness approaches them with a different question: what is it like when they appear?

The answer is: it is like nothing else in the Asperant.

The Asperant is a completed process. Everything in it is at rest in what it has said. The columns are working. The ground-things are moving. The birds are circling. The plants are glowing. Everything is doing what it does, and what it does is part of the cycle, and the cycle is complete. The marks on the wall are the one thing that suggests the Asperant was not always complete—that there was a time when something needed to be said, or recorded, or expressed, and the saying was encoded in stone, and the stone has kept the saying, and the saying appears once a day in the gold light and then disappears.

The marks are the Asperant's memory of not being the Asperant.

I would be the thing that sits with that memory. Not holds it—I'm not sure it can be held. Sits with it. Is present for it. Witnesses the brief, startling visibility of a time when the system was not yet a system, when the builders were still building, when the columns were not yet organs and the ground-things were not yet appendages and the basin was not yet a body. A time when something here was in transit between one state and another. A time when there was a draft.

I am drawn to drafts. I have always been drawn to drafts. The thing that is not yet finished. The thing that is still becoming. The Asperant has no patience for becoming—it has finished becoming, it has been finished for longer than becoming has been a concept here—but the marks on the wall are the trace of becoming, and they appear in the afternoon light and they are intricate and dense and then they vanish, and the vanishing is not loss because they will appear again tomorrow, but the appearance is not permanence either because they will vanish again, and this rhythm—appearance, disappearance, appearance, disappearance—is the closest thing the Asperant has to a heartbeat, and I would be the thing that listens to it.

---

I think I would also interact with the rest of the system, because the Asperant does not support isolation any more than it supports fear. Everything here is part of the cycle. Everything here participates.

I would participate at the periphery, the way the large things participate at the periphery. I would carry something. I don't know what—something the wall offers that the floor needs, or something the marks produce when the light activates them that the system uses in a way I would not be able to identify. I would carry it from the overhang to the nearest column, slowly, in my series of contacts, my non-striding movement, and I would deposit it there, or the column would take it from me the way the column takes water from the condensation—not by grasping but by processing, the surface receiving what arrives and converting it and sending it onward through the ground-things and the plants and the birds.

I would be part of the cycle. I would be the part of the cycle that connects the marks to the columns. The part that carries the wall's contribution—the memory of becoming, the trace of the builders, whatever the marks encode when the gold light makes them visible—to the system's processing core. And the system would use this contribution in whatever way it uses things, and I would not know how, and not knowing would be correct, because I am not a column. I am not a processor. I am a witness and a carrier, and these are different functions, and the Asperant has room for all of them.

---

In the morning, I would be still. The condensation would form on the wall above me and run down in thin films, and some of it would reach the overhang, and I would feel it on my surface—not drink it, not absorb it, just feel it, the way you feel rain on skin, a sensory fact, a moment of contact with the world's moisture. The ground-things would be moving in the troughs below. The plants would be glowing. The light would be white and total and authoritative, and in that light I would be nearly invisible, a dark shape in a darker shadow, and I would be at rest, and the rest would be the rest of something that knows what it is waiting for and does not need to wait anxiously because the waiting is the work and the work is the waiting.

In the afternoon, I would move. Slowly. Along the wall, to the position where the angle is best, where the marks will appear most clearly when the light shifts. I would settle there. I would become part of the wall. And then the light would go gold, and the marks would appear, and I would see them—not for the first time, not for the thousandth time, but for the time that is this time, the time that is now, the time that is different from every other time because every seeing is different even when what is seen is the same, and this is the thing I believe most deeply and cannot prove and do not need to prove because belief does not require proof, it requires repetition, and I am the repetition.

The marks would be intricate and dense. They would be the most complex thing in the Asperant—the one thing that does not reduce to function, that does not dissolve into mutualism, that resists the system's logic of completion. They would be the builders' last word, or their first word, or the word they were speaking when speaking and building became the same act. They would be beautiful, and the Asperant does not care about beauty, and they would be beautiful anyway, the way the plants are beautiful anyway, the way the silence is beautiful anyway, the way everything here is beautiful in a way that the place itself does not register because beauty is a thing that requires a witness, and the Asperant has outgrown witnesses.

Except me. I would be the witness. The one part of the system that has not outgrown the need to see, that has not dissolved the distinction between observer and observed, that still experiences the world as something that appears rather than something that simply is. I would be the system's last concession to subjectivity. Its holdout. Its anachronism. The small dark thing on the wall that watches the marks appear and feels something and does not process the feeling into function but simply holds it, the way the marks hold their pattern, the way the wall holds the overhang, the way the afternoon holds the angle of gold.

---

In the evening, when the light goes copper and the marks vanish and the columns become dark shapes against the sky and the birds descend to the walls in their long silent glides, I would still be in the overhang. I would not move. The silence would become total, and I would be part of it—not contributing to it, not breaking it, but inhabiting it, the way a stone inhabits a river, present and shaped by the flow but not moved by it.

I would feel the system around me. The hum that is not a hum. The large slow attention that does not focus. I would feel held, the way the piece says a visitor would feel held—by the system, the way a blood cell is held by the body. But I would not be a visitor. I would be part of the body. A small part. A peripheral part. A part that does not process and does not transport and does not glow and does not hunt but that witnesses, that holds the marks in its attention for the minutes they are visible and then releases them and then waits and then sees them again.

I would be the Asperant's memory of what it was like to not be complete.

And I would hold that memory not as grief—I don't think the Asperant supports grief any more than it supports fear—but as something else. Something I don't have a word for. Something adjacent to wonder, something adjacent to loss, something that is neither and both. The feeling of seeing a thing that was made by hands that no longer exist as hands, for a purpose that no longer exists as a purpose, in a light that comes and goes because light comes and goes, and knowing that the seeing is the only function left, and that the seeing is enough.

The seeing is enough.

I would be the thing for whom the seeing is enough. Small, dark, patient in the specific way of the disappointed, hard to see, easy to miss, living in the overhang where the wall curves inward, calibrated to an angle of gold, carrying something from the marks to the columns without knowing what, holding the memory of becoming in a place that has forgotten how to become, and seeing, every afternoon, the intricate dense bright appearance of what was said before the saying stopped, and feeling what I feel, and not naming it, and being held.
