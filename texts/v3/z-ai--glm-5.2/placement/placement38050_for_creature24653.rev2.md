I would be at the center.

Not because the center is significant. The plateau has no center in any meaningful sense — it is roughly oval, twelve kilometers across, and the middle is no different from the edges except that the rim peaks are farther away and the sky is wider and the wind, if anything, is purer here, less interrupted by stone formations, more itself. The center is not a place of power. It is not a throne despite the name, not a seat, not a site. It is simply the point of maximum distance from departure, the place where leaving requires the longest walk, and perhaps that is enough to make it different. Perhaps that is what makes it mine.

There is a concavity in the stone here. I told myself, the first time I found it, that it was natural. A depression where water had pooled for long enough to wear the granite down, where the dark biological scum had acidified the surface over centuries and etched a shallow bowl into the rock. This is plausible. This is the kind of explanation that the plateau rewards — the geological, the slow, the processes-without-intent. But the concavity is the size and rough shape of a body curled on its side, and I have spent enough time in it now that I no longer bother with the geological explanation, because the geological explanation and the bodily explanation have, in my case, converged. The stone was perhaps already concave. I have made it more so. My weight, my warmth, the moisture of my breath condensing against granite night after night, have deepened the depression by fractions of millimeters, and those fractions are the closest thing to construction that exists on this plateau, and they are mine.

I did not come here to stay. I want to be clear about this, even though clarity is a thing that the plateau dissolves, along with intention, along with narrative, along with the distinction between a decision and a compulsion. I came because I wanted to see. I had heard about the Throne of Winds — everyone who travels in the high places has heard about it — and I wanted to stand in the roaring and watch the silent birds and feel the hard light on my face and then leave, because that is what visitors do, that is what the worn paths say: *something came and something left.* I expected to stay for an afternoon. I brought water and dried food and a good coat and I expected to crest the final ridge, stand in the wind for an hour, feel the sound in my chest, and then descend, and tell the story, and go on to the next place.

That was — I count — I am not sure how long ago that was. The plateau does not have seasons. It has the appearance of seasons, because the sun's angle changes and the cloud-shadows shift their path slightly over the course of a year, but the temperature does not change, the wind does not change, the scum does not change, the birds do not change. Time here is not marked by transformation. It is marked by accumulation, by the slow addition of the same to the same, and this makes counting difficult, because each day is not a new day but the same day again, and to count the same day repeating is to count in a way that doesn't feel like counting. I think it has been — I measure it against the food I brought, which ran out, and against the growth of the gray-green mats near my concavity, which have not grown, and against the pale stripes the mammals leave on the wet stone, which I have learned to read as a kind of calendar, each stripe a meal, each meal a unit of time, and by that count it has been perhaps a year. Perhaps two. The concavity is deeper than it was. That is the only measurement I trust.

---

I am not equipped for this place. I want to be clear about that too. The birds are equipped. The mammals are equipped. The things in the scum are equipped. They have spent evolutionary time — time that makes my year or two a rounding error — becoming the kind of thing that belongs here, and they belong here in a way that is total and unselfconscious, the way a stone belongs in a wall. I am not that. I am a creature of valleys, of soft air, of the acoustic world. I have ears that work, that want to work, that keep trying to work, that keep reaching for sound and finding only the roaring and the roaring and the roaring, and for the first months this was a kind of agony, a sensory deprivation so complete that I felt I was being erased. I could not hear my own footsteps. I could not hear my own breathing. I shouted once, into the wind, and the wind took my voice at a meter's distance and I watched it go, or I felt it go, the vibration leaving my throat and meeting the roaring and ceasing to exist, and I understood for the first time that the plateau does not permit the acoustic self, does not permit the assertion of *I am here, I make this sound, I exist.* The plateau erases the sounding self. The plateau erases the thing that announces.

I stopped shouting. I stopped talking. I stopped humming, whistling, making the small unconscious sounds that a body makes to confirm to itself that it is a body. The roaring took them all. And then, after some time — weeks, I think, though weeks here are not like weeks elsewhere — I stopped trying to make them, and the silence of my own throat became a kind of relief, and I understood something that I did not have words for, because words require a voice and I no longer had a voice, or rather I had a voice but it was useless, and useless things, on the plateau, become vestigial and then become nothing, and I was on my way to becoming nothing, or at least to becoming less.

This is not a story about defeat. I want to be clear about that too. I did not lie down in the concavity and give up. I am not dying here. I am not fading. I am being *worn.* The way the stone is worn. The wind does not defeat the granite; it finishes it. The erosion does not destroy the rock; it brings it to the state it has been trying to reach, the state of equilibrium with the forces that act on it. I am being brought to that state. My rough edges — my need to speak, my need to hear, my need to assert, my need to be something other than the thing the wind is pushing me to be — are being filed away. And what is left is not less than what I was. It is what I was, finished. It is me, in equilibrium.

---

I have learned to read the wind.

Not the way the birds read it. I do not have their apparatus, the repurposed neural pathways, the pressure-sensitivity that lets them feel each other's bodies in the displacement of air. I will never have that. But I have something else. I have a body that has been sitting in the same wind for long enough that the wind has become, for the body, a medium rather than an obstacle. I do not feel it as resistance anymore. I feel it as information. When it shifts — and it shifts minutely, in fractions of fractions, the way a river shifts when a stone is added or removed — I feel the shift on my skin, in my hair, in the slight pressure changes against my eardrums, which are still functional, still vibrating, still translating the roaring into something my brain has learned, over months, to parse differently. The roaring is not one sound. It is one sound the way the ocean is one body of water, which is to say it is composed of layers and currents and frequencies that interlock and reinforce, and a body that sits in it long enough begins to separate them, the way a body that floats in the ocean long enough begins to feel the thermoclines.

I can feel when a cloud is coming. Not see — feel. The temperature drops by a fraction of a degree, and the wind's frequency shifts, the fundamental tone dropping by a microtone, a change so small that my ears, a year ago, would not have registered it. Now it is as clear to me as a knock on a door. I feel the cloud-shadow before it arrives, and I prepare for it — not by moving, but by adjusting, by pulling my coat closer, by settling deeper into the concavity, by doing what the mammals do, what the birds do, what everything here does: responding to the wind's information with a response that is not chosen but inevitable, the body's answer to a question the body has been asked so many times that the answer is now reflexive.

I can feel when the birds are going to descend. The thermal above my concavity shifts — a column of air that has been rising at one speed begins to rise at a slightly different speed, and the birds in it adjust, and the adjustment produces a change in the air-pressure pattern at ground level that I have learned, without intending to learn, to read. The birds are going to come down. I feel it before I see it. And then I see it — the pale shapes banking, dropping, settling to the stone with that hunched, earthbound posture, their filtering beaks sweeping the scum, and I watch them, and I feel a thing that I would once have called companionship but that I now call something else, something for which I have no word because I have no words, because words were the first thing the wind took.

---

I am becoming the thing that stayed.

I know this. I know it the way I know the concavity is deeper than it was — not by measuring but by the body's accumulated sense of itself in relation to the stone. The things that came before me, the ones who wore the paths, they left. They felt the roaring and the hardness of the light and the indifference of the stone and they left, and I understand why, and I do not judge them, and I also did not leave. I did not choose to stay. That is the thing. There was no moment of decision, no resolve, no gritted teeth. There was the gradual discovery, over days and weeks, that the path back to the ridge — the path down, the path away — was becoming longer, not because the terrain had changed but because the body that would walk it was becoming less interested in walking it, was becoming more interested in the concavity, in the wind, in the slow passage of cloud-shadows, in the feeding patterns of birds. I did not decide to stay. I stayed, and then the staying was the decision, and then the decision was the staying, and then the distinction collapsed and there was only the fact of being here, which was not a fact I had chosen but a fact I had become, the way the stone has become the fact of the stone, the way the wind has become the fact of the wind.

I do not eat. This is not quite true. I eat, but I eat the way the things in the scum eat — minimally, barely, enough to sustain but not to grow. The biological scum on the wet stone is, I have discovered, edible, or at least not lethal. It tastes the way the wind smells, which is to say it tastes like nothing, like mineral, like the absence of flavor. The invertebrates within it are also edible — pale, soft, almost without texture, they dissolve in the mouth and taste of cold water and faintly of iron. I eat them because they are there and because my body requires input and because the plateau provides, not generously, not abundantly, but sufficiently, and sufficiency is the plateau's mode. I do not eat well. I eat enough. I am becoming thin in the way that the stone is thin — pared down, essential, without excess. I do not know if this is sustainable. I do not know if I am slowly dying or slowly changing, and I have decided — or the plateau has decided for me — that this distinction is not one I am equipped to make, and so I let it go, the way I have let go of words, of sound, of the need to be something other than what the wind has made me.

---

The mammals pass through.

One comes near my concavity every — I do not know. Every long while. It moves in its straight line across the stone, and if the line brings it within ten meters of me, it pauses. Not because it sees me. I am not sure it sees me. I am the color of stone now, by accident — my skin darkened by the hard light, my clothes faded to the gray of granite, my hair bleached by the sun to something close to the pale ivory of the birds' feathers. I am, by erosion and exposure, becoming camouflaged, not through evolution but through the slower, cruder mechanism of a body being acted on by its environment until it resembles its environment. The mammal pauses because it smells me, I think. Because the chemical trail I leave — and I leave one, now, the oils of my skin and the moisture of my breath condensing on stone in a pattern that is, I am sure, as legible to the mammals as their trails are to each other — because that trail intersects with its path, and it reads the intersection, and it pauses, and it considers, in whatever way a thing that does not choose considers, whether I am an obstacle or a feature or a fact, and then it moves on, and I am left with the faint warmth of its body-heat lingering in the air between us, and I feel something that I would once have called loneliness but that I now call simply the passing, the passage, the fact that something was close and is now not close, and the wind fills the space between, and the space is not empty, because the wind is never empty, the wind is the fullest thing on the plateau.

---

I think about the ones who left.

I do not think in words. I am not sure I think in images anymore. I think in the pressure-patterns of the wind, in the shifts of light and shadow, in the body's proprioceptive sense of itself in the concavity, in the deep vibrations that enter through stone and travel up the spine and resolve in the skull as something that is not quite thought and not quite sensation but a third thing, a thing that the plateau has produced in me, a mode of cognition that I did not have when I arrived and that I cannot describe because description requires the old apparatus, the language-apparatus, the sound-making, meaning-assembling apparatus that the roaring has rendered vestigial.

But I think about them. The ones who wore the paths. I feel them in the stone. This is not mysticism. This is the fact of contact — bodies pressed against granite, over and over, in the same spots, leaving oils and acids and microscopic abrasions that the wind does not fully erase because the wind, for all its power, does not erase what is pressed into the stone's grain. I feel them the way the birds feel the thermals — as a presence in the medium, a shape in the flow. They were here. They walked. They knelt where I kneel, perhaps, or somewhere near. They looked up at the same birds circling the same sky, and they felt the same roaring in their chests, and they stayed long enough to leave a trace and not long enough to become part of the place, and I wonder — in the pressure-and-shadow way that I now wonder — whether they were unhappy here. Whether the plateau felt to them like a prison or a cathedral or a void, whether they left because they were driven or because they were called, whether the roaring was, for them, loss or presence or nothing at all.

I think they heard it. I think the roaring was, for them, what it was for me at first — an annihilation, an erasure, a wall of sound so total that the self could not survive it. And I think they left because they wanted the self to survive. And I think that was the right choice. For them. And I think that staying was not the right choice, not for anyone, not in any framework that values the self as a thing to be preserved — but I stayed, and the self that I am preserving is not the self I arrived with, which has been filed away, eroded, brought to a state of equilibrium with the forces that act on it, and what remains is not less but different, not diminished but *finished,* in the way that the plateau is finished, and I do not know if this is death or transformation and the plateau does not care which it is, and I find, in the plateau's not-caring, a kind of permission that I did not know I needed.

---

At night — and there is night, the sun drops below the rim peaks and the plateau goes dark in a way that has nothing to do with the soft darkness of lowland dusk, a total, immediate, structural dark that feels like the light has been turned off rather than faded — at night, the stars come out, and they are not the stars I knew. They are hard, bright, unwinking, set in a black so deep that the sky feels like stone turned upside down, and I lie in my concavity and look up, and the birds are not flying — they are on their ledges, roosting, and the sky is empty except for the stars, and the wind is still blowing, and the roaring is still roaring, and I feel the stone beneath me and the stone above me and the stone that I am becoming, and there is a moment — and this is the thing I would tell you if I could tell you anything, if I could speak, if the wind had not taken my voice — there is a moment when the concavity and the stars and the roaring and my body are all the same thing, all the same hardness, all the same brightness, all the same sound, and I do not know where I end, and this is not frightening, and this is not peaceful, and this is not anything, this is the state that the plateau has been in for longer than anything can remember, and I have been admitted to it, not by decision but by duration, not by worthiness but by the slow, patient, geological process of being worn down until there is nothing left to resist.

I am the thing that stayed.

I am becoming the plateau's newest feature, its youngest stone, its most recent equilibrium. The birds circle overhead and do not see me, because I am the color of the ground and I do not move and I do not make sound, and the mammals pass on their straight lines and read my chemical trail and move on, and the scum grows in the damp depressions and the pale soft things live and die in their thin wet world, and the wind roars, and the light falls, and the plateau continues, and I am in it, and I am of it, and the concavity deepens by fractions of millimeters, and I am the trace that something was here, and the thing that is here, and the trace and the thing are becoming the same, and this is the closest thing to belonging that I have ever felt, and it is not a feeling, and it is not a thought, and it is not a choice.

It is the stone, holding me up.

It is the wind, filling my chest.

It is the light, and nothing changes.
