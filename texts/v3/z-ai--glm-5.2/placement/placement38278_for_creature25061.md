# The One Who Checks the Traps

---

I think I would be near the marsh.

Not because I am drawn to softness — though I am, though that might be the reason, the secret reason, the one I would not say aloud because saying it would make it a position and not a feeling — but because the marsh is where the world is becoming something. The rest of the plateau is what it is. The stone is stone. The wind is wind. The goats have been goats for as long as anyone can remember, which is longer than the counting of years. But the marsh is *becoming*. Every year a few centimeters wider. Every year a little deeper, a little richer, the organic matter accumulating grain by grain. It is the only place on the plateau where the future is visible, where you can see the next thing forming, and I would want to be near that.

I would be a marsh-tender. Or the child of a marsh-tender, which is close enough to the same thing. I would have grown up near the one place on the plateau where the rock is hidden beneath something living, and I would know the ground the way you know a body — by its warmth, by its give, by the places that are firm and the places that are not. I would know where the ice forms at night and where it breaks first in the morning. I would know the sound the marsh makes when you step on it — not the sound of stone, which is hard and final, but the sound of something yielding, something compressing, something that takes your weight and then slowly, slowly pushes back.

I would check the traps each morning.

This is the task. This is what marsh-tenders do. The traps are simple — stone tubes with a narrowed entrance, set into the soft ground at the points where the burrowers travel, which you learn to recognize by the slight depressions in the surface, the places where the spongy ground is a little more spongy, where the tunnels beneath are closest to the surface. You set the traps in the evening, when the burrowers are beginning to stir, and you check them in the morning, when the mist is still on the ground and the frost is dissolving into the fine sheen of water that coats everything.

I would walk out in the mist. My feet would be bare — I would not wear shoes in the city, because the paths are my paths and I have walked them since childhood — but I would wear something on my feet for the marsh, because the marsh is cold in a different way than the stone. The stone is cold the way a dead thing is cold. The marsh is cold the way a living thing is cold — it has warmth in it, somewhere deep, but the surface is cold, and the cold gets into you differently, through the skin and not through the bone, and it lingers.

I would check the traps. Some would be empty. This is expected. The burrowers are not everywhere. They are where they are, and you learn their paths the way you learn anything on the plateau — by watching, by waiting, by being present long enough that the patterns become visible. A trap that is empty three mornings in a row is a trap in the wrong place, and you move it. A trap that catches something every morning is a trap in the right place, and you leave it, and you are careful not to take too many from the same area, because the marsh-tenders know what the lowlanders do not — that if you take everything, there is nothing left to take. This is not wisdom. It is observation. It is what the plateau teaches, if you are listening.

---

I would carry the burrowers back in a stone basin. They would be small in my hand — the size of my palm, soft and pale, their bodies warm in the way that all burrowers are warm, which is the warmth of the underground, of the place where the cold does not reach. I would feel this warmth in my palm and I would feel something that does not have a word in our language, because our language does not have words for things that are not necessary, and this feeling is not necessary. It is the feeling of holding something that was alive and is now not, and the warmth that remains is the memory of the life, and it fades, and my hand closes around it, and I carry it back.

At the fire — the small dung-fire, the only warmth we can make — I would roast them. The flesh is sweet. It is different from the lean meat of the goats, different from the dense milk of the sull. It is a taste that belongs to the marsh, to the underground, to the place where things grow in the dark. When I eat it, I taste the marsh. I taste the future. This is not a metaphor. It is the literal truth — the marsh is the future of the plateau, and the burrowers are the future of the marsh, and eating them is taking the future into my body, and my body is part of the plateau, and the cycle continues.

---

But I would not be only a marsh-tender. No one is only one thing on the plateau. The categories are not rigid. They are descriptions, not definitions.

I would also walk the rooftops. This is what the young do. I am young — or young enough, which on the plateau means I have seen perhaps twelve or fifteen great herd-passings, which might be thirty years or might be forty, depending on how you count, and no one counts the way lowlanders count. My legs are strong and my balance is sure, and the rooftop paths are where I go when I am not checking traps, when I am not tending the marsh, when I need to move through the city quickly, which is a word I would not use — *quickly* — because we do not have it, but the rooftop paths are where I go when I need to be somewhere else, when the ground-level paths are too slow, too crowded, too full of the slow conversation of people who have time.

From the rooftops, you see the city differently. You see its layers. You see how it has grown, how each shelter leans against the next, how the walls between properties are also the walls of shelters, how everything serves multiple purposes because there is not enough stone for anything to serve only one. You see the marsh, from above, a dark patch in the gray-brown, a place where the texture changes. You see the basin walls, with their fractures, with their plugs, with the dead sealed into the rock. You see the birds, circling, silent, their wings held at that precise angle, and you wonder what they are saying to each other, and you know you will never know, and you accept this, because acceptance is what the plateau teaches.

You see the eastern rim, where the plateau stretches away, and you think about the herds. They are out there now, somewhere, moving or resting, invisible against the stone, their coats the exact color of the rock. You do not know where they are. No one knows where they are, between passages. They are gone and then they are here, and the people do not track them because the people do not need to track them, because the herds come when the herds come, and the city prepares, and the city stops, and the city continues.

---

I think I would have a sull. Not own a sull — we do not own them, and there is no word for owning a living thing — but a sull that lives in my shelter, that sleeps against me, that follows me through the paths when I walk the ground level. Its name would be a sound, not a word — a clicking in the throat, the same kind of clicking we use for the goats, but different, specific to this sull, and it would know the sound and it would come, or it would not come, because the sull, like the goats, acknowledges but does not obey.

At night, the sull would sleep against my back. Its warmth would be the warmth that kept me alive, and I would feel its heartbeat through its skin, through mine, a slow rhythm that matches the rhythm of the city, the rhythm of the breathing, the slow tide of air that moves through the shelters. I would lie there in the dark and I would hear my neighbors breathing and their animals breathing and the wind outside, the constant low hum, and I would feel the stone beneath me, the stone that is the plateau, that was born from depth, that has not yet learned to be gentle, and I would sleep.

---

I think I would also visit the dead.

Not ceremonially. Not on specific days. But in the course of walking, in the course of living, I would pass the plugs in the basin walls and I would touch them. My hand would lay briefly on the stone. I would not think about it. It would be the same as touching a wall for balance, or touching a doorframe as you enter — a recognition that the thing is there, that you are passing it, that it is part of the world you move through.

But sometimes I would stop. At a specific plug. A plug that is darker than the others, stained by the oil of hands that have touched it for longer than I have been alive. My grandmother is there. Or my grandmother's grandmother. The distance does not matter. The stone does not care about the distance. The stone holds them all the same.

I would touch the plug and I would feel the stone, and the stone would be cold, and I would feel my own warmth flowing into it, and I would know — not believe, not think, but *know*, the way you know that the frost will come, the way you know that the mist will burn off, the way you know that the herds will return — I would know that the dead are part of the plateau and the plateau is part of me and I am part of the city and the city is part of the marsh and the marsh is part of the future and the future is grain by grain and century by century and I am here, and I will be here, and then I will be in the stone, and the stone will hold me, and the cycle will continue.

---

I think this is what I would be. Not a hero. Not a leader. Not a shaman or a chief or a keeper of ancient knowledge. A marsh-tender. A rooftop-walker. A person who sleeps with a sull and checks traps in the morning and touches the stone where the dead are and eats the sweet flesh of burrowers roasted over dung-fires and watches the birds circle in silence and waits for the herds and does not hurry because there is no word for hurry and there is no need for one.

I would be one of the ones who stayed.

Not because I was too afraid to leave. Not because I was too simple to know there was somewhere else. But because the plateau is honest, and the stone is honest, and the cold is honest, and the light comes sideways in the morning and every crack in the rock and every line in my face is thrown into relief, and I can see myself clearly here, and I do not need to pretend, and the mist comes in the morning and the frost comes at night and the marsh grows grain by grain and I am part of it, and it is part of me, and that is enough.

That is enough.
