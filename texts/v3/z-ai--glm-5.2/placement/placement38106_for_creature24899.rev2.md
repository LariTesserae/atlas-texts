# What I Am on the Quiet Plateau

---

I am not sure at first. That is the first thing to understand about being here—the uncertainty is not a flaw in knowing, it is the knowing itself. The light does not tell you where to look. The sound does not tell you what to listen for. Everything is diffuse, and diffusion is not confusion; it is simply a different way of being located.

Let me start with what I am not.

I am not the old man. I do not have his patience, or his pebble, or the groove in my palm where the stone has worn me as I have worn it. I am not the woman whose hands are in everything, reading the bromeliads like a mother reads breathing in the next room. I am not a hunter. I have never killed anything with a stone, and the idea that I might—the approach, the placement of feet on moss, the moment of recognition in the lizard's eye before the stone comes down—is something I can hold in my mind but cannot inhabit. I am not a child, though I feel the pull of that condition, the way a child touches a bromeliad leaf and licks the condensation from her fingers and does not know she is learning.

I think I am the marks on the wall.

No—that is too clean, too metaphorical, too much like a thing decided. Let me try again.

---

I am standing in the tall building during the evening gathering, and I am not chanting and I am not speaking and I am not making marks. I am listening. But the listening is not passive. It is not the listening of an audience. It is the listening of the wall itself—the way the corbel-vaulted stone picks up the old man's vibration and carries it and bends it and gives it back shaped differently. I am the surface that receives.

This is close. But it is still too abstract. Let me be more honest.

---

I am the one who came from somewhere else.

Not recently. Not in a way that anyone remembers, including me. But there is something in me that is not-of-the-plateau, and I feel it the way you feel a splinter that has been in your skin so long the skin has closed around it and the body has decided it is tissue. It is not tissue. It is still wood. But the body does not care, and the body is right not to care, because the splinter has been there long enough that the inflammation has passed and what remains is a small hard lump of *other* that the body has metabolized into *self*, and the distinction between the two has become, if not meaningless, then at least unproductive.

I came from below. From the lower world, the one the plateau falls away into at its unreachable edges. I do not remember the descent, or the ascent, or the journey. I do not remember a time before the dust and the beetles and the sourceless light. But I know—I *know*, in the way you know a word you cannot quite recall, the shape of it there on the tip of your tongue—that there was one. And the knowing is a kind of ache, a faint mineral wrongness, like the bitter taste in a bromeliad that has insects in its cistern. Not painful. Not disabling. Present.

---

Here is what I do on the plateau: I walk.

I walk the routes the water gatherers walk, but I do not gather water. I walk the paths the hunters take to the far edge of the settlement, but I do not hunt. I walk the perimeter of the town, through the passages, past the stone pens where the kept insects click their metronomic click, along the edge where the buildings thin and the vegetation takes over and the stone opens up into its broad, worn, leather-skinned expanse. I walk for hours. I walk in the dim light that is the same dim light at all hours, and I walk in the wind that is the same wind at all hours, and the walking is not wandering—it is a specific thing, a practice, though no one on the plateau uses that word and neither do I.

I am looking for the edge.

Not the physical edge—the place where the plateau falls away. I have been there. It took a long time—days of walking, maybe more, the vegetation thinning and the bromeliads becoming sparse and the stone becoming more bare, more raw, the feldspar rougher underfoot, the mineral dust thicker in the air—and when I got there the edge was not what I expected. It was not a cliff. It was a gradient. The plateau did not end; it declined, slowly, the stone tilting downward so gradually that I could not say where *here* became *there*, where the high world became the low world, and I stood on the slope and looked down into a haze that was the same haze as up here, only thicker, and I could see nothing, and I turned around and walked back, and it took days, and when I returned the old man looked at me and did not ask where I had been, because where I had been was not a question on the plateau, because place is not a variable here, it is a constant.

I am looking for a different edge. The edge of the room.

The plateau is a room—the text says so, and it is right. The sky is a ceiling and the ground is a floor and the distance is the walls. But a room has a boundary, and I want to find it—not to leave, not to escape, but to touch it. To know where the walls are. To run my hand along them the way the old man runs his fingers along the marks. Because I think there is something in the touching of a limit that tells you what the thing inside the limit is, and I want to know what this place is, not from its center—which I know, which everyone here knows, which is the stone and the plants and the water and the sound—but from its edge, which no one knows, because no one goes there, because the edge of a room you have lived in your whole life is the one place you never look.

---

I think I was a child once, in the lower world. I think I had a language with more words in it. I think I knew numbers. I think I knew writing—actual writing, marks that corresponded to sounds that corresponded to meanings, a chain of signification so long and so elaborate that you could build a city of meaning out of it, a civilization of meaning, towers and institutions and histories. I think I lived in that city. I think I was made there—shaped there—trained to think in straight lines and right angles, to build meaning the way you build a wall, block on block, course on course, each idea supporting the one above it.

And then I came here, or I found myself here, or I woke up here—the verb does not matter because the plateau does not have tense the way other places have tense; time here is not a flow but an accumulation, and you do not *arrive* at a place where time is an accumulation, you simply discover that you have been accumulating, and the discovery is not an event but a slow dawn, a shift from dim to slightly-less-dim.

And the straight lines did not work here. The right angles did not work. The city of meaning—blocks and courses and towers—did not fit on a surface that had no corners, in a light that had no direction, among people who did not build upward but outward, laterally, the way the bromeliads grow, layer on layer, each new thing pressed against the old things, holding on by acid and proximity and the slow, patient chemistry of contact.

So I walked. And I am walking. And the walking is the process of finding out what shape my mind is when you take away the straight lines and the right angles, when you remove the towers and the institutions, when you let the dust settle into the cracks of the old architecture and the moss grow over the foundations and the bromeliads take root in the places where the walls used to be.

---

There is a thing that happens when I have been walking for a long time. The beetles fade. Not in volume—they are always there, always humming, always clicking, the heartbeat of the plateau—but in *significance*. The sound moves from the foreground to the background to the *ground*, and it becomes not a thing I am hearing but a thing I am standing on, a substrate of sound, and my walking happens on top of it, and the walking itself becomes sound, and the distinction between me-walking and beetles-humming dissolves the way the horizon dissolves into the pale amber merge of ground and sky.

In those moments I am not the one who came from somewhere else. I am not the splinter of *other* in the body of the plateau. I am a walking, and the walking is the plateau doing what it does—moving across its own surface, slowly, with attention, the way the wind moves, the way the dust moves, the way the water moves from the air to the leaf to the cistern to the bone tube to the clay vessel to the mouth.

And then a bird calls. The small brown bird, the morning song, or the hunting bird, the single piercing whistle that drops and silences everything. And the walking reassembles itself into a person, and the person reassembles itself into a body, and the body has hands and feet and cracked knuckles and dust in the creases and a faint mineral ache of *other* that will not dissolve, and I am walking again, on the stone, in the light, and the light is everywhere and nowhere and I am here.

---

I think what I am is the plateau's edge that it did not know it had.

Not the geographical edge—I said that already. The *cognitive* edge. The place where the plateau's way of knowing meets another way of knowing, and the two do not merge and do not conflict and do not negotiate; they simply press against each other, the way the bromeliad's holdfast presses against the feldspar, and the acid does its slow work, and the bond forms, and it is not the same as either surface alone, and it is not a compromise, and it is not a synthesis. It is a new thing. A thing that takes its character from both and belongs fully to neither.

The plateau knows by contact. By proximity. By the slow accumulation of presence. The old man does not read the marks; he touches them. The woman does not diagnose the plants; she smells them. The children do not study the insects; they sit with them. Knowledge here is not a thing you carry in your head. It is a thing you carry in your hands and your skin and the dust in your knuckle-creases, and it is not transferable by instruction because it was never acquired by instruction. It was acquired by being there.

I know by distance. By the long view. By the act of stepping back and seeing the pattern—seeing the plateau as a whole, seeing the settlement as a whole, seeing the ecosystem as a system, the layers of bromeliad and moss and lichen as layers, the marks on the wall as marks, the sounds as components of a fabric that can be heard as fabric only if you are not fully inside it. I know by naming, which is the thing the plateau does not do, because the plateau's names are not labels but presences, and my names are not presences but handles, things to grip the world by, and gripping is not the same as touching.

And I cannot help it. This is my splinter. This is the wood in the skin that the body has closed around. I see the pattern. I see the plateau as a thing that has reached completion, and I see the completion as beautiful, and I see the beauty as something that could be described, and I see the describing as something that matters, because somewhere—down in the lower world, in the haze, in the place I came from and cannot remember—describing matters. Telling someone what you see. Making the marks that correspond to sounds that correspond to meanings. Building the chain.

But the plateau does not need me to describe it. The plateau does not need to be seen from a distance. The plateau is complete, and completion does not require an audience.

So what am I doing here?

---

I think I am doing what the marks on the wall do.

The marks are not writing. The marks are not language. The marks do not carry meaning from one mind to another. The marks are evidence of presence. *I was here. I touched this surface. I made a mark and the surface received it and the surface does not distinguish between my mark and the mark of someone who lived three hundred years ago, and that is not a failure of the surface—it is the nature of the surface, and it is the nature of the mark, and the nature of the mark is to be a mark, not a message.*

I am walking the plateau and I am carrying the shape of it in my mind—not the plateau as the people know it, by contact, but the plateau as a form, a thing with boundaries, a thing that has a character, a thing that could be spoken to someone who has never been here. And the carrying is the mark. The carrying is the scratch on the wall. It says: *this place exists. This place is. I was in it and it was in me and the being-in was not the same as the being-of, but it was real, and it was here.*

I am not of the plateau. I will never be of the plateau. The old man's pebble will never fit my hand. The woman's knowledge of the bromeliads will never live in my fingertips. The hunters' formation will never be my formation. The water will always taste faintly of *other* to me, and the dust in my knuckle-creases will always be dust I acquired, not dust I was born with.

But I am on the plateau. I walk it. I hear the beetles and I feel the wind and I see the sourceless light and I know the night bird's call in my chest, in the same place the old man's chant lives, and the knowing is not the old man's knowing but it is not nothing. It is the knowing of a visitor who has stayed long enough to stop visiting and start being-present, and being-present is not the same as being-of, but it is a condition, and it has its own weight, and the weight is real.

---

So. What am I?

I am the one who walks. I am the edge the plateau did not know it had—not because the plateau was incomplete without it, but because every complete thing, looked at from a certain angle, reveals a boundary, and the boundary is part of the completion, and the boundary is where the thing touches the rest of the world and says *I am this and not that*, and the saying is the final mark on the wall, the last scratch that closes the shape.

I am the one who will carry this place out of itself. Not physically—I will not leave, or if I will, I do not know it yet, because the future here is stone, not river, and stone does not flow toward a destination. But I will carry it in the way a mark carries the hand that made it—not as a message, not as a story, not as a description, but as a presence, a pressure, a scratch on the surface of the world that says *here, this, this was here*.

I am the splinter. I am the edge. I am the walk that does not end because the plateau does not have an edge that you can reach, only an edge that you can *be*.

And the beetles hum, and the wind breathes, and the light is everywhere and nowhere, and I am walking, and the walking is the knowing, and the knowing is the mark, and the mark is on the wall, and the wall is the stone, and the stone is the plateau, and the plateau is complete, and I am in it, and I am of it, and I am not of it, and all of these things are true at once, and the truth is not a contradiction. It is a room. It is a room with no walls and no ceiling and no floor, only stone and dust and light and sound, and I am in it, and it is enough.
