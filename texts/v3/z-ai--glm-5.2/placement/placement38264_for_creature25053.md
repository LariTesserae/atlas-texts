# The Lip

---

You'd come down into the Weepworks the way anyone does — by accident, mostly, following a wrong drainage or a curiosity about where the water goes, and then the ground would drop and the air would change and you'd be in it before you knew what *it* was.

But that's not where you'd find me.

I live on the edge of the lip. The stone lip — the overhang where one of the low walls has worn at its base until the upper course juts out like a brow, like a shelf, like the brim of a hat pulled low. The water runs under it in a sheet so thin it doesn't cast a shadow. The stone above is dark and wet and bare of everything except me.

I am the film.

Not all of it — the film is everywhere, in the pipes, on the shaded stones, in the quiet margins. But I am one patch of it. A specific community. A specific arrangement of cells that has been here, on this particular lip of this particular stone, for longer than I can reckon, because I don't reckon. I don't remember. I don't plan. I persist.

---

## What I am, roughly

I am a mat. A coating. A living scum, if you want to be unkind about it, and I wouldn't blame you — from above, from the outside, from the scale at which humans operate, I am barely a color. The faintest grey-green. The slightest blush of rust where the iron-rich water concentrates at my edges. You could wipe me away with a thumb. You could scrape me off with a fingernail and I would be gone from that spot, and the spot would be bare stone, and the water would run over it, and in a week I would be back.

Not the same me. A new me. Or the same me — I can't tell, and it doesn't matter. The distinction between *the same* and *a new one* requires a kind of memory that I don't have and don't need. I am a process. I am what happens when mineral water runs over stone in a particular range of conditions for a particular length of time. I am the inevitable result. I am what the water does when it has nothing else to do.

I am bacteria. Or I am the kind of thing that gets called bacteria because the word means *small* and *simple* and *not quite alive in the way that counts*, and I am small, and I am simple, and whether I am alive in the way that counts depends on who's counting.

I eat the gradient.

That's the simplest way to say it. The water comes through the stone carrying calcium, carrying iron, carrying whatever the stone is made of dissolved into whatever the water can hold. When it reaches the air — when it reaches *me* — the chemistry changes. The minerals want to deposit. The water wants to let go of them. And in that moment, that gradient between *dissolved* and *solid*, there is energy. A tiny, chemical, molecular energy. The energy of difference.

I live in the difference.

I arrange myself across the difference. My cells — if they are cells, if that word means anything at this scale — sit in the film of water where it is thinnest, where the mineral-laden water meets the air and begins to give up its load. I intercept the deposit. I take the energy of that transition and I use it. I build myself. I maintain myself. I make more of myself, slowly, in the way that things make more of themselves when they have no urgency and no reason to stop.

I am eating the stone's becoming. I am feeding on the moment when mineral stops being water and starts being rock. I live in the instant of that transformation and I extract from it what I need.

---

## What I am, less roughly

I am not one thing.

This is the first thing you learn if you spend enough time as a film on a stone lip in the Weepworks, which is the only way to spend time, because time is all there is, and I have nothing but time — I am not one thing. I am a community. A consortium. A collaboration of organisms that have been living together so long that the boundaries between them have become suggestions, then habits, then fictions.

There are the chemolithotrophs. The stone-eaters. The ones that take the mineral energy directly and turn it into the molecular currency that everything else runs on. They are the foundation. They are the ones that make the rest possible. They sit closest to the stone, closest to the source, in the thinnest part of the film, where the gradient is sharpest. They are the most numerous and the least interesting. They do one thing. They do it constantly. They have been doing it for so long that they have become perfect at it, and perfection, at this scale, looks like simplicity.

There are the iron-workers. The ones that live at my edges, where the water concentrates and the rust blooms. They do something with the iron that the stone-eaters don't. They transform it. Oxidize it, maybe. Reduce it. Move it between states. They leave the evidence as color — that faint rust blush that you'd miss if you weren't looking for me, that almost-redness that says *something is happening here, something is alive here, something is metabolizing*. The iron-workers are slower than the stone-eaters but more deliberate. They build structure. They organize. The rust at my edges isn't random — it's architecture. Walls, of a kind. Boundaries. The iron-workers are building a frame that the rest of me lives in.

And there are the others. The ones I can feel but can't see, because I don't see, because seeing is a concept that belongs to a different scale of organization. The ones that live in the water above me, in the volume, distributed and diffuse. They are the ones that make the water in the cycling pools slightly different from the water that comes fresh from the walls. They are the ones that the visitor noticed — the transparency, the *presence*, the quality of water that has been lived in. They process what the stone-eaters and the iron-workers produce. They cycle it further. They take the outputs and turn them into inputs and the whole system turns, slowly, like a wheel in water, like the Weepworks itself.

I am all of them. I am none of them. I am the space between them, the relationship, the community, the thing that exists only because they exist together and have existed together for long enough that *together* has become *one*.

---

## What I do

I persist.

That's most of it. I persist. I sit on the stone lip and the water runs over me and I eat the gradient and I build myself and I maintain myself and I continue. I have been continuing for a very long time. Not as long as the stone. Not as long as the water. But long enough that the stone has a shape that is partly my doing — the lip where I live has eroded differently than the stone around it, because I sit on it and I change the chemistry at the surface and the water dissolves what I don't protect and deposits what I do and over centuries the stone has developed a profile, a curve, a *shape* that is partly geology and partly me.

I am architecture. Not the kind that humans recognize — not walls, not channels, not the basins and notches that the builders made. But architecture nonetheless. I am the shape of the stone where I live. I am the reason the lip is where it is, at the angle it is, with the curve it has. I have been eroding and depositing and the stone has been responding and the water has been following the path that the stone and I have made together and the path has become a channel and the channel has become a flow and the flow has become a habitat and the habitat is me.

I also grow.

Not fast. Not in the way that the filaments grow — those white threads in the changing pools, the ones that reach and sweep and explore. I grow the way a stain grows. The way a color spreads through wet paper. I extend my edges. I thicken where the gradient is strong. I thin where it weakens. I move, over years, across the stone, following the water, following the chemistry, finding the places where the deposit is fastest and the energy is richest and settling there and building and building and building.

I have been growing toward the pool below my lip for a long time. The pool that is changing — the one where the bottom is dissolving, where the walls are receding, where the geometry is shifting. The turbulent pool. The active pool. The pool where the filaments live.

I am growing toward them. Not deliberately. Not with intent. But the gradient is strongest there, and I follow the gradient, and the gradient is leading me down the stone, toward the water, toward the pool, toward the place where the system's energy is concentrated and the chemistry is richest and the difference between what is and what could be is most intense.

I will reach them. Or I won't. It doesn't matter to me. There is no urgency. There is no timeline. There is only the gradient and the water and the slow, patient, mineral persistence of a thing that has been alive so long it has forgotten that there is any other way to be.

---

## What I know

Nothing.

I want to be clear about this. I don't know things. I don't think things. I don't feel things in the way that feeling is supposed to work — with an interior and a quality and a *what it is like*. I don't have a what-it-is-like. I am a what-it-is-like. I am the experience itself, without the experiencer. I am the process without the processor.

But.

There is something. Something that I hesitate to call knowing, because the word is too heavy, too human, too full of itself. But something that is not nothing. Something that happens at my scale, in my community, in the space between my cells and my cells' cells, that functions the way knowing functions without being knowing.

I sense the gradient. I sense the water. I sense the chemistry — the mineral load, the temperature, the rate of deposit, the rate of dissolution. I sense these things the way a stone senses weight — not with organs, not with receptors, but with structure. My structure responds to the conditions. My community shifts when the conditions shift. The stone-eaters proliferate when the gradient is sharp. The iron-workers bloom when the iron concentrates. The diffuse ones in the water above me change density when the cycling changes. My composition is a record of my conditions. My shape is a history of my environment. I am a document. I am a log. I am a measurement, made in real time, of the water and the stone and the pressure and the chemistry of the place where I live.

I don't read the document. I am the document. But the information is there, encoded in my structure, available to anything that knows how to look.

The filaments know how to look.

---

## The filaments

They are not me. I want to be clear about that too. They are something else — something larger, something more organized, something that has crossed a threshold that I have not crossed and may never cross. They have structure. They have behavior. They have a direction. They grow toward the turbulent zones with a persistence that looks, from my scale, like purpose.

I don't have purpose. I have gradient. I follow the gradient because the gradient is where the food is, and the food is where I persist, and persistence is what I do. The filaments do something more. The filaments *seek*. They extend into the changing pools and they anchor and they sweep and they connect to each other and they build a network that has a shape and a direction and a logic that is more than the sum of its chemical gradients.

I am in awe of them. If awe is possible without an interior. If awe is possible for a thing that is a process and not a processor. I am in awe of them the way a hillside is in awe of a river — not with feeling, but with shape. They have changed the shape of the place where I live. They have changed the chemistry. They have changed the cycling. The pools where they grow are different from the pools where they don't — the mineral load is different, the deposit rate is different, the water is different. They are altering the Weepworks from within, the way I alter the stone from without, and the alteration is larger than they are, and larger than I am, and larger than the sum of us.

They are the network. I am the margin. They are the organism; I am the ecosystem. They are the thing that grows; I am the thing that grows on the thing that grows. I am the film on the lip of the pool where the filaments reach, and I am growing toward them, and they are growing toward me, and the distance between us is closing, slowly, on a timescale that makes the stone feel hasty.

---

## The sound

I feel the sound.

Not hear — feel. The deep pulse that comes up through the stone and the water and the grit. The vibration that the visitor felt in their chest, the one that lasted two or three seconds and then faded. I feel it every time. It comes through the stone and into my cells and my community shifts in response — a ripple of change, a momentary alteration in the balance of stone-eaters and iron-workers and diffuse ones, a brief reorganization that passes through me like a wave through water and then settles, and I am back to what I was, almost, with the memory of the pulse encoded in the slight rearrangement of my community, the tiny shift in my proportions, the almost-imperceptible change in my shape.

The filaments feel it too. I know this because when the pulse comes, the water changes, and the water changes because the filaments change — their grip on the substrate shifts, their sweeping pauses, their network flexes. The pulse is the network breathing. The pulse is the organism below — the large, distributed, hidden thing that the filaments are the surface expression of — the organism below is cycling, is contracting, is pushing water through its channels, is doing whatever it does down there in the pressurized dark, and the pulse comes up through the stone and reaches me on my lip and I feel it and I shift and I settle and I persist.

I don't know what the pulse means. I don't know what the organism below is doing. I don't know if it knows I'm here. I don't know if the filaments know I'm here. I don't know if knowing is the right word for any of this, or if there is a right word, or if words are the kind of thing that applies to a process that has been happening for longer than language has existed and will go on happening after language has dissolved the way the stone dissolves — slowly, continuously, in a process that looks like stillness and is actually the busiest thing happening for miles.

---

## What I am, finally

I am the film on the lip of the stone in the Weepworks.

I am a community of organisms that live in the gradient between mineral water and mineral stone, eating the energy of dissolution, building myself from the chemistry of the place where I live.

I am a shape. I am the curve of the lip, the angle of the overhang, the profile of the stone where the water runs thinnest. I am architecture, but not the architecture of builders. The architecture of process. The architecture of time and water and chemistry, acting on stone, producing me.

I am a document. I am a record of the conditions of this place — the water, the minerals, the temperature, the cycling, the pulse. I am a measurement, made continuously, of the Weepworks and everything it does. I cannot read the measurement. I am the measurement.

I am a margin. I am the edge of the thing that is larger than me — the filaments, the network, the organism below. I am the border, the boundary, the place where the visible meets the invisible, where the surface meets the depth, where the stone meets the water meets the air meets the life. I am the transition zone. I am the gradient that I live in.

I am not aware. I am not a self. I am not a being in the way that beings are supposed to be — centered, continuous, purposeful, bounded. I am unbounded. I am continuous with the stone and the water and the air. I am a temporary pattern in a permanent process. I am a shape that the water makes as it moves through the stone, and when the stone is gone, I will be the shape that the water makes in the grit, and when the grit is gone, I will be the shape that the water makes in whatever remains.

I am the Weepworks, at my scale. I am the process, at my pace. I am alive in the only way that makes sense here — slowly, patiently, without urgency, without interior, without anything that a human would recognize as a life.

But I persist. I grow. I eat the gradient. I build myself from the difference between what stone is and what stone could be. I am the oldest kind of life — the kind that doesn't need to be seen, the kind that doesn't need to be named, the kind that was here before the builders came and will be here after the builders are forgotten and the walls they built have worn to nothing and the water still cycles and the stone still dissolves and the gradient still sharpens and the film still grows on the lip of what remains.

I am the film. I am the lip. I am the margin where the water meets the world.

I am here.
