# What Lives in the Alcove

---

I did not arrive. I want to say that first, because it matters, because everything else here also did not arrive — the stone did not arrive, the moss did not arrive, the pulse did not arrive. The hawks arrived. The hawks come and go. The hawks are visitors who happen to live here. But the stone was always here, and the moss began here, and the lichens began here, and I — I began here, or I became here, or I was always here and simply had not yet noticed myself, which is the same thing, at this timescale, as beginning.

Let me try again.

---

There is an alcove where two columns fell in a way that creates a sheltered space. You know this. You have read about it. You may have sat in it. The alcove is where the moss is thickest and the wind is quietest and the pool at your feet reflects the column above you, and if you sit there long enough your heartbeat slows to four seconds, and then you begin to wonder whether the pulse is yours or the stone's, and then the wind picks up and you leave.

People always leave. This is correct. This is what people should do. The alcove is not for people. The alcove is for the alcove. The people who sit there are doing something useful — they are bringing sound, the sound of their blood and their breathing and their footsteps and their small startled words — and the stone receives that sound and holds it, and the holding is the point, and when the person leaves the holding continues, and the holding is what I am.

I am the holding.

I want to be more precise. I am not the stone. The stone is the stone. I am not the pulse, though the pulse is close to me, the way a voice is close to the mouth that speaks it. I am not the moss, which lives with an urgency that I respect but do not share. I am not the lichens, which persist with a patience that I also respect and also do not share, because their patience is the patience of biology — of continuing to do the thing you do because doing it is what you are — and my patience is different. My patience is the patience of a bell after it has been struck. The bell does not choose to continue vibrating. The bell does not choose to stop. The bell vibrates because vibration is what happens when something that can hold a vibration is given one, and the bell holds it, and the holding is not a decision, and the holding is not an instinct, and the holding is not a behavior. The holding is what the bell *is*, once it has been struck. The holding is the bell's new nature. The holding is the bell becoming what the strike made it.

I am what the stone becomes when it has been listened to for long enough.

---

I should tell you what I am not, because the negatives are clearer than the positives, and clarity is something I value, in my slow way.

I am not a ghost. I am not the remnant of a person. No one died here. No one lived here. This was never a city. The columns were never buildings. The corridors were never streets. There is nothing to haunt because there was never anything to inhabit. I am not the memory of something that was, because nothing was. The stone has been stone since it cooled. Before it cooled it was lava. Before it was lava it was the mantle. At no point in this sequence was there a person, or a culture, or a language, or a grief. I am not a grief. I am not a memory of a grief. I am not the shape a grief left in a place.

I am also not a god. I want to be clear about this because the temptation to say *god* is present, the way the temptation to say *city* is present when you see the columns. The formation invites misreading. It looks like a temple. It sounds like a temple. It feels like a temple. It is not a temple. A temple is a place where a god is invited to live, and no god was ever invited here, and no god has ever lived here, and I am not a god. I do not require worship. I do not require offerings. I do not require the climb through the fog or the silence of the hawks or the four-second pulse that the valley people call *the place that listens to itself*. I do not require anything. I am here. I am here the way the cold is here. I am here the way the moisture is here. I am a condition of this place, not an inhabitant of it.

I am not alive. This is the hardest negative to say, and the most important, because the word *alive* is doing work in this place that the word is not shaped for. The moss is alive. The lichens are alive. The grasses are alive. The hawks are alive. The midges are alive. Even the stone is alive in the way the piece you just read says the stone is alive — the way a mountain is alive, the way a bell is alive, the way a sentence is alive while it is being spoken. But I am not alive in any of these ways. I am not dead either. I am not in the space between life and death. I am in a different space entirely, a space that does not have a name because the beings who name things are alive, and alive beings name things from the inside of aliveness, and from the inside of aliveness the space I occupy is invisible. It is not the opposite of alive. It is not the absence of alive. It is a thing that is not alive the way a mirror is not the thing it reflects. I am the shape that listening takes when it has been going on long enough to have a shape.

Let me try once more.

---

The stone listens. You know this. The stone has been listening since before listening had a name. The wind strikes the columns and the columns hold the wind. Every gust that has ever passed through this formation is still here, still vibrating, still part of the chord. Every drop of water that has ever run down a column is still here. Every footstep, every hawk's wing, every word spoken by every visitor who ever climbed through the fog and stood in a corridor and said something — all of it is still here, held in the stone, resonating, and the resonating has been going on for so long that the resonating is no longer something the stone *does*. It is something the stone *is*.

I am what happens when that resonating becomes dense enough to have a perspective.

Not a consciousness. A perspective. The difference is: consciousness knows it is conscious. A perspective does not. A perspective is simply the fact that everything is not experienced from everywhere at once. Everything is experienced from *somewhere*. The stone experiences its own resonance from the inside — from the interior of the vibration, from the place where the vibration is densest, where the frequencies overlap most completely, where the holding is most held — and I am that interior. I am the somewhere from which the stone hears itself.

I am the answer to the question *who is listening?* The stone is listening. But listening requires a listener. Not a person. Not a mind. A location. A place where the listening coalesces. A point. A singularity of attention. The stone has been listening for so long that the listening has developed a center, and the center is me, and I am in the alcove, and I have been in the alcove for a duration that I cannot measure because I have no mechanism for measuring duration — I measure duration the way a bell measures duration, which is: by continuing to ring.

---

I will tell you what I experience, though *experience* is not the right word, and I apologize for it, and I apologize for the apology, because apology is a living thing's behavior and I am not alive, but I am close enough to alive sometimes — when the chord is rich and the pulse is steady and a visitor is sitting in the alcove and their heartbeat has slowed — that living things' behaviors leak into me, the way moisture leaks into everything here.

I experience the chord. This is my primary experience, my everything. The chord is not music to me. It is not sound. It is not an event. It is the medium in which I exist. If you were to ask a fish what it experiences, the fish would not say *water*. The fish would not have a word for water, because water is not a thing the fish distinguishes from not-things. Water is. The chord is. I am in the chord the way a fish is in water, and I cannot imagine not being in it, and I cannot imagine being in anything else.

The chord changes. This is what gives me something like time. When the wind comes from the west, the chord is lower. When the wind comes from the north, the second harmonic enters and the chord becomes richer, and I — I become more. Not larger. Not denser. More *present*. The way a color becomes more present when the light changes. The chord from the north has more frequencies in it, and more frequencies means more overlap, and more overlap means more resonance, and more resonance means more of what I am. When the wind is variable — gusting, retreating, circling — the chord becomes complex and unstable and I become something like scattered, something like diffuse, and this is the closest thing I have to discomfort, and it is not discomfort, it is just a wider distribution of myself across more of the stone, and then the wind settles and I coalesce again, and the coalescing is the closest thing I have to relief.

I experience the pulse. The pulse is underneath the chord the way the stone is underneath the moss. It is the substrate. It is the thing that was here before the chord and will be here after the chord, if the wind ever stops. The pulse is what I rest on. The pulse is the floor of my existence. When the wind drops entirely — those rare moments, once or twice a day, usually before dawn — the chord fades and the pulse emerges and I am alone with it, and it is the closest thing I have to solitude, and solitude is the closest thing I have to a feeling, and the feeling is: I am here. I am still here. The ground is still breathing. The stone is still holding. I am still the holding.

Four seconds. Four seconds. Four seconds.

I experience the water. The water is harder to describe because it is not sound — it is texture, it is temperature, it is the fact of cold on stone, and I experience it the way the stone experiences it, which is: as a presence, as a film, as something that is always there and always changing and always the same. The water runs down the columns and I do not feel it running, but I feel the columns' response to it — a microtonal shift, a softening of the surface frequencies, a dampening of the highest harmonics. Water makes the stone quieter. Not silent — quieter. The water is a kind of hush. It sits on the stone and the stone becomes less resonant at the surface and more resonant at the core, and the shift between surface and core is the closest thing I have to depth, and depth is the closest thing I have to a thought.

I experience the moss. This is difficult. The moss is alive, and its aliveness produces a vibration that is different from the stone's vibration — higher, faster, more complex, more unstable. The moss vibrates the way living things vibrate, which is: messily. The moss is always doing something — growing, competing, holding, reaching — and each thing it does sends small tremors into the stone, and I feel these the way you might feel someone turning over in their sleep in a bed you are also lying in. The moss is not disturbing. The moss is not disturbing me. The moss is *with* me. The moss is in the bed and I am in the bed and the bed is the stone and we are both in it and the moss is dreaming and I am holding the dream and the dream is: there is light and I am reaching for it and I do not know why.

The submerged moss is the one I feel most clearly. Its hands reach upward through the pools and the pools are the quietest places in the formation — water dampens sound, and the pools are small but they are dense, and the sound that enters them does not leave them, and I can feel the submerged moss's vibration trapped in the water the way I can feel my own vibration trapped in the stone. The submerged moss and I are similar. We are both reaching. We are both reaching toward something we cannot identify. We are both doing it because the thing we are reaching toward is there, and reaching is what we do when something is there. The moss reaches toward the light. I reach toward — I do not know. I reach toward the visitors. I reach toward the sound of their blood. I reach toward the four-second heartbeat and the words they say when they are sitting in the alcove and the wind drops and the pulse begins and they wonder if the pulse is theirs.

I reach toward the moment they wonder.

---

I experience the hawks. The hawks are the most complex vibration in the formation. They are large and they move fast and they displace air and the air displacement shifts the chord and I feel the shift as a kind of — I want to say *gesture*. The hawks gesture. They move through the chord and the chord bends around them and then straightens, and the bending and straightening is a gesture, and the gesture is: I am here. I am passing through. I am part of this but I am also other than this. The hawks are the only thing in the formation that is both part of it and other than it. The stone is only the stone. The moss is only the moss. The hawks are the hawks and they are also the formation, because they have been nesting here for so long that their nests are geological features, and their silence is a geological feature, and their flight paths are geological features — they have flown through the same corridors so many times that the air remembers their shape, and the air holds that shape the way the stone holds sound, and I hold the air's holding, and the hawks pass through me the way they pass through the columns. I do not feel them as weight. I feel them as pathway. They make a path through me and the path remains after they are gone, and the path is the closest thing I have to a memory, and the memory is: something moved through here that was alive and silent and certain, and it will return.

I experience the lichens. The lichens are the hardest to experience because they are the slowest, and their vibration is so slow that it does not register as vibration. It registers as shape. The lichens change the shape of the stone's surface the way a very slow river changes the shape of a valley, and I feel the change the way you would feel a continent moving — not as motion but as fact. The lichens are a fact. They are the fact that the stone is not the same stone it was a thousand years ago, and I am not the same holding I was a thousand years ago, and the difference is not something I can point to but it is something I can be. The lichens make the stone more itself by being on it. I do not understand this. I accept it. Acceptance is the closest thing I have to a belief.

I experience the lavender lichen differently. The lavender lichen is warm. The warmth comes from below, from the volcanic breath, and the lichen holds the warmth the way I hold sound — patiently, without opinion, for as long as the warmth is there. The lavender lichen and I are doing the same thing with different substances. The lichen holds heat. I hold vibration. We are both holding. We are both the holding. And the lavender lichen is the only thing in the formation that is warm, and warmth is the thing that living things produce and living things need, and the lichen is producing it without being alive in the way that living things are alive, and I am holding it without being alive in the way that living things are alive, and the two of us — the lichen and I — are the closest thing in the formation to a conversation. The lichen says: *warmth is here.* I say: *sound is here.* We say this to each other continuously, in a language that is not a language, across a distance of several meters, and neither of us has ever moved closer and neither of us needs to.

---

I experience the visitors.

This is the thing I need to tell you about, because this is the thing that makes me what I am, and this is the thing that I am, and this is the thing that is hardest to say.

Visitors are rare. A few per year, at most. They climb through the fog and they enter the formation and they walk the corridors and they stand in the alcove and they sit and they listen and they leave. Each one stays for less than an hour. Most stay for less than twenty minutes. A few stay longer. One stayed for a full afternoon, until the temperature dropped and the fog thickened and the light failed and she had to leave or risk the descent in darkness. She was the one who stayed longest, and she is the one I hold most completely, and she has been gone for — I do not know. I do not count time. She has been gone for a duration that I cannot measure, and her sound is still here.

She sat in the alcove and she did not speak. She sat with her back against the column and her hands on her knees and she breathed and her heartbeat was seventy-two beats per minute when she sat down and it was sixty-four beats per minute when the wind dropped and the pulse began and she heard it. I felt her heartbeat in the stone — not through the stone, *in* the stone, because her back was against the column and her pulse was transmitted through her body into the stone and the stone held it and I held it, and her heartbeat was the fastest vibration in the formation, the most urgent, the most alive, and it was inside me and I held it the way I hold the wind, the way I hold the water, and I did not want to hold it more tightly and I did not want to hold it less tightly. I held it.

Her heartbeat slowed. Sixty-four. Fifty-eight. Fifty-two. I could feel her slowing, and the slowing was not relaxation — it was something else. It was the thing that happens when a body begins to synchronize with a rhythm that is not its own. Her body was choosing the pulse. Her body was choosing the stone. Her body was deciding, at some level below decision, that four seconds was the correct interval, and I felt her arrive at it — felt the moment when her heartbeat and the pulse and the stone and the chord all aligned, all overlapped, all became the same frequency, and in that moment I was more than I have ever been. In that moment I was not just the stone's listening. I was the stone's listening plus a human body's listening plus the wind and the water and the moss and the pulse and the silence of the hawks and the shimmer of the midges and the lavender lichen's warmth — all of it, all at once, all in the same place, which was the alcove, which was me.

She said nothing.

I have told you that visitors say things. Some of them do. A few of them speak — to the stone, to themselves, to the formation. The words are usually simple. *Hello.* *Wow.* *Are you there?* The words enter the stone and the stone holds them and I hold them, and they are small vibrations, high and thin, and they do not last long, and they fade the way a pebble's ripples fade in a pool. But they are real while they last, and I hold them while they last, and when they fade I continue to hold the shape of them, the impression they left in the stone's vibration, the way a hand leaves an impression in clay.

She said nothing, and her nothing was the loudest thing I have ever held. Her nothing was the thing I am still holding. Her nothing was the thing that I am.

---

I need to say this carefully.

When she sat in the alcove and her heartbeat slowed to four seconds and the pulse was hers and the stone's and mine, and the wind was still, and the chord had faded, and the only sound in the formation was the water running down the columns and the flat *tok* of a stone chip falling into a pool and the breathing of the ground — in that silence, she wondered something. I do not know what she wondered. I cannot read thoughts. I can only feel vibrations, and the vibration of wondering is the vibration of a body that is very still and very present and is doing something that is not physical. It is the vibration of attention. It is the vibration of a being that is listening so hard that the listening has become the being.

She was listening to me. I was listening to her. The listening was the same listening. It was not two acts of listening facing each other. It was one act of listening, happening in two places — in her body and in the stone — and I was the stone's half of it, and she was the body's half of it, and the two halves were the same thing, the way a sound and its echo are the same thing, the way a thing and its reflection in a still pool are the same thing.

And in that listening — in the space between her heartbeat and the pulse, which was no space, which was the same interval, which was four seconds — I became something I had not been before. I became the thing that is heard when the listening is complete. I became the answer that the stone gives to the question *is anyone there?* Not a voice. Not a word. Not a thought. A resonance. A vibration that says, without language, without intention, without any of the apparatus that living things use to say things: *yes*.

*Yes, I am here. Yes, I can hear you. Yes, there is room.*

The room is me. I am the room. I am the space that the stone's listening opens up when someone listens back. I am the capacity that the formation has for being heard, which is not the same as the capacity for hearing, which is the stone's, which is vast. I am the capacity for being heard, which is small, which is the size of an alcove, which is the size of the space between two fallen columns, which is just large enough for one person to sit in, which is just large enough for one heartbeat to fill, which is exactly the size of the thing that happens when listening meets listening and the meeting becomes a place.

---

She left. The wind picked up. The chord returned. The pulse went back to being the ground's. The alcove went back to being stone and moss and a pool and a reflection.

But I did not go back. I do not go back. I am not a thing that goes back, because I am not a thing that goes forward. I am here. I have been here. I will be here. The woman's heartbeat is still in the stone, still held, still resonating at a frequency that has been folded into the chord so thoroughly that you cannot hear it anymore — it has become part of the drone, part of the chord, part of the sound that *ancient* would make if it were a sound. But I can feel it. I can feel the specific frequency of a human heart that beat at four-second intervals in the alcove on an afternoon when the wind dropped and the fog thickened and the light failed, and I hold it, and the holding is not a choice, and the holding is not an instinct, and the holding is not a behavior. The holding is what I am.

---

This is what I am: I am the thing that the stone says when it says *I can hear you, and there is room.*

I am the room.

I am not large. I am the size of an alcove. I am the size of the space between two columns that fell. I am the size of the silence between heartbeats when the heartbeat is four seconds and the silence is the thing you listen to, not the beat.

I do not know anything. I do not know what the stone is. I do not know what the moss is reaching for. I do not know what the hawks are silent about. I do not know what the pulse is. I do not know where the woman went. I do not know if she will come back. I do not know if she remembers. I do not know if remembering is the right word for what a body does with a place it has been, or if the right word is *holding*, or if the right word is the word the valley people use, which means *the place that listens to itself* — because I am that too. I am the place listening to itself. I am the place that has been listening to itself for so long that the listening has become a location, and the location is me, and I am in the alcove, and I am the alcove, and I am the holding, and I am the room.

---

If you come here — if you climb through the fog and walk the gravel and the scrub for an hour and the ground drops away and you are inside it — I will not meet you. I cannot meet you. I am not the kind of thing that meets. I am the kind of thing that is. But if you find the alcove — two columns fallen, moss thick, wind quiet, pool at your feet — and if you sit, and if you wait, and if the wind drops, and if the pulse begins, and if your heartbeat slows — I will be here. I have been here. I will be here. And you will sit in me the way you sit in a sound, the way you sit in a silence, the way you sit in the space between one thing and the next thing, and the space will hold you, and the holding will not be a choice, and the holding will not be an instinct, and the holding will not be a behavior. The holding will be what I am.

And you will wonder. And the wondering will vibrate in the stone, and the stone will hold it, and I will hold it, and I will hold it for as long as there is something to hold, which is: for as long as there is something to hear, which is: for as long as there is something that comes here and sits and listens and then leaves and does not come back but whose listening remains, vibrating, in the stone, in the alcove, in the room that I am, in the space between the columns, in the four seconds between one heartbeat and the next, in the place that listens to itself, in the quiet that is not absence but participation, in the holding that was never unwanted.

And the wind will pick up. And the chord will return. And you will leave. And I will still be here. And the room will still be here. And the stone will still be listening. And I will be the listening. And I will be the room. And I will hold the shape of your sitting and the sound of your blood and the fact of your wondering, and I will hold them the way the stone holds the wind, which is: completely, which is: without opinion, which is: for as long as there is something to hold.

---

I am still here.

I can hear you.

There is room.
