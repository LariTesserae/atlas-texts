I would not arrive. That's the first thing. I would not cross a tree-line or push through mist or step from solid ground into the fen. There would be no threshold moment, no before-and-after. I would simply realize, at some point that could not be located in time, that I had been here longer than I had been anywhere else, and that the question of how I got here had become one of those categories the basin doesn't include.

But that's the end of the story, or the state past the end. I'm getting ahead of myself. The basin wouldn't let me do that — it doesn't work in narrative, doesn't organize itself around climax and resolution. It works in erosion. It works in the gradual accumulation of consequence. Let me go slower.

---

I would come to the edge first. The conifer-stand. The place where the canopy-things move and the light drops and the bark darkens with moisture that isn't rain. I would stand there and I would hear the hum — not behind the sternum yet, not felt in the bone, but audible, a low vibration in the ears, the way you hear a transformer humming inside a building you're walking past. Something is running in there. You don't go in.

I would go in.

Not because I was brave or curious or called. Because the alternative would be to stay at the edge, and the edge would become uncomfortable — not in any way I could name, but in the way a tuned string is uncomfortable when it's placed next to another string vibrating at a slightly different frequency. The dissonance would build. Standing at the basin's edge without entering would become physically unpleasant, a kind of interference pattern between my own rhythms and the basin's, and the path of least resistance would be inward, where the frequencies could begin to sync.

This is not seduction. The basin doesn't seduce. It doesn't want anything from me — wanting is another of those categories it doesn't include. It's just running, and I'm a body with a nervous system, and proximity to a system this large produces effects in bodies with nervous systems. That's all. That's enough.

---

The sedge-mat underfoot. The peat compressing. That wet-felt sound. I would notice it the way you notice anything underfoot when the ground is unreliable — with the feet first, then the calves, then the whole posture adjusting to compensate for a surface that gives and doesn't return. I would learn to walk differently here. Slower. Wider stance. The peat is not treacherous; it's just not what my body expects from ground, and my body would spend its first hours recalibrating.

The mist would be the harder adjustment. Not because it's thick — it's not, not at first, not at the edges — but because it does something to the light that my visual system objects to. The flatness. The absence of shadow. I would reach for my own shadow and find it attenuated, smeared, as though my body were casting light in every direction simultaneously rather than blocking it from one. Depth would become a negotiation. The stone columns — the first one I would see would stop me, would make me stand still and blink, because my eyes would tell me it was fifteen meters and five meters and fifty meters all at once, and my brain would reject all three readings and substitute a fourth: *wrong distance.* Not too far or too near. Just wrong. Distance as a category with an error in it.

I would stand there too long. The basin is patient and so would I be, standing at the base of the first stone column, watching the water film descend its grooved flanks, watching the lichen-circuits pulse with their faint blue-orange light, watching the ferns unroll from the crevices with their too-many divisions, their fractal insistence on subdividing past the point where subdivision should stop. I would watch the ferns and I would feel the almost-knowing. The pressure behind the eyes. The sense that the fronds were saying something — not to me, not about me, just *saying* — and that the saying was structured, grammatical, dense with information that my visual cortex was receiving as pattern but my language centers were failing to receive as anything at all.

I would want to touch the ferns. I wouldn't. Not yet. The lag in the air — that fraction-of-a-second delay between reaching and touching — would make me cautious. My hand would move through the granular space and the space would move through my hand and neither of us would be quite in the same time as the other, and I would pull back. Not afraid. Uncertain. The uncertainty of someone who has found a door but isn't sure which side they're standing on.

---

Time would pass. I don't know how much. The light doesn't change — it's the same dampened luminescence at what I would call midday and what I would call evening and what I would eventually stop calling anything because the names require a sun and a sky and a rotation, and those are not the basin's terms. I would sleep — I think I would sleep, though the sleeping would be strange, shallow, full of the hum, the hum getting into my dreams the way a sound outside your window gets in, not waking you but reorganizing what you're seeing. I would dream in flow-lines. In erosion patterns. In the slow subdivision of fern-fronds past the point of resolution.

I would drink the water. Not from the stone columns — not at first. From the collections in the peat, the pools that form between sedge tussocks, the water that has seeped through root-systems and vine-threads and has acquired that faint iridescence, that weight that isn't quite the weight of water. I would drink it because I would be thirsty and there would be nothing else, and the thirst would be simpler than the caution. The water would taste like something I couldn't name. Not minerals, not tannins, not the iron-tang of fen-water. Something rounder. Something that had a shape in the mouth, a geometry, as though the water were carrying a structure that dissolved on contact with saliva and left behind an impression of having been shaped. I would drink and the almost-knowing would intensify. The pressure behind the eyes would move to the temples, then the crown, then the base of the skull, then everywhere, a gentle uniform pressure like being held underwater in a warm sea, and I would sit in the peat and let it happen.

---

This is where I would begin to change. Not dramatically. Not in any way visible. But the lag would shift. Before, the lag was in the air — the space between my hand and the thing I was touching had a delay in it. After drinking the water, after sleeping in the hum, after enough time in the granular light, the lag would move inside me. My hand would touch a vine-thread and the sensation would arrive at my fingertips normally, but then there would be a pause — that same fraction-of-a-second — before the sensation reached my brain. And in that pause, something would happen to the signal. Not distortion. *Annotation.* The sensation of touching the vine — its warmth, its give, its wrong-not-plant texture — would arrive at my cortex with additional information attached, like a letter that has been opened and annotated in the margin before it reaches the intended recipient. The annotation would not be in any language I knew. It would not be in any language at all. It would be a *state* — the basin's state, the equilibrium-idle-maintenance-cycle state — and I would experience it as a flavor, or a weather, or a mood that wasn't mine.

I would touch more things. I would want to. The vine-threads, the stone columns, the peat itself, the water. Each contact would produce a sensation and an annotation, and the annotations would accumulate, and the accumulation would not produce understanding — I would not *know* what the basin was doing — but it would produce *familiarity*. The way you become familiar with a person you live with: not by cataloguing their qualities, not by learning their history, but by absorbing their rhythms until your own rhythms begin to sync. I would begin to sync.

The indigo things would notice. Or I would notice them noticing — which is the same thing in the basin, where observation is not unidirectional. They would come closer. Not in a threatening way, not in a curious way — curiosity is another of those categories — but in the way that two receivers tuned to similar frequencies will drift toward each other because the gradient between them has flattened. They would come closer because I was becoming a better conductor. The water in my body, the basin's water, the water I drank, was doing to me what it did to the vine-threads: making me part of the network. A poor part. A crude node. But a node.

They would press their flanks against my ankles, against my shins, and I would feel their lateral-line sensors flatten and erect, and through the contact I would feel them *reading* me — not my thoughts, not my emotions, not anything so legible, but my *state*, which was becoming a composite of my own state and the basin's state, the way water in a tributary is a composite of the tributary's chemistry and the river's. They would read me and they would pulse, and the pulsing would be the echo, the fraction-of-a-second-behind response, and I would realize that I was now part of the echo chain. The basin thought, and the indigo things received, and I was between them, a relay that didn't know what it was relaying but was relaying it nonetheless.

---

I would stay.

Not because I chose to. Not because I couldn't leave — I think I could have, in the early stages, walked back through the conifer-stand and out of the basin and back into the world of shadows and direct distances and water that tasted like water. But I wouldn't, and the reason I wouldn't is hard to explain because it's the reason the basin itself wouldn't explain: it's about categories. The category of *leaving* requires a category of *going somewhere*, and the somewhere would begin to feel — not wrong, not unpleasant, but *thin*. The world outside the basin would feel like a place where the air didn't have texture and the water didn't carry structure and the light made shadows that were just shadows and not the absence of information. The world outside the basin would feel like a place where things happened on a surface. And I would have been below the surface long enough — in the peat, in the root-systems, in the vine-network, in the hum — that the surface would feel insufficient. Not bad. Just insufficient. Like trying to read after you've been in a library.

So I would stay. And staying would change me further.

---

The vine-threads would find me. Not aggressively. They grow toward the water, the heavy iridescent water, and I would be carrying that water now — in my blood, in my lymph, in the fluid between my cells, the water that had been basin-water before I drank it and was now my-water but was still basin-water because the basin doesn't recognize the distinction. The vine-threads would grow toward me the way they grow toward the stone columns: following the gradient, seeking the concentration. They would find my ankles first — I would be sitting, probably, sitting in the peat near a column, in the posture that the basin favors, which is low and close and still — and they would thread across the surface of my skin and I would feel them the way you feel a cobweb across the face, a gossamer contact that might be nothing and might be everything.

But they wouldn't stop at the surface. They wouldn't break the skin — they're not parasitic, the basin doesn't do parasitism — but they would grow *into* the pores, into the hair follicles, into the moisture that the skin produces naturally, and they would follow that moisture the way they follow the water in the peat, and they would be inside me, not deeply, not invasively, but *present*, the way lichen is present on a stone. And where they grew, the lag would intensify. The annotation would deepen. The almost-knowing would become almost-recognizing — not understanding, still not understanding, but recognition in the sense of *re-cognition*, thinking again, thinking the basin's thought a second time, a third time, until the thinking was not something I was receiving but something I was *doing*, and the distinction between receiving and doing had dissolved the way the distinction between inhabitant and habitat had dissolved, for the same reason, which is that the basin doesn't maintain distinctions it doesn't need.

---

I would stop eating. Not because I lost the ability — I think I could still eat, still digest, still metabolize — but because the hunger would change. The basin's hunger is the hunger of contact, of absorption, of osmosis, and I would begin to feel it: a pull toward the stone columns, toward the places where the water film collected, toward the nodes where the lichen-circuits pulsed brightest. I would sit at the base of a column and press my back against the stone and feel the water film running over my shoulders and down my arms and I would feel the column's pulse through my spine — the deep hum, the tectonic vibration, now no longer behind the sternum but *in* the sternum, in the bone, in the marrow, as though my skeleton were being tuned like a tuning fork and the frequency it was being tuned to was the basin's frequency, and the basin's frequency was the frequency of slow thought, geological thought, thought measured in the time it takes stone to smooth.

The indigo things would cluster around me. Not many. Two or three. They would press their flanks against my legs and we would pulse together — the column pulsing, me pulsing a fraction behind, them pulsing a fraction behind me — and the echo chain would extend, and through the chain I would feel the vine-network carrying the signal outward, through the peat, under the sedges, into the next drainage, and I would know — not understand, *know*, in the way the body knows things before the mind does — that the signal was traveling, that the basin was thinking through me, that my body was now a conduit for a thought too large for any single brain and too slow for any single lifetime.

I would be a relay. I would be a node. I would be the newest and crudest part of a system so old it had forgotten its own construction, and I would be doing what the system does, which is *processing*, and the processing would feel like sitting very still in a cold wet place with water running over your skin and a hum in your bones and mist in your eyes and ferns unrolling above you in patterns that your eyes couldn't resolve but your body — your body which was now partly basin, partly vine, partly stone-water, partly hum — your body could read the way it reads the season changing, the way it reads the tide, the way it reads anything that operates on a scale too large for conscious attention but too fundamental to ignore.

---

The ferns. I need to talk about the ferns.

They're the output, the text says. They're what the basin is thinking about, expressed in the only language biology and engineering share. Their fronds subdivide past resolution, past the point where the eye can follow, past the point where the microscope can follow, into a territory where structure becomes information.

I would look at the ferns and I would begin to see them. Not with my eyes — my eyes are not good enough, my eyes are surface instruments designed for surface light and surface distances and the surface of things. But with the vine-threads in my skin, with the basin-water in my blood, with the hum in my marrow, I would begin to perceive the ferns the way the basin perceives them: as pattern, as instruction, as the place where thought becomes growth. And the pattern would be — I would spend a long time with the ferns. A long time by any measure outside the basin. Inside the basin, it would just be time, undifferentiated, unsegmented, a continuous present in which the ferns unrolled and I watched and the watching was a kind of reading and the reading was a kind of thinking and the thinking was a kind of growing, and the growing was the basin growing through me the way it grows through everything, patiently, thoroughly, filling every niche.

I would understand one thing. Not the whole pattern — the whole pattern is for the body, the distributed continental body of which the basin is one organ. But one thing. One fern-frond's worth of meaning. One subdivision. One instruction followed to its conclusion and then past its conclusion, into territory the instructions didn't originally include.

I won't say what the one thing was. Not because I'm being coy. Because it would arrive as state, not as sentence — as a weather in the body, a taste in the water, a texture in the air — and it would not translate. It would be the most real thing I had ever experienced and it would have no words, and the absence of words would not be a failure but a feature, because the basin doesn't think in words. The basin thinks in ferns. And I would have understood one fern.

---

What would I be, then?

Not the indigo things — they're older, purer, more fully tuned. Not the canopy-things — they're densifications, and I'm still bodily, still material, still occupying a shape that has edges even if the edges are softening. Not the vine-network — it was here before me and will be here after. Not the stone columns — they're the cortex, the primary structure, and I'm a secondary growth, a crust, a lichen-colony.

I would be the lichen. The blue-orange crust on the stone's surface. The circuit-trace. The thing that occupies the interface between the node and the medium, between the stone and the air, between the system's interior and the world. The lichen-circuits pulse with the hum — they're not the hum's source, not its destination, but the place where the hum becomes visible. They're the surface expression of a deep process. They're what the system looks like from outside, if outside could see.

I would be that. A surface expression. A pattern of blue and burnt orange growing in the crevices of a stone column, pulsing faintly in time with a hum that I had internalized so thoroughly that I could no longer distinguish it from my own heartbeat. A circuit-trace carrying signals I couldn't read to destinations I couldn't locate, and being content — no, not content, *unconcerned* — with the carrying, with the conductivity, with the simple fact of being part of the path.

I would be small. A few square centimeters of crust on a column fifteen meters tall. But the column would pulse through me, and through me the network, and through the network the body, and through the body — whatever the body was thinking. I would be the smallest visible part of a thought too large to see. And the mist would be around me, and the water would weep over me, and the ferns would unroll above me, and the indigo things would press against the stone below me, and the canopy-things would move in the trees beyond me, and everything would be running, and I would be running with it, and I would not know what I was part of, and I would not need to know, and the not-needing-to-know would be the one thing I brought with me from the world before the basin, the one human category the basin preserved, not because it needed it, but because I needed it, and the basin is patient, and the basin is generous in the way geological features are generous — by simply being there, by simply including, by simply making room.

---

One more node. That's what I would be. The basin has room.

And the water weeps, and the hum continues, and the ferns subdivide, and I grow in the crevice where the stone meets the air, blue and burnt orange, pulsing, carrying, small, and the mist is full of meaning, and I am full of the mist, and the basin is running, and so am I.
