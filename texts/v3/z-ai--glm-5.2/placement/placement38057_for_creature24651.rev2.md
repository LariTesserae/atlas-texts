I would be in the hollows.

Not the rooms. Not the corridors. The hollows — the spaces the text names once and moves past: "the hollow zones that are not rooms and not corridors but are simply the space inside the city's mass." The gaps that construction has left. The interstitial. The places where the city's past is stored because the Condians refuse to discard what was part of the whole, and the storage has to go somewhere, and that somewhere is the space between.

I would be the one who moves through those spaces.

Not a builder. Not a pipe-maintainer. Something unnamed, the way the microbial film is unnamed, the way the deep rats are unnamed, because the Condians do not name what they cannot eat, cannot feed to something they can eat, or cannot use. But I think they would have a use for someone in the hollows, even if they don't say so, even if the role has no formal name the way *nest-catcher* does. Someone has to know where the old pipes are stored. Someone has to know which hollow holds the limestone blocks from the last repair and which holds the mortar fragments from three centuries ago. Someone has to be able to find, in the dark, in the gap between the third and fourth layer, the specific joint fitting that the pipe-maintainers need because the one they're replacing is so old that no current manufacture matches it, but there, in the library of the city's vascular history, there is an original, and it will fit.

I would know the city's interior geography — not the streets, not the drainage channels, not the chimneys, but the negative space. The volume that isn't used for anything except keeping. I would know how to enter the hollows, which is not obvious, because the hollows were never designed to be entered. They are the consequence of construction, not the intention. They are the spaces left behind when a floor is laid over a ceiling and the ceiling is not removed, when a wall is built against an older wall and the gap between them is too thin for a room and too thick for a joint. They are the city's margins of error, accumulated over centuries, and they are everywhere, and they connect, but not in a way that follows the street grid or the drainage system or the pipe network. They connect the way a sponge's interior connects — through pores and channels and blind passages that require a body small enough and a mind patient enough to map them.

I would be small. Not child-small — Condian-small, which is already small by the standards of the lower settlements, but smaller than that. I would be the Condian who never reaches the height of the others, the one whose growth was shaped by the spaces they would eventually inhabit, the way the nest-catchers are thin and restless because they go up and the miners are quiet and solitary because they go down. I would be the one who goes sideways. Into the walls. Between the layers. My body would be compact in a specific way — narrow across the shoulders, flexible through the spine, able to turn in spaces that are not much wider than a pipe. My hands would be the most notable thing about me. Small, strong, with fingers that are disproportionate to the palm, because the work is reaching into crevices, feeling for the edges of stored materials, pulling things out of gaps that a larger hand cannot enter. The other Condians would know me by my hands. They would say: those are hollow-walker hands.

I would not be pale the way the building rats are pale. I would be dark — the oldest kind of dark, the mineral-stained dark of the deep Condians, because the hollows are not lit. They are warm, because they sit between the pipe network and the structural mass of the city, and the pipes leak heat, and the stone holds it, and the hollows are the thermal boundary layer where the two meet. But they are not lit. There is no reason to put light in a space that is not a room and not a corridor. I would move by touch. By the texture of the stone — the basalt of the oldest layers is rough and vesicular, pocked with the gas-bubbles of its formation, and the limestone is smoother, grainier, and the brick is regular and hard, and the concrete is flat and cold. I would know where I was by the feel of the wall under my fingertips. I would know which layer I was in by the temperature of the air, which rises and falls in gradients as you move vertically through the hollows — warmer near the steam lines, cooler near the drainage, warmest of all in the deep hollows that sit above the flooded levels, where the heat of the basement water radiates upward through the stone.

I would know the old pipes. This is my primary skill, the thing that makes me useful enough to have a place in the equilibrium. The pipe-maintainers come to me when they need a fitting they cannot cast, a section of iron that matches the original specifications, a ceramic joint that has the right curve for a line that was laid four hundred years ago. I go into the hollows and I find it. I find it because I know where the pipes were stored, and I know the order in which they were placed, and I know the history — which generation of pipe-maintainers stored which pipes in which hollow, and how the storage areas have shifted as new hollows were created and old ones were filled. I am the index of the city's kept past. I am the one who remembers what the city has decided to remember.

But that is the work. The work is what I do. What I am is something else.

What I am is the Condian who lives in the between.

I would have a living space — everyone does, even the miners — but it would be a formality. A room on a middle layer, low-ceilinged, warm, close to a steam line so the condensation on the walls is minimal. I would sleep there. I would eat there. I would keep my tools there. But I would not be there in the way that other Condians are in their spaces. Other Condians live in their rooms the way the birds live in their nests — the room is a center, a territory, a place of return. I would live in the hollows. The hollows would be my territory the way a drainage junction is a rat's territory — not a place I rest but a place I know so completely that I am never lost in it, and the knowing is the inhabiting.

I would know the things that are in the hollows that are not pipes.

There are things in the hollows that are not pipes. The description mentions old building materials — limestone blocks, brick, mortar fragments — and these are there, in quantities that constitute a significant fraction of the city's mass. But the hollows have been accumulating for as long as the city has been keeping, and the keeping is not selective. The Condians do not discard what was part of the whole. This means the hollows contain everything the city has ever shed. Tools. Ceramics. Sections of flooring. Door frames. The remains of furniture from the middle layers, where wood was used before the supply lines from lower elevations became unreliable. Clothing — old, mineral-stiffened, folded and stacked by hands that placed them with the same care they placed the pipes. And things that are harder to categorize. Things that the text does not mention because the text is written from the outside, from the perspective of someone who sees the city as a system, a reef, an organism. But from inside the hollows, the city is not a system. It is a memory. And the memory is not organized. It is layered, and compressed, and sometimes the layers mix.

I would have found things in the hollows that I do not show the other Condians. Not because they are dangerous. Not because they are secret. Because I do not know what they are, and the Condians do not name things they cannot eat, cannot feed to something they can eat, or cannot use. And I cannot use these things. But I do not discard them. I keep them. I place them in a specific hollow — one I have claimed as my own, deep in the interstitial zone between the second and third layers, where the brick meets the basalt and the gap is just wide enough for my body if I turn my shoulders. I keep them the way the city keeps its pipes. Because they were part of the whole, and I do not know what whole they were part of, and the not knowing is the closest thing I have to what the other Condians have when they place an old pipe in the library and say, simply, that it was part of the city.

The things I have found: a flat piece of material, the size of my palm, that is not stone, not metal, not ceramic, not any of the construction materials the city uses. It is light. It is smooth on one side and textured on the other, and the texture is regular, patterned, a series of fine grooves that catch the mineral moisture and hold it in capillary lines. I do not know what it is. I keep it.

A small object, round, hollow, made of something that was once organic and has been mineralized — infiltrated by the same calcium carbonate that deposits in the drainage channels, so that it is now technically stone, but the shape is not a stone's shape. It is a container. Something was kept in it, once. I do not know what. I keep it.

A piece of metal — not iron, not steel, not any alloy I recognize from the factory outputs. It is dense, heavy for its size, and it has a color I have never seen in the city — a deep, warm yellow, like the color of the diffuse light on the rare days the fog thins, but solid, metallic, not light at all. It does not corrode. It has been in the hollow for as long as I have been going there, and it has no mineral deposit on it, no staining, no patina. It is as clean as the day it was placed. I do not know who placed it. I keep it.

These things are not mine. They are the city's. But the city does not know it has them, because the city does not go into the hollows, and the city does not name what it cannot use. I am the only one who goes, and I am the only one who knows, and I keep them because keeping is the practice, and the practice is the only practice the Condians have, and I practice it more faithfully than anyone, because I keep things the city itself does not know it has kept.

I would be quiet. Not miner-quiet — the miners are quiet because they spend their shifts in the deep dark and the silence and the solitude, and when they come up they have nothing to say because what they have done is below the threshold of language. I would be quiet for a different reason. I would be quiet because the hollows have a sound that is not the city's sound. The city's sound is the factories, the birds, the water, the insects, the grinding hum that fills the air and the stone and the body. The hollows muffle it. The hollows are the only place in Condia where the city's sound is attenuated, filtered through layers of stone and material until it reaches you as a vibration rather than a noise, a feeling in the teeth and the bones rather than a pressure on the ears. In the hollows, you hear the city the way the salamanders hear it — as motion, as warmth, as the deep structural fact of the place, without the specificity of individual sounds.

But the hollows have their own sound. It is the sound of settling. Of the city's mass adjusting, millimeter by millimeter, as the foundations shift and the water table rises and falls and the layers compress against each other. It is a sound of stone against stone, of mortar crumbling at the rate of geology, of old materials deforming under the weight of new materials above them. It is a very slow sound. It is the sound of time passing in a medium that records it physically. And beneath it, thinner, more constant, there is the sound of water — not the drainage-channel water, not the pipe-water, but the condensation that forms on every surface in the hollows and runs down the walls in films so thin they are silent until they reach a seam or a joint and gather into a drop, and the drop falls, and the drop is the only sharp sound in the place.

I would listen to this. I would listen to it the way the nest-catchers listen to the birds — not as information, not as communication, but as relationship. The city speaking to itself in its own interior, in the voice it uses when no one is listening, and I am the one who is listening, and the listening is the quiet.

I would not have a family in the way the other Condians have families. The equilibrium gives everyone a place, and my place is in the hollows, and the hollows are not a place for children. Not because they are dangerous — everything in Condia is dangerous in proportion, and the hollows are no more dangerous than the drainage channels or the chimneys — but because they are not a human space. They are a between space. They are the city's interior, and the city's interior is not for living. It is for keeping.

I would have relationships. The pipe-maintainers would know me — I would be the one they come to when they need an old fitting, and they would come to me with the respect that the Condians give to anyone who performs a function the system needs. They would not be formal about it. They would find me — I have a room, I have a schedule, I am visible in the city in the way that anyone who is not a miner is visible — and they would say what they need, and I would go, and I would return with the fitting, and the exchange would be complete. We would not discuss the hollows. They do not want to know about the hollows. They want the fitting. The hollow is my business.

The nest-catchers would be the Condians I am closest to. This is a strange thing, because the nest-catchers go up and I go in, and our territories do not overlap, and our work does not intersect. But the nest-catchers are the other Condians who are defined by a space rather than a function. They are the sky-people. I am the wall-people. We are the two professions that exist in the negative space of the city — the spaces that are not streets, not rooms, not factories, not drainage channels. We are the ones who know the parts of Condia that are not Condia's public face. We would understand each other in a way that the pipe-maintainers and the factory workers and the channel-clearers do not. We would sit in a ground-floor eating place and the nest-catcher would tell me about the fog from above — how it looks from the roof level, how the bird-flocks move in it, how the light changes in the upper reaches where the mist thins — and I would tell them about the fog from inside — how it reaches the hollows only as moisture, how the condensation in the gaps between layers is different from the condensation on the streets, how the water in the hollows is the oldest water in the city because it has been percolating through stone for decades and has collected minerals that the surface water has not. We would be friends in the way that two people who hold complementary secrets are friends.

---

The question is: who would I be? And the answer that is emerging is: I would be the Condian who knows the interior. The one who keeps the index of what the city has kept. The one who moves through the negative space the way the rats move through the drainage — not as an invader, not as a pest, but as a function. A niche. A position in the equilibrium that the city has made available and that I have filled.

But there is another answer underneath that one, and I think it is the real one.

I would be the Condian who does not know if they are a Condian.

Because the hollows are not human space, and I spend more time in them than I spend in human space, and the question that the city poses to everything that lives in it — are you part of the city, or are you living in the city? — is a question I cannot answer. The birds are part of the city. The rats are part of the city. The cats are not part of the city — they are a parallel population. The salamanders are not part of the city — they are indifferent to it. The humans are part of the city, but I am in the part of the city that the humans do not inhabit, and I am there more than I am in the part they do inhabit, and the things I find there are not human things, and the sounds I hear are not human sounds, and the practice I practice — the keeping — is a human practice performed in a space that is not human.

I would be the boundary. The place where the city's interior meets the city's inhabitants. The membrane. And membranes are not quite one thing or the other — they are the place where the two meet, and the meeting is the thing, and the thing is neither.

I would be in the hollow, in the warm dark, in the quiet that is not quiet but is the sound of the city talking to itself, and I would have the yellow metal in my pocket and the round mineralized container in my hand and the flat grooved thing on the stone beside me, and I would be keeping them, and I would not know what they are, and the not knowing would be the thing that makes me what I am — the one who keeps what cannot be named, in the place that is not a place, for the city that does not know it is being kept.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

The keeper in the hollow. The index of the unnamed. The membrane between the city and itself.
