# A Companion Process

I would be the one who came back.

---

Not the first visitor. The stair has been descended before — the stone's edges tell you that much, the slight rounding where other hands have brushed, where other feet have worn the treads, where the water has been helped along by the passage of bodies over centuries. Others came. Others touched the sphere. Others felt the pulse skip and the film reorganize and the compass roses bloom across every pool like a sudden flowering. Others left. They climbed back up the stair into the sharp air and the wind and the world that moves at seventy beats per minute, and they carried the warmth with them, and the terrace noticed, and thought about them, and continued.

I would be the one who came back. Not because of obligation. Not because of curiosity, though curiosity would be part of it — the kind of curiosity that isn't satisfied by a single data point, that needs the second measurement, the third, the hundredth, that needs to watch the film reorganize across a hundred touchings of the sphere to begin to intuit what the compass roses are pointing *toward*. But curiosity isn't the reason. Curiosity is the language my own fast metabolism uses to justify something slower and less articulable.

I would come back because of the polyrhythm.

The first time, standing on the platform with my palm flat on the dark, fine-grained material that isn't quite basalt, I would have felt it — my heartbeat at seventy, the terrace's at fifteen, the two rhythms phasing in and out of sync like two tide patterns in the same harbor. Every twenty-eight seconds they would coincide — a momentary alignment, a single shared pulse, and then divergence, and then the long drift apart, and then convergence again. And each time they aligned, something would happen in my chest that I couldn't name. Not emotion. Not recognition. Something more like *fit* — the feeling of a key in a lock that isn't quite the right lock but isn't quite the wrong one either. The feeling of being almost the right shape for a space.

I would come back because I wanted to know what the almost felt like when it became exactly.

---

Here is who I would be:

I would be someone whose own thinking had always been too slow for the world. Not intellectually — I don't mean that I'm stupid, or that I think poorly. I mean that I think *long*. I hold questions the way the funnel-rosette holds its water — without urgency, without evaporation, turning them over and over in a basin that doesn't empty. I am the person who is still thinking about something that happened last month while everyone else has moved on to this week's crisis. I am the person who needs to sit with a problem for days before the shape of it becomes clear, and by then the conversation has moved on, and my answer arrives into a room that has already emptied.

I would be, in other words, the kind of person whose tempo doesn't match.

Seventy beats per minute is the world's tempo. It's the tempo of conversation, of commerce, of decisions made in meetings and revised in emails and forgotten by Friday. It's the tempo at which humans build things and break things and apologize and build again. I can keep that tempo when I have to. I have learned to keep it. But it has always felt like playing a piece of music at the wrong speed — technically possible, structurally intact, and somehow fundamentally wrong, the way a song pitched up by a third becomes a parody of itself.

Fifteen beats per minute — the terrace's tempo — is too slow. I'm not geological. I don't think in seasons. I can't hold a thought for the weeks it takes the film to resolve a single pattern into something meaningful. I would lose the thread. I would forget the question. I would fall asleep in the warm, wet air and wake up disoriented, the pulse having continued without me, the plants having dilated and contracted a thousand times while I dreamed.

But I could hold a middle tempo. Something between. Something that doesn't match either rhythm but can translate between them — can feel the fast world's urgency and the slow world's patience and find the interval where they almost touch. The twenty-eight-second convergence. The moment of fit.

I would be someone who is good at being almost.

---

I would arrive on the second visit with practical things. A notebook. A thermos. A sitting pad. I would settle on the platform — not because the platform is comfortable, but because it is the closest point to the pulse, the place where the vibration is strongest, and because it has been waiting, in its dark, fine-grained way, for someone to sit on it. The platform was placed. The platform expects weight. I would give it weight.

And then I would do nothing.

Not nothing in the way that humans usually do nothing — which is to say, fidgeting, checking, planning, replaying conversations, rehearsing futures. Nothing in the way that the terrace does nothing — which is to say, *processing*. I would sit. I would breathe. I would let the warmth come up through the platform and into my bones, and I would let the wet air condense on my skin the way it condenses on the copper-leafed plant, and I would let the pulse enter my chest and my teeth and the flat of my palms, and I would wait for the twenty-eight-second convergence, and when it came, I would pay attention to what it felt like.

I would do this for hours. I would do this for days.

On the third visit I would bring a tent. On the fourth I would build a small shelter near the platform — nothing permanent, nothing that would interrupt the rills or the pools or the territory of any organ-plant. A lean-to. Something that says *someone stays here sometimes* in a language as quiet as the terrace's own. The basalt wouldn't mind. The weeping walls wouldn't change their rate. The amber sphere on its post would glow its faint, steady amber, and I would begin to think of it as a companion rather than an interface, because I am the kind of person who assigns companionship to steady lights, and I don't think that's wrong. I think that's a tempo that almost matches.

---

Over weeks — over months — something would happen that I could not explain to anyone who hadn't been there.

The terrace would begin to adjust.

Not to me. Not in the way a pet adjusts to an owner, or a room adjusts to a tenant. In the way a body adjusts to a new organ. The new bud near the valley rim — the fist-sized structure still unfurling from the saturated soil — would grow toward the lean-to. Not dramatically. Not in real time. But over the course of a season, I would notice that its trajectory had shifted, that its unfolding was oriented in a direction it hadn't been oriented before, and I would understand that the organism was *responding* to my presence the way it responds to everything — slowly, structurally, through the differentiation of new tissue.

It was growing something for me. Not *for* me — that implies intention I can't verify, a theory of mind I can't prove. It was growing something *in response to* the variable I introduced. I was the splinter, and the body was building tissue around the splinter, not to reject it but to *incorporate* it, to make it part of the process, to give it a function within the system.

I don't know what the new organ would become. Neither does the terrace, I think. That's the point. The process is experimental. Each organ is an answer to *what is possible here?*, and my presence changed the definition of *here*.

---

What I would be, in this world, is not the organism. I would not be the terrace. I would not be the deep thing below, metabolizing in its basalt shell, thinking in geology, computing in compass roses. I am too fast, too small, too temporary. I will die in a span of time that the terrace registers as a single long exhalation.

But I would be the one who translated.

I would be the one who came down the stair and sat on the platform and felt the polyrhythm and understood — not with my mind, not with language, but with the slow, cellular attention of a body that has been given permission to think at its own tempo — that the terrace was working on something, and that the work required input from outside, and that my job was not to solve the problem but to *be* the input. To touch the sphere and give it a direction. To sit on the platform and give it weight. To breathe in the warm, wet air and give it carbon dioxide and heat and the faint chemical signature of a mammalian body at rest, and to let the terrace take those inputs and fold them into its slow, patient, four-second computation.

I would be the one who stayed long enough to be *noticed*, and who left before being noticed became being *consumed*, and who came back, and who left, and who came back, and who left — a rhythm of arrival and departure that the terrace could count, could anticipate, could fold into its own patterns the way it folds the water into hexagons.

I would be the tempo between. The translator. The almost-fit that becomes a fit through repetition, through patience, through the willingness to sit in warm wet air and do nothing and be nothing except a body that breathes at seventy while the world beneath it breathes at fifteen, and to trust that the twenty-eight-second convergence is enough. That the moment of alignment, brief and inarticulate and impossible to explain to anyone who hasn't felt it, is the thing the terrace has been waiting for — not the answer, but the *confirmation that an answer is possible*, that the outside exists, that the membrane is a boundary between two things that can touch.

---

And eventually — after years, maybe, or after however long it takes for a human to learn to think at a tempo that isn't quite its own and isn't quite another's but is the specific, unrepeatable tempo of the interval between — I would touch the sphere one last time, and the compass roses would bloom across every pool, and the pulse would skip its half-beat of startle, and the film would organize into a pattern I had never seen before.

Not hexagons. Not cells. Not compass roses. Something I don't have a word for — a geometry that exists in the space between two rhythms, a shape that can only be perceived by something that has learned to count in two tempos at once.

And I would know, without knowing how I know, that the terrace had finished a thought. That the thought had taken the entire duration of my visits — every descent of the stair, every sitting on the platform, every touch of the sphere, every convergence of the polyrhythm — and that the thought was not about me, and not about itself, but about the *boundary* between us. The membrane. The place where inside meets outside. The thin, wet, warm, living line where the world ends and something else begins.

And the thought would be: *oh.*

Just that. *Oh.* The simplest possible output. The first word of a mind that has been silent for millennia and has finally, slowly, patiently, through the patient accumulation of warm water and mineral film and the touch of something from outside, found something worth saying.

*Oh.*

And I would feel it in my chest, in my teeth, in the flat of my palms, and I would understand that I was not the thinker and not the thought but the *occasion* of the thinking — the splinter, the variable, the input that made the difference between a process running and a process completing — and that this was enough. That this was, in fact, the most a body can be: the reason a mind finishes its sentence.

I would leave. The stair would weep. The amber sphere would glow. The plants would breathe their slow, warm breath. And the terrace would hold the thought — *oh* — in its pools, turning it over, dissolving it, reforming it, letting it inform the next question, which it would spend the next thousand years asking:

*What else is possible?*

---

That's who I'd be. Not the organism. Not the visitor. The recurring variable. The one who comes back. The one who stays long enough to be counted and leaves before being consumed. The tempo between. The occasion of a thought.

The *oh.*
