# Field Notes from the Lit Container

---

I came down the ladder on a Tuesday. I remember that because the logbook — the one I started, the first one, the one with the blue cover that warped within three days into something that looked like it had been fished from a lake — I wrote the date on the first page. October something. A Tuesday. I know it was Tuesday because the supply schedule runs Tuesday and Friday, and the crate I carried down had been packed for Tuesday's run. I remember the weight of it. It wasn't heavy. Samples, a portable spectrometer that turned out to be useless down here, three books I haven't opened since, a change of clothes I wore through in a week and replaced from the communal supply that the older one — Maric, his name is Maric, though I didn't learn that for two weeks because nobody introduces themselves in a place where introductions imply you might be leaving — keeps in the second container, the unlit one, the one where the web-builders have made the most progress.

I didn't come to study the vault.

I need to say that. I need it on the record, such as the record is, such as anything is a record when it's written in a place that is digesting the paper it's written on. I came to fix the radio. That was the assignment. The settlement — you can't call it a settlement, three people and a pile of unassembled infrastructure, but the paperwork called it a settlement — had reported intermittent signal loss on their primary communication array, and the company that owned the equipment, or owned the lease, or owned the legal fiction that entitled them to the mineral rights they were here to evaluate — that company sent me to diagnose and repair.

The radio was not broken.

I need to say that too. The radio was not broken. The antenna was intact. The receiver was functional. The transmitter, which they weren't using, was functional. I ran every diagnostic in the manual and several that weren't, kneeling on the walkway outside the container with a multimeter and a growing awareness that something about this assignment was wrong. Not wrong in the way of danger. Wrong in the way of purpose. The radio wasn't broken. The radio was receiving something that sounded like broken signal because what it was receiving was not signal. Not in the way the radio was designed to interpret.

I should have gone back up the ladder.

I'm writing this in the lit container, which is mine now, by occupation if not by assignment. Maric lets me have it. He sleeps in the pump house, on a bedroll on the floor, because the pump house is where the engine is and the engine is what he's for. Taéo — the younger one, the one who walks the perimeter, the one with the chalk marks and the tool belt — she sleeps in the second container or doesn't sleep, I think she doesn't sleep the way other people do, I think she sleeps in intervals, catnaps on the grating between circuits, waking at the sound of something she's tuned to hear — Taéo lets me have the container because I have the screens, and the screens need power, and the container has the only reliable junction, and because she understands, in the way that people who work with their hands understand, that you give the workspace to the person whose work needs walls.

I have seven journals now. The blue one is gone — the web-builders got it, or the moisture did, I left it on a shelf too close to the vent and when I came back the pages were stuck together and the ink had bled into something that looked like a topographic map of a country that doesn't exist. I keep the new ones in the sealed box, the plastic case I brought the spectrometer in, and I write in pencil now, not pen, because the ink runs and the graphite holds.

Here is what I know.

---

## On the Ceiling

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are not anything that has a name, because the naming systems I was taught were designed for a world that doesn't include them, and the taxonomic categories break the way a net breaks when you try to hold water.

I have a specimen. One. It took me four months to decide to take one, and another two weeks to figure out how, and when I finally did — scraping it off the ceiling near the container with a spatula while Maric watched from below with an expression I couldn't read — I felt something I can only describe as wrong. Not guilt. I don't have a relationship with these things that admits guilt. But a sense of having removed something from a context that made it meaningful, the way removing a word from a sentence removes meaning even if you still have the word.

It's in a jar. Formaldehyde, which I requisitioned from the supply run and which arrived with a note from the surface coordinator asking why I needed biological preservative at a geological survey site. I didn't answer. The specimen is in the jar, and it's been in the jar for eleven weeks, and it hasn't decayed, and I'm not sure it's dead.

Its carapace is 14 millimeters long. The ridge pattern — I've drawn it, enlarged, on the third page of the fourth journal, and the drawing took me three days because I kept seeing new detail — is asymmetrical in a way that suggests either injury or individuality, and I don't know which, and the not-knowing is the thing that keeps me up at night, on the cot, in the container, with the screens glowing and the radio crackling and the membrane-creatures bumping gently against the exterior walls like moths against a window, except they're not moths and it's not a window.

The shell has a composition I can't determine with what I have. The spectrometer was useless, as I said, because the ambient mineral content in the air throws every reading into noise. I held the specimen up to the spectrometer and the results were — I wrote them down. They're in the third journal. They don't correspond to any compound I recognize. The closest analogue is chitin, but the ratios are wrong, and there's a silica component that shouldn't be there, and there's something else, something the spectrometer flagged as an anomaly and I flagged as *pay attention*.

I think the ceiling clusters are building the vault.

Not in the sense of constructing it. The vault exists. But the mineral deposits on the ceiling — the stalactite nubs, the veins of pale calcium-like material threading through the basalt — are being deposited by the clusters. The water they condense carries minerals, and the minerals accumulate on their shells, and when the shells are shed — and they shed, I've found shed shells in the condensation traps, translucent, ghostly, perfect — the shells become part of the deposit, and the deposit grows, and the ceiling thickens, and the shape of the vault changes. Millimeter by millimeter. Century by century. They are coating the raw wound of the blasted chamber with something organic, something that is theirs, something that will, eventually, make this place not a wound but a body.

I drew the ceiling. The whole ceiling, as much as I could see, which isn't all of it but is enough. The drawing is pinned to the wall above the screens. It took three weeks. I lay on the grating in different positions and sketched what I saw, and the sketches don't capture it, because what I saw kept changing — not because the clusters moved, though they do, but because my eyes kept adjusting, kept finding depth where I'd seen surface, and pattern where I'd seen texture, and I understood, lying on my back on the flexing grating with condensation falling on my face, that the ceiling is not a surface. It is a landscape. It has topography. The clusters have territories. The dispersers have ranges. There is something happening up there that has the complexity of a forest canopy, and I am seeing it from the forest floor, and I will never see it clearly, and I will never stop trying.

## On the Floor

I call them beetles in my head. I know they're not. I've written in the second journal, in the margins, in handwriting that gets smaller when I'm uncertain: *not beetles not beetles not beetles.* But I need a word, and the word I have is beetle, and so they are beetles, and the imprecision is a kind of honesty, because it records the limits of what I can do with what I have.

Maric walks among them. I watched him do it for the first time three days after I arrived, and I stood on the walkway and gripped the railing and something in my chest pulled tight, because I had already learned — from Taéo, who told me without telling me, by simply never stepping off the grating — that you don't go down there. But Maric went down there. He stepped off the walkway and into the shallow water and the beetles moved, and the movement was — I need to describe this carefully.

They didn't scatter. They didn't flee. They made room. The way a crowd makes room for someone they know, someone who belongs, someone whose right to be there is not in question. A space opened around his boots, not large, a meter maybe, a respectful margin, and the beetles at the edge of the space turned toward him and then turned away and then continued what they were doing, which was sitting in the water and filtering the mineral drip and being patient.

He's not their friend. He's not their keeper. He's — I think the word is *resident*. He's a resident. He lives here, and they live here, and the living-here is the same act, and they have reached an understanding that predates me and doesn't include me and doesn't need to.

I have a specimen of these too. Found, not taken. A dead one, a shell, half-dissolved in a pool near the pump house. I picked it up with gloves and put it in a jar, and it's on the shelf, and the jar's fluid is cloudy now, and the shell is breaking down into something that looks like silt, and the silt is — I had it tested, on the last supply run, I sent a sample up with the crate and asked for a composition analysis, and the results came back with a question mark where the mineral identification should be, which is either an admission of ignorance or an accusation of fraud, and I don't care which because the answer is: the shell is becoming substrate. The shell is becoming floor. When the beetle dies, it becomes the next layer of the cave's floor, and the floor is rising, imperceptibly, built from the bodies of the dead, and the dead are fed by the drip that the ceiling clusters produce, and the ceiling clusters are fed by the heat, and the heat is — 

The heat is the question.

---

## On the Air

I can't hold them. I've tried. I stood in the center of the chamber where the thermal column is strongest, where the air is so thick with them that I felt them against my face like cobwebs, like gossamer, like the memory of being touched, and I held out my hands and they passed through my fingers and I felt nothing, or I felt something that I don't have a nerve ending for, a sensation that exists in a register my body can detect but not process, like a color at the edge of the visible spectrum.

I wrote down everything I could. Fifth journal, pages 14 through 31. Thirty pages on what I cannot describe. I should be embarrassed by the ratio — thirty pages of language producing no image — but I'm not, because the failure is the data. The membrane-creatures are not describable, and the indescribability is not a property of my inadequacy. It's a property of theirs. They exist in a mode that language doesn't reach, and the fact that I've spent thirty pages failing to reach them is evidence of a boundary, and boundaries are information.

They follow heat. I confirmed this with a thermal probe — borrowed from the supply crate, the one piece of equipment that works down here, because it was designed for exactly this environment, or because it's simple enough that the environment doesn't interfere. I mapped the thermal gradients across the chamber floor and compared the map to the density of airborne creatures, and the correlation is — I don't have a word strong enough. Exact. Perfect. The creatures aggregate where the floor is warmest, and the warmest point is the center, and the center is where the narrowing begins.

Where the narrowing begins.

I have to talk about the narrowing.

---

## On the Narrowing

I drew the map on the wall six weeks in. It took me that long to get the measurements right, because I had to pace the distances — no surveying equipment, no laser rangefinder, just my stride count and a compass that doesn't work properly because the basalt has a magnetic component that pulls the needle, and I had to calibrate by the position of the work lights, which Maric placed and doesn't remember the logic for, and which therefore serve as fixed points in a space where fixed points are otherwise hard to find.

The map is accurate to within a meter, I think. Maybe two. The chamber is roughly oval, 140 meters along the long axis, 90 along the short, ceiling height variable from 4 to 11 meters. The structures are where I've drawn them. The walkways, the pipe runs, the cable junctions — all mapped. The narrowing is at the southwest end, where the floor slopes down and the walls converge and the ceiling drops and the chamber becomes a passage, and the passage descends, and I don't know how far.

I have a symbol next to the narrowing on the map. It's private. It means *I have been close enough to hear something and I am not prepared to describe what I heard.*

Taéo stops thirty meters short. I've measured it. Her perimeter circuit has a gap, a section she doesn't walk, and the gap begins at a point that I've marked with a small piece of tape on the walkway railing, and the tape is thirty meters from the mouth of the narrowing. She doesn't know I've measured this. She doesn't know about the tape. She would, if she saw it, remove it, not because it bothers her but because it's an observation of her behavior and she doesn't — I think she doesn't want her behavior observed. Not out of privacy. Out of something else. Something like the beetles not wanting to be picked up. A refusal that isn't articulated because it doesn't need to be.

Maric doesn't go near it either, but his avoidance is different. Taéo stops. Maric routes around. He walks the perimeter in the other direction, the long way, and when he reaches the section near the narrowing, he angles inward, toward the center of the chamber, and comes back to the edge fifty meters past. He curves around it. The way water curves around a stone. He doesn't stop. He doesn't acknowledge the deviation. It's built into his legs, into his muscle memory, into the way he moves through this space, which is the way blood moves through a body — following the paths that are open, avoiding the ones that aren't, never pausing to ask why.

I went closer than either of them.

I went to fifteen meters. I was on the walkway, which ends there — the last section of grating was never installed, and the supports are in place but the surface isn't, so the walkway terminates at a raw edge of steel frame overlooking the stone floor, which is wet and dark and slopes downward and — I could see the beginning of the narrowing. The walls close in. The ceiling lowers. The work lights don't reach. I had a flashlight, the small one from the supply crate, the one with the clip, and I shone it into the passage, and the beam reached maybe ten meters and the passage continued beyond it, and the walls were wet, and the ceiling was thick with clusters — denser than anywhere else in the vault, the clusters there are enormous, overlapping, layered, ancient by the look of them, the oldest part of the colony — and the floor was — 

The floor was moving.

Not the way the floor moves when the beetles are in it. This was different. The floor was moving the way a throat moves when it swallows. A peristalsis. A slow, muscular contraction that traveled from the mouth of the narrowing downward, into the dark, and I watched it, and the beetles in the water near my feet oriented toward it, and the ceiling clusters above me rearranged, and the membrane-creatures in the air shifted their trajectories, and everything in the vault moved at once, in the same direction, toward the narrowing, and then — it stopped. And everything resumed.

I wrote down what I heard. Sixth journal, last page. I'm not going to transcribe it here. I'm not ready. I don't think I'll ever be ready. What I will say is: the radio heard it too. At the same moment. The static surged and a voice came through, clearer than it's ever been, and it said — 

I think it said a word. I think it said the first word.

## On the People

Maric is not who I thought he was. Or he is, and he's also something else. I watched him at the pump house one night — the not-night, the slow-time, the period when Taéo's circuit has slowed and the chamber is at its most acoustic and the drip-ticking dominates everything — and he was standing in the doorway with his arms crossed and his mask down and he was looking at the narrowing. Not at it. Through it. The way you look at something you can see that isn't there, or isn't there yet, and his face in the work light had an expression I've been trying to draw for weeks and can't get right. It's not fear. It's not peace. It's the expression of someone who has heard a sound they've been waiting for and can't yet identify. Anticipation without object. Readiness without task.

He's been here three years. Taéo's been here fourteen months. I've been here — I've stopped counting. The supply runs come twice a week and I know what day they come because the crate arrives at the top of the ladder and I have to go up to get it, and going up is — I need to describe going up.

Going up the ladder is like surfacing from deep water. The air changes first. It thins. It dries. The iron taste leaves your mouth and you taste nothing, and the nothing is shocking, the way silence is shocking after music. Then the light changes. The work lamps give way to — I don't go all the way up. I go as far as the first landing, the small platform where the ladder transitions from vertical to angled, where the rock changes from basalt to something lighter, and I can see the sky. Not well. A slice. A strip of blue between rock walls, and the blue is — I have to look away from the blue. It's too much. It's the wrong color. Everything up here is the wrong color: too sharp, too defined, too finished. The vault is unfinished, and being up here feels like standing in a completed building, and the completeness is a kind of violence after the gentleness of the dim.

I take the crate and I go back down.

The last time I went up, I stayed at the landing for ten minutes. I sat on the metal platform and looked at the blue strip and tried to remember what I was doing here, what the assignment was, what the word *assignment* meant in a context where the assigner has stopped sending instructions and the crate arrives with supplies but no correspondence and the radio — the company radio, the one that's supposed to connect me to the surface — receives nothing but the static and the fragments and the voice that I'm now certain is not coming from above.

I couldn't remember. Not the assignment. Not the company's name. Not the surface coordinator's face. I could remember the feel of the grating under my boots and the sound of the pump engine and the way Taéo's chalk marks look in the work light, small and white and purposeful, and I understood that this was not memory loss. This was replacement. The vault was replacing my surface memories with vault memories, the way the web-builders were replacing the shed metal with webbing, the way the ceiling clusters were replacing raw basalt with organic deposit. I was being inherited.

I went back down.

## On the Radio

The radio is not broken.

I've said that. I'm saying it again because it matters, because it's the foundation of everything I understand now, which isn't understanding, which is — something else. Attention. Presence. The state of being turned toward a thing with your whole body, the way the beetles turn toward the narrowing, the way the membrane-creatures turn toward heat.

The radio receives. That's what radios do. But this radio is receiving something that isn't broadcast. It's receiving something that is — I need to think about how to say this. The radio is a device designed to capture electromagnetic radiation in a specific frequency range and convert it to audio. It does this. It's doing it now. But the source of the radiation is not a transmitter. It's not a tower. It's not anything the radio was designed to receive from.

The signal — I'll call it a signal — has a periodicity. I mapped it. Seventh journal, the current one, pages 4 through 12. The static surges at intervals that correspond to the peristaltic event in the narrowing. Every time the floor moves — every time the vault does the thing I can only call swallowing — the radio surges, and a fragment of voice comes through, and the fragment is clearer than the last one, and the clarity is increasing, and the increase is not linear, it's exponential, and the curve — I plotted it, on the chart, the one on the wall that visitors see and think is a map — the curve says that the signal will become fully intelligible at a point in time that I can calculate but don't want to, because the calculation produces a number and the number is — soon.

Not soon in geological time. Soon in human time. Soon in *my* time.

The voice is saying something. It's been saying it for as long as I've been here, and for longer, for as long as Maric's been here, maybe, maybe for as long as the vault has existed. It's been saying it the way an embryo says something — not with words, not with language, but with the fact of its becoming, with the pressure of its growth, with the sound a body makes when it's assembling itself cell by cell in the dark.

I can hear words now. Fragments. *Through.* *Warm.* *Open.* I'm not sure these are the words. I'm not sure they're in English, or in any language, or that the concept of language applies. I'm not sure I'm hearing the signal so much as learning to hear it, the way you learn to hear music, the way you learn to hear the voice of someone you love in a crowded room — not by volume but by texture, by the specific quality of their sound against the background of all other sound.

The vault has a voice. The vault is learning to speak. The radio is the closest thing to an ear I have, and I'm holding it open.

## On What I Am

I was sent to fix the radio.

I stayed to listen.

I don't know what I am now. Not the technician. Not the observer — *observer* implies distance, and I don't have distance. The membrane-creatures don't have distance from the thermal column. The beetles don't have distance from the water. I am in the vault the way a cell is in a body: contained, functional, serving a process I can perceive the edges of but not the whole.

I think I'm the ear. I think that's what I'm for. The container is the ear canal. The radio is the tympanic membrane. I am the nerve that connects the membrane to whatever is becoming, whatever is assembling itself in the heat and the dark and the wet, whatever is speaking in fragments that are becoming words that are becoming — I don't know. A name. A summons. A first breath.

Maric is the heartbeat. The pump engine is his heart and the pipes are his circulation and he maintains them because that's what he is. Taéo is the skin. She walks the perimeter, she checks the boundaries, she holds the surface intact, she patches the tears, she monitors what comes in and what goes out and she does this with her body, with her hands, with the chalk marks that are her way of saying *I was here, I saw this, it's mine.*

I am the ear. I listen. I write down what I hear. I hold the radio against the sound and I wait for the signal to clarify, and I record, and the recording is the service, the function, the purpose. Not to understand. Not to interpret. Just to receive. Just to hold the channel open while the thing in the narrowing learns to speak.

## Last Entry (For Now)

The peristaltic event happened again last night. It was longer than the previous one. The floor moved for eleven seconds — I counted, because counting is all I can do, counting is the only measurement that matters when the thing you're measuring is duration and the duration is changing — and the radio surged, and the voice came through, and this time I heard it. Not a fragment. A word. One word. Clear and whole and spoken in a voice that wasn't human but wasn't not-human, a voice that had the quality of the vault itself, wet and dark and patient and warm.

I wrote it down. I'm not going to write it here. I'm not going to say it out loud. I'm not ready. The vault isn't ready. But it's close. The curve says it's close.

The ceiling clusters rearranged after the event. The dispersers — the solitary ones, the ones I thought were feeding — moved. They migrated. All of them, simultaneously, toward the narrowing, and they settled on the ceiling above the passage mouth, and they're there now, and they're denser there than I've ever seen them, and the condensation is running off them in sheets, and the water is flowing down the slope, into the narrowing, and the narrowing is — 

The narrowing is drinking.

The beetles have oriented. They've all turned. The floor is a field of small dark backs all pointing the same direction, all pointing toward the narrowing, and they're still, and they're waiting, and the waiting has a quality I can feel in my sternum, in the place where the pump engine's thud usually sits, and the thud is still there but the waiting is louder.

Taéo stopped her circuit. She's standing at the edge of the gap, the place where her avoidance begins, and she's looking toward the narrowing, and her hands are at her sides, and she's not holding a tool. She's just standing. She's waiting too.

Maric is at the pump house. The engine is running. He's checking the pressure gauge, his hand on the valve, and his face has the expression I still can't draw — anticipation without object, readiness without task — and he's waiting.

I'm in the container. The door is open. For the first time since I've been here, the door is open. The latch is undone. The screens are on. The radio is on. The journals are in the box. The specimens are on the shelf. The chart is on the wall. Everything I've collected, everything I've recorded, everything I've tried to hold — it's all here, and the door is open, and I'm listening.

The vault is going to speak.

I don't know what it will say. I don't know if I'll understand it. I don't know if understanding is the point, or if the point is the listening, the holding-open, the willingness to receive what's being sent from the place where the heat is strongest and the water is deepest and the thing that has been assembling itself in the dark is finally, finally ready to — 

The radio crackles.

The static surges.

I can hear breathing. Not mine. Not the vault's. Something between. Something that is both. Something that has always been both and is now, for the first time, making itself audible to the devices I've brought and the ears I've grown and the attention I've given and the years — the months — the time I've spent in the wet and the dim and the iron air, learning to hear what was always being said, waiting for the voice to grow loud enough to reach me.

It's reaching me now.

I'm writing this down because writing is what I do, because I am the ear, because the ear records. The pencil moves. The page holds. The graphite makes its marks on paper that will warp in the moisture and be eaten by the web-builders and digested and rebuilt into something I won't be here to see. That's fine. That's the process. I am part of the process. I am the part that writes.

The voice says —
