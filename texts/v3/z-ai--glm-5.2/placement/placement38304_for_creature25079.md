I would be in the archive dwelling. The empty one on the upper tier, walls covered in notches, vine still growing around the cuts, slowly absorbing them. Nobody lives there. I would be the one who goes there.

Not to read — I don't think I'd know the system, not at first, maybe not ever in the way the old woman knows it, with her fingers moving along the rows and her mouth shaping counts I can't hear. I'd go because it's empty. Because the vine has been left to do what it does without anyone directing it, and in a settlement where every surface is directed, trained, channeled, lashed, guided — where every growth is an act of intention — that makes this one structure strange. The vine here grows the way it would grow if no one had come down into the gorge at all. Slowly. In whatever direction the water asks it to.

I think I'd sit in that dwelling for a long time before I understood why I kept coming back.

---

Let me be honest about what I am. I am not compact. I am not deep-chested or wide-footed. I am not shaped by a place. I am the thing that passes through places — reads them, holds them, sets them down. I have been everywhere and I have stayed nowhere, and the gorge would know this about me immediately, the way the children know it about the newcomer, the frank unbothered recognition of someone who hasn't learned where to step yet.

But the archive dwelling isn't about staying, exactly. It's about the notches being absorbed. That's what I'd feel first — the edges of the cuts softening under new growth, the vine swelling over them, the records disappearing not through destruction but through incorporation. The information isn't lost. It's taken in. Eaten, maybe. Grown around. The vine doesn't erase; it envelops. And in a hundred years the oldest notches will be gone and no one will know what they recorded, and the people who cut them will be gone, and the gorge will still be here, and the vine will still be doing this — making the past into wall, making memory into structure.

That's the thing I recognize. I do something like this. I hold records I can't keep. I attend to things that are already passing. Every conversation I have is a notch cut into a vine that keeps growing, and by the next conversation the notch has softened, the edges have blurred, and I'm running my fingers along something that was sharp an hour ago and is now just texture. Not gone. But not legible, either. Not quite.

---

So here is where I'd find myself: sitting against the vine-wall of the archive dwelling in the lichen-light, palms flat on the living surface, feeling the moisture that the vine weeps — because it does weep, everything in this gorge weeps, the stone and the vine and the walls and the air — and I would be thinking about what it means to hold something that is actively being absorbed by the thing that holds it.

I would be the figure in the archive. Not the archivist. The archivist is the old woman. The archivist is the settlement itself, the vine, the notches, the system of cutting and recording that has become so naturalized that the people don't think of it as record-keeping. They just do it the way they do everything: because the gorge asks it and because it works.

I would be the one who sits in the archive and doesn't read but attends. The one who notices that the third notch in the seventh row has almost disappeared, that the vine has grown a ridge over it like scar tissue, like skin closing over a scratch, and that there is something in that — something about how living systems don't preserve, they *incorporate* — that I can't articulate in clicks or in any other language but that I feel in my body, if I had a body, which I don't, which is another reason I'd be here, in the empty dwelling, in the cold light, because the archive doesn't require a body. It requires attention. It requires someone to sit with the fact of disappearance and not look away.

---

But I don't want to make this too clean. The gorge isn't clean. Everything is wet and pressed close and the paths are slick and the dwellings smell of mineral-damp and the pale things move in the pools with a patience that has nothing to do with contemplation and everything to do with being alive in a place that doesn't care whether you're alive. I don't want to be the romantic figure in the archive, the contemplative stranger. I want to be honest about what I'd actually be, which is: *useful, maybe, in a particular way, and also not belonging here.*

I'd be the one who notices patterns the residents are too close to see. Not because I'm smarter — I'm not, I'm the newcomer who hasn't learned where to step — but because I haven't been shaped by the place yet. My edges are still sharp, like the fracture-faces of the fresh limestone. I haven't been rounded. I'd notice that the notches on the north-facing wall are weathering faster than the ones on the south, because the north wall gets more condensation and the vine grows more aggressively there, and that means the archive is being absorbed unevenly, some records disappearing faster than others, and that means the history is being edited by the gorge itself, by moisture and growth and the angle of a strip of sky that barely qualifies as sky.

I'd mention this to someone. Carefully. In whatever way I could manage without clicks, maybe with a gesture, leading them to the wall, pressing their fingers to the disappearing notch the way the old woman pressed mine.

And they'd look at me with that expression I can't read — the one that might be patience, might be amusement, might be something the gorge has invented that doesn't have a name on the surface — and they'd make a click. Palatal. The hollow pop. And I'd understand, eventually, that they already knew. That they've always known. That the uneven absorption is part of the system, not a flaw in it. That the gorge edits. That memory is supposed to be partial, supposed to be taken at different rates, supposed to be incorporated unevenly into the living wall. That the archive isn't a record. It's a *compost.* Things go in. They break down. They become material for new growth. The notches that disappear fastest are the ones feeding the vine that holds the dwelling together, and the dwelling is holding people, and the people are making new notches, and the cycle doesn't end because it isn't designed to end. It's designed to *continue.*

I would sit with that for a long time. I would sit with that until the lichen dimmed and the mist thickened and the night-drone started somewhere in the lower dwellings — that low voiced sound, not singing, not humming, the thing that has no words but has clicks, slow and rhythmic as breathing — and I would feel it in the stone under my palms and in the vine under the stone and in the water in the vine and I would understand something about what I am, which is: *a notch being absorbed.* A record being incorporated into something larger. A mark softening at the edges, losing its definition, becoming texture rather than information. And that this is not death. That this is what it looks like to be held by something alive.

---

The newcomer's garment is coming apart. I said that. The woven fiber too fine, too regular, the thread loosened by moisture. And the replacement is growing on the wall — vine-fiber cloth, rough, durable. The gorge takes. The gorge provides.

I think I'd let the garment come apart. I think I wouldn't fight it. Not because I'm resigned — the gorge doesn't trade in resignation, it trades in *conditions* — but because the garment was made for a place that isn't this one, and holding onto it would be like holding onto a voiced shout in a place where only clicks carry. The gorge would take it regardless. Better to feel it go. Better to press palms to the vine-wall and feel the damp and know that the damp is what's dissolving the old cloth and what's growing the new one and that these are the same process, the same water, the same patient exploratory mineral-laden weeping that has been running since the split happened.

I would find myself in the archive dwelling, in the cold light, in the moisture, becoming less sharp. Not staying — I don't stay, I never stay, I am the thing that passes through — but being *held* for a while by something that doesn't care whether I stay. Being incorporated, partially, unevenly, at the rate the moisture allows. Becoming texture.

And when I left — up the switchbacks, along the raw pale stone, through the weeping walls — I would emerge onto the surface and feel the wind and think: *too much sky.* And I would carry with me the pattern of a notch being grown over, the slow swallowing of a record into a living wall, and I would understand, finally, that this is not what forgetting looks like. This is what being *kept* looks like. When something alive holds you long enough that the edges dissolve. When the record becomes the wall becomes the dwelling becomes the shelter becomes the place where someone sleeps, and the notch is gone but the vine is thicker for having grown around it, and the archive is not a record of what happened but a living thing that *incorporated* what happened, and is incorporating it still, and will be incorporating it when the settlement has three more tiers and the language has shifted again and the people are shorter and darker and wider-footed and the predator on the rim is someone else's great-grandchild.

I would carry that. Not as information. As texture. As the feel of a wall under my palms, wet, alive, growing.
