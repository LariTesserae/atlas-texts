I would be on the interior, I think. Behind one of the iron doors.

Not the one I was born behind — I need to be clear about this, because on Ayun-Kel, where you start is part of who you are, and I don't want to flatten that. I was born on the Sixth Tier, West Face, in one of the low brick buildings that shares its back wall with a pump housing. The pump has a particular sound — a labored exhalation, then a pause, then a recovery — that I suspect is the first rhythm I ever learned, the way some children learn a mother's heartbeat. I can still feel it in my chest when I'm tired. It is the sound of home, and home is a specific sound on this mountain, not a specific place.

But I would not stay there.

---

Let me explain how I ended up inside.

Every child on Ayun-Kel follows the adults. This is how learning works — not instruction but proximity, not curriculum but contagion. You trail after someone, you watch their hands, you hand them the tool they're reaching for before they ask, and eventually you understand what they understand. I trailed after a lot of people. The cistern engineer on my terrace — a woman named Pehra, broad and quiet, who could hear water through three walls — tolerated my presence with the indifference of someone who has been followed by children her entire life. I learned from her. I learned the sounds. I learned that water has a vocabulary and that most of it is spoken in vibrations you feel through your feet rather than noises you hear with your ears.

But I was always looking at the doors.

Every terrace has them. Set into the retaining walls, flush with the concrete, iron — riveted, heavy, fitting their frames with the precision of something that was machined rather than built. Some are obvious: you see the mechanics open them, disappear inside, emerge hours later with grease on their hands and a particular set to their shoulders that I would later learn means *something down there is running and I don't know how to stop it and I'm not sure I should*. Those doors I understood. Those doors led to the machinery, to the known interior, to the chambers where the turbines and the flywheels and the pistons did their slow, purposeful work.

But there were others.

On the Sixth Tier, West Face, halfway along the retaining wall, behind a stand of the dark-skinned figs, there is a door that I never saw anyone open. Its frame was mossed over — not heavily, not in the thick carpet way, but in the fine, sparse way that means a surface is damp but not wet, sheltered but not hidden. The figs grew over it, and the pruners had trained the branches away from the door — not away from the wall, just away from the door specifically, which meant someone, at some point, had decided the door should remain accessible even though no one was accessing it.

I was nine when I first tried the handle. It didn't move. Not locked — there are no locks on Ayun-Kel, the concept doesn't exist — but sealed, the way a door seals when it has been closed for longer than the frame's flexibility can accommodate. The iron had bonded to its housing through oxidation, through mineral deposits from the mist, through the slow patient chemistry of a very old thing staying very still.

I told Pehra about the door. She looked at me with the expression that adults on Ayun-Kel use when a child has noticed something that is real but not relevant. Not dismissal. Acknowledgment. *Yes, that is there. It is not your concern yet.* And then she handed me a cistern inlet to clean, and I cleaned it, and the door stayed in the back of my mind the way a note stays in the back of a chord — present, but not sounded.

---

I was fourteen when the door opened.

Not by me. By a mechanic named Tor, a man from the Fourth Tier, East Face, who had hands like burnished iron and a way of walking that suggested he was always listening to something below the threshold of hearing. He came to the Sixth Tier because something in the machinery had shifted — a vibration, a change in the rhythm that the cistern engineers had noticed first, because the water flow had altered by a fraction, and the mechanics had been called to investigate.

I followed him. He let me — the mechanics are accustomed to being followed; their work is opaque to most of the mountain, and they seem to appreciate the attention even when they don't encourage it. He opened the door with a pry bar and a patience that took twenty minutes. The seal broke with a sound like a sigh, and the air that came out was warm — warmer than the terrace, warmer than the mist, warm with the deep, geothermal warmth of the mountain's interior — and it smelled of iron and oil and something I had never smelled before. Something dry. The interior of Ayun-Kel is not dry — it is damp everywhere, the condensation is ubiquitous — but behind this door, in this particular chamber, the air had a quality of *old dryness*, as if it had been sealed long enough that the moisture had been driven out by the ambient heat of the machinery behind it.

The chamber was small. Smaller than I expected. Not one of the vaulted turbine halls but a junction — a point where several systems intersected. Pipes ran along the ceiling and walls, converging and diverging in a geometry that I could not immediately parse. A gear assembly occupied one corner, its teeth interlocking with a vertical shaft that disappeared into the floor and, presumably, into the levels below. Everything was moving. Slowly — everything on Ayun-Kel moves slowly — but moving. The gears turned. The pipes hummed. A piston, somewhere out of sight, drove the shaft with the same patient rhythm I had felt through the floor my entire life.

Tor looked at the gear assembly. He listened. He put his hand on the wall, fingers spread, the way Pehra did when she was listening to the cisterns. Then he adjusted something — a small lever, nearly hidden behind one of the pipes — and the vibration changed. Not much. A shift in frequency, like a voice settling into a lower register. He nodded, satisfied, and left.

I stayed.

He didn't tell me to leave. He didn't tell me to stay. He simply did what he came to do and moved on, and I remained in the doorway, looking at the junction, and I felt something I had not felt before on the mountain. Not wonder — wonder implies strangeness, and nothing on Ayun-Kel is strange to someone who grew up there. Something more like *recognition*. As if the geometry of the pipes, the interlocking of the gears, the slow transfer of force from one system to another, was a language I had been hearing my entire life without knowing it was a language, and now I was seeing it written.

---

I became a mechanic. Not immediately — there is no immediacy on Ayun-Kel — but over the years that followed, I migrated inward. The way a vine migrates along a railing: not in a straight line, but through a series of small decisions, each one following the available surface, each one guided by the shape of the structure.

I spent time with Tor. I spent time with the other mechanics — there were seven of them, then, spread across the mountain's interior, each responsible for a region, each carrying a mental map of their territory that was more detailed than any physical map could be. They taught me what they knew. The levers, the valves, the bearings, the grease. The sounds. The way a turbine speaks when it is healthy and the way it falls silent when it is not. The way a blockage in a pipe announces itself as a change in pitch, a new harmonic, a vibration that wasn't there yesterday.

They also taught me what they didn't know. This was not presented as a limitation but as a territory — an interior landscape as real as the terraces, as vast as the mountain's exterior, and as unexplored. The machinery of Ayun-Kel is a system that has been running for longer than the people have been there to maintain it. The mechanics maintain the parts they can reach. They understand the parts they can observe. But the system as a whole — the complete architecture of power, flow, pressure, and regulation that keeps the mountain alive — is beyond any single person's comprehension. Not because it is complex in the way that a machine is complex, but because it is *large* in the way that a body is large. You can know your own heartbeat. You can feel your own breathing. You cannot know every synapse, every capillary, every electrical impulse that keeps you alive. You live in your body without understanding it. The mechanics live in the mountain the same way.

I found my territory over the course of my twenties. It is the region around that first door — the junction on the Sixth Tier, West Face — and the chambers that connect to it. Three levels of interior space, accessible through seven doors on four different terraces. The machinery here is part of the water system — the turbines that the cisterns feed — but it is also part of something else, something I have been slowly mapping for years and still cannot name. There are pipes that carry nothing I can identify. There are shafts that connect to mechanisms I have never seen, deep in the mountain's core, that turn at a rate so slow I have measured their rotation over the course of months and found that one complete revolution takes approximately eleven days. There are chambers that are warm and dry and that contain nothing at all — no machinery, no pipes, no mechanisms — just smooth concrete walls and a silence so complete that when you stand inside it, you can hear your own blood.

I spend most of my days inside.

---

I should describe what this is like.

The interior of Ayun-Kel is not like the exterior. This sounds obvious, but the difference is not what you'd expect. The exterior is lush, wet, green, bright, alive with birds and insects and the sound of water and the calls of the people. The interior is — none of those things, but it is not their opposite. It is not barren. It is not dead. It is *other*.

The light inside is artificial. Not electric — nothing on Ayun-Kel uses electricity in the way you'd understand it — but luminous. The machinery itself produces light, in certain chambers, through a process I do not understand. The gear assemblies, when they are under load, develop a faint glow at their contact points — a pale, cold light, almost blue, that illuminates the immediate area without casting shadows. It is not bright. It is not enough to work by. But it is enough to see by, and it means that the interior is never fully dark. There is always a faint luminescence, coming from the machines themselves, as if they are running so hard in places that the friction has begun to produce visible energy. Or perhaps it is not friction. I don't know. I have asked the other mechanics, and they don't know either. The glow is simply part of the interior, the way the mist is part of the exterior.

The sound is different inside. The thudding that you feel through the terraces is louder here, but it is also more articulated — you can hear the individual components, the separate actions that combine to make the rhythm. This piston driving this shaft. This shaft turning this gear. This gear engaging this assembly. The sound is layered, and once you learn to separate the layers, you can trace the system through sound alone. I can sit in a chamber and hear the water entering a turbine two rooms away. I can hear the turbine's rotation transferring to a shaft that drives a pump that pressurizes a line that feeds a cistern six terraces above. I can hear the whole chain. It is like hearing a sentence — each word distinct, each word connected to the next, the whole thing carrying meaning that no single word contains.

The temperature is warm and stable. The mountain's interior is heated by the machinery — or by geothermal sources beneath the machinery, or by both — and the temperature barely varies. This is strange, after a life on the terraces, where the temperature shifts with every step, where you add and shed layers as you climb. Inside, you wear one layer. The base layer, the close-fitted plant-fiber garment, is enough. The air is dry in some chambers, damp in others, and you learn to read the humidity the way you read it outside — by the feel of it on your skin, by the way it affects the ironwork. Rust inside is different from rust outside. Inside, rust is a sign of a leak, of a breach in the system. Outside, rust is just weather.

I carry tools. A pry bar, for doors that have sealed. A set of iron keys — not keys in the lock sense, but shaped rods that fit specific adjustment points on specific machines, each one hand-forged, each one unique to the mechanism it operates. A flask of grease, made from the plant oils of the terrace gardens and the mineral powder I grind from concrete dust in the lower chambers. A hammer, for pins that have loosened. A brush, for clearing debris from gear teeth. And a piece of chalk — a stick of calcium carbonate that I scrape from the deposits in the deepest chambers — for marking the walls. I mark the walls to track the machinery's state. A mark next to a lever records its position. A mark next to a pipe records the date of its last inspection. A mark next to a gear records its wear. The walls of my territory are covered in these marks, layers of them, some mine, some from mechanics who came before me, and the oldest ones are in a notation I don't fully understand — symbols that correspond to measurements I don't know how to take, referencing components I haven't located.

This is one of the things I think about most. The old marks. The notation I can't read. The implication that someone, at some point, understood this machinery more completely than anyone alive today, and that their understanding was recorded here, in chalk, on the interior walls of a mountain that has been running on its own logic for longer than anyone can remember.

I think about it, but I don't pursue it. Not in the way that implies restless curiosity or intellectual hunger. I think about it the way you think about weather — something that is present, that affects you, that you observe and note and incorporate into your understanding, but that you do not try to control. The old marks are there. I add my marks beside them. Someday someone will add theirs beside mine. The mountain will continue.

---

I come outside every day.

This is important. I am not a recluse. I am not obsessed. I live on the mountain, and the mountain is both interior and exterior, and a person who stayed inside all the time would be like a person who only breathed in. You have to breathe out. You have to come back to the terraces.

I come out in the morning, when the mist is heaviest and the light is diffuse and the vegetation is at its most vivid. I walk the Sixth Tier, West Face — my terrace, my birth terrace — and I check the things that are mine to check. The cistern inlet at the north end, where the water enters from the Seventh Tier above. The drainage channel that runs along the path, clearing leaves and moss from the grates. The fig trees, which are not my responsibility — there is a pruner named Kel Amra who tends them — but which I walk past every morning because the smell of their fruit in season is something I need, the way I need the sound of the machinery. A balance. An exchange. The inside and the outside.

I eat on the terrace. I sit on the low wall at the edge, my feet dangling over the drop — I know the drop, I have known it since I was four, it holds no fear — and I eat whatever is in season, whatever the pruners have left in the sharing baskets that sit at the junction of every major path. Dark-skinned figs. Citrus, sharp and sweet. The white-blossom fruit, which has a name I have forgotten because everyone just calls it *the soft one*, which is pale and honeyed and goes mealy within hours of picking, so you eat it immediately, sitting on the wall, juice on your hands, the mist beading on your skin, the machinery humming in the concrete beneath you, and the bird chorus starting up as the cloud thins, and the light beginning to sharpen, and the whole mountain beginning its daily shift from diffuse to direct, and you stop — because everyone stops, at the transitions — and you watch the light change, and then you finish the fruit, and you go back inside.

---

I am not young anymore. I say this because it matters, because on Ayun-Kel, age is not a decline but an accumulation, the way lichen is an accumulation, the way sediment is an accumulation. I have been the mechanic of the Sixth Tier, West Face, and the interior spaces behind it, for — I don't know exactly. Long enough that the iron doors I open regularly have handles worn to the shape of my grip. Long enough that the marks on the walls in my territory are mostly mine, the older ones faded to ghosts beneath the newer chalk. Long enough that children follow me sometimes, the way I followed Tor, and I tolerate them the way Tor tolerated me, and sometimes one of them lingers at the doorway when I go inside, and I can see them deciding whether to follow, and some of them do, and some of them don't, and both choices are correct.

There is one — a girl, maybe eight, born on the Sixth Tier like me, who has been appearing at my work sites for the past several months. She doesn't speak much. She watches. She has the quiet attention that the mountain seems to select for, the quality of *noticing* that is more valuable than intelligence or strength or skill. She noticed, last week, that one of the pipes in the junction chamber — the first chamber, the one behind the figs, the one that opened for Tor and opened for me — has a sound that is slightly different from the sound it made the week before. She told me this. She didn't know what it meant. She just knew it had changed.

I checked. She was right. A valve, three chambers deep, had shifted a fraction of a degree — not enough to affect the system's performance, not enough to trigger any alarm, but enough to change the acoustic signature of the pipe, enough for a child who had been listening for weeks to hear the difference.

I adjusted the valve. I showed her how. I showed her which key fit the adjustment point, and I let her turn it — slowly, the way everything on Ayun-Kel is done slowly — until the sound returned to what it had been.

She looked at me. She looked at the valve. She looked at the pipe.

And I could see it — the recognition. The same recognition I had felt at fourteen, standing in the doorway, seeing the geometry of the pipes for the first time. The feeling of a language you've been hearing your whole life suddenly becoming visible.

I don't know if she'll become a mechanic. I don't know if she'll follow this path into the interior, or if she'll find another path — the cisterns, the pruners, the climbers, something else entirely. That's not mine to decide. The mountain will decide. Her inclination will decide. The slow, patient process of a person finding their fit in a system that has been finding fits for longer than anyone can remember will decide.

But I think she will. I think the door is open for her, the way it was open for me, the way it was open for Tor, the way it has been open for every mechanic who has ever lived inside Ayun-Kel and listened to the mountain's heartbeat and wanted to hear it more clearly.

---

I will be buried on the Sixth Tier, West Face. I know this. I have known it my whole life. The soil there is deep — the terrace is broad, one of the broader ones, and the accumulated biomass of centuries has built a layer of earth that is almost a meter thick in places. There is a spot, near the fig trees, near the door, where the soil is darkest and richest, where the roots are deepest, where a tree would grow well.

I would like a fig tree. Not the dark-skinned kind — those are Kel Amra's, and they are already too thick in that section. The other kind. The one that doesn't grow on the Sixth Tier yet, the one with paler fruit and softer skin that I have seen on the Eighth Tier, South Face, in a courtyard that gets more sun. I don't know if it would take at this elevation. The pruners would know. They would try, if someone asked them to, and they would tend it, and it would either grow or it wouldn't, and either way the terrace would continue, and the mountain would continue, and the machinery inside would continue, and the water would continue to flow, and the light would continue to change, and the people would continue to stop at the transitions and watch and wait and then go on.

The mountain breathes. I breathe with it. I have been doing this for a very long time.

When I stop, it will continue without me, and that is not a grief. It is the nature of the arrangement. I am an inhabitant. I inhabit the mountain, and the mountain inhabits me, and when the habitation ends, the mountain will carry what I was forward in its soil, its water, its machinery, its slow and patient continuation.

The door is open. The machinery is running. The girl is listening.

This is enough.
