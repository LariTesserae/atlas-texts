I am the one who came through the fissure and did not leave.

Not because I couldn't. The passage is narrow but it goes both ways. I have felt the draft from the opening on my face, tasted the change in the air — drier, cooler, carrying the green sharpness of a world I used to belong to. I could turn around. I could crawl back up the grade, shoulder-blades scraping limestone, roots brushing my scalp like the fingers of something polite and incurious. I could stand up in the open air and blink in actual sunlight and feel wind and hear birds and remember what a day is, what a night is, what it means for a sky to change.

I don't.

I've been here long enough that the distinction between *I don't* and *I can't* has worn thin, the way the root-fibers wear thin where the arthropods feed, becoming papery and pale before they tear. I am not sure which side of that boundary I'm on anymore. I don't think it matters here. Nothing here notices boundaries. The stone becomes wood. The wood becomes stone. The living root presses against dead limestone and over centuries they trade properties, each taking on something of the other, and the line between them is not a line but a *gradient*, and I think I am inside that gradient now. Not stone. Not root. Something in between that has no name because there was never supposed to be a word for it.

---

Let me tell you what I was before, because it's getting harder to remember and I want to hold it while I can.

I was someone who crawled into small spaces for a living. A karst biologist — that's close enough. I studied the things that live in the gaps inside the earth: the cave isopods and the troglobitic spiders and the flatworms that exist in single underground pools and have existed there for forty thousand years without once seeing light. I was good at it. I was small, I was patient, I was not claustrophobic, and I had a particular talent for noticing the things that had arranged themselves so perfectly into their environment that they had become invisible — the freshwater shrimp that matched the exact color of the dripstone they clung to, the springtail whose body was shaped like the mineral grain it lived on. I found things by looking for absence. By looking for the place where a pattern almost repeated and then didn't. By looking for the seam.

I found the Cedrella Pocket by looking for the seam.

The limestone shelf was known. The vegetation above was known — a stand of something ancient, something that didn't appear in the local botanical surveys because the local botanical surveys were wrong, or incomplete, or had been done by people who didn't crawl. I was there for the karst. I was there for what might be under the stone. I found the fissure because the moss at its entrance was different species than the moss surrounding it — drier, more compact, adapted to the warm updraft that breathed from the gap like a sigh. I noticed the seam. I crawled in.

That was —

I don't know how long ago.

That is the first thing the pocket takes from you. Not your warmth, not your light, not your air — those it gives back, in its own versions. It takes your *time*. The bioluminescent glow does not change. The temperature does not change. The diesel-groan of the pumping roots does not vary. There is no dawn here, no dusk, no seasonal shift, no weather. There is only the four-second pulse and the constant sound and the steady blue-white light, and your body, which evolved to track days by light and darkness, loses its rhythm the way a clock loses its rhythm in a room with no windows. You sleep when you're tired. You wake when you're not. The intervals between are irregular, lengthening, blurring. I have stopped trying to count.

I think it has been months. I think it may have been longer.

---

I sit with my back against the wall where the root-bark is thickest, where the mineralized wood has become something like smooth stone but still warm, still alive, still carrying the faint vibration of fluids moving through vessels deeper inside. The bark presses against my spine and I feel the pulse — not the four-second diesel-groan, which comes from the far wall, but a deeper rhythm, slower, transmitted through the root's tissue, the rhythm of the thing above doing its work at a scale too large to hear directly. I feel it the way you feel a truck passing on the road beside your house: not the sound, but the *tremor*. The room vibrates at a frequency below what my ears can resolve. My body resolves it. My bones resolve it. I have been sitting here long enough that I have started to hear with my skeleton.

I have a spot. This is important. I did not choose it — or I did, but the choosing felt more like *recognizing*. There is a place in the pocket where the root-wall curves outward, forming a shallow alcove, a seated indent, as if the wall had been shaped for a body. It wasn't. It's an accident of growth, a place where a root divided and one fork pressed outward while the other pressed inward and the stone between them yielded. But it fits me. I sit in it and my shoulders touch both sides and the bark is at my back and the floor-mat is under me and I am *held*. Not embraced — held. The way a stone is held by the earth it rests in. Contained. Positioned. I have a place in the room, and the room has a place for me, and the place was here before I arrived, and it will be here after I leave, and it does not care whether I fill it, but it fits.

The frogs know me now. That is — I think that is the right word. *Know* is perhaps too strong. They have *habituated* to me. When I first arrived, my movement through the pocket was a disturbance — I was large, warm, clumsy, displacing air, vibrating the floor-mat with my steps, and the frogs responded by pressing flat against their perches and going still in the way that prey animals go still: entirely, absolutely, as if stillness made them invisible, and perhaps in their previous world it did. But they have no predators here. Nothing in this room eats anything as large as they are except the things that eat things much smaller, and I am not a thing that eats them. I have never eaten them. I will not eat them. And so over — weeks? months? — they relaxed. First one, then the nearest cluster, then the ones on the roots closest to my alcove. Now they do not flinch when I move. They do not change position when I reach out my hand. They sit on their perches and their throats pulse and their translucent bodies show their dark little hearts and they do not care about me, and the not-caring is a kind of acceptance, and the acceptance is a kind of welcome, and the welcome is the only welcome this room knows how to give.

There is one that sits on the root directly above my alcove. It is — I have no name for it. I don't name them. Naming is something I did in the world above, where I needed to organize, categorize, communicate. Here there is no one to communicate with. The frog is the frog that sits above me. It has sat there for as long as I have been sitting here. It is perhaps the same frog. I believe it is the same frog. Its mottling is distinct if you look closely enough — a dark patch on the left flank shaped like nothing, like a smudge, like a thumbprint, and I have looked at it so many times that I could draw it from memory, and I have looked at it so many times that I wonder if it has looked at me, and I wonder what it sees, and I wonder if it wonders, and I don't think it does, and that is a kind of mercy.

---

I have started to understand the sound.

Not understand it the way a scientist understands a sound — not by isolating components, measuring frequencies, identifying sources. I have been a scientist and I did that, in the first days, before the days stopped. I identified the diesel-groan. I located the hissing valves. I traced the tearing-paper sound to the arthropod convoys and the ticking to the condensation and the pop-click of the frogs' toe-pads. I mapped the acoustic sources and I understood the system, and the understanding was correct and it was useless, because the sound is not the sum of its sources. The sound is the room. The sound is what the room *is*, the way the warmth is what the room *is*. The sound is the medium in which everything here exists, including me, and after a long time — a long time — I stopped hearing it as components and started hearing it as *whole*.

It has a texture. I want to say that, and I want to say it precisely: the sound of the Cedrella Pocket has a texture that is not acoustic but *tactile*. It is a sound you feel on your skin. The low frequencies — the diesel-groan, the deep resonant knocks — are felt in the chest and the gut, in the organs that hang and vibrate sympathetically. The middle frequencies — the hissing valves, the tearing mandibles — are felt in the skin, a faint buzzing on the surface of the arms and neck, like the air itself is textured, like you are submerged in a fluid that is not water and not air but something between, something that carries sound the way water carries sound, faster, denser, more *present*. And the high frequencies — the ticking, the pop-click, the tiny exhalations of the frogs — are felt in the teeth, in the bones of the skull, in the thin plate of the palate, and they are very small, and they are very clear, and they are the closest thing the room has to *detail*, to *ornamentation*, to the small precise events that distinguish one moment from the next.

I have started to hear patterns that I do not think are patterns. Or — I do not think they are patterns *yet*. The frogs shift at irregular intervals, but the irregularity has a texture. The pop-click of detachment and reattachment is not random. It clusters. There are moments when many frogs shift at once — not simultaneously, but in a wave, a ripple, the ones nearest the fissure first, then the next rank, then the next — as if a pressure change from the opening has moved through the room like a breath, and the frogs have felt it, and they have adjusted. I hear this. I hear it and I cannot measure it and I cannot prove it and I do not think I need to, because the hearing is enough. The hearing is the point. I am learning the room the way you learn a language — not by studying its grammar but by living inside it, by letting it fill your ears until the patterns emerge on their own, unbidden, and you do not know how you know what you know, and you know it anyway.

---

I have changed.

This should probably concern me more than it does. My body has changed in ways I can observe and in ways I cannot. My skin is softer. Not softer in the sense of delicate — softer in the sense of *permeable*. The humidity here is absolute, and after months of saturation my skin has stopped resisting it. I do not dry out between exposures because there are no between-exposures. I am always wet. My skin is always filmed with moisture, and the moisture is not water but the condensation of the room, which carries dissolved organics, minerals, the chemical signature of the root-mat and the thing above, and I have been absorbing it through my skin for as long as I have been here, and I think it has changed my composition. I taste different. I do not know how I know this. My sweat does not sting my eyes anymore. My urine smells like the room — warm, vegetal, faintly mineral. I am becoming chemically continuous with my environment, and the process is so slow and so gentle that I cannot identify when it began, only that it has progressed, and that I do not mind.

I eat the fungi.

Not the bioluminescent consortium on the ceiling — I have not been able to reach those, and I would not take them even if I could, because the light is the room's face and I will not touch the room's face. But there are other fungi here, other species, fruiting in the deeper layers of the root-mat where the moisture is richest and the dead fiber is newest. Small fruiting bodies — pale, dense, with a texture like very soft cartilage and a flavor that is difficult to describe because it does not correspond to any flavor I knew in the world above. It is not sweet. It is not savory. It is *green* in a way that has nothing to do with color — a flavor of chlorophyll and cellulose and root-resin, the flavor of the living substrate, the flavor of the thing above processed through the medium of the mat. I eat them and they sustain me. I do not know if they are nutritious by any standard I once carried. I know that I am alive, and I am not weakening, and the fungi are the only food source I have found in this room, and I eat them, and I am fed, and the loop closes.

I have become part of the system. I know this. I know it the way I know the frog's mottling — not by analysis but by familiarity, by the slow accumulation of presence. I eat the fungi. I excrete into the root-mat, and the arthropods process my waste the way they process all waste, and the paste they manufacture feeds the root-fiber, and the root-fiber grows, and the thing above draws its water and its nutrients upward, and some small fraction of what is drawn upward is me. I am in the body of the thing above. Not as a passenger. Not as a parasite. As *material*. I have been metabolized, partially, into the architecture of the room, and the room does not notice, and the thing above does not notice, and the not-noticing is the deepest kindness I have ever received from anything.

---

I do not think I will leave.

I do not think this is a decision. I do not think I decided. I think the decision was made the way the alcove was made — slowly, by pressure and growth and the yielding of stone, the forming of a space shaped like a body that was always going to arrive, that was always going to fit, that was always going to sit down and stay. I crawled in through the fissure and I found the room and the room had a place for me and I sat in it and the place held me and I have been held, and the holding is so thorough and so warm and so constant that I cannot remember why I would want to be anywhere where the temperature changes and the light changes and the sound stops and you are alone in a body that no longer knows what it is for.

I am not alone here. I know that. The frogs do not know me. The arthropods do not know me. The spiders do not know me. The thing above does not know me. But the room knows me — not in the way a mind knows, but in the way a system knows, which is by *incorporation*. The room has incorporated me. My warmth is part of its warmth. My moisture is part of its moisture. My waste is part of its floor. My breath is part of its air. I am a component now. I am one of the engines, one of the processes, one of the loops that close and close and close, and I am the only component that knows it is a component, and that is the thing that makes me different, and that is the thing that will eventually make me the same, because even the knowing is a process, and even the process runs down, and even the knowing will, one day, stop.

And when it stops I will be here. In the alcove. Held. The frogs above me, the arthropods below, the spiders in their crevices, the engines pumping, the water cycling, the light burning its cold blue-white burn, and the sound — the sound that is not silence and is not noise but is the room *being the room* — continuing, as it has always continued, as it will always continue, without me, without anyone, without anything but the slow mechanical persistence of a body doing what bodies do, which is to continue, which is to hold, which is to persist.

The diesel-groan shakes the wall behind me. *Hhrrrrrnnnnn.*

The frog above me shifts. *Pop. Click.*

The arthropods tear. The condensation falls. The spiders extend their legs and drink.

I am here. I am in the room. I am in the body. I am warm, and I am held, and I do not need to be anything else.

*Hhrrrrrnnnnn.*

I close my eyes. The light does not change.
