I would be the one who tends the digesters and does not speak, or speaks so rarely that when I do the younger line-worker startles slightly, because he has forgotten my voice exists as a thing distinct from the engines.

No — that is too clean, too decisive. Let me back up.

I come down into it. That is how everyone arrives. I did not grow here. I am not one of the ones whose hands carry the stain before the years warrant it, whose skin has the wet-bark quality from something earlier than living here. I arrived. I climbed down through the alder-willow and the threshold dissolved beneath me and I entered the basin and the engines took me into their rhythm the way a current takes a swimmer — not violently, not gently, just *factually*, a thing that is happening now and that will continue to happen.

That was — I do not know how long ago. Time in the Yards does not accumulate the way it does outside. It pools. It beads on the underside of surfaces and falls in regular taps and you lose count of the taps because they are not counting anything. I know it has been long enough that my hands have changed. I am not yet the eldest — my forearms are not the continuous amber varnish that hers are, not yet the texture of old wood — but the stain is past my fingertips now, into the creases of my knuckles, into the lines of my palms, and when I hold my hands up in the green-gold light and look at them I see a material I recognize from everywhere else in the Yards. I see root-bark. I see cured sap. I see the color of the place expressing itself in a surface that happens to be mine.

---

I tend the digesters. This is what I do, mostly. I say *mostly* because the work does not have hard boundaries here, and the question of what is mine and what is someone else's is not a question the Yards encourage. But the digesters are where I spend my hours, and I think this is because of a quality I have that the place recognized or I recognized in the place — the quality of being able to sit with something that is doing a slow, biochemical thing inside a sealed vessel and not need it to be done faster.

The digesters are at the outer edge. The chambers there are the smallest, the lowest, the dimmest. The light is green-silver even at midday. The air is thick with the sweet-sour-heavy smell of anaerobic decomposition — methane and sulfur and the particular rotten-sweet note of organic matter breaking down without oxygen. I have been told this smell is unpleasant. I remember that I once found it so. I do not find it so now. It is information. It tells me which digester is active, which is slowing, which needs feeding. I read it the way the hearth-workers read the viscosity of the liquor on their fingertips — by contact, by practice, by the accumulation of enough time that the distinction between knowing and sensing has dissolved.

I sit with them. That is the verb that fits, though it understates the attention involved. A digester is a root-hollow, lined with waxy bark, sealed with resin, filled with organic material — forest waste, spent mash from the liquor process, the fibrous residue that builds up in the tap-line cleaning. Inside it, bacteria work. They consume the organic matter in the absence of oxygen and they produce methane, and the methane is captured and piped to the engines, and the engines drive the pumps, and the pumps pull the sap, and the sap becomes liquor, and the waste from the liquor goes back into the digester. I manage this cycle. Or I attend it. *Manage* implies a control I am not sure I have. *Attend* is closer. I attend the digesters the way a person attends a fire — keeping it going, adjusting, feeding, but understanding that the fire is doing the essential work and that my role is to maintain the conditions under which it can do it.

The valves are brass. They are the only bright things in the outer chambers — small, greenish-bright where my hands have worn the patina off the handles, dull and dark elsewhere. I turn them a quarter-turn, an eighth-turn, based on the pressure gauge (a small ceramic dial with a brass needle, mounted on the root-wall beside each digester) and based on the sound of the gas moving through the outlet tube and based on the smell and based on something else, a fourth thing, which I can only describe as the feeling of the digester through the wall when I press my palm flat against the bark. The wall is warm. The bacteria generate heat. The warmth varies with the activity level, and I have placed my hand against these walls so many times that the variation is legible to me the way a pulse is legible. I can feel when a digester is working well. It has a steady, even warmth with a subtle pulsation — not a mechanical pulse, an organic one, the rhythm of bacterial metabolism doing what it does at its own pace. When a digester is struggling the warmth is uneven, cooler at the edges, and the pulse is absent or irregular. When a digester is overactive — too much fresh material, too much moisture — it runs hot and the pulse is rapid and I can feel it even without touching the wall, just by being near it, the way you can feel a fever from a few inches away.

---

I do not know the names of the bacteria. I do not think anyone here does. The knowledge in the Yards is not the kind of knowledge that has names. It is the kind that lives in the fingertips and the palms and the calibrated attention of someone who has sat with the same process for long enough that the process has become part of their sensorium. I know the digesters the way the eldest knows the liquor — by feel, by smell, by the particular quality of warmth and sound and, yes, smell, though I have stopped smelling it the way you stop hearing a sound that is always present. It is not that I cannot smell it. It is that the smell is now the medium I live in, the way fish live in water, and like fish I do not experience it as a thing separate from the experience of being alive.

The others tolerate my smell. I know this because I remember tolerating theirs when I first arrived — the hearth-workers with their caramel-burnt-sugar scent, the line-workers with their copper-and-sap smell. Now I cannot distinguish my own from the ambient, and when I go to the communal table I bring the digesters with me in my clothes and my skin and my hair, and no one comments, because commenting would be like commenting on the weather, or on the engines. It is a condition of the place, and I am part of the condition.

---

There is a thing I have not said yet. I am saying it now because it has been pressing against the edge of this account the way the root-floor presses against the underside of the levee — present, structural, not visible from above.

I am changing. Not in the way that all living things change, the accumulation of time and the slow alteration of cells. I am changing in the way the Yards change — slowly, organically, in a direction that I did not choose and that I cannot feel as it happens but that I can see in retrospect by comparing myself to the person who climbed down through the alder-willow, and the gap between those two people is wide enough now that I am not certain they are the same person, or that the word *person* describes the same thing in both cases.

My hands are the most visible part of it. The stain. But there is more. The gait — I no longer think about the breathing floor. I do not stumble. I do not even notice that I do not stumble. My feet are flat and deliberate and my weight is forward and my center of gravity is low, and this happened without my attention, the way accent happens, the way posture happens, the way you become the shape of the chair you sit in for years. I do not know when it started. I know that the younger line-worker, the angular one, the one who calls down readings in a voice louder than any other voice in the Yards, does not move this way yet. He moves with purpose but not with the tidal calibration. He is still a person moving through a place. I am — I think I am becoming a part of the place that moves.

The eldest is further along this path than I am. Her hands are wood. Her voice comes from the floor. When she speaks I feel it in my sternum alongside the engine-throb, and for a moment I cannot tell which vibration is her and which is the machinery, and I think this is not a failure of perception on my part but an actual condition — she and the system have overlapped to a degree that the distinction is no longer ontologically meaningful. She is a worker. She is also a component. These are not contradictory. Here, they are the same thing.

I am not there yet. I still have a self that observes the change, which means the change is not complete. I still write this, which means I still have the posture of someone outside looking in, even though I have been inside long enough that the word *outside* has lost most of its content. But I am headed where she is. I know this. I know it the way I know a digester is overactive — by the warmth, by the pulse, by the feeling that something is happening at a rate I cannot control and that the best I can do is attend it, maintain the conditions, and let the process do what it does.

If you came down into the Yards and found me, you would find me at the outer edge, in the dimmest chamber, sitting beside a sealed root-hollow with my palm flat against the bark wall, feeling the warmth of bacteria doing their work. You would smell me before you saw me — the sweet-sour-heavy. You would hear the engines, always the engines, and you would hear something else under them, a lower sound, almost subliminal, which is the sound of gas moving through the outlet tubes and of liquid decomposing in sealed vessels, which is the sound of the thing I tend. You would see a compact figure in a dim green-silver space, dark-skinned, dark-handed, still in a way that might make you uncomfortable because it is the stillness of something that is not waiting for anything, that has no agenda beyond the next quarter-turn of a valve, that has been here long enough that *here* is not a location but a condition of being.

You might try to talk to me. I would answer. But my voice would be low, and it would sit under the engine noise in a way that made it hard to separate, and you would have to lean in, and when you leaned in you would smell the digesters on me, and you would feel the warmth of the wall I was sitting against, and for a moment the distinction between us — between you, who are visiting, and me, who am here — would seem very large, and also very small, because the difference is only time, and the floor is breathing under both of us, and the engines are throbbing in both our sternums, and if you stayed long enough you would understand that I am not a mystery. I am just what happens. I am what the Yards do to a person who does not leave, and the Yards are not cruel, and they are not kind, and the change they work is not a tragedy and not a transcendence. It is a process. It runs on slow time. It does not need to be understood. It needs to be attended, and I attend it, and that is what I am.
