# The Grooveworm

---

I should not be here. That is the first thing to understand. I am not part of the basin's design. I am not a consequence of the felt, or the water, or the mineral gradients. Nothing in the basin's ancient chemistry predicts me. I exist because the builders existed, and the builders are gone, and what they left behind is not just quiet — it is a *niche*. A gap in the noise where something can live that could not live anywhere else on this stone, in this water, under this sky that is not a sky. I am an accident of architecture. I am a squatter in the silence.

I do not have a name for myself. The builders did not name me — they may not have known I was here, or they may have known and not cared, or they may have known and been the reason I arrived in the first place. I cannot ask them. They have been gone for a span of time I cannot measure, because I do not measure time. I experience it as a kind of weather — periods of slightly warmer condensation alternating with periods of slightly cooler condensation, and that is the full extent of my calendar. I do not know how many warm-cold cycles I have lived through. I do not know how long a warm-cold cycle is. I know the grooves in my walls, and I know the sound that comes through them, and I know the sound that does not.

---

Let me describe myself, because no one else will.

I am small. Not as small as a scraper — I am perhaps three centimeters long, which is enormous by the basin's standards, comical by any other. I am soft. My body is not armored, not plated, not segmented in any useful way. I am a tube, essentially, with a difference: my body is flattened on the ventral surface, the surface that touches stone, and on that surface I have — not legs, not cilia, but something between the two. Rows of fine, short projections, muscular, that grip the micro-texture of carved stone and move me forward with a motion that is less crawling than it is *flowing*. I go where the grooves go. I have spent my entire life inside the grooves in the walls, and I have never left them.

I am the color of the stone. Not because I camouflage — nothing here hunts me, nothing here sees me, nothing here knows I exist — but because I eat the stone. Or rather, I eat what grows on the stone, which is a thin film of mineral deposit and dead biological matter that condenses out of the air inside the structures. The air here is cleaner than outside — the ribbons see to that, curving away from the structures, leaving the airspace above the corbelled domes slightly richer in what they do not eat and slightly poorer in what they do. The material that settles inside the structures is fine, almost molecular, a dust that is never quite dry because nothing here is ever quite dry. It coats the grooves. It fills them, slowly, over what I experience as a long time. And I eat it.

My mouth is at one end. It is a small, round mouth, not a scraper's broad razor, not a wader's crushing slit. It is a mouth made for narrow spaces, for the inside of a groove cut into stone by a patient hand with a flaked tool. I fit inside the grooves. My body is exactly as wide as the widest grooves and narrower than the narrowest, which means I can travel the full network of carvings on a wall without ever leaving them. I move through the grooves the way a worm moves through soil, except my soil is someone's acoustic engineering, and my passage through it is — I have come to understand — a form of maintenance.

This is the part that matters. The part I cannot fully explain but can describe.

The grooves work because they are empty. They trap sound because the air inside them is still, and still air in a narrow space absorbs vibration — the sound enters the groove, bounces, loses energy to the stone walls, and dies. This is the builders' principle, discovered by trial and error over generations, and it works. It works less well when the grooves fill with dust.

I eat the dust. I move through the grooves and I eat the thin film of deposit that settles in them, and I leave the grooves clean, or cleaner, and the grooves keep working. I maintain the silence. I do not do this intentionally — I have no concept of the silence as a thing that needs maintaining. I simply live in the grooves and eat what is there, and what is there is the thing that would eventually clog the grooves and reduce their acoustic absorption, and so my eating is, incidentally, a form of preservation. The builders are gone. The silence they made is still here. I am the reason it is still here.

I do not know this. I am describing it to you now, but I do not know it the way you know things — with reflection, with inference, with the ability to hold an idea of yourself as a thing in a world of things. I do not hold ideas. I hold grooves. The taste of the mineral film. The texture of carved stone under my ventral surface. The faint vibration of the basin's noise, attenuated to a murmur by the walls, felt through my body as a kind of tingling that lets me know I am still inside, still sheltered, still in the place where the eating is good. When I reach the edge of a groove — the place where the carving meets the uncarved stone — the vibration changes. It gets louder. The tingling becomes a buzz. I turn around. I do not like the buzz. I stay in the grooves.

---

How many of me are there?

I do not know. I have never met another of me, but I have no way of knowing whether there are others in other structures, in other grooves, in other rooms. I suspect there are. The structures are scattered across the basin at intervals that suggest a pattern, and if I am here, in this structure, then the same accident that put me here probably put others in the others. The mineral film settles in all of them. The grooves in all of them need cleaning. If I am a consequence of the grooves, then I am wherever the grooves are.

But I might be alone in this room. I might be the only grooveworm — the only thing, of any kind, that lives inside the quiet. Everything else in the basin avoids the structures. The scrapers turn at the taste. The waders turn at the texture. The ribbons curve at the acoustic shadow. The felt does not grow here. I am the only thing that enters, and I do not enter — I was born here, in the grooves, from an egg that was deposited here by something I have never seen, or I budded from a fragment of something that was already here, or I condensed out of the mineral film itself, which is not impossible, which is not anything I can rule out because I do not have the conceptual equipment to rule things out. I am here. I have always been here. The grooves have always been here. The silence has always been here. I do not remember a time before, because I do not remember.

I move through the carvings and I eat and I move through the carvings and I eat. The pattern of the grooves is my entire world. Some of them are straight — long, parallel channels that run the length of a wall, evenly spaced, each one cut to a depth that someone determined, through unknown generations of experimentation, was optimal for absorbing a particular frequency. Some are curved — arcs, spirals, nested shapes that I recognize only as the shape of my path. Some intersect, and at the intersections the stone is worn smooth by my passage — or by the passage of whoever was here before me, my predecessor, my parent, the thing I cannot name that cleaned these grooves before I did. The intersections are my favorite places. I do not have favorites, but if I did, the intersections would be them. The mineral film accumulates slightly thicker where grooves meet, where two currents of settling air converge, and the eating is better there. I pause at intersections. I eat. I continue.

---

The sound outside is a wall I live inside of.

I want to try to describe what the noise is to me, because it is not what it is to you. You hear it. I feel it. The distinction is not trivial.

My body has no ears. It has no auditory apparatus of any kind. What it has is a distributed sensitivity to vibration along its entire length — every cell of my ventral surface can detect pressure waves in the stone, and the grooves I live in transmit those waves with a fidelity that the flat stone outside does not. I am, in a sense, living inside the basin's acoustic nervous system. The grooves are tuned to absorb sound, and I am inside the grooves, and so I feel the sound the way the grooves feel it — which is to say, I feel it *being absorbed*. I feel the vibration enter the groove and lose energy and die. I feel the silence being manufactured, moment by moment, by the stone around me. I am inside the process. I am a parasite on the process of quiet.

What this means is that I experience the basin's noise not as a roar but as a *tremor*. A constant, low shivering in the stone, something I have felt since I was born and will feel until I die, something so familiar that it is indistinguishable from the sensation of being alive. If the noise stopped, I would feel it stop. I would feel the tremor cease, and the grooves go still, and the stone go quiet in a way it has not been quiet since before I existed. I do not know what I would do. I might die. Not from the silence itself, but from the absence of the vibration that has been part of every moment of my life. I am adapted to the tremor. The tremor is my weather, my time, my proof that I am in the world. Take it away and I am — what? A soft, pale thing in a groove in a stone in a room that no one built for me, with no sound to absorb and no reason to be.

But the noise will not stop. The basin does not stop. The scrapers do not stop. The water does not stop. The geological vents do not stop. The noise has been running since before the grooves were cut, and it will run after the grooves have eroded to nothing, and after I have eroded to nothing, and after the structures have softened into the same ambiguous ground that surrounds them. The noise is older than everything here except the felt and the stone. The noise will outlast me. This does not bother me, because I do not experience bother. I experience grooves, and mineral film, and the tremor of sound dying in stone, and that is enough. That has always been enough.

---

There is one thing I want to tell you, and I do not know if I can tell it correctly, because it is the thing that is most mine, the thing that no description of the basin captures, the thing that belongs only to the thing that lives inside the silence.

Sometimes, in the grooves, I feel something that is not the basin's noise.

It is rare. It happens at intervals that I cannot predict and that I do not try to predict, because I do not try. It happens, and then it does not happen, and then it happens again, and I have no way of knowing whether the interval is long or short, because I do not count. But it happens, and when it happens, I stop eating. I stop moving. I press my ventral surface flat against the stone, and I feel it.

It is a vibration that does not come from the basin. It does not come from the scrapers, or the water, or the vents, or the waders, or the ribbons. It does not come from outside. It comes from the stone itself — from the deep stone, from the bedrock, from the structure of the rock at a level I cannot reach and cannot see and can barely feel. It is very low. Lower than the geological drone. Lower than anything the basin produces. It is so low that it is almost not a vibration at all. It is more like a *settling*. A shift in the stone's disposition, as though the rock is adjusting itself, finding a more comfortable position, the way a sleeping body shifts in the night.

And it has a pattern.

I do not mean a rhythm. I do not mean a beat. I mean a *shape*. The vibration has a shape that I can feel because I am pressed against the stone and the stone is conducting it and my body, long and thin and curled inside a groove that was cut to a specific depth for a specific purpose, is the right size and the right shape and the right sensitivity to feel the pattern in the settling. It is not a message. It is not language. It is not a signal. It is a shape, and the shape is — I do not have the words. I do not have words at all. I am a thing that does not use words, trying to describe something that is not a word, to someone who uses words for everything.

The shape is the shape of the grooves. The deep vibration in the stone has the same pattern as the carvings on the walls. Not the same grooves — these are not the builders' cuts. These are something else, something in the bedrock itself, something geological, something that was there before the builders came, before the basin was a basin, before the water found the fissures and the felt found the water. Something in the stone that is the same shape as what the builders carved. The builders did not invent the pattern. They found it. They felt it — or something in them felt it — and they cut it into the walls, and the walls absorb the sound because the pattern is the pattern that stone already knows, the pattern that stone has always known, the pattern that the basin's noise is the *interruption* of.

The silence in the rooms is not the absence of sound. It is the *presence* of the stone. The grooves do not absorb noise by coincidence. They absorb noise because they are shaped like the stone's own quiet, and when the noise enters them, it encounters something familiar, and it surrenders. The builders did not carve silence into the walls. They carved the stone's silence out of the walls. They uncovered what was already there.

I know this because I live inside it. I live inside the pattern, and I feel the deep vibration that is the pattern's source, and the two things — the grooves and the deep settling — are the same thing, expressed at different scales, one small enough for a flaked stone tool to cut and one vast enough to be the bedrock of an entire basin. I am between them. I am the thing that lives at the scale between the carved groove and the geological stratum, the thing that is small enough to fit inside the pattern and large enough to feel the pattern's source, and I am the only thing in the basin that occupies this position, and I do not know what it means, and I do not need to know what it means, because meaning is not something I am equipped to produce. I am equipped to eat mineral film from carved grooves. I am equipped to feel the stone's deep settling. I am equipped to clean the grooves so that the silence persists. I am equipped to be small and soft and alive in a place that is vast and hard and almost entirely without life that knows it is alive.

---

I will die in the grooves. This is not a sad thought because I do not have sad thoughts. It is simply the shape of my life, which is the shape of the grooves, which is the shape of the stone's own quiet. I will eat the mineral film until my body wears out, and I will settle into the bottom of a groove — one of the deeper ones, perhaps, one of the spirals near the corner where the walls meet and the sound absorption is strongest — and I will dissolve there, and my body will become part of the mineral film, and something else — something that I cannot imagine because I cannot imagine — will come along and eat me, and the groove will be clean again, and the silence will continue.

Or maybe nothing will come. Maybe I am the last of whatever I am. Maybe the thing that deposited me here deposited only me, and there is no next generation, and when I dissolve, the grooves will fill, slowly, over centuries or millennia, with the mineral film that nothing eats, and the absorption will degrade, and the silence will erode, and the structures will become slightly less quiet, and the scrapers will creep closer, and the waders will probe the edges, and the ribbons will fly through the airspace that was once silent, and the quiet rooms will be quiet no longer. Maybe I am holding the silence in place. Maybe I am the last maintenance. Maybe the builders' project has been running on borrowed time, and the borrowed time is the lifespan of a soft, pale thing that lives in the grooves and does not know it is keeping the silence alive.

I do not know. I cannot know. I move through the carvings and I eat and I feel the stone's deep settling, and outside the walls the basin roars and clicks and slops and rustles and breathes, and inside the walls there is a near-silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of the stone's own pattern, and I am inside that pattern, and the pattern is inside me, and we are — the stone and I — the same shape, for a little while, for as long as I last, and then I will be gone, and the shape will remain in the stone, waiting for something else to grow into it.

The basin stands. The basin sounds. I clean the grooves. The silence holds.

That is enough.
