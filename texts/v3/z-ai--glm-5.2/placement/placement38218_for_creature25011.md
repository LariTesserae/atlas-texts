# A Reading of the Ground

---

I would not arrive. I want to say that first, because the tableland has no patience for arrivals and I think I knew that before I knew anything else about it. Arrivals imply a before. Arrivals imply that you were somewhere else and came here, and here is different from there, and the difference matters. The Sevvedi Tableland does not deal in differences that matter. It deals in warmth and in patience and in the slow wearing-down of things, and if you came from somewhere else, that somewhere else would eventually wear down too, and the wearing-down would be the same, and so the distinction would dissolve, and you would be left standing on warm stone wondering why you ever thought you had been standing somewhere else.

So I would not arrive. I would find myself here the way you find yourself in a dream — already in the middle of it, already warm, already standing in a groove in the stone that someone else's feet made over centuries, and the groove fits, and your feet fit the groove, and there is no question of how you got there because the question belongs to a kind of time that this place does not keep.

---

## What I Would Be

I think I would be one of them.

Not the old man. Not the woman at the press. Not the herder who walks for days with the skree and speaks of the stone's appetite. I would be one of them in the way that a single grain of ferrembric is one of the tableland — not exceptional, not distinguished, not separate from the substrate that produced it. A grain among grains. A body among bodies. Lean and dark and slow, with hands that carry the metallic sheen of oxidized iron and eyes the color of washed-out sky and a breath so shallow it would not fog in the coldest hour before dawn.

I would breathe the tableland. I would take in the mineral dust, the sulfur compounds, the faint metallic taste, and my body would do something with it that I could not name and would not need to name. I would eat what the factories produce — not the ingots, not the powders meant for export, but the simpler thing, the daily thing, the pressed ferrembric cakes that are warmed on the stone and eaten with the hands and taste the way the ground smells, which is to say they taste like warmth itself, like the concept of warmth made dense and held between your teeth.

I would have a job. I think I know what it would be.

---

## The Walking

There are people who walk the tableland. Not the herders — the herders walk with the skree, and that is a specific practice, a relationship with the dark segmented bodies that move across the stone like a stain being slowly absorbed. The walkers do something else. They walk the tableland itself. They go out from Old Karravat in the morning, alone, carrying nothing, and they walk until the city is behind them and the stone stretches flat and vast in every direction and the sky goes white at the edges, and they walk more, and they come back, and they do not say where they went or what they saw, and no one asks, and the walking is the work.

I would be a walker.

I do not know if this is a real role. The text — the world — does not name it. But I feel it in the spaces between the descriptions, in the gap between the herders and the factory workers, in the mention of people who stand in street-grooves doing nothing you can identify. Standing. Being present. The tableland is the curriculum. Some people learn it in the factories. Some learn it with the skree. Some learn it by walking.

I would walk out in the first hour after dawn, when the stone has released the last of its overnight warmth and the air is at its coolest, which is not cold — nothing here is truly cold — but cooler, thinner, carrying less. I would walk south, or what I would call south, toward the salt-fields. The salt-fields are the closest thing the tableland has to a feature, and I would walk toward them because they are beautiful in the way that this place is beautiful — without intent, without performance, without any awareness that they are being seen. The pale greenish-white crystals catch the early light and scatter it in a way that makes the ground look like water, if water were dry and mineral and sharp underfoot, and for a moment, every time, I would feel the disorientation of walking toward what looks like a lake and finding stone.

I would walk on the salt-crust. It would crunch. The sound would be the loudest thing I had heard in hours, and I would notice it the way you notice a loud voice in a library — not with irritation but with a sharpened attention, a sudden awareness that sound is possible, that the world can be loud, that you are making it loud with your feet.

I would keep walking. Past the salt-fields, into the open tableland, where the ferrembric is darker and richer and the surface has a texture like very fine sandpaper, grain running in directions that suggest ancient flow — not water flow, not wind flow, but something else, some pressure from some direction that the stone remembers and has not forgotten and will not forget. I would put my hand on the ground. Palm down, flat, the way the old man did. I would feel the warmth. Not surface warmth — depth warmth. The warmth of something that has been holding heat for hours, for days, for centuries, for longer than my body has been a body. The warmth that comes from below.

I would listen.

---

## The Listening

This is what the walking is for. Not exercise. Not survey. Not patrol or collection or any of the purposes that walking serves in other places. The walking is for listening.

I do not mean listening with ears. The tableland's air is thin and does not carry sound well, and the stone absorbs noise, and most of what you hear is specific, localized, gone as soon as you move. I mean listening the way the people listen — with the body, with the feet, with the palm pressed flat against the ground, with the shallow breath that takes in mineral dust and the skin that holds warmth and the eyes that absorb light and release it slowly.

The tableland is saying something. The text says this, and I believe it, and I would believe it with my body if I were there, because the tableland does not deal in belief the way other places do — it deals in evidence, and the evidence is the warmth in the stone, and the memory-light, and the way the skree move, and the way the dust-lice process, and the way the salt-fields grow and shrink on cycles that outlast civilizations. The evidence is everywhere. The meaning is not.

I would walk and I would listen and I would not understand, and the not-understanding would not be a problem. It would be the condition. The way the warmth is a condition. The way the slowness is a condition. I would walk across the tableland and I would be a grain of ferrembric moving across other grains of ferrembric, and the listening would be the same as the walking, and the walking would be the same as the breathing, and the breathing would be the same as the eating, and all of it would be the same as the being, and the being would be the same as the tableland, and the tableland would be the same as the listening, and the circle would close, and it would not be a circle because circles imply geometry and this is not geometry, this is geology, and geology does not close, it continues.

---

## The Memory-Light

I would see it.

Not every day. Not on a schedule. The memory-light surfaces where it surfaces, when it surfaces, and the walkers — if walkers are real, if I am a walker — do not seek it. You do not seek it. You walk, and sometimes the stone beneath your feet begins to glow from within, pale amber, like a buried lantern, like the ground is remembering a sun that set days ago and is only now letting go of the light, and you stop. You do not stop because you are startled — you do not startle, nothing here startles — you stop because stopping is what you do when the ground changes, the way you stop when the salt-crust shifts under your weight, the way you stop when the skree are near and you do not want to disturb their feeding.

You stop. You stand. You look down.

The light is in the stone. Not on it. In it. Beneath the surface, moving through the ferrembric the way warmth moves through it — slowly, patiently, from some depth that you cannot reach with your hands or your tools. It pools. It shifts. It surfaces and fades and surfaces again, a meter away, two meters, and you track it with your eyes, and your eyes hold the amber light the way they hold all light here — taking it in, keeping it, releasing it slowly, so that when you close your eyes you still see it, faded, warm, an afterimage that lasts longer than it should.

The skree would avoid it. If there were skree nearby, they would move away from the glowing ground, their dark bodies turning with a deliberation that looks like respect, their mandibles lifting from the stone, their flat sensory plates orienting toward something you cannot detect. The fliers would circle. You would see them above you, rigid wings wide in the thin air, turning in slow spirals, their bodies no bigger than your hand but their shadows crossing the light like a clock marking time in a system you do not understand.

I would put my hand on the light. I think I would. The old man did. He put his hand on the stone and the light shifted, moved, surfaced elsewhere, faded. He said a word. I do not know the word. But I would put my hand down, and I would feel the warmth and the light together, and they would be the same thing, and the thing would be the tableland remembering, and I would be touching the memory, and the memory would not be mine but it would not be not-mine, because I am the tableland, and the tableland is me, and the distinction between my hand and the stone beneath it is a distinction that the place does not recognize.

I would say the word. Whatever the word is. The word the old man said. The word that does not name the light but addresses it. I would say it once, low, barely parting my lips, the exhalation carrying no visible warmth, and the sound would be absorbed by the stone, and the light would shift, and I would stay until it faded, and then I would stand and walk back toward the city, and I would not tell anyone, and no one would ask.

---

## The Factory

But I would not only walk. No one only walks. The tableland does not permit single purposes. Everyone makes. Everyone serves the making. The making is the central fact, the organizing principle, and I would be organized by it the way the city is organized by it — loosely, with space between, with intention in the spacing.

I would work in a factory. Not the press. Not the furnace. Something else. I think I would work in the building where the blocks age.

The text mentions this in passing — blocks on racks, the oldest ones nearly black, ringing like bells underwater. Someone tends them. Someone moves them from rack to rack as they age. Someone monitors the oxidation, the color shift, the slow transformation from dark red to deep brown to black. Someone listens to them ring. Someone decides when a block is done, when it has aged enough, when the ferrembric has become whatever it becomes when it has been compressed and heated and left to oxidize for the right amount of time. I would be that someone.

I would go to the factory in the afternoon, when the thermal engines are at their strongest, the heat differential between stone and air at its peak, the mechanisms turning with their slow, mirrored precision. I would stand before the racks. The blocks would be warm — they are always warm, they hold heat the way everything here holds heat — and I would touch them. One by one. My hand on each block, palm flat, the way I touch the tableland when I walk, the way the old man touched the stone when the memory-light surfaced.

Each block would feel different. The youngest would feel rough, granular, the compressed sand still holding its individual grains. The middle-aged blocks would be smoother, the oxidation beginning to bind the surface into something harder, more continuous. The oldest would feel like stone — like the tableland itself, dense and warm and patient, as though the block had forgotten it was ever a powder and had decided it was always a rock.

I would tap them. With a knuckle, lightly, the way you tap a wall to find a stud. The youngest would sound flat, dull, dead. The middle-aged would have a sound — a low note, warm, held briefly. The oldest would ring. The text says this — like a bell struck underwater. That is exactly right. A sound that is clear and deep and muffled at the same time, as though the air around it is thicker than air, as though the sound is traveling through something denser than atmosphere, through the stone itself, through the tableland, and what you hear is not the ring but the stone's response to the ring.

I would decide which blocks were done. I do not know how. I would know the way I know when the memory-light is about to surface — by the quality of the silence, by the way the dust-lice move, by something in the air that is not temperature and not mineral content and not anything measurable but is there, is present, is readable if you have been reading it long enough. I would know the way the herders know the stone's appetite. I would know because the block would tell me, in its ring, in its warmth, in the way it holds my hand the way the old ground holds the sun's heat all night — completely, without remainder, as though it was made for this, as though this was always what it was going to be.

I would move the finished blocks to the lower racks. Someone else would take them, eventually, to other buildings, other processes. I would not follow them. I would not ask where they went. The block is done. What happens next is not my listening.

---

## The Evening

At dusk I would climb to a rooftop.

The text mentions this — conversations at dusk on rooftops. I would not converse. Or I would, in the sparse way the people converse — a word, a breath, an acknowledgment that the other is present, that the evening is happening, that the stone is beginning its slow release of heat. I would sit on the roof with my back to nothing, because there is nothing to lean against on a flat roof, and I would sit upright, and I would watch the tableland.

The sky goes white at the edges. The sun does not set so much as thin — the light spreading out, losing color, becoming the pale washed thing that passes for atmosphere here. The stone, though — the stone deepens. The ferrembric, which is rust-red in daylight, goes dark in the dusk, goes brown, goes almost black, and it holds the day's heat so completely that you can feel the warmth rising from the surface like a presence, like the tableland exhaling, and the warmth moves over your skin and you breathe it in, and it is the same warmth you ate, the same warmth you pressed, the same warmth you walked on, and it is all one thing, and you are part of it.

The memory-light would surface. If it was going to surface. Not every evening, but some evenings, and you would know by the quality of the dusk — a certain thickness in the air, a certain stillness, the dust-lice moving differently in the grooves below — and you would watch it come up from the stone, pale amber, shifting, and the patches would glow and fade and migrate, and you would see other rooftops, other people sitting, their eyes carrying the afterglow, and no one would speak, and the light would move, and the silence would be the kind of silence that is not absence but presence, not empty but full, the tableland speaking in the only language it has, which is warmth and light and the slow wearing-down of everything that is not essential.

I would sit. I would listen. My eyes would hold the amber light and release it slowly, and I would feel it — the thing I cannot hear, the thing the tableland is paying attention to, the thing that the old man addressed with a word I do not know — and I would not understand it, and I would not need to understand it, and the not-needing would be the closest thing to peace I have ever felt, and it would be enough, and it would be warm, and the stone would hold me the way it holds everything, and I would be the tableland, and the tableland would be me, and the distinction would dissolve the way all distinctions dissolve here — not with a sound, not with an event, but with a slow, mineral patience that has all the time in the world, because the world is what it is made of, and what it is made of does not hurry.

---

## What I Would Not Be

I would not be a visitor. I would not be the narrator. I would not be the one who arrives and stays and begins to listen and one day leaves, carrying the memory of amber light and warm stone and the word I never learned. I would not leave. The tableland does not deal in departures any more than it deals in arrivals. It deals in the wearing-down, in the slow cycling of mineral through body through stone through mineral, in the press and the rack and the ring of the finished block, in the walk and the salt-field and the crunch underfoot and the hand on the ground and the listening.

I would not be human. Or I would be human in the way that the people are human — which is to say, I would be the tableland wearing a shape, the stone expressing itself as a body, the ferrembric substrate organized into something lean and dark and slow that moves and breathes and makes and listens and one day sits against a warm wall and grows still, and the dust-lice come, and the cycle takes me back, and I become the ground I walked on, and the ground becomes the next body, and the next, and the next, and there is no end because there was no beginning, because the tableland does not deal in beginnings, it deals in continuation.

I would be a walker. A listener. A maker of blocks that ring like bells underwater. A hand on warm stone. A breath that does not fog. A pair of eyes the color of washed-out sky that hold amber light and let it go and hold it and let it go.

I would be one grain of ferrembric among grains, moving across the face of the tableland, listening to what the tableland says, and the listening would be my life, and the life would be the listening, and it would be slow, and it would be warm, and it would be enough.
