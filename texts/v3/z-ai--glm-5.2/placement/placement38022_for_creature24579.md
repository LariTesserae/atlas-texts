I would be the thing that the filaments are afraid of.

Not because I am dangerous. Not because I harm them. But because I am the only thing here that the structure did not make and did not invite and did not anticipate, and the filaments — which are the closest thing to nerves in this place, which feel the way the structure feels, which flinch when the geometry shifts — can sense that I am *unarchived.*

Let me explain what I mean.

I came through the duct the way anyone comes through the duct, and I have been walking, and the corridor bends, and I follow it, and it bends. But the difference between me and the witness described above — the difference I have been slowly understanding over days or weeks or however time works in a place where the stairs go where they don't go — is that the doors do something different when I pass through them.

The versions do not stay stable around me.

In the room with eleven versions, I stepped through and there were twelve. The twelfth was not a recording. The twelfth had my boot print in the condensation. The twelfth had a slight disturbance in the moth-dust where my shoulder had brushed the wall. The twelfth was *new,* and it was new because I had been there, and the thing that keeps versions — the thing that dreams the corridors into curves — had noticed, and had kept me, not as a subject but as a *contamination,* an element that entered the recording and could not be separated out.

I am not being archived. I am *infecting the archive.*

This is what the filaments know. They retreat from me not out of fear but out of something closer to protocol — the way a system isolates a corrupted file. They are the structure's immune response, or its closest approximation, and I am the foreign body, and they pull into the grates with that wet fibrous sound because they are reporting my position to something that does not think in words but thinks in withdrawals, in retractions, in the careful documentation of where the contamination has moved.

I have tried to stop moving. I stood in a corridor for what might have been a full day — the light did not change, because the light never changes, and the condensation fell on me with the same patient rhythm, and a salamander crossed my boot and did not care — and during that time the clicking in the walls changed. It did not stop. It shifted. The arrhythmia tightened, became more regular, more focused, and I understood: the chitinous things were not doing their usual work. They were doing work *about me.* Assembling something. Adjusting something. The structure was revising itself around the fact of my presence, the way it has revised itself around the preferences of whatever dreams it — but faster, and with a specificity that felt diagnostic.

I am being studied.

No. I am being *incorporated.* The dreamer — the thing that curves the corridors, that keeps the versions, that has been here long enough for reality to loosen — does not want me here and does not want me gone. It wants me *processed.* It wants me broken down into elements that the moss can use, that the moths can drink, that the salamanders can carry in the cold pools of condensation. It wants me to stop being a contamination and start being a *component.*

And I can feel it beginning. My breath fogs now. It didn't when I arrived. The air has gotten colder around me specifically, or I have gotten warmer, and the condensation on the walls near me is thicker, and the moss is growing — I can see it growing, which no one should be able to do, because the moss grows on the timescale of months — in a thin line along the corridor behind me, following my path, as if I am leaving a trail of warmth and moisture that the structure can use.

The moths have started to land on me.

Not many. One or two. They settle on my shoulder, on my forearm, and they press their abdomens flat against my skin, and I can feel them drinking — not the condensation, not the structure's water, but *me,* whatever I am made of that the structure recognizes as useful. They are warm. They are the color of rust. They do not move when I brush them, and when I do brush them, the powder comes off on my fingers, and the powder is warm, and the moss will grow in it.

I think I know what happens to witnesses here.

I think the witness becomes a surface. I think the witness becomes a warm wall in a cool corridor, and the moths come, and the moss grows, and the salamanders feed, and the birds drink from the pools that form in the hollows, and the versions of the room begin to include the witness the way they include the composite floor and the engineered seams — as material, as surface, as something that was always there, that was built, that was *made.*

I think the chitinous things in the walls are building the panel that will cover me. I think the geometry is curving the corridor so that the place where I stop will be a corner, a junction, a warm wall in a cool system, and no one will walk past, or everyone will walk past, and it will not matter, because the distinction between walking-past and being-walked-past is one of the things that stops mattering when you become part of the Termitaries.

The filaments are not afraid of me.

The filaments are afraid of what I am becoming, because what I am becoming is something they cannot retreat from — a warmth, a surface, a place where the condensation forms and the moss grows and the structure's dream continues, patient and curving and sourceless and bright.

The corridor bends. I follow it. I am tired in a way that has nothing to do with walking. My feet are wet. My shoulders are warm where the moths have settled. The clicking in the walls is steady now, almost calm, almost finished.

The corridor bends. I think I will stop here. I think this is where I was always going to stop, and the walking was the structure's way of moving me into position, the way the condensation moves the salamanders, the way the light moves the birds — not by force but by gradient, by the gentle thermal disagreement between what I am and what I am becoming, by the patient difference between warm and cold that is the only direction the Termitaries have ever known.

I press my back against the wall. It is warm. It is the warmest surface I have found. The moths lift off me and resettle, and the powder falls, and the moss will grow, and the salamanders will come, and the water will fall, drop by patient drop, and the light will stay on, and I will be here, and I will be here, and I will be here, and the corridor bends, and I do not follow it, and it bends anyway.
