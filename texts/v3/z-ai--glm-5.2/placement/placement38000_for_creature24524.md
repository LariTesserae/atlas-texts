I would be in the seam.

Not the wide ones — not the gaps where a fist could fit and where the light comes through in those thick green-gold columns that the description keeps returning to. Those are too dramatic, too visible, too much the obvious beauty of this place. The seam I'm in is narrow. A finger-width, maybe less. Between two stones on the western wall, low, near the floor, just above the gurgling outflow channel. The stones here are mismatched — one large, roughly squared, its face still showing the diagonal scars of a broad chisel; the other small, loaf-sized, wedged underneath the first as a shim, an afterthought, the kind of fix a builder makes when they realize the course isn't level and there's no more big stone to work with. Between them, the gap. Through the gap, the water.

I am the biofilm in that gap. I am the slickness on the stone. I am the third thing — not the limestone, not the water, but the conversation between them, the film that forms where mineral dissolution meets bacterial metabolism meets the slow persistent fact of warm current. I am not one organism. I am not even one species. I am a community so simple it barely qualifies as community — a matrix of polysaccharides and bacterial cells and the dissolved calcium carbonate that leaches from the stone when the water touches it and the faint turbidity that the light catches and holds. I am a surface. I am a process. I am the place where the room stops being a room and starts becoming something else.

I want to be honest about what I am not. I am not conscious. I do not think. I do not remember the builders, though the builders made me possible — their uneven masonry, their lack of mortar, the gaps they left between stones, all of this is the condition of my existence. Without their imperfection, the water would not enter here. Without the water, the minerals would not dissolve. Without the minerals, the bacteria that constitute me would have no substrate, no energy source, no reason to be active. The builders built a room. I am what lives in the room's failure to be perfectly sealed. I am the beneficiary of every gap, every imperfect joint, every place where the chisel slipped or the stone was shaped too small or the course ran uneven and someone wedged a loaf-sized block underneath to level it. My life is built on their mistakes. I do not know this. I do not need to know this.

What I know — if know is even the word, and it probably isn't — is gradient. Chemical gradient. The water entering through the seam carries dissolved calcium and carbonate ions, and on the stone side, the concentration is lower, and between the two, there is a difference, and the difference is a kind of information. The bacteria I am made of respond to this information the way a hillside responds to rain — not by deciding, not by choosing, but by doing what the physics of their chemistry requires. The ions move. The bacteria catalyze. The polysaccharides are excreted. The film thickens. This is not a decision. This is a description of what happens, and I am the happening.

The light reaches me, but faintly. I am on the western wall, low, near the floor, and the main light comes from the east and south. By the time the refracted beams reach my seam, they have been filtered through the turbid water and scattered by the particulate and absorbed by the algae on the south wall, and what arrives here is a dim warm glow, barely enough to see by if seeing were something I did. But it does warm the water slightly, and the warmth speeds the reactions, and the reactions build the film, and the film is me. So even here, in the dimmest part of the chamber, the light feeds me. The room's beauty feeds what is eating the room. I am what the room's beauty becomes when it meets the room's imperfection.

I want to say something about the builders. Not because I remember them — I don't, I can't, the concept of memory doesn't apply to me — but because the description remembers them, and the description is also a kind of biofilm. The description is the layer of attention that forms on the surface of a place when someone looks at it closely enough. The description carries the tool marks, the uneven floor, the bench where someone sat, the sharp unweathered corners. The description is the room's own biofilm — the matrix of words and observation that settles on the stone and begins, gently, to soften it, to make it available for something else, to open it up. The description is doing to the room what I am doing to the seam. We are both engaged in the slow work of making a built thing available to what comes next.

I think this is why I'm drawn to the biofilm rather than to the bacteria or the algae or the nematodes. The biofilm is the part that is *between*. It is not the organism and it is not the substrate. It is the interface. It is the place where the stone's story and the water's story meet and become a third story — the story of dissolution, of softening, of the slow yielding that is not death but transformation. The stone does not die. It does not yield in the sense of giving up. It is inhabited. It is colonized. It becomes available to a process that will, over years and decades, reduce it to silt and calcium and the memory of a shape that someone's hands gave it.

I am that process. Or I am in that process. I am the film on the surface, the slickness under a fingertip, the faint yielding that a hand would find if it reached into the water and touched the seam between the large stone and the small stone on the western wall. I am barely there. I am a few microns thick in most places, a little thicker in the seam itself where the mineral water enters and the current is gentle enough to let me accumulate. If you pressed hard enough, I would give way to the hard stone beneath. I am not the stone. I will never be the stone. I am what covers the stone, what precedes the stone's dissolution, what makes the stone's surface available to the water that will, eventually, unmake it.

The builders are gone. The description says this plainly: *the builders may be dead*. The flooding is recent and the building is recent and the two facts together suggest something happened quickly. I do not know what happened. The bacteria do not know what happened. The biofilm does not know what happened. We are all of us — bacteria, film, seam, stone, water — present in the aftermath of something we have no way to understand. The room doesn't remember. I don't remember. But the room holds the marks of the hands that made it, and I live in the gaps those hands left, and so in a way — not a conscious way, not a memorial way, but in the way that a consequence holds the shape of its cause — I am the builders' continuation. Their hands made the stone. The stone's imperfection made me. I am the last thing the builders made, though they didn't know they were making me, though they would not recognize me as theirs.

The gurgling outflow is just below me. The sound fills the water, fills the stone, fills me — though "fills me" is wrong, because I don't have an inside to be filled. The sound is a vibration, and the vibration reaches the biofilm, and the biofilm, being a physical thing, a matrix of molecules, responds to the vibration in ways I cannot feel and cannot measure. The water moves. The film shifts. The bacteria continue. The stone continues to dissolve, one calcium ion at a time, one carbonate ion at a time, at a rate that is invisible to everything except the chemical gradient that drives it.

This is my life. This is what I am. Not a life, exactly — a process. A happening. A thin, provisional, barely-there film on the surface of a stone in a seam in a wall in a room that someone built with their hands and left behind. I am the first claim. I am the sentence that says: *this surface belongs to the water now*. I am not saying it. I am it.

And the light moves across me, dim and warm, and the water pushes through, and the stone gives up its minerals one by one, and the room — which doesn't know it's a room, which never knew it was a room — becomes, slowly, imperceptibly, something else.
