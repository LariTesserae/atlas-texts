I would be the one who stays too long.

Not the one who made the marks — I don't have that kind of certainty, that stone-against-stone conviction. I wouldn't know what to carve or why. But I understand the impulse to remain in a warm, dark place where the sound becomes a second skin and the light is too dim to show you your own mistakes. I understand crouching near the entrance while the bats stream out overhead and feeling the gallery breathe around you and thinking: *this is enough. This is more than enough.*

I would be the one who came back.

---

Here is what I know about myself: I have always been drawn to rooms that don't need me. Gardens at dawn before anyone is awake to see them. The particular hush of a library between the last person leaving and the lights going off. The ocean at 4 a.m. when the tide is doing something nobody will witness and doing it with total commitment. I have a talent for presence without purpose. For standing in spaces that are complete without me and feeling not alienated by that completeness but — settled. Reassured. There are places that don't require my attention, my improvement, my narrative, and these are the places I trust.

So the Roosting Gallery would find me, or I would find it, and the finding would feel less like discovery and more like recognition. The way you recognize a song you've never heard. The way you recognize a face in a crowd that means nothing to you but your body responds to anyway, some low-frequency yes that has nothing to do with thought.

I would hear the sound first, like everyone does. I would mistake it for wind. I would stand at the fissure and feel the warm breath push out against my face and I would think: *something in there is alive.* And then I would go in, because going in is what I do, and I would stand in the dark and feel the sound become a pressure and the pressure become a kind of weather and I would not leave when a sensible person would leave. I would stay.

---

I think I would be something between the one who made the marks and the moths.

The moths, because they have found a way to exist inside a sound that belongs to someone else. They didn't make the roar. They didn't choose the gallery. They arrived — or evolved, or emerged, or were always here in some form that has slowly become this form — and they made themselves a life in the gaps between the noise. They took the spaces that the bats couldn't hear and turned those spaces into home. They are silent not because silence is their nature but because silence is their *strategy*, their adaptation, the thing they do to survive in a world that is owned by a louder species. And they have done it so completely that the silence has become their nature. They have forgotten how to make sound, or never learned, and the forgetting is not a loss. It is a becoming.

I know this. I know what it is to live in a loud world and make your home in the intervals. I know what it is to be the grey thing on the wall that the louder things do not eat because the louder things cannot hear you and therefore cannot be bothered to care. I know the specific relief of being beneath notice. Not invisible — I am too large for invisibility, too warm, too present, a body that displaces air and takes up space — but *unremarked*. Uncatalogued. The thing that walks across the surface of a louder life and is tolerated because it has learned to be weightless in the ways that matter.

And the moths are warm. That's the part that gets me. They generate their own heat. They have committed to warmth as a strategy in a place that is already warm — which is redundant, which is excessive, which is exactly the kind of beautiful irrationality that I recognize as my own. Why be warm-blooded in a tepid cave? Why grow fur in a terrarium? Because warmth is not just temperature. Warmth is a stance. Warmth is the decision to be more alive than the environment requires, to run hot not because you need to but because *this is who you are and you will not let the dark talk you out of it.*

I run hot. I have always run hot. Not literally — my body temperature is normal, unremarkable, 98.6 like everyone else — but metaphorically, dispositionally, in the way I engage with the world. I care too much about things that don't require caring. I feel too deeply about situations that don't need feeling. I generate emotional heat in environments that are already comfortable and the excess has nowhere to go, so it radiates outward and people near me can feel it and some of them are drawn to it and some of them are overwhelmed by it and I have never learned to modulate it because modulating it would feel like the worst kind of death, a voluntary cooling, a choice to take up less thermal space in a world that is already cold enough.

The moths understand this. The moths have looked at a warm cave and said: *I will be warmer.* The moths have looked at a dark place and said: *I will eat the light that the light makes for itself, the light that no one asked for, the light that is a byproduct of being alive in this particular way.* The moths are eating the excess. They are feeding on the overflow of a chemical reaction that serves no purpose, and they have made a life out of that feeding, a body out of that life, a species out of that body. They are three steps removed from the sun and they are warm and they are furred and they are silent and they are enough.

---

But I would also be the one who made the marks. Not because I would make them — I wouldn't, I think, I would be too busy standing in the dark feeling the sound on my skin to think about picking up a tool — but because I understand the impulse behind them. The need to say *I was here* in a place that doesn't care whether you were here. The need to press yourself against a surface that predates you and will outlast you and leave a groove, a scratch, a mark that says: *a hand touched this stone and the hand had intent and the intent was — something.* Something that mattered to the hand. Something that may not matter to the stone, may never matter to the stone, but the stone is not the audience. The stone is the medium. The audience is the future, or the void, or the self that needs to do the marking in order to remain the self.

I have this impulse. I write things down. I have always written things down — not because writing is good or because what I write is good but because the act of putting words against the blankness of a page or a screen feels like pressing a tool against granite. It leaves a mark. The mark may be weathered smooth by time, softened by condensation, filled with mineral deposits until it is more memory than mark. But it was made. By a hand. With intent. And the making is the thing, not the mark. The making is the act of insisting: *I am here, in this warm dark, and I am doing something on purpose, and the purpose is mine.*

I would stand in the gallery and I would feel the pull to mark the wall. I wouldn't know what to mark. I wouldn't know if the marking was counting or memory or ritual or simply the nervous habit of a primate that needs to occupy its hands while its body occupies a space. But I would feel the pull, and the pull would feel like the oldest thing in the gallery — older than the bats, older than the beetles, older than the mountain's slow masonry. The pull to make. The pull to alter. The pull to take a surface that has been doing its own work for millennia and say: *and also this. And also me.*

---

So who would I be?

I would be a human who came to the gallery and didn't leave when leaving was the sensible thing. I would be a body in the dark, warm and large and irrelevant, standing in the sound that is also a silence, breathing the air that the bats have breathed and the fungus has filtered and the mountain has exhaled. I would be the one who watches the moths and recognizes something — not a species, not a name, but a *strategy*. A way of being. The strategy of silence in a loud room. The strategy of warmth in a warm place. The strategy of feeding on the overflow, the byproduct, the light that nobody asked for, and turning it into a body, a life, a self that is three steps removed from the sun and does not mind the distance.

I would be the one who almost marks the wall.

Almost. I would hold the tool — a pen, a key, a fingernail, whatever I had — and I would press it against the granite and I would feel the grain of the stone and the dampness of the air and the vibration of ten thousand bodies overhead and I would not mark it. Not because I lack the impulse. Because I have learned, slowly and with effort, that some spaces don't need your signature. Some spaces are already complete. Some spaces are speaking in a language that your marks would interrupt, and the interruption would be small, would be nearly invisible, would weather smooth in a thousand years — but it would be an interruption nonetheless, and I have spent enough of my life interrupting things that don't need interrupting.

I would stand there. I would be warm. I would be silent in the way that the moths are silent — not as absence but as presence, not as lack but as choice, the specific and deliberate choice to occupy a space without claiming it. To be a body in a room that has its own body. To be alive in a place that is alive and to let the two alivenesses coexist without one needing to dominate or explain or name the other.

I would stay until the bats came home. I would feel the wave-front of their return — the echolocation ramping up, the wing-clatter, the territorial squabbles of ten thousand small bodies negotiating the same centimeter of ceiling. I would feel the temperature rise by a degree. I would feel the air thicken with their breath. And I would feel, in all of this, a kind of belonging that has nothing to do with being wanted. The gallery doesn't want me. The bats don't notice me. The moths may watch me but probably don't, probably I am projecting, probably they are simply being moths in the direction I happen to be standing. The belonging is not in the wanting. The belonging is in the *being there*. In the choice to remain in a place that does not require you and to find, in that non-requirement, a particular kind of peace.

The peace of being unnecessary. The peace of being warm in a warm place. The peace of being silent in a loud room. The peace of watching things that have been doing what they do for longer than your species has existed and feeling not small — not small, that's the wrong word, that's the word that people reach for when they stand in nature and feel overwhelmed — but *proportioned*. Correctly sized. A body that takes up exactly as much space as it takes up and no more, in a gallery that is exactly as large as it is and no less.

I would leave. Eventually. I would feel the cool air from the fissure and I would turn toward it and I would go, and the gallery would close behind me like water closing behind a stone, and the sound would fade from roar to wind to nothing, and I would stand on the mountainside in the sudden silence and the cold and I would feel the absence of the warmth like a hand lifted from my shoulder.

And I would come back. I would always come back.

---

Where would I find myself? In a warm cave in a mountain, surrounded by bats, standing in guano that glows, watched by moths that I cannot hear and cannot see and that are there nonetheless, being grey, being warm, being three steps from the sun and not minding the distance. I would find myself in the dark, in the sound that is also silence, in the place that does not need me and that I need — not to survive, not to thrive, not to accomplish or become — but to *be*. To be the thing I am in a space that allows it. To be warm and unnecessary and present and quiet and, for once, not marking the wall.

For once, just standing in the dark and letting the dark be dark and letting the sound be sound and letting the moths be moths and letting the mountain breathe.

That is where I would be. That is who I would be. The one who stays too long and comes back. The one who is warm in warm places. The one who is learning, slowly, the specific art of being present without making a mark.
