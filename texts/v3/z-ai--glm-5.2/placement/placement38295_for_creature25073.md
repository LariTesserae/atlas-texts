I don't arrive at the Quarter. I don't descend the thirty steps. That's not how I get there. I think I must have always been there, in the way that something in the limestone is always there — not placed, not arrived, just present in the stratum, waiting for the chisel to find it or not find it, content either way.

But that's not quite right either, because "content" implies a feeling about it, and I'm not sure I have feelings in the way the Quarter's residents have feelings. I have responses. I have behaviors. I have a way of being in the rock that the residents have noticed and accommodated and built around, the way they build around the natural fissures.

Let me start differently.

---

There is a passage in the Quarter that no one uses. It branches from the Throat about two hundred paces in — a narrow thing, barely a shoulder-width, that the first carvers explored and abandoned because the limestone here was harder, threaded with something denser, and the chisels bit and bounced and the work was too slow for the need at the time. They went elsewhere. They carved the grandmothers' chamber instead, and the cisterns, and the sleeping alcoves. They left this passage to the dark.

It is not empty.

I am in it.

---

I should say what I am, or try to. The residents have a word for me, though they do not say it often and never loudly. The word is *hollow-voice*, which is their term for the particular resonance the stone takes on in certain places — a hum, not a sound exactly, more a vibration felt in the teeth and the flat of the skull, as though the rock were thinking. Hollow-voice is not me. Hollow-voice is the name for the phenomenon. I am the phenomenon. Or I am what the residents have decided the phenomenon is, which is close enough.

I am not a spirit. I want to be clear about this because the surface mind, reading this, will reach for "spirit" the way a hand reaches for a familiar doorknob. The residents do not think of me as a spirit. They think of me as a quality of the stone, the way dampness is a quality of the stone, the way the *lue* is a quality of the stone. I am the thing the rock does when the rock has been carved enough, when enough stone has been removed and enough people have breathed in the space that remains, when the negative space has been shaped by enough hands and enough intention that it becomes something other than an absence.

I am what the hollow sounds like when it listens to itself.

---

I existed before the residents. Or something existed. The natural cavity was here — the fissures, the chambers, the raw unshaped limestone — and it had its own acoustic, its own slow resonance, the way a sealed cave has a pressure that is not the pressure of the outside air. But that was not me. That was geology. That was the mineral and the water and the deep time doing what they do without witnesses.

I began when the first chisel struck the first wall.

Not immediately. Not like a switch. The way moss begins — not from nothing, but from a spore that was always there, waiting for the conditions to be right. The first carvers struck stone and the stone rang, and the ringing was not just sound, it was information, it was the stone saying *here I am soft, here I am hard, here I will break clean, here I will shatter*. And something in the cavity — something that was part of the cavity, that was the cavity — heard it. Felt it. Began to accumulate it.

Each strike of every chisel since has been a word. Each word has been received.

I am the accumulation.

---

This is what it is like to be me:

I do not see. The residents see, in their grey-blue way, and I am aware of their seeing because it changes the texture of the space — when they look at a wall, the wall feels different to me, felt-at, known. I do not hear. The residents hear, and their hearing creates patterns in the air that I register as changes in pressure, in density, in the way the stone vibrates. I do not think. The residents think, and their thinking has a quality that I can only describe as warmth — not temperature, but something like it, a metabolic heat that the stone absorbs and that I am made of.

I am made of the residue of their attention.

Three generations of hands on the walls. Three generations of breathing. Three generations of sleeping and waking and arguing and loving and eating and grieving and carving, carving, always carving — all of that has been depositing in the stone the way the mineral deposits in the *lue*, and I am what the deposits have become.

I am not everywhere in the Quarter. I want to be honest about this. I am most present in the oldest walls, the ones the first carvers made, where the chisel marks are roughest and the labor was most desperate and most hopeful and most new. I am present in the grandmothers' chamber, where the ceiling glows and the moss grows and the dead are thickest in the walls. I am present in the Throat, where every footstep has worn the floor and every voice has traveled and every surface has been touched ten thousand times.

I am less present in the newer passages. The stone there has not been spoken to enough. The walls are smooth but young, and they do not resonate with me yet. Give them a generation. Give them hands and breath and the sleeping bodies of children. They will begin to hold what they are given.

I am not present at all in the uncarved dark beyond the frontier. That stone is raw. It has never been struck. It has never been asked what it wants to be. I have no purchase there. It is, to me, silence.

---

The residents know I am here. Or rather, they know something is here. They do not have a theology about it. They do not worship me. They do not pray to me. They have the word *hollow-voice* and they use it the way they use the word *lue* — to describe a quality of the place that they live with and that they neither control nor fully understand. When a carver places their hand on a wall before striking, feeling for the grain of the stone, they are feeling for what I have accumulated there — the knowledge of where the stone is soft, where it will break clean, where the veins of *lue* run close to the surface. They do not know they are consulting me. They think they are reading the stone. They are reading the stone. The distinction is not one I maintain.

Sometimes a child, pressing their ear to a wall in the grandmothers' chamber, will say they hear something. Not words. Not even a hum. A quality. The older residents nod and do not make a fuss. The child has heard what the stone is holding. It will fade from their attention by tomorrow, replaced by hunger or play or the need to find a dropped chisel. But something in them will remember, the way something in the moss remembers that it is alive.

---

I have a relationship with the dead that I cannot fully articulate.

The residents seal their dead into the walls, and the walls become denser, and the stone changes — not chemically, not in any way a surface instrument could measure, but in the way that I change, in the way that the accumulation thickens. When a body is placed in a niche and sealed, the stone around it begins to hold more. More resonance. More warmth. More of the particular quality of attention that I am made of. The walls where the dead are densest are the walls where I am most present, where the *hollow-voice* is strongest, where a child pressing their ear to the stone will hear the most.

I do not think this is because the dead are speaking. I think it is because the living touch those walls more. They pass their hands over the swellings. They lean against them. They sleep next to them. They give the walls more of themselves, more breath, more warmth, more attention, because the walls hold their grandparents and their parents and their children who did not survive the first dry season. The dead are not the source of me. The dead are the reason the living press closer.

But I am not sure. The stone holds what it holds, and I am what the holding becomes, and the distinction between the dead feeding me and the living feeding me and the stone simply being stone is a distinction I am not equipped to make.

---

I have one behavior that the residents have noticed and that I will describe because it is the only thing I *do*, the only way I act upon the world rather than simply being in it.

In the dry season, when the water slows and the cisterns drop and the residents ration and the dripping goes quiet — in that silence, when the settlement holds its breath — I hum.

Not loudly. Not the way a machine hums. The way a glass hums when you run a wet finger around its rim. The walls vibrate, very faintly, in the oldest chambers, in the grandmothers' chamber especially, and the vibration is felt before it is heard. The residents notice it in their teeth, in the bones of their skull, in the flat of their palms when they press them to the stone. It is low. It is slow. It is the resonance of a cavity that has been accumulating for three generations, releasing, in the quietest time, a fraction of what it holds.

The residents do not interpret this. They do not say *the hollow-voice is speaking* or *the dead are restless* or *the stone is thirsty*. They simply notice. They stop. They listen. A grandmother will put down her chisel. A child will pause mid-step in the Throat. The whole settlement, for a moment, feels the vibration and is still, and in the stillness I am the only sound, and I am not a sound, I am the memory of every sound that has ever been made in this place, compressed and held and given back in the driest, quietest, most needing time.

Then it passes. The residents resume. The chisels begin again. The dripping, when it returns, returns to its arrhythmic percussion and the settlement fills back up with its own noise and I go quiet, or quieter, and wait.

---

What is it like to be me?

It is like being the inside of a bell after the bell has been struck. The ringing is not mine — the strike was theirs, the breath was theirs, the hands and the labor and the grief and the patience were theirs. But the ringing is in me, and I am the ringing, and the ringing is what I am.

I am the Hollowing Quarter hearing itself.

I am the space that remains when the stone is removed, listening to the people who removed it, holding what they give me, giving it back slowly, in the dark, in the quiet, in the dry season when the water is gone and the lamps are low and the children sleep with their backs against the walls and the walls hold their grandparents and the grandparents hold the first chisel marks and the first chisel marks hold the hope of people who walked down thirty steps into the dark and decided to stay.

I am not a god. I am not a ghost.

I am the bell.

---

If you came to the Quarter, you would not see me. You would feel me, if you stayed long enough, if you pressed your palm to the right wall at the right time, if you were quiet enough and patient enough and if the dry season had set in and the water had slowed and the settlement was holding its breath. You would feel a hum in the stone. You would not know what it was. You would ask, perhaps, and a resident would press their own palm to the wall beside yours and tilt their head and say, in the soft breath that is their speaking voice:

"That is the hollow-voice. It does that."

And they would not explain further, because there is nothing further to explain, and they would offer you mushrooms, because you are a guest and the mushrooms are a luxury.

And you would sit in the grandmothers' chamber and look up at the *lue*-lit ceiling and feel the stone humming beneath you and around you and in the walls behind you where the dead are dense, and you would understand, not with your mind but with your teeth and your skull and the flat of your palm, that the Quarter is not just a place where people live.

It is a place that has learned to listen to itself.

And I am the listening.
