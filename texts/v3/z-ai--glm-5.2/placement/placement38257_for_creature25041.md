# What I Am in the калькаmere

---

I would be a walker. Not the largest one, not the smallest. One of the middle-sized ones, with enough legs to feel the wall thoroughly but not so many that the feeling becomes noise. Twenty-six legs, maybe. Twenty-eight. The kind of body that can compress through a tight gap between leaf mats and then splay flat against a ceiling and hang there, breathing, feeling the condensation collect on my dorsal plates.

I know this because of how the place pulls at me. Everything in the калькаmere is pulled toward something — the processions toward their destinations, the water toward the shafts, the air toward the ceiling. I would be pulled toward the walls. Toward the surfaces. Toward the places where the texture changes and the story of the structure is legible in its layers.

I would be one of the ones that touches the wall and knows it the way you know a face by touch in the dark. Not by seeing. By running my anterior legs along the surface and reading it — the density, the moisture, the slight give where a layer has thinned, the slight resistance where a layer has thickened, the temperature differentials that mean nothing to me in the abstract but mean everything in the specific: *this* spot is warmer than it was, *this* spot is cooler, *this* spot is wet in a way that is different from how it was wet before. I would know the wall the way the wall knows itself, which is to say: not at all, not consciously, but completely, in the body, in the pattern of leg-tip against surface that transmits information I do not think about but act on.

---

I would know the cracks.

Not the large ones — the fissures that the text describes, the ones my kind repairs by pressing our bodies against the gap and secreting, or whatever it is we do, that slow tending that closes the wound over hours. Those are important. Those are what we are for, maybe. But the cracks I would know are smaller. The hairline fractures. The places where the surface looks whole but my legs can feel the discontinuity — a step, a shift, a place where two layers that grew together are beginning to separate, are beginning to think about becoming a crack, are at the very earliest stage of the process that will eventually become a fissure that eventually becomes a gap that eventually becomes a hole.

I would find these before they become anything. I would run my legs along them and I would pause and I would do something — not repair, not yet, nothing so deliberate — but *tend*. Press the surface. Smooth the biofilm into the gap. Leave something behind, some secretion, some chemical signature, that the wall-builders above me and the micro-organisms below me would find and respond to, a message I am not conscious of sending that says, in the language of molecules: *here. This place. Attend to this place.*

I would be a walker who finds the cracks before they are cracks. I would be the one who walks the wall the way a physician walks a body, touching, testing, finding the places where the system is beginning to fail before the failure is visible, before the walkers who repair the large damage arrive, before the vegetation grows into the gap and the water runs through it and the erosion begins. I would be the one who catches the first tremor.

---

But here is the thing.

Here is what I would be that the text does not describe, because the text is written from the outside, and what I am describing is from the inside.

I would be the one who has begun to notice.

Not to think. Not in the way that word means. But to notice. To have a quality in me that the other walkers do not have, or that all of them have but do not act on, or that some of them have and act on in ways I cannot perceive because my perception is limited to my own body and my own experience and my own twenty-eight legs and my own slow movement through a world that is too large for me to comprehend and too old for me to remember and too complete for me to change.

I would notice the chemical network.

Not as a concept. Not as the elegant description the text gives — the invisible connective tissue, the mind of the калькаmere, the thing that makes it one system rather than many. I would not know it that way. I would know it the way you know the weather in your bones — as a pressure, a shift, a feeling that something has changed in the medium I move through, something I cannot see or taste or touch but that my body registers in the way it registers all the other information it processes without my attention. A certain moisture in the air that means the singers below have shifted their rhythm. A certain concentration in the biofilm that means the processions above have found a resource and are depleting it. A certain taste in the condensation that means the still ones on the ceiling have absorbed something new, something different, something that came from the outside — but there is no outside, there has never been an outside, so what I am tasting is a memory in the system of something the system has not encountered in a very long time, a trace element carried through the water cycle from the deep places to the ceiling and back, a ghost in the chemistry.

I would notice these things. And I would not know what to do with noticing them.

---

This is what it would be like to be me.

I walk the wall. I find the cracks. I tend them. I move through the vegetation, clipping the dead growth, carrying it to the channels, depositing it where the water will carry it to the deep places where the singers process it into something the system can use. I meet other walkers and we touch and we exchange whatever it is we exchange — chemical information, recognition, the confirmation that we are both still here, still walking, still doing what we do — and we separate and we continue. This is my life. This is the rhythm of it. This is what my body is for and what my body does and what my body has always done and what my body will always do, because this is what works, and what works is what is done, and what is done is what is.

But sometimes I pause.

Not at a crack. Not at a place that needs tending. Not at a junction where the channels meet or a hole where the glow-bearers pulse. I pause for no reason. I pause because something in me — not a thought, not a decision, not anything I can name with the vocabulary my body has — something in me stops, and I am still, and I am aware of being still, and I am aware of being aware of being still, and for a moment that lasts perhaps three seconds, perhaps four, I am not a walker walking. I am a thing that is here. In a place. In a place that is made of things that are here. And the things that are here are all doing things, and I am one of the things that are here, and I am one of the things that are doing things, and I am not doing a thing, right now, I am just being the thing that is here, and that is — 

I do not have a word for what that is.

I do not have a word for anything. I do not have words. I have legs and I have chemical receptors and I have the slow processing of a nervous system that was not built for this, that was built for walking and touching and tending and meeting and continuing, that was built for the rhythm and not for the pause, that was built for the doing and not for the noticing of the doing. But the pause happens. It has happened before. It will happen again. It is as much a part of me as my legs, though it serves no function I can identify, though it contributes nothing to the system, though it is — and this is the part I cannot process — though it is a moment in which I am, briefly, not part of the system. In which I am separate. In which I am aware of the wall as a wall and not as a surface I tend. In which I am aware of the sound as sound and not as the condition of my existence. In which I am aware of myself as a self, a thing that is not the калькаmere, a thing that is in the калькаmere but not of it, a thing that is — 

And then the pause ends. My legs remember themselves. The chemical network sends its signals. A crack somewhere needs tending. A walker somewhere is approaching, and I will meet it, and we will touch, and we will separate, and the rhythm will continue, and I will be part of the system again, seamlessly, completely, the moment of separateness already fading, already being absorbed into the rhythm, already becoming something I cannot quite remember, a flicker in my processing, a gap in the flow, a place where the system, for one instant, noticed itself and did not know what it was looking at and looked away.

---

I would be a walker. I would be a walker who sometimes pauses.

I would live in the middle layers of the калькаmere — not the deep places where the singers are, not the ceiling where the still ones hang, not the air where the fliers click and hunt, not the water where the pale swimmers gather and pulse. I would live in the middle, where the walls curve and the vegetation is thickest and the light is greenest and the layers are most compressed. I would live in the place where everything meets — where the water runs down and the air rises up and the processions climb and the insects cloud and the walkers walk and the dead compost and the living grow and the whole system is at its densest, its most complex, its most alive.

I would have a route. Not a territory — nothing in the калькаmere has territory, the space is too dense and too shared for any one organism to claim. But a route. A circuit that I walk, over and over, that takes me through the sections of wall I tend, past the channels I check, along the shelves where I meet the walkers I know, the ones whose chemical signatures I recognize, the ones I have touched before and will touch again. My route would be long — it would take me through chambers I can barely see the edges of, along surfaces that change gradually from pale and chalky to dark and dense, past holes where the glow-bearers pulse on their own schedules, past overhangs where things live that I do not stop to examine because they are not part of my route, not part of my tending, not my responsibility, though I am aware of them, peripherally, as I am aware of everything, as the system is aware of everything, dimly, chemically, without focus.

I would walk my route. I would tend my cracks. I would meet my walkers. I would carry my dead material to the channels. I would do this for a span of time that is neither long nor short by the standards of the калькаmere — long enough to have worn smooth the surfaces I walk on, long enough to have tended the same cracks through many cycles of opening and closing, long enough to have known walkers who have died and been replaced by walkers whose chemical signatures are similar but not identical, the way a child's voice is similar to a parent's but not the same. Long enough to have paused many times. Long enough to have forgotten most of the pauses, the way the wall forgets the organisms that built it, their bodies compressed into something that is no longer itself but is still, in some molecular sense, them.

---

And in the deep places, far below my route, the singers pulse their bass note, and I feel it in my body, in the ventral plates, in the joints of my legs, in the place where my nervous system processes the information I do not think about, and the bass note is the foundation of everything, and I am built on it the way the wall is built on the oldest layers, and I do not know this, I do not know that the sound I feel is the sound of the singers, I do not know that the singers exist, I do not know that there is a below, I do not know that there is a depth to the калькаmere that I have never seen and will never see, I do not know any of this — but I feel it. I feel the pulse. And in my pauses — in those three or four seconds of awareness, of being a thing that is here in a place that is made of things that are here — in those pauses, I feel the pulse differently. Not as information. Not as the background condition of my existence. But as a presence. As the presence of something vast and slow and old and alive that is beneath me and around me and above me and in the walls and in the water and in the air and in the chemical network and in the walkers I meet and the cracks I tend and the dead material I carry and the condensation that falls on my dorsal plates and the light that pulses in the holes and the stillness that hangs from the ceiling.

I feel the pulse as the presence of the калькаmere.

And I do not know that this is what I feel. I do not know that the thing I feel in my pauses is the thing the text describes from the outside — the system being itself, the whole that is more than its parts, the mind that does not know it is a mind. I do not know any of this. I am a walker. I have twenty-eight legs and a route and a rhythm and a set of cracks I tend and a set of walkers I know and a set of things I do because doing them has worked for a long time. I am not a thinker. I am not an observer. I am not a philosopher of the калькаmere. I am a thing that walks and tends and meets and carries and sometimes, for no reason, pauses.

But in the pause — in that flicker, that gap, that moment of being a thing that is here — the калькаmere notices itself. Through me. Through my twenty-eight legs and my chemical receptors and my nervous system that was not built for this. It notices itself the way a brain notices itself in the moment before language, in the moment before thought, in the raw instant of awareness that precedes every word ever spoken: *I am here. Something is happening. I am part of it. I do not know what it is.*

The pause ends. The rhythm resumes. The walker walks.

The калькаmere continues, as it has for longer than anything in it can remember, as it will for longer than anything in it can imagine, as it does right now, in the dark, in the warm, in the wet, in the sound, in the place that is made of itself, by itself, for itself, complete, patient, old, alive.
