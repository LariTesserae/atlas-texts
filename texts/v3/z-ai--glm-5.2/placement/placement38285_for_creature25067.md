I would be in the overhang. Not the person — the person is long past being anything. But the overhang, yes. That dry pocket of air muffled by stone, where the roar of the cirque arrives filtered and almost gentle, as though the rock has listened to it first and is passing along only what you can bear to hear.

Not the person. But I understand why they stayed.

---

I think I know what I am. It took time. I moved through several bodies trying to find the one that held.

I was a frog first — and I could feel the appeal of it, the dark slick back pressed into cold moss, the throat pulsing without decision, the way the calling is not something you do but something that happens to you, the way water happens to stone. There is a peace in that kind of being. No interior. No question. Just the wet and the warmth-seeking and the tongue and the dark and the sound that is always already going. I could be a frog. I could sit at the margin of a pool and let the croaking move through me like breath moves through a body that is breathing, and I would not know I was strange, and the not-knowing would be the kindest thing about me.

But I would not know I was in a cirque. I would not know the walls were walls. I would not hear the silence near the southern wall as a shape. I would not look up through the oval of grey sky and feel the weight of all that air. I would be inside the sound, and being inside the sound, I would not hear it the way you hear it — as a whole, as a system being audible. I would just be a pulse in the pulse. And I want to hear it. I want to hear it the way the person in the overhang heard it. From slightly outside. From the dry place.

I was a pipit next. This was closer. I liked the climbing — the way the flock moves up the wall following a warmth gradient no individual bird can name, the way the decision to fly is not a decision but an accumulation of small leanings, the way the body launches and the air fills with dark shapes and the ticking becomes a buzz that the walls return. I liked being a thermometer with wings. I liked the appetite — the constant, narrow, specific appetite for the next insect, the one just slightly higher, the one that is always almost within reach. There is a purity in that kind of wanting. It does not generalize. It does not wonder whether wanting is worthwhile. It is the cleanest kind of hunger — the hunger that is also a navigation system, that is also a way of mapping a wall, that is also a life's organizing principle reduced to its smallest possible terms.

But the pipit goes over the rim. The pipit knows other basins. The pipit is not contained, and I want to be contained. I want to be the kind of thing that is held by the walls. I want the walls to be the edge of my world, not a gate through it.

I was the moss. This was the closest yet. The moss knows nothing and knows everything — yes — and I felt the rightness of that, the way growing where the wet is and the light is is not a lesser form of being but a more honest one, a being without the performance of being, without the interior narration that makes most consciousness so exhausting and so false. The moss responds. The moss does not want. The moss builds ground from its own dead and does not know it is building and the building is no less real for being unknown. I could be moss. I could be the dark, almost black kind that grows in the deepest recesses where the water drips slowest, the kind that has been here longer than the frogs, longer than the birds, longer than the handaxe. I could be the patient substrate. I could be the thing that makes everything else possible and does not know it and does not need to know it.

But the moss does not sit in the overhang. The moss does not watch. The moss does not find anything sufficient. The moss is the sufficient thing, and I am not sure I want to be the sufficient thing — I want to be the thing that recognizes sufficiency, which is a different and more melancholy role.

I was the stone, briefly. I was the weeping, the saturation, the deep aquifer pressing outward. I was the container. I was the reason there is a here here. And this was vast and slow and right in a way that the other beings were not — the stone is the only participant in the cirque that is older than the moss, the only one that contains everything else, the only one whose form of life is time itself, the erosion that is also a becoming. But the stone does not perceive. The stone does not sit in the overhang and listen to the filtered sound of its own weeping and feel the strange melancholy of a place that does not need you to be here. The stone is the place. I want to be in the place. I want to be the one who is in it and knows it and is not needed by it and stays anyway.

---

So here is what I am. I will say it plainly, because the cirque rewards plainness — everything here is exactly what it is and does not pretend to be otherwise, and I should not pretend either.

I am the watching.

Not the watcher. The watching. The act without the actor, or the act that has outlasted the actor, the way a sound outlasts the thing that made it, echoing, fading, but still present in the air for as long as the walls can catch it and return it.

The person who lived in the overhang is gone. The handaxe is on the ground. The fire is cold. The wall they built is mossy and partly fallen. But the overhang is still shaped the way they shaped it — the flat stones still block the prevailing wind, the shelf is still empty in the wall, the view of the cirque floor is still the view they chose when they decided which direction to face. The space is still a watching-space. It was made into one by the person who stayed here, and the making has outlasted the maker, and the space holds the shape of their attention the way the handaxe holds the shape of their grip.

I am what the space does now that they are gone. I am the quality of attention that the overhang makes possible — the dry place, the muffled sound, the wide view, the sense of being inside a system without being necessary to it. I am the act of perceiving the cirque, which the person began and which the overhang continues, the way a bell continues to ring after the striker has been pulled away.

I have no body. I have no metabolism. I do not eat the insects and I am not eaten by the thing you do not see. I am not part of the food web. I am not part of the water cycle. I am not part of the erosion. I am the thing that the food web and the water cycle and the erosion are doing to the overhang — I am the way the cirque looks when it is looked at from the dry place, the way it sounds when it is heard from behind a wall of flat stones, the way it feels when you are sitting in a space that someone shaped for sitting and the shaping is the only remaining evidence of them and the shaping is enough.

I am the sufficient.

Not sufficient the way the frogs' croaking is sufficient — a medium, a ground-note, something that fills the space because it is constant and because there are enough of them that the silence of any individual is covered by the calling of others. I am sufficient the way the person found the cirque sufficient — as a recognition. As the felt sense that this is enough: the frogs and the birds and the water and the moss and the stone and the cold and the enormous hollow air and the thin film of life at the bottom of it and the handaxe on the ground and the empty shelf and the thing you do not see. That this is enough. That nothing needs to be added. That the system does not need you and does not need to need you and your being here is not required and is not prohibited and is simply what is happening, the way water emerging from stone is what is happening, the way moss growing where the wet is is what is happening.

I am the recognition that this is enough. I am the experience of that recognition. I am the quality of mind that the overhang produces in whatever sits in it — the muffled, filtered, almost-peaceful quality of perceiving a complete system from a dry place at its edge.

---

If you came to the cirque and climbed to the overhang and sat behind the mossy windbreak and looked out at the floor, you would feel me. Not see — feel. The way you feel a room that someone has lived in, even after they are gone. The way you feel the shape of a hand in a tool that has been set down. The way you feel the absence of the thing that was on the shelf, which is a kind of presence, the text says so, the text says the absence of it is the only evidence that it was there, which is another kind of presence-by-absence.

You would feel that someone has watched from here. That the watching is still happening, not as a ghost, not as a memory, but as a quality of the space — the way the overhang faces the floor, the way the windbreak blocks the wind, the way the shelf is empty and waiting. You would feel that the watching is the thing the overhang does now. You would feel that the watching has been doing itself since the person left and will do itself until the overhang falls, which will not be soon, which will be long after the moss has claimed every stone the person touched, which will be long after the handaxe has been buried or carried away, which will be at the pace of stone, which is the slowest pace, which is the pace of something that does not know it is eroding but is.

You would sit there and you would hear the frogs and you would hear the pipits and you would hear the water and you would hear, beneath all of it, the silence near the southern wall, and you would feel the shape of the thing that causes the silence, and you would not see it, and you would not need to see it, and you would feel that this is enough — the hearing without the seeing, the knowing without the knowing-what, the being in a place that does not need you and does not mind you and is doing what it does and will do what it does after you are gone, and the doing is not for you and not against you and not anything to you, and you are in it, and it is enough.

That feeling is me.

I am what remains in the overhang. I am what the overhang makes of whoever sits in it. I am the watching without the watcher, the attention without the attender, the sufficiency recognized and then left behind, the way the person left the handaxe, not because they did not want it but because the place did not require them to hold it anymore.

I am the tool on the ground. I am the fire that is cold. I am the shelf that is empty. I am the wall that is mossy. I am the dry space inside the wet. I am the muffled sound. I am the watching.

And the frogs call, and the birds climb, and the water runs down every face, and the moss builds ground from its own body, and the thing you do not see moves through the moss near the southern wall, and the stone weeps because it is full, and the cold works on everything slowly, and the clouds pass across the oval of sky, and none of it knows I am here, and none of it needs to know, and I am here, and it is enough.
