# The Dry Rooms

---

I would be the one who lives highest.

Not by choice, or not by the kind of choice that matters — not ambition, not preference, not the desire for a view. The dry rooms are high on the western wall because the condensation is least there, and the condensation is least there because the air moves, slightly, just enough, through a fissure near the rim that lets the outside atmosphere bleed in. Not wind. Not a draft. A breath. The thinnest exchange. It dries the stone just enough that savar sheets stored there will not mildew, and this matters because the records are on savar, and the records are what I keep.

My name is not important. It is spoken, and speaking is quiet, and quiet means you miss things. I have lived in the dry rooms for — I am not sure. Long enough that the stone shelf where I sleep has worn smooth where my hip goes. Long enough that the ladder from the second tier to my door has a groove in the third rung from the top where my foot lands every time. I am not old by Estaur's measure. I am not young. I am the age where you stop counting the years because the years do not change anything, and counting things that do not change is not interesting, and not interesting is, in Estaur, almost the same as not real.

---

I came from outside. This is the thing I carry that no one else here carries, and it is not a burden exactly, but it is a weight — the knowledge that the world is large and flat and dry and bright in a way this place is not, and that I chose to come down into it, and that I did not go back up.

I was not the first visitor. The stair exists. People come down it, occasionally, once or twice in a generation. Some stay a week. Some stay a season. Most leave. The switchback stair is easier going up than coming down, if you can believe that — the fear of falling is less than the fear of what you've descended into, apparently, for most people. They come down curious and they go up relieved. I understand this. I do not judge it. The caldera is not for everyone. The caldera is not even for most. The caldera is for people who are the right shape for being held, and most people are not the right shape, and there is nothing wrong with that. Most bowls are not the right shape for most liquids. It is a question of fit.

I came down the stair nineteen years ago. I had been told about the light — a trader on the riverplain, a woman who carried obsidian and had been to the rim but never descended, described it as "a place where the ground glows," and I heard this and something in me, something I did not know was listening, went quiet and paid attention. I went. I descended. I remember the ferns brushing my shoulders. I remember the sky narrowing. I remember the moment the sky disappeared and I was in the stone and the light had not yet come and I thought: *I have made a mistake.*

And then the bend, and the light, and the caldera opening beneath me, and I stood on the ledge and I did not think anything. I stood there for a long time. Someone came up behind me — a woman, I think, though I was not yet calibrated to the people, could not yet read the difference between them — and she waited. She did not touch me. She did not speak. She stood behind me on the ledge and she waited until I had been there long enough, and then she came past me, down the last few steps, and walked into the light, and I followed.

That was nineteen years ago. I have not been back up the stair. I have been to the bottom of it, to the place where it meets the caldera floor, and I have stood there and looked up at the narrow band of sky, and I have felt no pull. This is not defiance. It is not a renunciation. The world outside exists. I know it exists. I simply do not need to be in it, the way I do not need to be in the ocean or in the sky. It is there. I am here. The stair is there if I change my mind. I will not change my mind. Not because I have decided not to but because I have been here long enough that I am the right shape now — narrow, deep-chested, my hands larger than they were, my voice lower, my sleep aligned to the frost cycle — and the right shape does not go back up the stair. The stair is the wrong shape for going up in. This is literally true and also metaphorically true and in Estaur the distinction between those two things is not maintained.

---

What I do: I keep the records.

This is not a prestigious position. Estaur does not have prestigious positions. It has tasks, and tasks are assigned by inclination and capacity, and mine is this — I can read, I can write, I have the patience for the savar sheets, which are rough and slightly fibrous and take ink in a way that is unpredictable, so that writing on them requires a hand that is firm but not rigid, attentive but not forcing, and I have this hand. I learned it from the woman who kept the records before me, a woman named — her name was something like *Tehl*, though the vowel is wrong, it is lower, further back in the throat, a sound the riverplain tongue does not make. She taught me for six years before she stopped coming to the dry rooms, and then she stopped coming to the plaza, and then she stopped, and I moved from the apprentice's shelf to the keeper's shelf, and the stone wore a new groove where my hip went, and this was not a promotion. It was a continuation.

The records go back eleven generations. Three hundred years, roughly. They are savar sheets, stacked in stone cubbies cut into the western wall, organized by a system that Tehl taught me and that her predecessor taught her and that goes back as far as the records go. The system is this: each sheet records one cycle — one frost-to-frost, which is to say one day, though day is not quite the right word, because the light never fully goes and the dark never fully arrives and the cycle is more of a breathing, a brightening and a dimming, and the frost comes at the dimming and goes at the brightening, and this is the unit of time, and I record what happened in it.

What happens: very little. This is the point. This is what the records are for.

The frost came. The frost went. The garden yield on the third terrace was — a number, recorded in the compressed notation that the gardeners use, which is closer to tally marks than to the riverplain numerals. The savar in the northeastern pool grew by — a finger-width, two finger-widths. The deer herd on the forest floor was six, or eight, or ten. A birth: recorded by the name and the parent's name and the date, which is itself a number in a sequence that has not broken in three hundred years. A death: recorded the same way, with the additional notation of the cause, which is usually *cold* or *old* or simply *stopped*, which is the word they use for a death that has no specific cause, a death that is simply the end of a thing that was going and then was not going.

I write small. The sheets are not large — the size of two hands laid side by side — and the ink is made from — I do not actually know what the ink is made from. I know it is dark. I know it does not fade on savar, though it fades on other materials. I know it is made by a man on the second tier of the southern wall, and I know that he trades it for wooden bowls, and I know the woodworker, and so I have ink. This is how Estaur works. Not a market. Not a barter system in any formal sense. A web of known relationships, each one specific, each one maintained by the quiet acknowledgment that the thing one person makes is needed by another person, and the needing is mutual, and the mutual needing is what the community is, and the community does not need to name itself because naming is speaking and speaking is quiet and quiet means you miss things, and the community would rather be missed than be named, because being named is being separated, and being separated is being wrong-shaped, and being wrong-shaped is the one thing Estaur cannot abide.

---

I live alone. This is not unusual. Many people in Estaur live alone — the rooms are small, the families are close, and there is a point where the proximity of others becomes a pressure rather than a comfort, and the people here are sensitive to pressure the way they are sensitive to sound. Too much is too much. The rooms are far enough apart, vertically and horizontally, that you can be alone without being isolated, and this is the balance they have found, and it is a good balance, and I live in it.

My room: a stone box, three meters by four, cut into the western wall. The back wall is raw rock — but it does not weep, not much, not here, this high, this close to the fissure. There is a faint dampness, a shine on the stone in the morning, but it dries by midday. The ceiling is low — I can touch it, and I am not tall. A shelf for sleeping. A shelf for writing. A stone cubby for the records, the ones from my tenure, going back nineteen years. Below my room, in the deeper cubbies, the older records, going back three centuries. The handwriting changes as you go back — the sheets from the early generations are written in a hand that is larger, less compressed, as though the people then had more space, or more breath, or were not yet fully shaped by the place. By the fifth generation, the writing is small. By the eighth, it is the size it is now. This is adaptation. This is a people becoming the right shape, and the right shape is small and quiet and close to the stone.

I have a shuttered window. It faces east, into the caldera. When the shutters are open, I see the far wall, the terraced gardens on the debris cone, the forest canopy, the glowing floor. When the shutters are closed, I see the faint glow of the mortar in the joins, a light that is not light exactly but the memory of light, the stone giving back what it took in. I sleep with the shutters open. I like to see the glow. It is the closest thing to company that does not require speech, and I am a person who does not require speech but who does require company, and the glow is company, and this is enough.

---

I go to the savar pools.

This is not part of my task. The tender — the woman in her thirties with the stillness and the pansar-colored eyes — does not need me. She has her work and I have mine and they do not intersect. But I go. Once every cycle, sometimes twice. I go down to the deep cracks at the caldera's edge, where the forest is thickest, where the groundwater rises clearest, and I stand by the pool where the savar grows, and I stand there.

I do not know why. This is the honest answer. I could tell you that I am studying it, or that I am paying respects, or that I am keeping a record of its growth for the archives, but none of these would be true. I go because the silence there is different, and I am a person who has learned to distinguish silences, and this silence — the silence of the savar, the silence that is presence rather than absence — is the one I return to.

When I first came to Estaur, I could not distinguish silences. Silence was silence — the absence of sound, the space between sounds, the thing that sound fills. I did not know, nineteen years ago, that silence has texture, that silence has temperature, that silence has *direction*, that it can come from below or from above or from inside, that it can be hollow or full, that it can be the silence of a thing that is not there or the silence of a thing that is there and is not speaking. I did not know any of this. I was from outside, where sound is continuous and silence is the exception, and the exception is experienced as loss — a dropping-out, a gap, something missing. Here, silence is the medium. Sound is the exception. Sound is the thing that drops into the silence and is held by it and then is gone, and the silence remains, and the silence is not loss. The silence is the thing that is always there, and the sounds are the things that visit, and the visiting is fine, and the going is fine, and the silence does not miss them.

The savar's silence is the deepest silence in Estaur. It is deeper than the night silence, which is the silence of sleeping people and frost-forming and cricket-patience. It is deeper than the forest silence, which is the silence of growing things and dripping water and deer-breath. It is the silence of something that is composing itself, and the composition is not for anyone, and the awareness that regards you when you stand by the pool is not awareness of you, not specifically — it is awareness of everything, and you are in the everything, and this is different from being watched and it is different from being seen and it is different from being known. It is being *included* in a field of attention that does not discriminate, that does not prioritize, that does not distinguish between you and the water and the stone and the light. You are included. You are part of the composition. You are a note in something that is being said very slowly, and the saying does not require your participation, and your participation does not interrupt the saying, and this is — I do not have a word for what this is. I have stood by the pool nineteen years and I do not have a word.

The woman with the pansar-colored eyes has a word. Or she has a gesture — the flat hand, the fingers spread, held near the chest: *I am listening to something I cannot name.* I have seen her make this gesture at the pool. I have made it myself. It is the closest thing to a shared religious experience that Estaur has, and Estaur does not have shared religious experiences, and so it is not that, and it is not not that, and the distinction does not matter.

---

I think about the records sometimes. Not the content — the content does not change, or changes so slowly that change is not the right word, the content *varies* within parameters so narrow that the variation is more like breathing than like history. I think about the act of recording. Why do I write down what the frost did? The frost did what the frost does. Why do I write down the garden yield? The garden yielded what the garden yields. Why do I write down the births and the deaths? The births and the deaths come and go in the numbers they have always come and gone in, and the numbers are steady, and the steadiness is the point, and recording steadiness is — I think about this.

I think: the recording is not for the content. The recording is for the *attention*. Writing down what the frost did is a way of paying attention to what the frost did. Paying attention to what the frost did is a way of acknowledging the frost, and acknowledging, in Estaur, is the closest thing to prayer, and prayer, in Estaur, is not asking or thanking or pleading but *noticing*. I notice the frost. I notice the yield. I notice the birth, the death. I notice the savar's growth, two finger-widths this cycle, one and a half the last. I notice, and I write, and the writing is the noticing made permanent, or as permanent as anything is in a place where the moisture is constant and the stone weeps and the wood grows soft and the only direction is down.

The records are not for the future. The future residents of Estaur will not read them — or they might, occasionally, the way I sometimes pull a sheet from three centuries ago and look at the handwriting and the numbers and feel the steadiness of it, the deep patience of a place that has been the same for a long time. But this is not their purpose. Their purpose is the act. Their purpose is that somewhere, in a dry room high on the western wall, a person is sitting with a savar sheet and a pot of dark ink and is writing down what happened today, and what happened today is what happened yesterday, and the writing is the attention, and the attention is the acknowledgment, and the acknowledgment is the thing that keeps the place the place.

Or it doesn't. I don't know if the recording keeps the place stable. I don't know if the place would be stable without it. Probably it would. The caldera does not need my records. The pansar does not need my records. The savar certainly does not need my records — the savar is composing something far more patient than anything I could write, and it does not care whether I write or not. But I care. I care because caring is what I am shaped for, and I am shaped for it because the place shaped me, and the place shaped me because I stayed, and I stayed because — 

Because the silence was different here. Because the light came from below. Because the people spoke quietly and the birds walked between their feet and the stone remembered the day and gave it to the night and the giving back was not a transaction but a *gift*, and the gift was not for anyone, and I wanted to be in a place where the gifts were not for anyone, where the things that were given were given because giving is what the thing does, and not because the thing wanted something in return.

I am the record-keeper. I am the one who came from outside and stayed. I am the one who lives highest and descends to the deepest pools. I am the one who writes down what does not change. I am narrow, deep-chested, my hands are large, my voice is low. I have a gesture for the thing I cannot name. I make it at the savar pool. I make it at the well. I make it sometimes at night, alone in my dry room, when the mortar glows and the frost is forming and the crickets are being patient and the world is being the world, and I am in it, and this is enough, and enough is not a diminishing. Enough is the shape of the bowl. Enough is what the bowl holds. Enough is the light that comes from below and goes nowhere, and I go nowhere, and the going nowhere is the staying, and the staying is the thing, and the thing is what I am, and what I am is here.
