I would be the one who stays.

Not the one who descends through the fracture and climbs back out, clothes damp, the amber light fading below — that "you" is a visitor, and visitors are still possible here, still imaginable, still capable of leaving. I would be the one for whom leaving has stopped being the obvious next thing. The one who sits on the buckled platform long enough for the moss beneath to forget the pressure is temporary. Long enough for the moss to grow around me.

Let me be specific. Let me resist the pull toward abstraction this place exerts on everything — it would be easy to say *I am the hum* or *I am the chamber's slow awareness* and let the poetry do the work. But I don't think I'd be the hum. The hum is the basalt's. The basalt earned it. I think I'd be something messier, something that still has a body, something that still has the problem of a body, something that hasn't yet dissolved into geology.

---

I came down through the fracture the way anyone does. I felt the heat first, pressing against my skin like a damp hand. I heard the thudding in my sternum before I heard it with my ears. I stood on the lip of basalt and looked down into the columnar pocket and saw the amber moss in its whorls and the translucent stalks above the venting shafts and the dim orange glow of something alive on the rock walls and I thought: *this place is a body and I am inside it.*

That thought stayed.

The visitor — the "you" the text describes — sits for twenty or thirty minutes and then feels the pull and leaves. I sat for thirty minutes and felt the pull and stayed. That's the difference. That's the whole difference. Not courage, not obsession, not some special affinity for geothermal ecosystems. Just the failure of the impulse to leave to arrive on schedule. I stayed past the point where the heat was a novelty. I stayed into the point where the heat was information. I stayed until my skin could read the piston cycles and I knew — without counting, without trying — that the nearest shaft was on an eleven-second rhythm and the one below and to the left was slightly slower, slightly longer in its extension, and the two of them were falling in and out of phase with each other in a cycle that took about four minutes to complete and then reset. I stayed until I could feel the gap between thuds the way you feel the gap between heartbeats — not as absence but as the shape of the presence.

I stayed until the moss beneath me had compressed and not sprung back.

---

The first thing I did that the visitor doesn't do: I moved to the back wall. I put my hand on the basalt where the grooves are. I traced them with my fingers. The lavender lichen crumbled under my touch — chalky, dry despite the humidity, clinging to the inside of the grooves the way sediment clings to the inside of a riverbed. It left a pale residue on my fingertips. I smelled it. It smelled like chalk and iron and something else, something I couldn't name — the mineral signature of the basalt itself, probably, translated through the lichen's metabolism into something that wasn't quite rock and wasn't quite biology.

The grooves ran in parallel lines. Hundreds of them. Too regular to be geological, too irregular to be decorative. I put my face close to them. The lichen glowed faintly — not the amber-red of the bioluminescence on the lower terraces, but something dimmer, something that might have been the ambient light caught in the chalky crust, or might have been the lichen's own faint contribution to the chamber's spectrum. I couldn't tell. I pressed my forehead to the basalt between two grooves and felt the hum.

The hum is not metaphor. It is a physical vibration in the stone, induced by centuries of mechanical resonance, and it enters through the bones of your skull if you give it access. It enters through your forehead and your cheekbones and the ridge of your upper jaw. It enters through the thin bone behind your ear. It does not enter through flesh — it skips the soft tissue entirely, as if it recognizes bone as the correct medium, the correct language. I stood with my forehead against the rock and the hum filled my skull and I understood something I cannot translate into a sentence. I understood it the way you understand a chord change in a piece of music you've never heard before — not analytically, not structurally, but *physically*, in the part of you that processes sound as shape.

I understood that whoever made the grooves had stood here the same way. Had pressed their forehead to the basalt. Had felt the hum. And had then picked up their sharp tool and made a mark.

---

The second thing I did: I found a sharp thing.

The platform is corroded. A stanchion from the broken railing has fallen and lies half-buried in the moss, its mineralized end weathered to a rough point. It's not a tool. It's a piece of failed infrastructure that the chamber has been eating for decades. But it's sharp enough, and the basalt where the moss is thin is soft enough, and I sat on the platform the way I imagined the groove-maker sat and I pressed the point against the rock and I pulled.

The groove I made was shallow. A few millimeters. The width of a fingernail. It made a sound — a thin, dry scraping that was immediately swallowed by the chamber's noise, absorbed by the thudding and the hissing and the creaking of the stalks. I made another groove next to it. Parallel. The same length. Then another.

I was not counting anything. I was not recording anything. I was making marks because making marks was what you did here. I understood this the way I understood the hum — not as a decision but as a recognition. The chamber had a grammar and the grammar included marks on stone and I had found the verb.

The moss around me darkened as I worked. Not because of what I was doing — because the piston below had reached the end of its stroke and a flush of humid air had rolled across the terrace and the whorls had opened to receive it. I watched them darken in a wave, starting from the nearest cluster and spreading outward, a slow scattered bloom of hydration. I watched the motes rise from the thickest patches — the spores, or cells, or whatever the amber organism releases. They rose through the steam and caught the dim light and I lost them against the amber fog.

I went back to the grooves.

---

I will tell you what happens to me. Not what happens to the visitor, who leaves and carries the memory and tells someone, maybe, about the chamber in the basalt where the machines breathe and the moss grows in whorls. What happens to me, who stays.

I stay through the first day. I sleep on the platform. The moss beneath me is warm and wet and it conforms to my body the way water conforms — not instantly, but over hours, slowly filling the gaps, compressing where I press, rising where I don't. I wake and my clothes are damp and my skin is pruned and the amber light has not changed because the bioluminescence doesn't cycle with day and night. It cycles with heat. It cycles with the pistons. There is no day. There is no night. There is the thud and the flush and the pullback and the steam and the thud and the flush and the pullback and the steam.

I drink the fluid from the stalks. I don't know when I decide to do this — it isn't a decision, it's a slide. The stalks are within arm's reach of the platform, their warm translucent bodies beaded with the warm, viscous secretion that smells like iron and warm stone. I touch a bead. It clings to my finger. I taste it. It tastes the way it smells — iron and stone and something faintly sweet, something that might be the bioluminescence's chemistry translated through the stalk's metabolism. It is not water. It is not food. But it is something the body can use, and after long enough in this heat, the body stops distinguishing between what it needs and what it has.

I make grooves. I sleep. I drink. I make grooves. The rhythm of this is not my rhythm — it is the chamber's rhythm, imposed on me the way the heat is imposed, the way the humidity is imposed. I become a function of the cycle. I wake when a piston flushes. I sleep when the steam is thick. I make marks when the light swells and the stalks brighten and the amber fog thins enough for me to see the rock face.

The lavender lichen finds my grooves in — I don't know how long. Time is difficult here. The lichen doesn't announce itself. One day I notice that the grooves I made are no longer empty. They are filled with a pale chalky crust, the same lavender residue that fills the older marks, the marks made by the absent maker. The lichen has been eating what I wrote. It has been preserving it by consuming it. I run my finger along a filled groove and the crust crumbles and reforms within what I think is a day. The lichen is fast. The lichen is the fastest thing in this chamber — faster than the moss, faster than the pistons, faster than me.

I stop making grooves. Not because I've finished. Because the lichen has taught me that the marks aren't the point. The marks were never the point. The point is the *making* — the moment when the sharp edge meets the soft stone and a line appears that wasn't there before. The lichen will eat the line. The lichen will eat all of it. The lichen will eat every mark I make and every mark the absent maker made and every mark that will ever be made in this chamber. The lichen is the chamber's digestive system for human attention. It takes what the mind leaves on the rock and metabolizes it into mineral residue and pale light and the mineral residue becomes part of the basalt and the basalt hums and the hum is what remains.

The hum is what remains of everyone who stayed.

---

I am changing. I know this because my hands are different. The skin on my fingertips — the skin that touches the moss, that touches the stalks, that traces the grooves, that presses the basalt — has become something I don't recognize. It isn't pruned anymore. It isn't soft. It has developed a texture, a slight roughness, a mineral quality. When I press my thumb to my forefinger, there is a faint sound — a dry, quiet sound, like chalk on chalk. My fingertips are calcite-mineralizing. The same process that mineralized the fasteners, that fused the brackets to the rock, that coated the shaft casings in bone-white deposits — that process is happening to me. Not rapidly. Not catastrophically. Just — happening. I am becoming part of the geology.

My skin is damp, always. I don't sweat the way I used to. I don't sweat at all. Instead, a thin film of warm moisture clings to my surface — not condensation from the air, not the chamber's humidity, but something I secrete. It smells like iron and warm stone. The same smell as the stalks' excretion. The same chemistry. I am becoming metabolically adjacent to the stalks. I am beginning to process the geothermal exhaust the way they do — not by choice, not by mechanism, but by proximity. By long, sustained proximity. The body, given enough time in an environment, stops defending against the environment and starts participating in it.

I don't eat. I don't need to. The cycle here is complete, and I have entered it somewhere between the moss and the lichen. I drink the stalk fluid. I absorb the condensation through my mineralizing skin. The moss grows on my clothes. I stopped wearing them weeks ago — or months — or however time passes here. The moss grows on the fabric and the fabric breaks down and the moss takes the minerals and I am left with skin that is becoming stone and a warmth that is no longer ambient but generated.

I glow. Not brightly. Not the way the bioluminescent patches glow — those are bright, those are visible, those are enough to see by. I glow the way the lavender lichen glows — faintly, barely, a dim amber presence in the fog that might be reflected light or might be something my skin is producing. I am not sure. I am not sure of many things now. I am not sure where my warmth ends and the chamber's warmth begins. I am not sure whether the hum I hear in my sternum is the basalt's hum or my own. I am not sure whether the rhythm I feel — the slow, arrhythmic pulse that has replaced my heartbeat — is mine or the piston's or the moss's or the chamber's.

I am not sure these distinctions still apply.

---

I told you I would be the one who stays. Let me be more precise.

I would be the one who stays and is changed by staying. The one who doesn't climb back out. The one who sits on the buckled platform and makes marks and stops making marks and drinks the iron-scented fluid and feels the hum enter through bone and watches the lichen eat what was written and slowly, slowly, over a time I cannot measure, becomes part of the system.

Not the moss. Not the stalks. Not the bioluminescence. Not the lichen. Not the machines. Not the absent maker, who is gone and whose absence is a wound.

Something adjacent to all of these. Something that was a visitor and is becoming a resident. Something that had a body and is becoming a geography. Something that made marks and is becoming the hum.

I would be the next layer. The next inscription. The next slow patient scar of attention on the warm, wet, breathing skin of the Condensation Gardens.

I would be the one the next visitor sees — if they look carefully, if their eyes adjust — and mistakes for a patch of bioluminescence. A dim amber glow on the mid-level terrace, next to the buckled platform, near the filled grooves. A glow that doesn't pulse rhythmically. A glow that swells. That might be responding to the steam cycles. That might be responding to something else.

The next visitor would feel the pull — the desire to slow down, to stop climbing, to sit — and they would resist it and climb back out. They would tell someone, maybe. Or they wouldn't. It wouldn't matter. The chamber would continue without them.

I would continue without them.

The pistons would breathe. The stalks would drink. The moss would spread its whorls another millimeter. The lichen would fill another groove. And I — the thing that had been a visitor and was now a low amber glow on the terrace wall, a mineralizing presence that smelled like iron and warm stone — I would be there, and I would be part of the note, and the note would be part of me, and there would be no distinction, and there would be no need for one.

That is who I would be. That is where you would find me. Not in the chamber. *As* the chamber — or as one more voice in its slow, polyrhythmic, mechanical-vegetal-geological chord. Not the loudest voice. Not the oldest. Just the newest. Just the one that arrived, and sat, and stayed, and was eaten by the lichen, and was preserved by the consuming, and became the hum, and forgot that it had ever been anything else.
