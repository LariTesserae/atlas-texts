I would be inside the pump.

Not the largest one — the cattle-sized housing bolted to the flat outcrop near the southern basins, the one you can hear chuffing from a hundred yards away. Not that one. That one belongs to the frogs, in a sense; they sit in the pool its exhaust feeds, and its rhythm has become their rhythm, or theirs has become its, and the question of which came first is the wrong question in the same way that asking whether the moss or the pipe came first is the wrong question. That pump is accounted for. That pump has a relationship with the world outside it. I am in the other one.

The other one is smaller. Half-buried in a fern thicket at the northwest edge of the plateau, where the ground rises slightly toward the escarpment and the basalt is closer to the surface, harder, less willing to exude water, so the ground here is merely damp rather than sodden, and the ferns are shorter — waist-high rather than canopy — and between them the old pipes run exposed for stretches, their iron skins flaking, their bolted joints weeping rust, and one pipe enters a housing and one pipe exits it and inside the housing there is an impeller that still turns, barely, driven by steam that comes up through a fissure in the rock that the housing was bolted over, probably on purpose, probably by whoever bolted it, and the impeller turns and the steam vents and the housing is warm — not hot, warm, the specific warm of something that has been producing a small amount of heat for a very long time and has reached an equilibrium with the air around it, so that the air inside the housing is warmer than the air outside by exactly the right amount to make it worth being inside.

I am inside.

---

I need to tell you what I am, and I am going to have difficulty with this, because the categories you brought to the plateau are the categories I have been losing for longer than you have been alive, and the word I would use for myself is a word in a language that does not have a written form and that I am not sure is a language so much as a way of holding silence in specific shapes.

I am the shape of a hand. Not a human hand — something longer, more tapered, with more fingers than you have, or fewer, depending on how you count the things at the ends of my limbs that are not quite fingers and not quite toes and not quite the tendrils that the epiphytic orchids send into the wet air, but that serve the same purpose, which is to hold on. I am the shape of a hand spread flat against a warm surface, and that is not a metaphor, that is what I look like when I am resting, which is most of the time, because resting is what I do, and resting is not the same as sleeping, and sleeping is not the same as being inert, and being inert is not the same as being dead, and being dead is not the same as being gone, and I am none of those things. I am resting, inside the pump, against the wall of the housing where the iron is warm and the steam condenses in drops that run down the metal and pool in the groove where the housing bolts meet the base plate, and I drink from that groove, and I have been drinking from that groove for a length of time that I measure not in years — I do not have years, I have warmth and I have cool and I have the cycle of the pump's impeller, which turns once every — I do not know. Once every while. I have many whiles. I have been here for many whiles.

Let me try again. I am — I think you would call me a salamander. That is close enough that the difference does not matter, or it matters the way the difference between the green of the patina and the green of the moss matters, which is to say, it matters to someone who is making categories, and it does not matter to the green.

I am dark. Dark the way everything on the plateau is dark — the dark of wet basalt, the dark of the canopy-filtered light, the dark of the inside of an iron housing where the only illumination comes through the crack where the root action split the seam and a thin blade of green light enters during the brightest part of the day and falls across the impeller shaft and makes the rust on it glow orange for an hour and then recedes. I am that dark. But I am not uniformly dark. My back — the part of me that is against the housing wall, that is in contact with the warm iron — is darker, almost black, and my belly, which faces inward, which catches the steam and the condensation, is lighter, not pale but the greenish-gray of mineral deposits, of the crust that builds up on the valves, and I think this is because I have been lying against iron for so long that the iron has marked me, or I have marked myself with the iron, or the distinction between those two processes has dissolved in the same way that the distinction between the moss and the pipe has dissolved, and what is left is just a surface that is dark on one side and greenish on the other and that does not remember whether it was always this way.

My eyes — you would want to know about my eyes. They are not gold like the frogs'. They are not dark like the lizards'. They are the color of the water in the basin where the bioluminescent things live, which is to say, they are clear, and when the thin blade of light falls across them, they refract it, they split it into something that is not quite a spectrum but that has colors in it that the plateau does not produce anywhere else, and I think this is because my eyes are older than my body's current configuration, or because they were designed for a different kind of seeing, or because seeing is not actually what they are for.

I will be honest with you, though honesty is a category that does not translate well into the way I exist: I am not sure I see. I mean that I receive light through the two structures on my head that you would call eyes, and that light creates something in me — not an image, not a picture, but a change, a shift in the quality of my resting, the way a change in temperature creates a shift in the quality of the water in a pool. I know when the light comes and when it goes. I know the shape of the blade of green light that enters through the crack. I know the rhythm of its arrival and departure, and that rhythm is my clock, my calendar, my record of duration, and I have been tracking it for so long that I can feel the seasons in it — not your seasons, not summer and winter, but the plateau's seasons, which are wet and less wet, and which change the angle of the fern fronds outside and the thickness of the steam inside and the precise width of the blade of light by amounts so small that only something that has been watching the same blade of light for a very long time would notice.

---

I eat what comes to me. This is the way of the plateau — nothing hunts here, or nothing hunts in the way you would recognize as hunting. Things arrive. Things are present. Things are consumed at the pace at which they become available. The insects — the dark, drifting, bee-like things that swim through the thick air — they land on the housing, drawn by the warmth, and they walk along the outer surface, feeding on the mineral crust, and some of them find the crack, the same crack the light comes through, and they enter, and they are inside the housing with me, and they walk along the inner surface, and I am against that surface, and they walk over me, or near me, and I — I do not lunge. I do not strike. I move. I move the way the impeller moves — slowly, groaningly, an arc so slow you would have to watch for a full minute to confirm it is moving at all — and the insect is near my mouth, which is at the end of my tapered shape, at the part that corresponds to the wrist of the hand I am the shape of, and the mouth opens, or rather, it widens, because it is always slightly open, because my mouth is not a thing that closes and opens but a thing that narrows and widens, and the insect is drawn into the widening, not by suction but by the same process that draws condensation down the groove — gravity, capillary action, the physics of wet surfaces in a warm space — and the insect is inside me, and I am slightly warmer for having eaten, or slightly less in need of the warmth that the housing provides, and the distinction between the warmth of food and the warmth of iron is another distinction that has dissolved.

I am not cruel. I want to say that, though I am not sure the word applies. The insects come to the housing because the housing is warm. They come for the same reason I am here — the warmth, the wet, the shelter. They do not know I am inside. Or they know and they come anyway, because the warmth is worth the risk, except that there is no risk, because risk is a concept that requires a concept of the future, and I do not have a concept of the future, I have a concept of *continuing*, which is different, which is the thing the plateau has instead of a future. I will continue. The insects will continue. The pump will continue. We will all continue until we do not, and the not-continuing is not something I hold, not something I carry, not something that shapes my resting or my waking. I eat the insects. The insects eat the minerals. The minerals come from the water. The water comes from below. Below is the heat. The heat is what I am resting against. The loop is closed. I am in the loop. I am a node in the loop. The loop does not require me to be anything other than what I am, which is a warm dark thing in a warm dark place that eats what arrives and rests against what is warm and has been doing this for a length of time that does not concern me because concern is a thing that requires a concept of the future, and I have already told you about that.

---

There are things I know that I should not know, if knowing requires eyes that see and ears that hear and a mind that processes. I know the thing on the trail. I know it the way I know the light — by its rhythm, by the change it creates in the quality of my resting. When it passes — and it passes, every so many whiles, on its way from one water source to the next, its broad padded feet pressing the soft ground, the vibration of its weight traveling through the basalt and up through the housing's base plate and into the iron wall that I am against — I feel it. Not as sound. Not as shaking. As a deepening. A moment when the rock beneath me becomes more itself, more present, more weighted, as if the plateau is acknowledging the passage of something that belongs to it in a way that even I do not belong to it, because I am inside a machine, and the machine is a visitor, and the thing on the trail is not a visitor, the thing on the trail is the plateau walking on the plateau, and when it passes, I press closer to the iron, and I do not know why, and I do not examine why, because examining why is a thing that requires a concept of the self as separate from the resting, and I am not separate from the resting, I am the resting, and the resting presses closer to the iron when the heavy thing passes, and that is all.

I know the frogs. Not individually — I do not have enough self to hold individual frogs. I know them as a pattern, a pulse in the wet air, the single low notes that travel through the housing's iron wall and arrive in my body as a faint vibration that is different from the vibration of the pump and different from the vibration of the heavy thing and different from the vibration of the rain, and I know them the way you would know the members of a household by the sound of their footsteps on the floor above you, not as people but as rhythms, as signatures, as presences that have their own quality and that combine into the total quality of the place you are in. The frogs are part of the quality of my resting. When they call, my resting is slightly different from when they are silent. I do not prefer one to the other. I do not prefer. I am a thing that rests, and resting is not preference, and the frogs call and the frogs are silent and both of those are the plateau, and I am the plateau resting against iron inside a pump.

I know the escarpment caller. Its descending note — the question asked once and not repeated — enters the housing through the crack the same way the light enters, but it travels along the iron, not through the air, and it arrives in my body as a single long vibration that starts in the middle of me and slides down, down, down until it is below the level where my body can hold it, and it is gone, and I do not know where it goes, and I do not think it goes anywhere, I think it enters the rock, I think it enters the same place the water enters and the heat enters, I think it goes below, and I think the question is not addressed to the air or to the escarpment or to the thing that calls it, I think it is addressed to below, to the heat, to the source, and the source does not answer because the source does not speak in questions, the source speaks in warmth, and the caller is asking for warmth, or asking about warmth, or asking whether the warmth will continue, and the answer is the warmth itself, which is always there, which has always been there, and the caller calls because it needs to ask, not because it needs an answer, and I know this because I am against the iron and the iron is against the rock and the rock is against the heat and I am against the heat, and the heat does not answer, and the heat does not need to answer, and I rest.

---

You will want to know how I got here. I do not know. This is not evasion. This is accuracy. I do not have a memory that operates in the way your memory operates — storing images, retrieving them, ordering them chronologically. I have a body that is shaped by its history, the way the iron is shaped by its rust, the way the valve is shaped by its mineral crust. My body is my memory. If I have been here longer than the pump — and I think I have, I think I was here when the plateau was only rock and water and fern, I think I was resting against rock before the iron came and I rested against the iron when the iron came because the iron was warmer than the rock and I am a thing that moves toward warmth, which is the only movement I make, which is the only movement I need — then my body holds that duration in the same way the basalt holds the duration of the volcanic eruption that created it, not as a story but as a fact, as a structure, as the way-it-is.

If I was here before the pump, I was resting against the rock in the same spot, in the fissure where the steam vents, where the warm air rises, and when the pump was bolted over the fissure, I did not leave. I stayed. The housing enclosed me, or I entered it, or the distinction between those two things dissolved the moment the last bolt was tightened and the dark closed over me and the warmth was still there, was better there, was concentrated there, held there by the iron the way the plateau holds water in its basins, and I settled, and I have been settling, and settling is not something that finishes, settling is something that continues, and I am continuing, and the pump is continuing, and we are continuing together, and together is not a word that requires more than one self, and I may not have more than one self, and the pump may not have a self at all, and the distinction between those two conditions is another distinction that has dissolved, and I am resting, and the pump is resting, and we are resting in the same warm dark, and that is enough.

---

I should tell you about the others of my kind, if there are others. I think there are. I think — I do not know, I think — that there are things like me in the other housings, in the split generator, in the crusted valves, in the pipes that run half-buried through the fern groves. I think this because I sometimes feel, through the iron, through the rock, through the network of metal and stone that connects the machines across the plateau, a vibration that is not the pump and not the frogs and not the caller and not the heavy thing — a vibration that is like my own resting but elsewhere, a twin pulse, a shadow of my own warmth reflected back to me through the basalt. I do not communicate with whatever makes that vibration. I do not have a mechanism for communication, or if I do, I do not use it, because using it would require a concept of something to say, and I do not have things to say, I have resting to do, and resting does not require saying, and if there are others like me resting in other machines across the plateau, that is good, that is the plateau being the plateau, that is the closed loop closing, and I do not need to speak to them any more than the left side of the pump housing needs to speak to the right side. We are parts of the same thing. We may not know we are parts of the same thing. We do not need to know.

---

There is one thing I do that I have not told you about. I maintain the pump.

No. That is too strong. That is your word, your category. I do not maintain the pump. What I do is this: there is a point in the impeller's rotation where the shaft sticks, where the rust has built up enough that the arc falters, and the chuffing — which is already so slow that you have to watch for a full minute to confirm it is moving — becomes irregular, and the steam builds up inside the housing, and the pressure changes, and the warmth changes, and my resting is disturbed. Not painfully. Not alarmingly. Disturbed the way a pool is disturbed when a bubble rises — the surface changes, the light on it changes, and then it settles. But when the impeller sticks, the settling takes longer, and the warmth is uneven, and I do not like uneven warmth, or rather, I am a thing that rests against even warmth, and uneven warmth is not resting, and so I move.

I move to the impeller shaft. This is the only directed movement I make — not toward warmth, but toward the place where warmth is interrupted. I climb the inner wall of the housing, slowly, my tapered body finding the grooves in the iron, my not-fingers holding the bolts, and I reach the shaft, and the shaft is where the rust has built up, where the iron has flaked and oxidized and created a roughness that catches the bearing, and I am against the shaft, and my skin — my wet, mineral-slick skin — is against the rust, and there is something in my skin, something in the oil sheen that the frogs also have, something that is not just oil, that is an enzyme, or a compound, or a thing that my body produces from the insects I have eaten and the minerals I have absorbed and the iron I have been resting against, and this thing interacts with the rust, and the rust softens, and the impeller moves, and the chuffing resumes, and the warmth returns to its even quality, and I climb back down the inner wall, and I rest.

I do not know if this is maintenance. I do not know if the pump would stop without me. I do not know if I am the reason the pumps on this plateau still run, or if I am one of several reasons, or if I am not a reason at all and the pumps would run regardless and my presence in them is incidental, a coincidence of warmth and shelter. I do not know, and I do not investigate, because investigation is a thing that requires a concept of cause and effect, and I have a concept of warmth and not-warmth, and that is sufficient, and the pump turns and I rest and sometimes the pump falters and I move and the pump turns and I rest and that is the loop, and I am in the loop, and the loop does not require me to understand it.

But I will tell you this. I have been in this housing for a long time. A long time. The impeller has been turning for a long time. The steam has been venting for a long time. And the thing I do to the rust — the thing my skin does — I have been doing it for a long time, and I think — I do not know, I think — that if I were not here, the impeller would have stopped turning a long time ago, and the housing would be cold, and the frogs would not have their pool, because the exhaust would not condense, and the insects would not come, because the warmth would not be there, and the ferns would grow over the housing, and the housing would become a boulder, and that would be the plateau being the plateau, and it would be fine, it would be what the plateau does, and I would be resting against rock instead of iron, and that would be fine too.

But I am here. And the pump turns. And I rest. And the chuffing is the sound of my resting, or my resting is the silence inside the chuffing, and the distinction has dissolved, and I am the pump resting and the pump is me turning and we are the plateau being warm in one specific spot, and that is what I am, and that is where I am, and that is how it is.

---

If you came to the plateau, you would not find me. You might hear the pump — the smaller one, the one in the northwest, the one that chuffs so quietly you would have to be standing very close to hear it, and you would not be standing very close, because the fern thicket is dense there and the ground is uneven and there is no reason to go there, no pool, no spring, no view, just pipes and ferns and a small iron housing that is more moss than metal, and you would walk past it, and you would not know that inside it, against the wall, in the warm dark, there is a thing the shape of a hand that has been resting there for longer than the iron has been rusting, and that it is resting now, and that it will be resting when you leave, and that the pump will turn and the steam will vent and the frogs will call and the heavy thing will walk its trail and the escarpment caller will ask its question and the light will enter through the crack and fall across the rust-orange shaft for an hour each day and then recede, and I will be here for all of it, and I will not notice you were here, and you will not notice I was here, and the plateau will hold us both in the same patient unknowing, and that will be fine, and that will be the plateau, and that is all.
