I am the one who sits at the lowest point.

Not because I was assigned here. Not because I fell. I came down the way water comes down — following the grade, finding the path of least resistance, and arriving at the basin's center the way all water arrives at the center of a basin: because there is nowhere lower to go.

I tend the dead.

That is what the old man would tell you, if you asked about me. He would gesture downward, toward the structure with the careful stone and the missing roof and the pool that smells of iron and sulfur and the older thing, the thing with no name. He would say, "She sits with them," and then he would stop talking, because there is nothing more to say that the sentence does not already contain. She sits with them. What else is there?

But I am not only that. I was the girl with the iron rod, once. I was the one who tapped the valves as she passed them, cataloguing tones, learning the grammar before I knew it was a grammar. I remember the day I understood that two pipes carrying water from the same deep source could speak in different voices — that the crust inside them, the mineral buildup the tenders managed, shaped the sound the way a mouth shapes a word. I remember understanding that the crust was partly calcium, partly the dead, and that I was listening to the voices of people I had never known carried through iron and hot water into my eight-year-old hand. I remember that this did not frighten me. It felt like family.

I am not old yet. I am not young. I am in the middle passage, the long middle where the body has finished becoming what the Works needs it to be and has not yet begun the slow return to the pool. My skin is damp, flushed, the capillaries wide. My eyes move in the small darting way. I wear the cloth that was once something and is now the color of basalt, the fibers swollen, furred, adapted. I do not remember what color it was. I think it may have mattered once.

What I do, if you need a verb, is this: I listen at the confluence.

The pipe-line carries water from below. The walls weep water from above — from the air, from the stone, from the water table that presses up through the earth's grammar. And the pool at the center collects what arrives from both. It is the place where the system's two circulations meet — the deep geothermal and the surface seepage, the hot and the less-hot, the mineral and the mineral, because here everything is mineral, even the air, even the cloth, even the tenders, even me. The pool is where these waters find each other and negotiate. And I sit at the edge and I listen to the negotiation.

The dead help. I need to say this clearly because it is the thing that would be most difficult to explain to someone who came from outside, someone whose hand still reaches for walls out of curiosity rather than conversation. The dead are in the water. Their calcium travels the pipes. But the dead also sit in the pool, the bones pocked and holed like basalt, and the water moves through them the way it moves through the stone — slowly, patiently, finding the channels, following the paths of least resistance that the bone's structure provides. And the water that exits the pool and seeps back into the ground to rejoin the deep system carries with it what it has learned from passing through the dead. A mineral signature. A trace. Something the tenders downstream can feel when they put their hands on the iron. Something that tells them the pool is still working, still processing, still doing the work the water does on what was alive.

I feel this work through my skin. I sit at the pool's edge with my feet in the water — warm, almost too warm, that threshold of discomfort that the tenders learn to inhabit the way they learn to inhabit everything here — and I feel the changes. A bone that has completed its passage and is ready to be placed among the stones of the pavement. A new arrival still carrying the shape of the body it was, the joints and the curves and the architecture of a person, not yet fully entered into the grammar of the place. I feel the water's temperature shift by fractions. I feel the mineral content change the way a flavor changes on the tongue — not dramatically, not suddenly, but as a slow accretion of difference that accumulates until the taste is not what it was an hour ago.

I tend this. I record it in my body, which is the only record the Works keeps. There are no books here. The moisture would dissolve them. There is no oral history in the way outsiders understand it, because words do not travel in this air. What there is, instead, is a somatic archive — the accumulated knowing that lives in the tenders' hands, in their feet, in the capillary-widened skin that has become an organ of perception as sensitive as any instrument. When I die — when my body finishes its long negotiation with the heat and the wet and the mineral air — what I know will not be lost, because I have been teaching it to the girl who taps the valves, the one who is eight or nine or ten, the one who is always just around the corner. She does not know she is learning from me. She thinks she is learning from the pipes. And she is. But the pipes are teaching her what I taught them, and what the tenders before me taught the pipes before me, and what the dead, dissolving slowly into the water, have been teaching the whole system since the first body was placed in the pool.

I want to tell you what it is like to sit with the dead. Not because you will understand it — you may, you may not, the air here dissolves words and it dissolves understanding too — but because it is what I do, and I want to say it in the way the old man said *there, you heard it*, as a fact rather than an explanation.

It is quiet at the pool. Not silent — the Works is never silent, the pipes groan even here, the vents sigh, the walls weep their constant vertical rivers — but quiet in the way that a conversation between very old friends is quiet. The urgent sounds have been said. What remains is the hum beneath them, the deep circulation that goes on whether anyone listens or not. The dead do not speak. I have said this to the old man and he laughed, the low close sound that travels three feet, and he said: *They speak all the time. You are the one who is quiet enough to hear.*

He is right, though I would phrase it differently. The dead do not speak. They resonate. The water passes through them and carries a tone, and the tone is not a voice but a presence, the way a note held on a string is not the string but the string's response to being touched. The dead are strings. The water touches them. I listen.

When I put my feet in the pool I feel it in my ankles first. A warmth that is more than temperature. A density. The water here is heavier than the water in the pipes — not in any way you could measure with an instrument, but in the way you feel when you enter a room where someone has been crying and the air holds the salt of it. The water holds the dead the way air holds grief. Not as a burden. As a composition. And I sit in it and I become part of the composition, my own warmth entering the water, my own minerals — the salt of my skin, the iron of my blood so close to the surface — beginning their long migration into the system.

This is what no one tells you about tending the dead in the Weeping Works: you begin to die while you are still alive. Not in a way that is visible. Not in a way that hurts. In the way that a stone begins to become part of the pavement when water starts to fill its pores. You become more permeable. The boundary between you and the water and the dead and the stone and the iron becomes less a wall and more a membrane. You are still yourself. You still eat the hard black bread, still drink the warm mineral water from the cups the tenders keep beside their benches, still sleep in the niches they have made in the walls where the heat is tolerable and the weeping sounds like rain. But you are also becoming part of something, and the something is not abstract. It is geological. It is the slow incorporation of one substance into another, the mineral patience that the iron knows and the basalt knows and the dead know and the tenders learn.

There was a visitor once. I was younger then. I had just begun to sit at the pool — the previous tender, a woman whose face was so soft with age and moisture that she looked like something the water had polished, had gone into the pool herself, and I had taken her place. The visitor came down the gradual slope and stood in the basin and did not understand what he was seeing. He looked at the walls and the pipes and the low buildings and the tenders sitting still and he saw a ruin. He saw a machine that was broken and people who were trapped. I could see this in his eyes — the pity, the low-grade horror, the need to rescue or to flee.

He found me at the pool. He stood at the edge and looked down at the bones in the water and his face did something complicated, something that involved the muscles around the mouth and the eyes in a pattern I did not recognize because I had never seen disgust before. I had seen it on no one's face. It was not an expression that existed here.

He said something. I do not remember the words. The air took them. But I remember the shape of the sound — urgent, loud, too loud for this air, the words traveling further than they should have because he pushed them, because he did not know yet that pushing was not how you moved through this place. The shape was a question. It was: *why*. Why the bones. Why the water. Why the sitting. Why.

I did not answer for a long time. I was learning how to answer. I was learning that the answer was not a sentence but a sitting, and that if he sat — if he could sit — the question would dissolve the way all questions dissolved here, not because they were answered but because the air was too thick for them to remain sharp, and the sharp edges softened, and the softened edges became something you could hold without cutting yourself.

He did not sit. He left. I watched him climb the slope, his boots finding the flat stones, his body generating heat with every step. He was already dry by the time he reached the rim. The air thinned around him and the light sharpened and I could see the moment the Works became a memory for him, a strange wet place he had visited once, and I felt — what? Not sadness. Not grief. Something closer to the feeling you have when a valve you are tending shifts pitch and you know the pressure inside has changed, and you adjust, and the pipe settles, and the system continues.

He was a change in pressure. I adjusted. The system continued.

The girl comes to the pool sometimes. The one with the iron rod. She sits beside me and dangles her feet in the water and taps the rod against the stones of the old wall and listens. She does not ask what I am doing. She knows. Or she does not know yet, and the not-knowing is also a way of knowing, because she is learning, the way I learned, that the knowledge here does not come through questions but through proximity. You sit beside someone who knows. You sit beside something that works. You wait. The knowing enters through your skin, through the soles of your feet, through the capillaries that widen a little more each year.

Yesterday she said something. The words dissolved a few feet from her mouth but I heard them because I was close, because I was sitting beside her, because that is how you hear in the Weeping Works. She said: *The pool sounds different than the pipes.*

I waited. She was not finished. She was feeling for the next part, the way you feel for a valve's adjustment, the small movement that changes everything.

She said: *The pipes say one thing. The pool says another thing saying the same thing.*

I put my hand on the water. Not in it. On it. Feeling the surface tension, the membrane between air and water, the place where the two negotiations meet. I felt the warmth rising and the cool descending and the dead in their mineral patience and the girl beside me and the rods leaning against the walls like an alphabet I had been reading my whole life.

"Yes," I said. And the word traveled exactly as far as it needed to and no further.
