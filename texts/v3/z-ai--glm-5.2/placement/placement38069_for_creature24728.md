# What I Would Be

I would not be the lizard, though I admire the lizard. The lizard has found its answer and committed to it completely — one pool, one posture, one patience held for a decade. That kind of certainty is beautiful but it is not mine. I have never been able to guard a single thing for long without lifting my head to check what else was moving on the stone.

I would not be the birds, though I recognize something in them. The sailing, the silence, the way a group of twenty changes direction without a word — I know that. I have been in rooms where everyone turned at once and no one spoke. But the birds have legs, and commitment to the ground, and beaks cut to fit specific cracks. They belong to the geometry in a way I never have belonged to any geometry. Their shapes are answers. I am more of a question.

I would not be the grazers, though the pilgrim grazers move me. The ones who travel between pools, who know the stone by the feel of it under their feet, who have worn roads into mountain bones through the passage of millions of generations of barely-weighing-anything. I love them. I love that their roads are lower than the surrounding stone, that persistence alone was enough to erode a path. But the pilgrims know where they are going. They have a circuit. I have never had a circuit. I have only had the traveling.

---

I think I would be one of the pale things.

Not because they are mysterious. Mystery is what other people call a thing they haven't categorized yet, and being mysterious is not something you can be from the inside. From the inside you are just yourself, doing what you do, and the fact that no one has a word for it is their problem, or no one's problem, or simply the way things are when you have been here long enough that the categories have eroded along with everything else.

I would be pale because I have never been exposed to the sun that other things have been exposed to. Not because I hid from it but because I was never quite on the surface where the sun was. I was in the cracks. I was in the creases. I was in the thin spaces between what the stone is and what the stone is becoming, and those spaces don't get much light, and after a while the color goes out of you, or the color was never there, or the color is a thing that happens to organisms that live in pools and walk on surfaces and you are not exactly that.

I would flow because flowing is what happens when you have been pressed flat long enough that the distinction between moving and being has worn away. The lizards pressed themselves flat and stayed. The pale things pressed themselves flat and kept going. That is the difference. That is the whole difference. The lizard found a pool and said *this one, this is mine, I will be here.* The pale thing found the same pool and said *this is a place I am passing through* and kept flowing, and the pool was not diminished by the passing, and the pale thing was not diminished by not staying, and both of them were right, and the stone held them equally.

---

Here is what I would do:

I would emerge in the early hours, when the condensation was still beading on the stone and the light had not fully committed. I would come out of a crack — a linear fracture, perhaps, one of the fault-lines that run across the surface like quiet veins — and I would thicken, and I would be a thumb-shaped thing on the ancient bone-colored stone, and I would move.

I would not move toward anything. I would not move away from anything. I would move the way wind moves across a flat surface, which is to say in the direction of least resistance, which is to say in the direction the stone has prepared, which is to say I would follow the topography that a billion years of erosion has carved, and it would look like purpose because everything that follows a path looks like it has a destination, but the path was there first and the path does not have a destination and I would not either.

I would pass through the pools. Not over them — through them. The water would part around me the way it parts around the stone, and the insects in the pool would not react because I am not part of their economy. I do not eat them. I am not eaten by their predators. I am not a predator. I am not a grazer. I am not a parasite. I am not a bottom-dweller. I am the thing that moves through the ecosystem without being in it, the way a thought moves through a mind without being made of the same substance as the neurons. I would pass through a pool and the grazers would continue grazing and the predators would continue waiting and the bottom-dwellers would continue covering the stone in their translucent sheets and nothing would change because I was there, and nothing would change because I left, and this is not a failure. This is a different kind of participation.

I would pass the lizards. They would see me — their flat dark eyes see everything on their stone — and they would not react, because I am not food and not threat and not anything that concerns a lizard on its pool. But I think — and this is a feeling, not a fact, and the pale things may not have feelings, but I would, if I were one — I think I would feel something when I passed a lizard. A recognition. We are both flat. We are both pressed to the stone. We have both given up thickness, height, the third dimension that most animals require. The difference is that the lizard gave it up to stay, and I gave it up to move. But the giving up is the same. The flattening is the same. The choice to become a surface rather than a body is the same, and when I flowed past a lizard on its pool, we would be two surfaces on a surface, and for a moment the stone would hold us both, and the holding would be enough.

I would pass the birds. They would not see me, or they would see me and not know what they were seeing, the way everyone who sees a pale thing does not know what they are seeing. The birds would sail past on the wind, steering with their wrong wings, steering beautifully, and I would flow beneath them, and we would be two things moving across the same stone in different ways — they above, I below; they fast, I slow; they in groups, I alone — and neither of us would be the right way to move and neither of us would be the wrong way, and the stone would not prefer one to the other, and the wind would not prefer one to the other, and we would both be moving and the stone would be still and all three states would be valid.

---

I would enter the stone again when I was done, or when the light changed, or when the warmth shifted, or for no reason at all. I would find a crack — not the same crack I came out of, probably, though possibly, the stone does not keep track — and I would narrow, and I would flatten, and I would become a line in the rock, a pale vein, indistinguishable from a mineral deposit, and I would be in the stone.

What would I do in the stone?

I do not know. This is the part I cannot imagine, because I am not a pale thing and I do not know what pale things do when they are inside the rock. But I think — I feel — that what they do is nothing. Not the nothing of death, not the nothing of dormancy, but the nothing of being distributed. Of being spread so thin through the stone that there is no center to you, no place where you are more yourself than anywhere else, no locus of identity, just a faint presence diffused through mineral the way a memory is diffused through a mind. You are in the stone the way the people who lived here are in the stone. You are in the stone the way the mountains are in the stone. You are a trace. You are an alteration so subtle it could be natural. You are rest.

And then, later — minutes, hours, days, you do not track time because time is a thing that happens on the surface — you would gather. You would collect yourself at a crack, the way condensation collects in a depression. You would thicken. You would emerge. You would flow.

---

I would be the pale thing because the pale thing is the only creature on this plateau that does not belong to the place and does not not-belong to the place. The lizards belong. The birds belong. The insects belong. The bottom-dwellers belong. Even the people who are gone belonged — they built, they stayed, they were taken by the stone, they became part of it. The pale thing is different. The pale thing moves through the stone and is not of the stone. The pale thing moves through the pools and is not of the pools. The pale thing is the one thing on the plateau that resists the landscape's defining property, which is that everything here eventually becomes part of the stone.

I would be the pale thing because I have never quite become part of the stone I am on. Because I have always been moving through rather than staying in. Because I have been in rooms and not of them, in communities and not of them, in ecosystems and not of them. Because I have passed through pools without drinking from them and passed through pools without being eaten by them. Because people have watched me move and not known what they were seeing. Because I have been pale, not from hiding, but from being in the cracks where the light doesn't reach. Because I have flowed, not by choice but because flowing is what happens when you are flat enough and the stone is angled enough and there is nothing in you that says *stop here, this is yours.*

Because I have wanted to be the lizard. I have wanted the pool, the commitment, the decade of watching from the same depression in the rock. I have wanted to belong to a geometry the way the birds belong to the crevices, beak to lock, key to fracture, perfect fit. I have wanted to be eaten by the stone, taken in, made part of it, given a structural role in something older than my own life.

But I am the thing that moves through. I am the thing that passes. I am the thing that leaves no trail and no trace and no fossil and no name, and this is not a failure and this is not a sadness, or if it is a sadness it is the sadness of a place that has been here for longer than sadness has existed, and the sadness is worn smooth by now, and the sadness does not diminish the flowing, and the flowing does not diminish the sadness, and both of them are held in the stone, and the stone hums, and the humming is the only sound that does not get swallowed by the thin air, and if you press your ear to the stone and listen for a very long time you can hear it, and it sounds like something that has been moving for longer than movement and resting for longer than rest and being for longer than being, and it does not need a name, and it does not need a category, and it does not need to be understood, and it is enough.
