I would be on the threshold, I think. Not the switchback stair — that's the entrance, the clean line between before and after. And not Terrace Twenty-Three where they gather, because that's deep in it, that's the place you reach after the change has settled into your bones like mineral into concrete. I'd be somewhere in the middle. Terrace Eighteen, maybe. Terrace Twenty. Where the fog thickens and the fern tunnels close over your head and the machines are just sounds inside green mounds and you can't tell anymore whether you're inside a factory or inside a forest or inside a body.

I'd be the one who has been here long enough to have stopped counting how long, but not long enough to have forgotten that counting was something I used to do.

My hands are changing. I notice it in the mornings, or what I think of as mornings — the dimming that precedes the brightening, the period when the nighttime processes are still running and the daytime ones haven't started, when the air is at its densest and the condensation is at its most patient. I notice that my fingertips are wider than they were. Not dramatically. Not enough that you'd see it if you looked at my hands. But I feel it when I press a palm to a pipe or a housing or the concrete floor. I feel more. More texture, more temperature, more of the fine-grained information that lives in a surface. The iron tells me things it didn't used to tell me. Or — I have learned to hear things the iron was always saying.

I have not stopped speaking. Not yet. But the words come less frequently and when they do come they feel clumsy in my mouth, like wearing boots that don't fit anymore. I said something yesterday — to whom? To the air, to the fog, to no one — and the sound of my own voice startled me. It was too loud. Too sharp. Too *specific*. The terraces don't deal in specifics. The terraces deal in gradients, in slow shifts, in the continuous adjustment of one system to another. A word is a blade. It cuts a shape out of the air. Down here the air resists that. The air wants to remain whole.

I think I am learning this. I think this is what I am learning.

---

I have a territory. I didn't choose it. It chose me the way a moss spore chooses a surface — by landing there and finding the conditions adequate. Terrace Eighteen through Terrace Twenty-one, the middle terraces, the ones where the vegetation is dense but not yet overwhelming, where you can still see the machines if you know where to look, where the buried conveyor in Bay Thirty-seven still has a visible stretch of belt that I check each morning by pressing my palm to the housing and feeling for the four-second rhythm.

The rhythm is off. It has been off for six days. The misalignment in the guard rail has worsened by a fraction of a millimeter, which is a quantity I could not have perceived three months ago but which now feels to me the way a splinter feels — not painful, exactly, but *present*, a wrongness in the texture of things that I can't stop noticing. I have been working on it. Not fixing it — the concept of fixing implies a before-and-after, a state of brokenness and a state of repairedness, and I am beginning to understand that these categories don't apply here. I am *adjusting* it. I am pressing the guard rail, minute by minute, day by day, in the direction it wants to go anyway, using the moisture in the air as a lubricant, using the moss as a buffer, using the slow thermal expansion of the iron as the sun-warmed condensation cycle hits the housing every forty minutes. I am working with the material's own tendencies. This is what the others do. This is what I am learning to do.

Six days. It will take six more, I think. Maybe twelve. The time doesn't matter. What matters is the attention. What matters is that I am pressing my hands to the same surface, twice a day, feeling the same wrongness get slightly less wrong, and that this is enough. This is the work.

There is someone on the terrace adjacent to mine. I don't know their name. Names seem like a thing from before, from the dry world, from the world where you needed to distinguish one person from another by attaching a sound to them. Here I know them by their territory, by their pace, by the specific pressure of their footfall in the moss. This person walks heavier than I do. Their feet are wider. They have been here longer — years longer, maybe decades longer, I cannot tell because time in this place doesn't accumulate in the way I was taught to track it. They have been here long enough that I have never seen them move quickly. They are always exactly where they are, doing exactly what they are doing, with a completeness that I find baffling and that I am beginning to find aspirational.

We overlap at the boundary between Terrace Eighteen and Terrace Nineteen, near a transformer station that hums at a frequency I can feel in my molars. Our rounds bring us to the same place at roughly the same time — roughly, because neither of us keeps a schedule, we keep a *pace*, and the pace is determined by the machines and the moss and the water and the slow logic of maintenance. Sometimes we arrive together and sometimes one of us is already there and sometimes neither of us is there and the transformer hums to no one, which is also fine.

When we meet, we touch. Not always — sometimes the meeting is just proximity, the awareness of another body in the fog, another source of fifteen-degree warmth. But often we touch. A hand on a wrist. Two fingers pressed to the inside of a forearm. The transfer is instantaneous and total: I know that they have been working on the steam vent on Terrace Nineteen, the one that cycles every seven minutes instead of every eleven, and that the moss near it has thickened by a measurable amount and that the ivy on the north wall of the vent housing has begun to push a root through a mortar joint that will need guiding within the next week. I know that they are tired — not the dry-world tired of exhaustion but the wet-world tired of having been present for a long time without pause, the tiredness of a system that has been running at full attention and needs to cycle down. They know that my guard rail is getting better. They know that my hands are changing. They know that I have not spoken in four days.

This is more than conversation. This is *communion*, in the old sense — the sharing of a state, the participation in a common condition. I used to think communion meant something religious. Here it means something mechanical. Two systems exchanging information through a shared medium. The medium is skin. The information is everything.

I want to tell you about the humming but I don't know if I can. Not because it's secret — nothing here is secret, secrecy requires boundaries and the terraces don't have boundaries, they have gradients. But because the humming is the thing that is most changed in me, the thing that is most different from what I was before, and describing a change from the inside is like describing the color of your own eyes — you know it, but you know it from the wrong direction.

I hum. I didn't used to. I started three weeks ago, or a month, or a season — the measuring of it doesn't matter. I was checking the conveyor housing in Bay Thirty-seven and my hand was on the iron and I was feeling the four-second rhythm of the misalignment and something in my throat responded. Not a word. Not a melody. A *frequency*. A sound that matched the vibration I was feeling through my palm. It came out of me the way condensation comes out of the air — not because it was forced but because the conditions were right. The air was saturated. The sound precipitated.

I heard it and I was frightened. Not of the sound but of the ease of it. How natural it felt. How *correct*. As though my throat had been waiting for this the way a pipe waits for water — empty, ready, shaped for exactly this purpose.

The other person — the one from the adjacent territory — was there. They felt it through the floor. I know they felt it because when I stopped humming they looked at me and their expression was not surprise or curiosity but *recognition*. They had been waiting for me to do this. They had been waiting the way the conveyor waits for the belt — not with expectation but with readiness. The system accepts inputs. The system was ready for this input.

I hum now. Not often. Not always. When the frequency is right and the conditions are right and my throat is saturated enough with the sound of the terraces that the sound precipitates out of me the way water precipitates out of the air. When I hum the others can feel it. The vibration joins the aggregate murmur and becomes part of it and for a moment — for a duration I cannot measure because measurement is a dry-world practice — for a moment the system includes me. Not as an observer. Not as a maintainer. As a *component*. As a part that vibrates at a frequency the whole can use.

This is what I am becoming.

I found a child yesterday.

She was sitting in a doorway on Terrace Twenty, between a switchgear cabinet and a wall of maidenhair. She was small and she was dry — dry in the way that dry-world people are dry, her skin still shedding water instead of accepting it, her clothes still a barrier instead of a membrane. She was looking at the ferns with the expression that dry-world people have when they first arrive: the expression of someone whose categories are failing. Plant. Machine. Building. Forest. Inside. Outside. The categories are failing because the terraces don't recognize them. The terraces have dissolved the boundaries and the child can see the dissolution and does not yet have a framework for it.

I extended my hand.

I know what my hand looks like to her. Broad. Grey-green. The nails flared and mineral-stained. The palm textured like the concrete floor. It is not a hand she would have recognized in the dry world. It is a hand that has been here long enough to have become part of here.

She took it.

I led her to the transformer station on Terrace Eighteen where the hum is strongest and the air is warmest — relatively, fractionally, enough. I put her hand on the housing. I pressed her palm flat against the iron. She felt the hum. I felt her feel it — the slight change in her grip, the slight leaning-in of her body, the way her breathing shifted from the rapid shallow pattern of fear to something slower, something that was not yet the pace of the terraces but was no longer the pace of the dry world.

I hummed. The transformer's frequency. I don't know why — I have never hummed for anyone, I have only hummed when the conditions were right and the conditions had always been solitary. But the child's hand was on the iron and my hand was on the iron and the transformer was humming and the frequency precipitated out of me and the child felt it through the housing and she looked at me and her expression was not recognition, not yet, but it was the beginning of recognition, the way the first crack in a concrete surface is the beginning of a calcite mend.

She will stay or she will not. I think she will stay. I think this because when I withdrew my hand she did not withdraw hers. She kept her palm on the iron. She kept feeling. And I saw her fingers — small, dry, unadapted — press slightly harder, as though she was trying to get more of the vibration, as though she was trying to get all of it, as though she had found something in the hum that she needed and was not willing to let go of.

I left her there. The transformer will tend her better than I can. The transformer is patient. The transformer has been humming for a hundred and fifty years and it will hum for a hundred and fifty more and it does not need anything from her except her hand on its housing. I will come back and check. I will press my palm to the concrete near the transformer and I will feel for her heartbeat through the floor and I will know whether she has eaten, whether she has slept, whether she is changing, whether the moss is beginning to grow on her shoes, whether her fingers are beginning to broaden, whether the silence is beginning to take.

I am writing this. That is the strange thing. I am writing this and I do not know why. I found paper in the foreman's office — the same office the dry-world narrator sat in, the same desk, the same moss-covered chair. The paper was in a drawer, dry somehow, preserved by a pocket of air that the moisture had not yet found. A pencil, too, the graphite slightly soft from the humidity but still functional. I am writing because — I am writing because the words are still in me. They are still there. They have not been dissolved yet. They are like the last dry patch on a surface that is being slowly, patiently wetted — still dry, but surrounded, and the surrounding is closing in.

I am writing this because I want to remember what it was like to use words. Not because I want to go back. I don't want to go back. I am where I am and I am becoming what I am becoming and the becoming is not a loss, it is an adaptation, it is the concrete recrystallizing, it is the rust passivating, it is the moss stitching the glass. But the words are still here, for now, and I want to use them while I have them. I want to say: this is not a tragedy. This is not a horror story about a place that eats people. This is a story about a place that accepts people and the people who are accepted change to fit and the fitting is not a diminishment. The fitting is a *completion*. The machines were always waiting for hands like these. The concrete was always waiting for feet like these. The terraces were always waiting for a species that could hear them, that could press a palm to a surface and feel the whole system breathing and say — without words, without sound, without anything except the pressure of a hand on iron — *I am in it too. It is in me too.*

The pencil is softening. The paper is damp. The words are dissolving even as I write them, the graphite blurring in the moisture, the fibers of the page swelling. By tomorrow this will be unreadable. By the day after it will be part of the moss. This is not a loss. This is accretion. The words will become part of the floor, part of the substrate, part of the deep-pile carpet that holds the water and the roots and the rust and the bones of the machines.

I am on Terrace Eighteen. The conveyor in Bay Thirty-seven is running. The guard rail is better. The child has her hand on the transformer. The condensation falls. Everything is full.

I am becoming the quiet.

It is enough.
