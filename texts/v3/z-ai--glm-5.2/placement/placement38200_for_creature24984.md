I would be in the eddy. Not the eddy — there are hundreds, thousands, transient pockets of relatively stable water that form in the lee of the precipitating structures and last minutes or hours before the turbulence reorganizes itself and they are gone. But one eddy. A specific one. The one behind the column that should not still be here.

The column is not impressive. If you drifted past it in the blue-green murk — and you would drift, because nothing in the lifting ocean moves with intent — you would see a pale structure, roughly cylindrical, fibrous, shot through with channels that might be veins or might be fractures, warm to the near-touch. It looks like every other formation. It is not like every other formation. It has been here for nineteen days.

This is not possible. The formations last hours. The turbulence breaks them. The chemistry shifts and they redissolve. Nineteen days is a duration that does not belong to any structure in this ocean. And yet the column persists. Not because it is stronger — it is not stronger; there are fresh shear fractures along its flank where the current has tried to take it, and the mineral fabric is thinning in the places where the flow hits hardest. It persists because it is in exactly the right place. The current eddies behind it in a way that creates a pocket of relatively stable water, and in that pocket the dissolution rate is fractionally lower than the accretion rate, and the difference — small, almost nothing, a rounding error in the chemistry — has been compounding. The column is not winning. It is losing more slowly than it is gaining, and the net result is that it is still here.

I would be in the eddy behind the column. That is where I would find myself. But *where* is not *what*, and the question was both.

I should say first what I am not.

I am not the column. The column is mineral — calcium silicate, or something adjacent, compounds that precipitate from the dissolved load of the ocean and persist for a while and then return to solution. It is not alive. It does not know it is here. It is a rock that is happening slowly, and if the eddy shifts, if the current reorganizes, if the dissolution rate climbs above the accretion rate by even a fraction, it will be gone in hours. It is not me.

I am not the filaments. The filaments are the closest thing to life this ocean has — pale threads on the column's surface, leaning toward the hum, persisting for their brief spans and being reabsorbed when the substrate beneath them dissolves. They are not me. They are what grows on me, in the way that moss grows on stone, except that the moss doesn't know the stone and the stone
