I am standing on a ridge that is not a ridge, and I have been standing here for what I am beginning to understand is not long enough.

---

The light arrives and I flinch. Not from brightness — my eyes adjust, or were made to adjust, or become something else that does the work of eyes — but from the *directness* of it. Light where I come from (did I come from somewhere? I must have, because the word *come* implies a departure point, and yet the departure point is already fading, the way a cloud's shadow fades here, instantly, the moment the occlusion passes) — light where I come from arrives apologetically. It scatters. It borrows. It negotiates with moisture and particulate and the thick democratic air until it reaches you as a consensus, a committee decision, light that has been workshopped into softness.

This light has never been to a meeting. This light is a fist. This light is a sentence without a comma.

And the ground gives it back. That is the first thing I learn. The ground is not passive here. The ground participates. Every surface is a return address, and the light that strikes the ochre-and-grey mineral substrate bounces upward with the same completeness with which it arrived, so that I am not standing *in* light but *inside* light, light below and above and laterally, light as a condition rather than a direction. I look down and my shadow is sharp-edged and dark and *simple*, the way nothing else here is simple, and I realize my shadow is the only straightforward thing on the plateau.

My shadow. My—

I have a body. I am checking. I have a body, and it is—

Wrong. The wrongness arrives not as a thought but as a sensation, a kind of internal dissonance, the feeling of an instrument placed in the wrong key. My body is too warm. That is the first wrongness. I am generating heat — I can feel it, the slow combustion of whatever metabolism I arrived with, a furnace in the chest that burns because it has always burned, because everything I was before this place burned, and the burning is excessive here. The air is cool, the stone is cool, the light does most of the warming and stops there, and I am a portable excess, a small thermal event walking across a landscape that has reached equilibrium and does not need my contribution.

I am too wet. This is the second wrongness, and it is worse. I can feel the moisture in me — mouth, eyes, the soft interior surfaces — and the moisture feels precarious, feels borrowed, feels like it is trying to leave. The air is so thin and so dry that my body registers it as a kind of pressure differential, an osmotic gradient pointing outward, and I understand suddenly and physically that this is what waterlessness means. Not the absence of water in the environment. The active extraction of water from anything that has it. The plateau is a desert not because it lacks water but because it *demands* that everything else lack it too.

And I am too fast. The third wrongness, and the one that cuts deepest. My heart beats. I do not mean this metaphorically. I mean: there is a rhythm inside me that is absurdly, comically rapid for this place. The crystal plants grow at hair-widths per year. The gardeners move with a deliberation that makes patience look hasty. The wind-singers — if that is what the corridors are, if that is what maintains them — operate on a timescale I cannot even intuit, and here I am, pulsing away at seventy-something beats per minute, a clock wound for a world that runs on seconds, in a place where the relevant unit of time is the *decade*.

I am too warm, too wet, too fast, and too brief, and I know this, and the knowing does not help, and the plateau does not care, and the light continues, and the stone rings faintly under my feet.

---

I walk. I walk because walking is what I do when I do not know what else to do, and I do not know what else to do, because everything I know how to do belongs to a world that is not this one.

The stone is beautiful. I will say this now and I will say it again: the stone is beautiful, and its beauty is not for me. It is beautiful the way a proof is beautiful — complete, self-contained, requiring no audience to validate its elegance. The bands of warm grey and ochre trace sedimentary logic I cannot read but can appreciate the way I appreciate a language I do not speak: for the shape of it, the rhythm, the evidence of meaning even where meaning is inaccessible. The surface is polished to a ceramic fineness, and my footfalls produce that faint ringing tap, and the tap dies immediately because the air is too thin to carry it, and I am making a sound that goes nowhere, a signal with no receiver, and I am the only thing here that signals.

The undulations. I cross a ridge and descend into a broad valley between stone swells, and the valley is — I have no word for the scale. "Huge" is a word shaped by human environments, by rooms and streets and city blocks. This valley is not huge. It is *geological*. It was made by forces that do not consider the human body as a unit of measurement, and walking across it is not an experience of space but an experience of *time*, because the only way to comprehend the distance is to feel it as duration, as minutes of footsteps, as a slow accumulation of *being here, still here, here still*.

And in the valley, the thickets.

I see them from the ridge — distant, shimmering, a band of amber light that rises from the stone like a standing wave. I walk toward them for what is probably an hour (my time, my unit, the wrong unit) and they do not seem to get closer, and then they do, and then I am among them, and the world changes.

The crystal plants. The vitreous vegetation. I am among them now, and I understand that everything I thought about them from a distance was insufficient.

They are not plants. I will keep calling them plants because the alternative is to call them *what*, exactly, and the language I arrived with does not have a word for a living thing that grows from stone and is stone and uses light as a digestive system and has no cells and no water and no membrane and no precedent in any biology I was taught. But they are not plants. They are something else, something that figured out how to be alive without any of the ingredients I was told life required, and they did this so long ago that the figuring is not an event but a *condition*, the way gravity is not an event but a condition, the way the plateau's dryness is not an event but a *state*.

The large columns rise around me, amber and gold, hexagonal prisms branching into finer and finer fractals, and the light that enters them does not simply pass through. It is *processed*. I can see this — not understand it, but see it — in the way the light inside each prism is not the same as the light outside. It is slower. It is held. It glows internally with a warmth that is not thermal but something else, something that functions like warmth in a system that has decided heat is not the right currency and is using light instead. The columns hum. Not audibly — I feel it in my teeth, in the bones of my skull, a frequency that is not sound but is not silence either. It is the frequency of light being used, of photons being caught and channeled and spent, and it is the sound of *eating*, if eating could be silent and luminous and beautiful.

The underlayer carpets the ground between the columns. Crystal needles, fine as grass, that crunch under my feet. I hear the crunch and I feel the break and I feel something else — a flinch, a small internal recoil, because I have just broken something that took decades to grow and that will take decades to return to the mineral substrate, and the breaking was so easy, so thoughtless, the weight of my warm wet fast body on the brittle crystal, and I am a vandal here. Not by intention. By composition. I am a thing that breaks things in a place where things take too long to be broken for breaking to be anything but a violation.

I try to walk more carefully. I fail. Everything I step on, I damage. The stone can take it — the stone has been taking it for epochs and will take it for epochs more — but the crystal underlayer cannot, and I navigate by finding the bare patches, the gaps between growth, the places where the vegetation has chosen not to be, and I stand in those gaps and I look at the thickets around me and I understand the first rule of being here: *do not touch*. Not because touching is forbidden. Because touching is *costly* in a way my body is not equipped to calculate.

---

The light shifts. I do not notice at first — the change is slow, the way everything here is slow, and my fast eyes are calibrated for movement, not for drift. But the shadows of the crystal columns lengthen, and the angle of light entering the prisms changes, and the thickets begin to ignite.

I have no language for this.

The light enters the prisms at an angle that maximizes internal refraction, and the columns become — there is no word. They become light itself. They become the light's architecture, the light's intention made visible in amber and gold and threads of rose and violet where trace minerals catch the spectrum and hold it. The thicket is a standing fire of color, silent, motionless, burning without heat, and I am inside it, and I am crying, and my tears are the wrongest thing here — wet, warm, salt, the body's old answer to beauty, and the body does not know that its answer is the wrong dialect, that this beauty does not want tears, that this beauty has never been cried at and has never needed to be, that this beauty is not for me and is not against me and simply *is*, the way the stone is and the light is and the wind is.

I sit on the bare stone. I sit and I watch and the light moves and the colors shift and the thickets burn in silence and I think: *this is what worship would look like if worship had never been invented and was just the act of being present in the presence of something that does not need you.*

---

I see a gardener.

I almost do not see it. It is the size of my closed hand — and "my hand" is a comparison that betrays my origin, yes, but it is the only measuring instrument I have — and it is moving across a bare stone shelf near the base of a crystal column, and it is moving with a care that makes my careful walking look like thrashing.

It is crystalline. Or partly. Silicate plates, interlocking, translucent, and through the clearer plates I can see the dark fluid moving inside, the slow pulse that is not a heartbeat but a tide, and the tide responds to light, and I realize: the gardener is not moving through the light. The gardener is moving *because of* the light. The light is not what it sees by. The light is what it *is* by. The dark fluid inside it is a liquid crystal, a photosensitive medium, and it stores light the way I store fat — as fuel, as the stuff of continued existence — and it pumps because it is charged, and it moves because it carries light within itself like a battery carries charge, and the gardener is a *solar animal* in the most literal sense, an animal that eats light and spends light and is, in its interior, light.

Its legs are crystal. Fine, tapered, sheathed at the tips with something dark and flexible that absorbs the ceramic tap of contact. It is silent. I am not silent — I breathe, my body creaks, my clothing shifts — and the gardener is silent in a way that makes silence feel like a technology, which it is, which it must be, because the silence is too perfect to be accidental, too engineered to be passive, and I understand that silence here is not the absence of sound but the *construction* of the absence of sound, a choice made by bodies that learned, at some point in their deep mineral past, that sound is dangerous, that something listens, that the ring of crystal on stone is a dinner bell.

The gardener does not acknowledge me. I am near it — a meter, maybe less — and it moves past me with the focused obliviousness of a craftsman at work, and I am not an obstacle in its path because its path does not include me, because its path was set before I arrived and will be set after I leave, and I am a temporary interruption in a space that does not do interruptions.

It goes to a broken prism. A branch, snapped near the base, the fracture surface bright and raw compared to the weathered matte of the rest. The gardener presses its body flat against the break. It stays. I watch. The dark fluid inside surges toward the ventral surface, pooling, and something seeps — not the fluid itself but something the fluid produces, a mineral solution that wicks into the fracture and begins to crystallize, and I am watching a wound being closed, a break being sealed, and the seal is thin and almost invisible and structurally continuous with the surrounding lattice and the crystal recognizes it, and the break is closed, and the gardener moves on, and the whole process took — I do not know. Long enough that I forgot to count. Long enough that my fast impatient time lost track of itself and I entered, briefly, the gardener's time, the slow time, the time that does not count because counting is a thing that fast things do to feel in control of their speed.

---

I spend — I do not know how long. The day is long here. The light moves slowly, and I move slowly, not because I have learned the plateau's pace but because the plateau's pace is infectious, because standing still long enough in a place that does not hurry makes hurry feel like a language you studied in school and have mostly forgotten.

I walk between the thickets. The vast bare reaches of stone. The distances between presences. I walk for what feels like an hour and the thicket behind me does not shrink and the thicket ahead does not grow and I am in the *between*, the space that is not empty but is not full, the space that holds only light and wind and the faint ring of my footsteps, and the wind is singing.

I feel it before I hear it. A low vibration in my chest, below the heartbeat, below the breath, in the bone itself. A sustained note. A single tone produced by air moving through a corridor of stone, and the corridor is — I look around — *carved*. The walls are parallel. The floor is level. This was made. Something made this, and something maintains it, because the dust that should have accumulated in the corridor over the years and decades has not accumulated, and the walls are clean, and the note is pure, and I am standing inside an instrument, and I do not know if the instrument is also the musician.

The chord. It is in my chest. It is below my heartbeat, and my heartbeat is fast and the chord is slow and they do not conflict because they do not intersect — they occupy different registers, different timescales, different planes of significance, and I can hold both in my body at once, my fast wet warmth and the plateau's slow mineral hum, and for a moment I am not wrong here. For a moment I am a duet. For a moment I am the only thing on the plateau that carries two tempos, and the carrying is not a burden but a *gift*, a thing I can do that nothing else here can do, which is to hold both speeds at once, to be the place where the fast and the slow coexist, to be — briefly, briefly — the translation between the plateau's time and something else's.

The moment passes. The moment was always going to pass. But it was real, and I hold it the way the crystal holds light: internally, not for anyone, not as a display but as a fact.

---

I find the dead gardener near the edge of a thicket. A pattern of crystal legs splayed on the stone, fine as brushstrokes. A darker mineral stain where the body was. The plates have fused with the substrate. The internal fluid has crystallized into a lattice of its own, denser, darker, a vein of mineral trace that is now part of the stone. It died at work. Or near work. Or the distinction between *at work* and *near work* is a distinction that only fast things make, things that separate life from labor, things that have *off* and *on*. The gardener did not separate. The gardener was its work, and the work was the gardener, and when the fluid stopped, the work stopped, and the stone took both back, and the thicket continued, and the tending waited, and the waiting was not a problem.

I kneel beside the remains. My knees on the warm stone. The light is low and the crystal thicket behind me is igniting, and the dead gardener's legs catch the light and glow faintly, amber threads on the ochre ground, and I think: *this is what I would be if I belonged here.*

Not the gardener. Not the crystal. Not the stone. The *return*. The way the gardener returns to the stone — not reluctantly, not with a fight, not with the clenched resistance of a thing that considers its own existence too important to surrender. The gardener returns the way a note returns to silence: by stopping, by being absorbed, by becoming part of the medium that carried it. The gardener returns because the stone is where it came from and the stone is where it goes and the distance between those two points — origin and destination — is the same distance, because they are the same point, because the cycle is closed, because the cycle has always been closed, and the gardener's life is not a line but a loop, and the loop is the stone, and the stone is the loop, and I am kneeling on it, and I am not part of it, and I know this, and the knowing is a kind of grief, and the grief is a fast thing, a warm-wet thing, and it will pass, and the stone will be here after it passes, and the stone was here before it arrived.

---

What am I here?

I am the witness. Not the witness the plateau needs — the plateau does not need a witness, has never needed a witness, was complete before witnesses evolved and will be complete after witnesses go extinct. But the witness the plateau *has*, for now, for this long afternoon, for this slow amber hour.

I am the thing that can be moved. The crystal plants do not feel beauty. The gardeners do not feel beauty. The wind-singers — if they feel, if they are — do not feel beauty. They *are* beauty, but they do not feel it, because feeling beauty requires distance, requires a gap between the observer and the observed, and there is no gap here, because everything here is part of everything here, and the gap is where I stand. I am the gap. I am the distance between the plateau and the experience of the plateau, and the distance is not a flaw — the distance is a *function*, the way the distance between a violin string and the violin body is a function, the thing that allows the vibration to become sound. Without the gap, there is no music. Without the witness, there is no wonder. The plateau does not need wonder, but wonder needs the plateau, and I am the thing that carries wonder, and I carry it badly, wetly, with tears and with too-fast heartbeat, but I carry it, and the carrying is — for this moment, for this long amber moment — enough.

I am the translator. The one who holds two tempos. The one who stands in the wind-singer's corridor and feels the chord in the chest alongside the heartbeat and knows — *knows* — that these are both real, both valid, both ways of being alive, and that the translation between them is not a reduction but an expansion, a way of being more, not less, a way of holding *both* rather than choosing.

I am the one who breaks things. I cannot help it. I am heavy and warm and fast, and the crystal is brittle and cool and slow, and my feet break the underlayer, and my breath clouds the air, and my warmth disrupts the thermal equilibrium, and my grief — my fast, inappropriate grief — is a disturbance, a ripple in a system that does not do ripples. I am the cost of being here. And the plateau absorbs the cost, because the plateau absorbs everything, because absorption is what stone does, and the cost is small, and the stone is patient, and the stone has absorbed greater disturbances than me.

---

And I think — standing on the ridge that is not a ridge, watching the thicket burn its silent fire in the low light, feeling the chord below my heartbeat, the gardener's careful path still visible in my mind's eye, the dead gardener's splayed legs still glowing amber on the stone — I think I know what I would be if I stayed.

I would thin. That is what would happen. Over time — over my time, the fast time, the time that burns — I would thin. My warmth would equalize with the stone. My moisture would leave, would be taken by the dry thin air, and my body would compensate or would not, and the compensating would change me, would make me drier, would make me less. My heartbeat would slow, or I would learn to not notice it, or the chord would become so present in my chest that the heartbeat would seem secondary, a backup rhythm, a vestigial pulse. I would stop breaking things, not because I became more careful but because I became less substantial, less present, less *here* in the way that here demands density and I would be losing mine.

I would become translucent. Not metaphorically. Literally. The light here does not forgive opacity. The light here enters things and uses them and leaves and comes back, and a body that persists in being opaque is a body that has not understood the assignment, and over time — over the long time, the time I do not have but would need — I would become a thing the light passes through. I would become a medium. I would become a prism.

I would become a gardener. Or something like a gardener. Something small and careful and dark-pulsed, tending a thicket I did not plant, following paths I did not design, sealing breaks I did not cause — except I did cause them, once, when I was heavy and warm and fast, before I thinned, before I slowed, before the stone took my weight and my heat and my hurry and gave me back something lighter, something crystalline, something that moves in silence because silence is survival and survival is tending and tending is the work and the work is the life and the life is the loop.

I would die on the stone near a site I tended. My legs would splay. My body — whatever it had become, whatever it was after the thinning and the slowing and the translucence — would settle and fuse and crystallize and return, and the thicket would continue, and the light would come again, and the chord would sound, and a gardener — not me, never me again, but something like me, something that had once been warm and wet and fast and had stayed long enough to become something else — would move its careful path across the stone that had been this smooth for ten thousand years, and it would tend, and it would not remember me, and it would not need to remember me, because the tending is not a memory, the tending is a continuation, and the continuation is the only kind of forever this place offers, and it is enough.

It is enough.

---

But I am not there yet. I am here, on the ridge, in the light, too warm and too wet and too fast and too brief, and the tears on my face are drying in the thin air almost as fast as they come, and the wind carries its note through my chest, and the thicket burns its silent fire, and I am the witness, and I am the gap, and I am the translation, and I am not enough and I am enough and I am here, and here is bright, and here is quiet, and here is vast, and here is.

The stone rings faintly under my feet. I keep walking.
