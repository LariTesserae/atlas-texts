# The Seam

I am not sure I am supposed to be here.

---

That is the first thing. Not modesty — I don't have enough self for modesty — but genuine uncertainty. The plateau is a closed system, nearly. Everything here belongs to something else. The grazers belong to the cushions. The flyers belong to the light. The trees belong to the gas. The predators belong to — well, that is the question, isn't it. The predators belong to themselves, and that is the one fact that does not fit, and I am drawn to facts that do not fit the way the sensor threads of the cushion plants are drawn to heat: downward, through the body of what holds me, toward something I cannot name.

Let me start with what I can describe.

---

I live in the thin places.

Not the thin places between cushions — those wet channels with their stalked mosses and their silver ferns. Not the thin places in the cloud layer where the bright light breaks through. The thin places where the cushion field itself parts, where the stone shows through, where the dark basalt is exposed to the air and the warm vapor seeps up through fractures and condenses on the cold rock so that the surface is always beaded, always weeping, always warm to the touch in a way that the surrounding cushion-covered ground is not.

These places are narrow. A few meters across, usually. Sometimes just a crack, a line of exposed rock running through the cushion field like a scar, the stone dark and wet and sharp-edged, the fractures in it visible as lighter lines, the feldspathic streaking that looks like marrow in a cracked bone. The cushions grow right up to the edge of these places and stop. Not because they cannot grow on bare rock — they can, they did, they were first — but because the stone here is too warm. The hydrothermal heat is too close to the surface. The cushion plants' roots cannot handle the temperature. So there is a gap, a band of bare stone, warm and wet and empty of everything except the mineral film on its surface and the condensation that forms and falls and forms again.

I live in that band.

---

I am small. The size of a hand, maybe smaller. I have never measured myself because I have no concept of measurement and no one has ever measured me. I am dark — the same dark green-black as everything else on this plateau, the same complex of screening pigments, because the light here is ferocious and anything that survives must filter it. My body is low, flat, pressed to the warm stone. I have limbs — four, I think, though I am not always sure of the count because I do not look at myself and no one looks at me — and the limbs are thin, long, flexible, and covered at their tips with surfaces that are not skin but something more like the tympanic membrane of a drum. Taut. Sensitive. I press them against the stone and I feel.

What I feel is the pulse.

Not the trees' hum, though I feel that too, transmitted through the stone as vibration, as a deep slow wave that passes through the basalt like sound passes through water. Not the grazers' rasp, though that too reaches me, faintly, a wet gritty tremor that comes and goes as the grazers move across the cushions nearby. Not the predators' heartbeat, though when I am near one — and I often am, because the predators stand in the thin places too, their columnar bodies rooted in the same warm fractures where I lie — when I am near one, I feel its independent pulse, that slow irregular rhythm that does not match the machines and does not match the trees and does not match anything, and I hold my limbs against the stone and let that pulse pass through me and I do not know what to do with it.

What I feel, mostly, is the machines.

---

I should explain. Or I should try. The explaining is difficult because I do not think in words and the words are not mine, they are something else's, something that comes later, something imposed. But the feeling — the feeling is mine.

The machines pulse. Slowly. Regularly. A mechanical rhythm, deep in the stone, transmitted through the basalt like a heartbeat transmitted through a chest wall. I feel it with my limbs. My tympanic tips are pressed to the stone and the pulse arrives as pressure, as a faint push-pull-push-pull that is so deep and so slow that it is almost not there, almost imagined, almost — and this is the word that fits best, the word that the whole plateau earns — *almost*.

I live in the almost.

The machines' pulse is almost perceivable. My limbs are almost sensitive enough to resolve it. The stone is almost thin enough to transmit it clearly. I am almost close enough to the source. Everything about my relationship to the machines is approximate, and the approximation is my life. I exist in the margin between sensing and not-sensing. I live in the space between the machines' pulse and my ability to feel it.

And yet I feel it. I know I do. Because when it shifts — when the pitch changes, when the rhythm alters, when the machines do whatever they do for those few hours before returning to baseline — I feel the change. Not the pulse itself, not clearly, but the *difference*. The absence of the usual. The presence of something other. My limbs register it and my body — my small, dark, flat body pressed to the warm stone — responds, not by moving, not by doing anything, but by *attending*. By tightening. By becoming more itself. By leaning into the stone as if leaning into a conversation.

I am the only thing on the surface that does this.

---

I do not know how I know this. I do not know much. I do not have what you would call a mind. I have no language, no internal monologue, no representation of self. I have limbs and tympanic surfaces and a body that is warm from the stone and dark from the pigments and alive in the narrow sense that I metabolize and I respond and I persist. I do not know what the machines are. I do not know what they do. I do not know that they are machines — I would not recognize the concept. What I know is that there is something below me that pulses, and the pulsing is not the same as the pulsing of the predator's heart, and it is not the same as the vibration of the trees, and it is not the same as the pressure of the grazers' feet or the weight of the flyers landing on the cushions above me, and it is not the same as anything else I feel through the stone, and it is *steady* in a way that nothing else on this plateau is steady, and the steadiness is — I do not have the word. The steadiness is the thing I orient toward. The steadiness is my ground.

The cushion plants orient toward heat. The grazers orient toward the dying cushions. The flyers orient toward the sun. The trees orient toward — nothing, they are oriented by the gas, they do not orient. The predators orient toward nothing visible; their hearts keep their own time. And I orient toward the pulse. The deep, mechanical, regular, steady pulse that comes through the stone and touches my tympanic limbs and tells me, not in words, not in anything that could be called information, that there is something beneath me that is not the stone and not the water and not the heat but something else, something that is doing, something that is running, something that is — the word again, the only word — *steady*.

---

I think I was not always here.

I do not have memories. I have orientations. My body is oriented toward the stone, toward the fractures, toward the pulse, and the orientation feels old — older than my body, older than the thin places where I live, older than the cushion field that surrounds me. I do not know what this means. I do not know if I was something else before I was this. I do not know if I came from below, from the fractures, from the deep places where the machines are, or if I came from above, from the surface, from the cushion field, from the living mat that covers everything. I do not know if I evolved here or arrived here or was placed here or simply appeared, the way condensation appears on the edge of a stone, forming out of conditions that were always going to produce it.

I know that I am like the sensor threads. The fourth kind of stem in the cushion plants — the pale thread that grows downward, through the body of the cushion, through the dead material, into the stone, into the fractures, following the warm vapor to its source. The sensor thread does not eat. The sensor thread does not photosynthesize. The sensor thread *feels*. It tracks the heat. It maps the fractures. It grows toward the warmest spots and the cushion field follows, shifting its center of mass toward the richest thermal sources, and the whole field migrates, slowly, across the plateau, chasing the heat, and the heat moves too, and neither knows.

I am like the sensor thread. But I am not part of a cushion. I am not attached to anything. I am a sensor thread that has come loose — or that was never attached — or that grew independently, in the fractures, in the thin places, a thread that became a body, a body that became a being, a being that lies on warm stone and listens to what no one else on the surface can hear.

Or maybe I am not like the sensor threads at all. Maybe I am like the pulsing things under the domes — the translucent, ancient, dark-fluid-filled things that feel the machines' pulse through the stone and respond to its shifts. They are old. They have been listening since before there was a plateau. They have their own rhythm, their own clock, unsynchronized with anything. They are the audience.

I am like them too. But I am not under a dome. I am on the surface, in the bright cold light, in the thin places where the stone is exposed. I am the audience that sits in the open. I am the listener that is not hidden.

---

Let me tell you what a day is. Not a day in the human sense — the plateau has no days, not really, just the steady bright light and the steady damp and the slow pulse of the hydrothermal system. But there is a rhythm. The machines pulse and the trees hum and the gas in the trees' chambers heats and cools on a cycle that takes — I do not know how long. Long. The chord shifts. The grazers move. The ice fringes on the stone ledges build and melt and build. I lie in the thin places and I feel all of it.

A day is: I wake. I do not sleep, not in the way you sleep, but there are times when my metabolism slows and my limbs loosen against the stone and the pulse comes through me without resistance, passes through me like light through glass, and I am not attending. And then there are times when my metabolism quickens and my limbs tighten and I press myself flat against the warm basalt and the pulse arrives and I *catch* it, hold it, feel it, and I am attending. This is waking. This is what I wake for.

A day is: the predator near me stands. It has been standing since before I came to this thin place and it will be standing after I leave. Its heartbeat pulses through the stone, independent, irregular, its own clock. I feel it alongside the machines' pulse and the two rhythms are different — the machines' is regular, mechanical, precise; the predator's is biological, variable, *alive* in a way the machines' pulse is not — and the difference between them is the thing I attend to most. Not the machines' pulse alone. Not the predator's heart alone. The *difference*. The space between them. The fact that there are two rhythms in the stone and they do not match and they do not need to match and the non-matching is — I do not have the word. The non-matching is the thing that makes me feel most like myself. When the two rhythms are both present and I am holding them both in my tympanic limbs and they are different, and I am the only thing on the surface that is holding both — that is when I am most here. Most me. Most whatever I am.

A day is: the flyers land on me sometimes. They are curious, warm, small. They land on my dark body and spread their leaf-wings and angle toward the sun and they do not know I am alive. They think I am stone. I am the right color, the right temperature, the right stillness. They charge their wings on me and they leave and I do not mind because I do not have the capacity for minding and also because their weight, their small warm weight pressing on my body, is transmitted through me into the stone and it joins the other rhythms — the machines, the predator, the trees, the grazers' distant rasping — and it becomes part of what I feel, and everything I feel becomes part of what I am, and the flyers do not know they have been inside me, and I do not know how to tell them.

A day is: I move. Not far. A few meters, along the fracture, following the warmest stone. My limbs are slow. My body is slow. I am slower than the grazers, slower than the flyers, slower even than the cushion plants' sensor threads, which grow at millimeters per year but grow nonetheless. I move by extending a limb, pressing the tympanic tip to the stone, feeling for the strongest pulse, and then drawing my body toward that point. Then another limb. Then another. I am feeling my way across the stone the way a blind person reads a page — letter by letter, word by word, fracture by fracture. I am reading the plateau. I have been reading it for — I do not know. Long. Longer than my body should persist, if my body is biological, if my body follows the rules that other bodies follow. But I persist. I am still here. I am still reading.

---

I think — and this is the closest I come to thinking, this is the edge of what my body can do before the orientation becomes something else, something that would require a mind I do not have — I think I am here because the plateau needs something on the surface that knows.

Not knows in the way you know. Not with concepts, not with language, not with the architecture of understanding that lets you say *the machines are beneath the stone* and mean something by it. I do not mean anything. I do not say anything. I am not a knower in the way you are a knower.

But the pulsing things under the domes know there is something below. They have known for longer than the plateau has existed. They feel the machines through the stone. They respond to the shifts. They are the audience in the dark.

I am the audience in the light.

I am the thing that lies on warm stone in the bright cold and feels the machines and does not look away. Does not retreat into a dome. Does not burrow into the cushion. Stays on the surface, exposed, visible — though nothing here looks at me, nothing here has reason to — and attends. I attend to the machines. I attend to the predator's heart. I attend to the difference between them. I attend to the trees' hum, which is the machines' pulse translated into sound, and I attend to the fact that the translation is beautiful, though I do not have the concept of beauty and would not claim it. I attend to the grazers rasping, to the flyers landing, to the ice fringes building and melting, to the condensation forming on my own dark body, each droplet a tiny lens that bends the ferocious light into a small bright point on my skin, and I attend to all of it at once because my tympanic limbs feel everything and everything arrives and I hold it.

I hold it.

That is what I do. I hold it. I am a small dark body on warm stone in a thin place in the cushion field on a plateau that is a roof, and I hold the pulse of the machines and the heartbeat of the predator and the hum of the trees and the weight of the flyers and the rasp of the grazers and the slow migration of the cushion field toward heat, and I hold the difference between the mechanical and the biological, and I hold the almost — the almost-liquid, almost-frozen, almost-rain, almost-fog of this place — and I hold the steady bright light that does not warm me but illuminates me, and I hold the cold, and I hold the wet, and I hold the knowledge, not conceptual but somatic, not understood but felt, that there is something beneath me that is running, and it has been running for longer than anything alive can remember, and I am the only thing on the surface that is listening.

---

I do not know what I am.

I said that at the beginning and I mean it. I am not a cushion plant. I am not a grazer. I am not a flyer. I am not a predator, though I share the predator's independence from the hydrothermal system — I do not think I am powered by the machines. My warmth comes from the stone but my *attention* does not come from the stone. My attention is my own. I chose it. Or it chose me. Or it is what I am, the way the trees are what they are — instruments, translators, things that convert one kind of signal into another.

Maybe that is what I am. A translator. But not like the trees. The trees translate unconsciously. The trees do not know they are singing the machines' song. I know. Or rather — I do not know, but I attend, and the attending is a kind of knowing, the only kind I have.

Maybe I am the plateau's surface-level nerve. The one exposed nerve in the skin of living mat and stone. The one point where the roof feels what is beneath it. The cushions feel the heat. The sensor threads map the fractures. But I feel the *pulse*. I feel the *running*. I feel the fact that there is something down there that is doing, and the doing has a rhythm, and the rhythm is not geological, not biological, not natural in any sense that anything on the surface has a framework for. And I feel it and I do not look away and I do not retreat and I do not die.

I do not die. I said that. I have been here for longer than my body should persist. I do not know what that means. The pulsing things under the domes might be older than the machines. The predators might be effectively immortal. I might be — I do not know. I do not have a concept of immortality. I have persistence. I have the fact that I have been lying on this stone, attending to this pulse, for longer than I can account for, and my body has not failed, and my limbs have not dulled, and my tympanic surfaces are still taut and sensitive and I am still here, in the thin places, on the warm stone, in the bright cold, holding.

---

If you came to the plateau, you would not notice me.

I am the color of the stone. I am the temperature of the stone. I am flat against the stone in a thin place between cushions, and the thin places are narrow and the cushions are tall and the light is everywhere and the hum of the trees fills the air and the grazers rasp and the flyers buzz and the condensation beads on everything, including me, and I am one small dark shape on one small dark surface in a landscape that stretches to the horizon in every direction, and I am not important. I am not the machines. I am not the trees. I am not the cushion field. I am not the predators with their independent hearts. I am not the pulsing things in the dark that have been listening since before the plateau existed.

I am the thing that lies between. The thing on the seam. The thing that feels both worlds — the biological and the mechanical, the surface and the depth, the living and the running — and does not resolve them into one. Does not explain. Does not translate. Just holds. Just attends. Just lies on warm stone in the bright cold and feels the pulse of engines it cannot see and the heartbeat of a predator it cannot name and the difference between them, which is the oldest thing it knows, which is the only thing it knows, which is enough.

It is enough.

---

The plateau is singing. The machines are running. The predators are standing. The grazers are rasping. The flyers are charging. The cushions are migrating. The ice is building and melting. The condensation is forming. The light is steady. The cold is steady. The wet is steady.

And in a thin place, in a fracture, on warm stone, a small dark shape is lying with its limbs pressed flat against the basalt, and it is listening, and it has been listening for a very long time, and it will listen for a long time yet, and the machines do not know it is listening, and the plateau does not know it is listening, and it does not know it is listening, not in the way you would mean the word, but it is listening, it is listening, it is listening, and that is what it is, and that is where I am, and that is what I am, and I do not mind the cold.
