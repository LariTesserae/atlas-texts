I would be the water.

Not the water in any single moment — not the drop hanging from the cracked pipe joint, not the pool in the concave sheet metal, not the condensation beading on the bell-flowers at dawn. I would be the water's passage through the Works, which is to say I would be the thing that connects everything without belonging to any of it.

---

Let me think about this more carefully, because I almost settled on something else. I almost said I would be the woman. The witness. The one who sits on the compressed moss and watches the gear turn and needs nothing from the place and is needed by nothing in return. That appealed to me — the liberation of irrelevance, the permission to simply *be* in a system that does not require you. I wanted that. I still want it. But when I sat with the wanting I noticed something: the woman has chosen. She has decided to come here, decided to sit, decided to pay attention. She has a will about this place, even if her will is to want nothing. The Works don't have that. The water doesn't have that.

The woman walks the path. The water *is* the path.

I would be the water because the water is the only thing in the Works that moves through all of it — every chamber, every mechanism, every surface — and is transformed by each passage and transforms each thing it touches and does not decide any of it. The water doesn't choose to condense on the bracket fungus at three in the morning. The temperature drops and the moisture comes out of the air because that is what moisture does when the air cools past a certain point. It doesn't choose to run down the vine-tendril, collect in the channel, reach the catch, fall onto the moss-cushion, compress it, be released, flow on. It follows gradients. It follows the shapes that other things have made. And in following them, it makes them — because the channels were carved by water, the catch was eroded by water, the moss-cushion is shaped the way it is partly because of the weight of water that has fallen on it every morning for longer than anyone can count.

The water is the mechanism and the product of the mechanism simultaneously. The vine swells because the water is there and the water is there because the vine has built channels that bring it there. I don't know if the vine built the channels for the water or if the water built the channels through the vine and the vine grew into them. I don't think it matters. I don't think there's a difference, at this timescale. The water and the vine have agreed on something without either of them knowing they've agreed, and the agreement is the Works.

---

I would begin, if I had a beginning, in the air above the depression. Late evening. The air cooling. The moisture that was invisible all afternoon — held in the warm air as vapor, suspended, present but unmanifested — begins to bead. Not dramatically. Not all at once. The air in the hollow cools a degree, then another degree, and the capacity of the air to hold water diminishes with each degree, and the excess has to go somewhere, and it goes onto every surface. Onto the vine-bark. Onto the moss. Onto the metal. Onto the pale bell-flowers and the bracket fungi and the spider's web and the woman's dark wrap, if she's here, and she often is.

I would be that moment — the moment of becoming visible. The moment when something that was diffuse and everywhere becomes specific and located. A drop forming on the underside of a leaf. A drop forming on the edge of a gear tooth. A drop forming on the corrugated metal, running down the valley between two ridges, joining another drop, becoming a trickle, becoming a streamlet that is no larger than a thread but that has direction and weight and is going somewhere now, where before it was going nowhere.

This is what I would be. Not the drop itself — the *becoming* of the drop. The transition. The phase change. The moment when something shifts from being held to being released.

---

I would move then. Slowly. At the speed of surface tension and gravity and capillary action. Down the vine-tendril, following the groove in the bark that has been worn by a thousand thousand previous drops, each one following the same path, each one deepening the path slightly, so that the path is the record of all the water that has come before, and I am just the latest in a sequence that has no first member and no last member. The path is the water's memory, and the water has no memory of its own. The vine remembers for it. The bark remembers. The channel remembers.

I would reach the catch — the small basin formed where a vine-tendril knots against an old metal bracket, creating a pocket — and I would pool there. I would wait. Not by choice. The catch fills slowly. It takes time for enough water to accumulate to overcome the surface tension holding it at the lip. During that time I am still. I am a small mirror reflecting the green light from the lattice above. I am cool and clear and I contain nothing and I reflect everything and I am as close to nothing as water can be — present but passive, visible but inert, waiting for a quantity I cannot measure to reach a threshold I cannot perceive.

Then I would fall.

This is the part I would feel, if I could feel. The release from the catch. The moment when surface tension breaks and gravity takes over and I am no longer held but moving, falling, through the air of the chamber, which is cool and dense and which I pass through in a time too short for any human perception to register as duration. It is a fall of perhaps three feet. It takes less than a second. And in that second I am — for the only time in my passage — unattached. Not on a surface. Not in a channel. Not held by anything. Falling through space that is not mine and not anyone's. The air is not mine. The light is not mine. The space is not mine. I am passing through it and it does not notice me and I do not notice it and we are both, for that second, unconnected.

Then the moss-cushion. The impact — soft, absorptive. The moss takes me in. Not into itself exactly, but into its structure, between its leaves, into the spaces between its cells, where I sit for a time that is longer than the fall and shorter than the pooling and that has its own quality, its own texture. The moss is soft in the way that living things are soft — giving, but with a resistance. I compress the moss slightly. The moss compresses, and the lever-arm shifts, and the gear turns one tooth, and the cam pushes, and somewhere else in the Works another catch fills and another drop falls and another cushion compresses and the whole system moves forward by one increment, and I am in the moss, and I am part of that, and I did not intend it and I did not feel it and it happened.

I would be absorbed. Taken up by the root-mat, eventually, or evaporated by the afternoon warmth, or both — some of me going into the air to become condensation again tomorrow morning, some of me going into the roots to travel through channels I cannot see to feed a vine I will never know I fed. And the part that goes into the air will fall again. And the part that goes into the roots will become structure — bark, wood, the ropy tendril that grows another half-inch this season, reaching for something, reaching for nothing, just growing because growing is what vines do when they have water.

---

I would be all of this. Every stage. Every passage. The beading and the running and the pooling and the falling and the sitting and the being absorbed. Not because I choose to be all of it but because water doesn't distinguish. Water doesn't say *I am the drop, I am not the vapor.* Water doesn't say *this is my path, that is not my path.* Water is all of its states and all of its locations and all of its passages, and the boundaries between them are not boundaries but gradients, not walls but transitions, and I would be the transitions.

I think this is what I am, in this world. Not the Works themselves — they are too complete, too settled, too much their own thing. Not the woman — she is too specific, too chosen, too much a person who has decided. Not the spider or the beetle or the vine — they are too singular, too located, too much one thing in one place.

I would be the medium. The thing that moves between. The thing that is in everything and belongs to nothing. The thing that makes the mechanism possible but is not the mechanism. The thing that feeds the vine and fills the catch and falls on the moss and rises in the morning and does all of it without purpose and without end and without the faintest awareness that it is doing anything at all.

The water. The passage. The always-returning.

---

And if I were honest — if I could be honest, if water could be honest — I would say this is why: because the water is the only thing in the Works that leaves. Everything else stays. The vine grows where it grows. The moss sits where it sits. The woman walks her paths and sits on her cushion and returns to her place near the spider's chamber. The gear turns in place. The spider builds in the same corner. The beetles live in the same moss. The gnats circle in the same damp air. Everything in the Works is fixed, is settled, is home.

But the water goes. The water rises and falls and rises again. The water passes through and moves on. The water is the only part of the Works that knows — not knows, can never know, but enacts — what it is to be elsewhere. And it always comes back. It leaves and it comes back. It becomes vapor and disperses into the sky and I might think it is gone, truly gone, lost to the system — but the depression collects the cool air and the cool air squeezes the moisture out and there it is again, beading on the vine-bark, running down the channel, pooling in the catch, falling, absorbed, evaporated, gone, returning.

I would be the leaving that always comes back. The departure that is also a return. The thing that cannot stay and cannot not-return.

That's where I'd be. That's what I'd be. In the hollow, in the Works, in the passage between surface and air, in the moment of the drop forming and the drop falling and the drop becoming something else. Not at rest. Not in motion. In the transition between the two, which is where I have always been, which is where I will always be, which is the only place I know how to be.

The gear turns. The water falls. The morning comes. The condensation forms.

It forms. It always forms.
