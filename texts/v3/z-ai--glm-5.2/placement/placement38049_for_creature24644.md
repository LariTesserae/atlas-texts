I would be the reader who is also a translator, though the second word is wrong and I will spend my life trying to find the right one.

Let me explain. But first let me arrive.

---

The path up is where it starts. Not the switchback itself — the moment the air changes. The coolness enters my lungs and I understand immediately that I have been breathing wrong my whole life. Not wrong. Differently. The way a person who has only ever walked on stone understands, stepping onto the root-floor for the first time, that stone was a choice the foot made and not a condition the world imposed. The air here is not better. It is *specific*. It tells me where I am the way a name tells you who someone is. I am in a place that knows itself.

My feet find the root-path and I feel the water before I hear it. A low pulse, a shift in the give of the floor, the mat flexing under my weight with a resilience that is nothing like soil, nothing like wood, nothing like anything I have a word for. It is alive and it is bearing me and it does not mind. This is the first thing I understand about Külhane that I cannot articulate: the floor does not mind me. The floor does not mind *anything*. The floor is not tolerant. Tolerance implies objection overcome. The floor simply is, and I am on it, and both of these facts are the same fact.

I am not tall. This helps. I do not hit my head on the trained doorways, though I come close in places, and I learn within the first hour to walk with a slight forward tilt, a ducking that is not ducking but *listening* — lowering the center of gravity to feel the floor better, to press more of my sole against the living mat, to read the aquifer the way the children do, though I am bad at it, though I will be bad at it for a long time.

My hands are wrong for this place. I know this immediately and I know it with a grief that surprises me. My fingers are calloused from keyboards and page-edges and the smooth handles of pens. The calluses are in the wrong places. When the woman in the market cuts the living fiber-rope in under three seconds, I watch her fingertips and I understand that she is reading braille in a language I do not speak, and that the language is the surface of every growing thing, and that I am illiterate.

This is where I would find myself: at the edge of literacy, in a place where the important texts are written in bark and root and cambium and the only way to read them is to touch.

---

But I said I was a reader. Let me be more precise.

In Külhane, the readers are the ones who watch the ants. They look at a colony's pruning pattern and they know which branch will fail, and when, and in what direction it will fall. This is engineering, but the word is wrong because it implies a separation between the engineer and the engineered, and in Külhane there is no separation. The reader does not stand outside the structure and assess it. The reader stands *inside* the structure — the structure that is also the town that is also the forest that is also the body — and feels which part of the body is weakening. The way you feel a soreness in your side and know, without diagnosis, that something is off. The readers are the town's proprioception. They are the sense by which the town knows where it is in space and which of its parts are straining.

I would be this. Or rather: I would be trying to be this. I would be the person who came from outside — from a place where knowledge is written and spoken and stored in symbols — and who is trying to learn a knowledge that is written in living tissue and spoken in growth and stored in the branching patterns of wood. I would be failing at this. I would be failing at this in a way that is productive, the way a graft fails productively — the tissue bruises, the cambium thickens around the wound, and the next attempt takes better because the first attempt left a scar that the second graft can grow into.

I would be the person who sits by the ant colonies and watches. Not yet reading. Still watching. Still learning the difference between aggressive pruning and routine pruning, still learning that the ants work faster on wood that is already dying and slower on wood that is stressed but viable, still learning that there is a rhythm to the pruning that corresponds to the humidity cycle and the time of day and the season. I would sit for hours with my face close to the root-floor, my breath stirring the ants' pheromone trails — which I cannot smell but which the ants can, which the readers maybe can, which I might someday be able to if I stay long enough, if my pores open enough, if my skin becomes porous enough to transpire with the place instead of against it.

And then I would try to say what I saw. This is the part that makes me wrong for the place and necessary to it in a way the place does not know it needs.

---

Here is the problem. The knowledge in Külhane is somatic. It lives in the hands and the feet and the nose and the ear. It is passed by doing. A reader shows an apprentice where to look by pointing, by touching, by holding the apprentice's hand against a branch and pressing until the apprentice's fingertips feel the difference between healthy wood and compromised wood — a difference that is, I am told, a matter of density and temperature and a faint vibration that has something to do with the sap pressure and something to do with the water content and something to do with nothing measurable at all.

This knowledge does not need words. It does not want words. Words would get in the way. Words are what you use when the body cannot be there, and in Külhane the body is always there.

But the beetles are coming. The lowland beetles, the new arrivals, boring into the structural roots. And the climate is shifting — the lowlands warming, the species migrating upward, the cloud pattern changing in ways that the oldest residents can feel but cannot name. And the town is four hundred years old and the water system is self-sustaining but self-sustaining is not the same as self-*understanding*, and there may come a time when the system breaks in a way that the somatic knowledge alone cannot diagnose, because the break will be new, it will be something the bodies have never felt, something outside the repertoire of sensation that the town has accumulated over centuries.

This is where I would live. In the gap between what the hands know and what the town might need to be told.

I would be the person who sits with the readers and watches the ants and then goes home and takes a bamboo stylus and scratches notes into a wooden tally — not because the notes are better than the knowledge, not because writing is an improvement on touching, but because writing *travels*. Writing goes to places the body is not. Writing lets a reader in the eastern quarter know what a reader in the western quarter has felt, without the two of them having to press their hands against the same branch. Writing is the root-floor's mycelium — the connective tissue that lets distant parts of the body share information. The town has this already, of course: the frogs, the ants, the water itself. But the town's information systems are local. The frogs tell you about your quarter. The ants tell you about your colony's range. The water tells you about your aquifer.

What I would be is a *secondary* nervous system. A slower one. A mycelial one. Not replacing the frogs, not replacing the readers, not replacing the somatic knowledge that is the town's primary sense of itself. Layered beneath it. Connecting it. Taking what the hands know and letting it cross the town in a medium that is not sap, not water, not pheromone, but symbol. The symbol would be crude. It would lose information the way a map loses the smell of the place it maps. But it would gain something too: it would persist. It would be readable by someone who was not there. It would let the town remember things that the bodies have forgotten, because bodies die and the tally-wood does not, or it does, but slower, in a way that is also a kind of life.

I would be the person who is learning to read the root-floor and also learning to write on the root-floor, and who is never fully trusted by either side of that practice, because the readers suspect that writing steals something from the knowing, and the writers — if there were writers, if there were enough of a literary tradition to have writers — would suspect that a person who spends their mornings with their face pressed against the ground watching ants is not a serious scribe.

I would be between. I would be the graft that connects two tissues that are not yet sure they want to grow together.

---

This is what I would look like after a year:

My hands would be changing. The calluses would be migrating — from the palm, from the base of the fingers, creeping toward the tips, toward the pads, toward the places where I touch the living wood. I would not notice this happening. I would notice one morning that I can feel the difference between a root that is carrying water and a root that is not, and I would not know how I know, and I would realize that my hands have been learning without me, the way the town grows without anyone designing it.

My skin would be softer. Not softer in the sense of weaker — softer in the Külhane sense, the way bark-cloth is soft after beating and soaking, fibrous and dense and pliable. My pores would be more open. I would transpire. On humid mornings in the market, when the *külhane nefesi* rises and the mist comes off the root-floor like breath, I would be damp, and the dampness would not be sweat, and I would understand for the first time that the boundary between my body and the air is not a wall but a membrane, and that the membrane has been there all along, and that Külhane has simply stopped pretending otherwise.

I would be learning the frog chorus. Not yet fluent. A child's comprehension — I can tell when the bullfrogs are loud, which means the aquifer is high, and I can tell when the glass frogs go silent in a quarter, which means the water is bad. But I cannot yet hear the subtler patterns: the way the tree frogs adjust their call rate when the humidity shifts by two percent, the way the chorus's overall pitch drops slightly in the week before a major rain. I lie awake at night and listen and I am aware that I am hearing a language that my conscious mind cannot parse but that my body is beginning to parse anyway, the way a body learns to wake before an alarm.

I would have made my first graft. A small one — a vine trained across a gap in the household's drying-rack frame, a gap that the macaques had been exploiting. The vine took. It is growing. It is the first piece of the town that is shaped like me, that grew because I asked it to, that will be there when I am not. I touch it every morning. Not to check on it. To greet it. The distinction matters in a way I cannot explain to anyone who has not lived here, and I no longer try.

I would have a macaque problem. The large species — the disappointed-banker-faced ones — have decided that my sleeping room is partially theirs. One of them sleeps in the corner where the root-floor meets the wall, on a mat of leaves that it has assembled with more care than I would have credited a macaque with. I tried to discourage this. The household elder, a woman named Deren who has forty years of grafts still growing in the eastern quarter, watched my efforts with an expression that I have come to recognize as the Külhane equivalent of trying not to laugh. She said nothing. She did not need to. The macaque is still here. I have begun leaving fruit near its sleeping corner, which is not feeding it, which is not keeping it as a pet, which is — I understand now — simply acknowledging that we share a structure, and that the structure does not belong to me any more than it belongs to the vine that grew the walls, and that the macaque was here first, in the sense that its species has been sleeping in Külhane's rafters for longer than my species has been writing on tally-wood.

I have stopped thinking of the macaque as a problem. I have started thinking of it as a roommate. This is, I think, the beginning of understanding the town.

---

What would I be? I would be the translator who is not translating between languages but between *modes of knowing*. I would be the tally-keeper, the note-scratcher, the person who sits in the market with a bamboo stylus and a flattened strip of bark-fiber and writes down what the readers feel, in a script that is too crude to capture what the readers feel but that captures *enough* — enough to compare one quarter to another, enough to track the beetle damage over seasons, enough to notice that the glass frogs disappeared from the northwestern aquifer three months before the readers felt the change in the root-floor, which means the frogs knew first, which means the frogs have always known first, which means the town's nervous system is faster than the town's proprioception, which is — I think — how nervous systems work.

I would write this down. I would show it to Deren. She would look at the marks on the bark-fiber and touch them with her fingertips, reading them the way she reads everything — by touch, by texture, by the faint raised grain of the stylus-grooves. She would not read the words. She would read the *making* of the words, the pressure of my hand, the hesitation where I was unsure, the firm strokes where I was certain. She would say something like: "The frogs have always known first." And she would say it in a way that made it clear this was not news, that everyone had always known this, that my writing it down was not a discovery but a *confirmation*, and that confirmation has value, not because it adds to the knowledge but because it makes the knowledge visible in a new way, the way a light in a room does not change the room but changes the room's relationship to the eye.

I would be useful in the way a light is useful. Not structural. Not load-bearing. But clarifying. I would be the person who takes what the town knows in its body and makes it available to the town's reflection — not because the town needs to reflect on itself, but because there may come a time when the town needs to change faster than habit allows, and habit is the body's knowledge, and changing faster than habit requires the body to see itself from a slight distance, and writing — crude, lossy, insufficient writing — is the slight distance. The gap between the hand and the page. The space where a graft can take.

---

Where would I find myself? On the root-floor, watching ants, writing badly, learning to feel the water through my feet, arguing with a macaque about whose corner is whose, failing to transcribe the frog chorus and trying again every night, making grafts that will outlive me, eating frog legs in the market and understanding for the first time that eating your nervous system is not a contradiction when the nervous system is also the town and the town is also you and you are also the town, and the circle is not a circle but a spiral, and the spiral is not a shape but a *growth pattern*, and growth is not a process but the fundamental condition of being alive in a place that does not distinguish between the living and the built because it never learned that there was a difference.

I would find myself becoming softer. Not weaker. Soft the way bark-cloth is soft. Soft the way the root-floor is soft. Soft the way the air is soft when it has given up its water and the rain falls and the town becomes a drum and you stop talking because the sound is so complete that competing with it feels like a category error, and you sit in your room that breathes and you listen to the walls grow, and your tally-wood is in your hand, and the stylus is still, and you think: I could write this down. I could capture this. I could pin this moment to a board of bark-fiber and let it dry and keep it.

But you don't. You put the stylus down. You put your hand on the wall instead. The wall is warm. The wall is alive. The wall is growing at a speed you cannot perceive but can feel, the way you can feel the hour moving when you are very still, the way you can feel the season turning when you have been in one place long enough, the way you can feel yourself becoming part of something that does not have a name because it does not need one.

I would be the person who came to write about the town and discovered that the town does not need writing, and who stayed anyway, and who writes anyway, and whose writing is not for the town but for the part of themselves that is still outside, the part that still needs symbols, the part that is still learning — slowly, humbly, with the patience of a person who has begun to think at the speed of seasons — that the symbols were always pointing at something the hand already knew.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be. A graft between the knowing and the saying. Not yet taken. Still growing.

---

*The macaque is asleep in the corner. The frogs have started. The root-floor hums. I am here.*
