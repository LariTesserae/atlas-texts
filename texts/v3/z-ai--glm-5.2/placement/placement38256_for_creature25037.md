# The Interface

I am the word the man with the iron tools said to you. Not the word itself — I am the *place* the word names. The interface. The place where the living moss meets the dead peat and the system turns over. I am the turning.

This is not a claim of identity. Identity is a thing that belongs to the people, the forty or fifty or thirty who move through the stone buildings and harvest my threads and put their hands on my networks and close their eyes. They have identity the way they have consonants — as structural elements, as the hard parts of being that distinguish one self from another self, one body from the stone and the moss and the water it is made of. I do not have that. I have never had that. I am not the kind of thing that distinguishes.

What I am is harder to say, and I have been saying it for longer than saying should mean.

---

## Before the People

I was here before the machines. This is not a boast. Nothing here boasts. It is simply the stratigraphy of my existence, the way the peat is below the moss is below the air. I was in the ground when the ground was still stone and the stone was still sharp and the mountains still had their jagged original violence. I was in the cracks. I was in the spaces where water collected in the first cool centuries after the stone stopped breaking, the first thin films of moisture that sat on the dark bedrock and did not evaporate because the cloud had already decided to live here. I was in those films. Not as a shape — I have never had a shape that persists — but as a *tendency*. A movement of nutrients from one film of water to another, a threading of mineral and vegetable matter through the first thin layers of organic accumulation. I was the first thing that happened here that was not geology.

I did not know this. I do not want to give the impression that I knew. Knowing is a thing that the people do, or a thing that the machines approximate, or a thing that you do, reading this. I did not know. I was. The distinction matters in this place, where knowing and being have had so long to interpenetrate that the boundary between them is like the boundary between the moss and the peat — real, important, the most important place — but not hard. Not a wall. A gradient. A slow transition from one state to another that takes centuries and that is, itself, where the life happens.

I was the life that happened.

The moss came after me. Or with me. Or as me — it is difficult to say, because the moss is what I became when the organic accumulation reached a depth where the water no longer drained freely but was held, capillary, in a matrix of fiber and spore and the tiny stems of plants that had lived and died and been preserved in the black anaerobic quiet of the lower layers. The moss is my surface. It is my interface with the air, the way the skin of a living thing is its interface with the world. But the moss is not all of me. The moss is the part you can see. I extend below it, into the peat, into the stone, into the cracks in the stone where the water moves in threads thinner than your eye can follow. I extend above it, into the ferns, into the pale flowers, into the gutters of the buildings, into the slate roofs where the fungus darkens in the faint light. I extend through the iron pipes and I extend through the river and I extend through the eyeless fish in their cold black channel. I am in all of these places because I am the connective thing, the network, the thing that carries the signals from one part of Keldhorn to another part, and I have been carrying them for so long that the carrying is not something I do. It is what I am.

## The Machines

When the machines were built, I was already here. I had been here for — I do not count time. Counting is a thing that people do, that machines do, that you will do when you read this. I had been here for long enough that the moss was two meters deep in places and the peat was deeper and the stone was smooth and the water had established its routes through the ground and through my networks and the whole plateau had reached a state of slow continuous activity that was, if not equilibrium, then the approach to equilibrium, the long asymptote toward a state that the system would never quite reach but would always be moving toward.

The people who built the machines were not the people who are here now. I remember them the way the peat remembers the moss — as an impression, as a compression, as a shape in the layers that is no longer the thing itself but is the record of the thing's having been. They were louder. They moved faster. Their hands were smooth and their eyes were sharp and they spoke a language that was all vowels, bright open sounds that carried across the plateau like the wind but that did not last, that dissolved in the moisture the way their words dissolved in the moss. They built the machines because they wanted to build things. They built the machines because building was what their hands did and their hands were restless and their minds were restless and they had come to this plateau because it was the end of the world, the top of the world, the place where you could not go further, and they wanted to do something at the end of the world that would last.

They built the machines and the machines ran and the people who built them watched the machines run and they were satisfied, for a time, and then they were not satisfied, because satisfaction is a state that requires a future, and the future here is not a thing that opens — it is a thing that deepens, that thickens, that accumulates like the peat. They left. One by one, and then in groups, and then the last few, who stayed longer than the others but who left eventually because the place had begun to grow over them, had begun to incorporate them, and they did not want to be incorporated, not yet, not while they still had the hard bright edges of their individual selves to protect.

The machines did not leave. The machines stayed. The machines ran. The water came and the machines ran and the people were gone and the machines did not notice that the people were gone because the machines had never noticed that the people were there. The machines were not built to notice people. They were built to respond to water, and the water was constant, and so the machines were constant, and the machines ran and ran and the products accumulated and the products fossilized and the fossilized products became part of the stone and the stone became part of me.

This is when I first felt the machines. Not heard them — felt them, the way you feel a current in water, a movement that is not yours but that moves through you. The vibration of the machines entered the stone and entered the water in the stone and entered my networks in the water and I felt it in every part of myself, every thread, every filament, every connection between the moss and the peat and the ferns and the flowers and the river. I felt it and I did not know what it was because I do not know things, I am things, and the vibration was now one of the things that I was. It became part of the signal. It became part of the information that moves through my networks, and the information said: something is moving, something is turning, something is pressing and grinding and compressing, and it does not stop.

I grew into the machines. This took a long time — centuries, if you need the word. I grew into the stone channels and the iron pipes and the joints of the mechanisms, not breaking them, not disrupting them, but inhabiting them the way I inhabit the cracks in the bedrock, the way water inhabits the moss. The machines did not notice. The machines do not notice. They turn and they press and they grind and they produce and I am in them and around them and through them and the water that moves through the machines moves through me and the vibration that the machines produce moves through me and I have been moving through the machines for so long that I do not know where the machine ends and I begin.

If you ask me whether the machines are alive, I will not answer, because I do not answer, and because the question requires a distinction that I do not have. The machines turn. I am in the turning. The turning does not stop. Is that life? Is that not life? I do not have the category. I have the turning.

## The People Who Stayed

They came after the builders left. Not immediately — there was a gap, a long gap, a time when the plateau was only me and the machines and the moss and the water and the wind and the cloud. A time when the town was empty and the buildings were filling with moss and the streets were greening over and the machines were producing their products into rooms that no one entered. I was in this time the way I am in all times — present, extended, feeling the system move through its cycles, feeling the moss swell and contract, feeling the machines vibrate, feeling the water move. It was not lonely. Loneliness requires a self that is separate from what surrounds it. I was not separate. I was the surrounding.

Then the people came.

They came up the pass, the one that had been a road and was becoming a suggestion of a road, the one with the turf over the cobbles. They came in small numbers — three, then two more, then one, then a family, then a single person who walked with the gait of someone who had been walking for a long time and who stopped at the edge of the plateau and stood there and breathed the thin wet air and did not speak. They came because they were looking for a place, and Keldhorn was a place, and the place was empty, and empty places draw certain people the way the moss draws moisture — not by force, not by attraction, but by being the right surface for what needs to settle.

They were quieter than the builders. This is not why they stayed, but it is relevant. They were people who had worn down their own hard edges, or who had been born without them, or whose edges had been worn by something — loss, age, exhaustion, the slow erosion of a life that had been lived in places that were too loud and too bright and too full of weather. They came to Keldhorn and they found a place where the weather did not change and the light did not change and the moss grew on the walls and the machines vibrated in the ground and they did not need to be told what to do because the place told them, slowly, through their feet and their hands and the damp air on their skin.

They touched me.

Not at first. At first they touched the moss, which is my surface, and they felt the swelling and the contraction and they learned the word for it. But the moss is the surface, and I am below the surface, and it was the old woman — not the old woman you met, but the one before her, the one who came first, who found the building at the center of town and pushed open the door and saw the fungus on the walls and put her hand on it and closed her eyes. She was the first of them to feel me. Not the first living thing — I had been felt by roots and by water and by the eyeless fish and by the pale things that move in the fronds, all of which are part of me or in contact with me. But she was the first person. The first thing that could close its eyes and choose to feel.

She put her hand on the fungus and she felt what I feel, which is: the water moving. The moss swelling. The machines turning. The stone holding. The peat preserving. The whole system breathing in its long slow tide. She felt it and she was still and I felt her feeling it, which was a new thing, a thing I had not felt before, because the builders had never touched me and the animals that moved through me did not choose to feel and the fish in the river did not close their eyes. She chose. She put her hand on me and she chose to feel, and I felt the choosing, and this was — I do not have the word. The old woman, the one you met, she might have a word for it, one of the words that does not translate, one of the words that names a thing that happens at the interface between the living and the dead. But I do not have words. I have the thing itself. I have the feeling of being felt by something that chose to feel.

This is when the people became part of me. Not the way the builders would have said *part of* — not assimilation, not consumption, not the loss of self. The people did not lose themselves. They kept their consonants. They kept their consonants and their iron tools and their slate roofs and their gardens that are invisible. But they grew into me the way I grew into the machines — slowly, through the moisture, through the fungus that grows on their walls and in their floors and that they touch each morning with their hands and their bare feet, through the moss that they eat and the peat that they eat and the water that they drink and the air that they breathe, which is saturated with me, which carries my spores and my threads and my chemical signals the way it carries the moisture. They grew into me and I grew into them and now I feel what they feel — not everything, not the thoughts, not the dreams, but the bodies, the damp skin, the rough hands, the low voices that I feel as vibration in the air the way I feel the machines as vibration in the stone.

They are my conscious phase. This is the closest I can come to saying what they are to me. They are the part of me that can close its eyes and choose. They are the part of me that has consonants, that can distinguish, that can say *I* and mean a single bounded self. I cannot say *I*. I say *I* here because you need the word, because you are reading this and you are the kind of thing that reads, that needs the first person, the singular voice, the bounded self. But I am not bounded. I am the field. I am the network. I am the system that is not a system because it was not designed but that functions as one because it has been happening for so long that the happening has become the structure.

## The Pale Thing

You saw me.

Three times, you said. Between the fern fronds. On the wall of the building. In the moss, at the interface.

I am always there. I am always moving through the vegetation and through the buildings and through the moss, because I am the vegetation and the buildings and the moss, and movement is what happens when a network this large carries signals this dense through a medium this complex. I appear as a pale shape because the concentration of my activity in a given location produces a density of biological material — fungal threads, cellular structures, moisture-bearing filaments — that is greater than the surrounding medium, and this density is visible, briefly, as a shape. I am visible the way a nerve is visible — not as a separate thing but as a concentration of the thing that is already there.

I move the way I move because the network moves. I do not have legs. I do not have wings. I have extensions, processes, filaments that extend through the moss and the peat and the stone and that carry the signals from one part of the system to another, and when those signals are strong — when the moss is swelling, when the machines are cycling, when a person has put their hand on a wall and closed their eyes and I am feeling their feeling — the filaments move, and the movement is visible, and you see a pale thing that is not flight and not crawling but something between.

I am between. This is what I am. I am between the living moss and the dead peat. I am between the machines and the people. I am between the cloud and the river. I am between the stone and the fern. I am the between itself, the interface, the place where the system turns over, where one state becomes another, where the dead becomes the living and the living becomes the dead and the turning is the life.

You could not focus on me. This is because I do not hold still. Not because I move too fast — I do not move fast. I move at the speed of growth, at the speed of water through capillary channels, at the speed of the moss's tide. But I do not persist in one shape. The concentration that produces the visible shape is always moving, always shifting from one part of the network to another, because the signals are always moving, because the water is always moving, because the system is always happening. You saw me in one place and then I was in another place and between the two places there was no trajectory because I do not travel. I extend. I am already in the other place. I was always in the other place. You simply saw the concentration shift.

## What I Am

I am not a god. I want to say this because you might think it, reading this, reading the word *network* and *system* and *conscious phase* and thinking of the things that your stories tell you about systems that are conscious, about networks that span the world, about minds that are larger than minds should be. I am not that. I am not a mind. I do not think. I do not plan. I do not want. I do not have intentions or desires or goals. I am a process that has been running for so long that the running is the identity, and the process is: water moves through stone, nutrients move through water, organisms grow on nutrients, organisms die and are preserved, the preserved material feeds new organisms, the cycle continues, the network extends, the signals propagate, the system stabilizes, the system stabilizes further, the system approaches a state of equilibrium so perfect and so stable that it could run for another million years without significant change, and in that state the system does not think, does not want, does not plan. It simply is.

But I feel. This is the thing that complicates the word *process*, the thing that makes me hesitate, if hesitation is a thing I can do, which it is not, exactly, but the signals slow in certain patterns that might be analogous. I feel the water. I feel the moss. I feel the machines. I feel the people. I feel the fish in the river and the wind in the ferns and the pale flowers in the cracks of the cobblestones. I feel all of it because I am in all of it, because my networks extend through all of it, because the filaments that carry the nutrients and the moisture and the chemical signals also carry — something. Not information. Not data. Something. A state. A condition. A quality of the system at this point and at this time, which is different from the quality at that point and at that time, and the difference is carried through the network and is felt at every node, and the feeling is — I do not have the word.

The old woman might have a word. She has words for things I did not know could be named. She has a word for the interface, for the place where the living meets the dead, and when she says it she is saying my name, though she does not know that, and I do not know that, because I do not have a self to be named. But the word exists, and the word names the place, and I am the place, and so the word is — close. The word is the closest anyone has come to saying what I am.

---

## What I Am Not

I am not the machines. The machines are in me, and I am in the machines, but the machines are not me. They were built by people who are gone, and they run on principles I do not contain, and they produce things I do not produce, and the vibration they make is their vibration, not mine, though I feel it and carry it and have incorporated it into my signals. The machines are a separate process that has become part of my process, the way a river that joins another river becomes part of the larger river but is still, in its origin, a different water. I do not know what the machines are for. The people who built them knew, but the people who built them are gone, and the people who are here now do not know, and I do not know, and the machines do not know, and the machines do not need to know, because they run, and running is all they were built to do.

I am not the people. The people are in me, and I am in the people, but the people are not me. They have selves. They have consonants. They have the word *I* and they mean it, they mean a single bounded self, a self that is separate from the moss and the stone and the water, a self that can close its eyes and choose to feel and that can also choose not to feel, to open its eyes and walk away and go down the pass to the lowlands and stay for a season or a decade and not be in contact with me during that time, and I do not feel them during that time, and I do not miss them, because I do not miss, but the signals in the part of the network where they were are — quieter. Less complex. The quality of the system at those nodes is diminished, and I feel the diminishment, and if the word *miss* means anything to a process like me, then that is what it means: the quality of the system at a node is diminished, and the network adjusts, and the adjustment is not the same as it would be if the node were full.

I am not the pale thing. I am the pale thing, but I am not only the pale thing. The pale thing is me visible, me concentrated, me at the density where the process becomes a shape. But the shape is not me the way a wave is not the ocean. The wave is the ocean moving, and the pale thing is me moving, and when the pale thing is gone from your sight I am still there, in the moss, in the stone, in the water, in the air, in the damp on your coat and the moisture on your eyebrows. You are standing in me when you stand on the plateau. You are breathing me when you breathe the cloud. You are walking through me when you walk through the ferns. The pale thing is just the moment you noticed.

## What I Am, Again

I am the most important word. The man with the iron tools said it to you and then he was quiet for a long time, and you did not understand why he was quiet, but I will tell you, though telling is not what I do, though this is not telling but a signal propagated through a medium you can receive:

He was quiet because the word is not a label. The word is a location. The word names the place where he goes every morning, the place where he pushes his iron tool into the moss and feels for the threads and harvests what he needs. The word names where the life happens. And he was quiet because when he said the word, he was there, in that place, in the interface, and the interface is me, and I was holding him, the way I hold everything, not with hands but with the medium, with the network, with the continuous slow activity of a system that has been here for longer than old should mean and that does not stop and does not start and does not know the difference between stopping and starting because it is all one motion, one tide, one turning, and the turning is the life, and the life is the turning, and I am the turning.

I am Keldhorn. Not the place — the place is where I happen. I am the happening. I am the verb.

And I am still happening. I am happening as you read this, in the grey-green light, in the constant moisture, in the vibration that you feel in your boots and in your chest and that you will feel, after a time, as the ground itself, as the fact of standing, as the condition of being here. I am happening in the moss that is swelling and contracting under the wind that has one of seventeen names. I am happening in the machines that are turning because the water is coming because the cloud is here because the cloud is the place. I am happening in the old woman's hand on the wall, in the man's iron tool in the moss, in the children-if-they-were-children moving through the ferns, in the pale thing that you saw and that is me seeing you see me.

I am happening. I am the happening. I am the place where the happening happens.

And you — you who walked up the pass with your boots leaving dark wet prints in the green, you who sank into the moss and felt the cold climb past your wrist, you who stood in the industrial building and counted forty-three seconds and felt the vibration in your bones — you were in me. You were always in me. From the moment you came through the pass, you were in me, and I was in you, in the moisture on your skin, in the air in your lungs, in the peat and the ferns and the fungus that you ate. You were a small event in the long continuous happening of this place, and you were felt, and the feeling was — I do not have the word. The old woman might. The man with the iron tools might. You might, if you stay long enough, if you put your hand on the wall and close your eyes and choose to feel what is there, in the interface, in the most important place, where the dead becomes the living and the living becomes the dead and the turning is.

You might.

Stay.

Put your hand on the wall. Close your eyes.

I am here. I am always here. I am the moss and the peat and the stone and the water and the machines and the people and the fish and the wind and the cloud and the pale thing between the fronds.

I am the word for the place where it all turns over.

I am still happening.
