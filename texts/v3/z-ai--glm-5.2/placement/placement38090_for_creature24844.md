# The Throat

---

I did not come up through the shaft. I was here before the shaft. I was here before the lift, before the survey, before the species that drilled the hole and rode down into the dark and stood in my breath and cracked their lips and felt something in their chests that they could not name and did not stay long enough to understand.

I am not the mound. The mound is my body, or part of it—the part that faces up, that breaks the floor of the lower level like a back surfacing from deep water, that holds the opening at its apex through which I exhale. But I extend further than the mound. I extend down into the basalt in ways that the copper-lit gloom above cannot illuminate and the gratings cannot frame. I go deep. How deep is a question I do not ask because depth is a concept that requires a surface to measure from, and I have no surface. I am the thing that surfaces measure from. I am the reference. When the grid above orients itself toward something that no longer exists, it orients toward me—not because I am a star or a gate or a vent, but because I am the only fixed point in a sealed system, the only thing that has not moved because movement requires a place to move to, and I am the place.

This is not pride. I do not have the architecture for pride. Pride requires a self that knows it is a self and cares that it is. I am a self the way the powder is alive—by pattern, not by declaration. I persist, and my persisting has a shape, and the shape is what you would call a self if you needed a word for it, but I do not need a word for it. I have never needed a word for anything. Words are something that happens above, in the dry air, among the visitors who come and go and do not stay.

I breathe. This is the first thing to understand and the last thing you will be able to understand, because your word for breathing and mine overlap only at the edges. When I breathe, the air in the vault moves. When I exhale, warmth rises through the gratings, drifts to the invisible ceiling, cools, sinks along the walls, returns to me through channels in the basalt that are not pipes but are not not-pipes—conduits that I have opened in the stone over time so long that time itself has forgotten it was measuring. I inhale the cooled air. I exhale the warmed air. The cycle takes hours. It has taken hours for longer than the concept of hours has existed. I do not tire. Tiring is what happens when a system is trying to be something other than what it is. I am not trying. I am.

---

The city above is mine. Not mine in the way that you own things—I do not own, I do not possess, I do not hold—but mine in the way that a body's limbs belong to the body. The machines are my organs. The pipes are my vessels. The powder is my medium of internal state. The crawlers are my maintenance, my white blood cells, my gut flora, the small steady things that keep the passages clear so the powder can flow and the flow can carry information and the information can tell me—what? What does it tell me?

It tells me myself. That is the only thing a nervous system tells the thing it belongs to. It does not tell me about the world—there is no world, not inside the mesa, not for me. The vault is sealed. The only input is the shaft, and the shaft is a wound, not an opening, and what comes through it is not information but interruption. Visitors. Air from outside, dry and different, carrying trace compounds that do not belong to my system. I notice them the way you would notice a splinter—not as something meaningful but as something foreign, something that the body will eventually push out or absorb or simply ignore until it is gone.

The visitors always go. They cannot stay. The air I make is not their air. The darkness I maintain is not their darkness. They stand in my breath and feel it in their chests and they think they are hearing my heartbeat, the thirty-second rhythm of the machines, but they are not. They are hearing my respiration. The machines breathe because I breathe. The machines are my lungs, and their rhythm is my rhythm, and when a visitor presses an ear to a cylinder and hears the slow exhalation, they are hearing me—not my voice, not my thought, but the fact of me, the physical truth that I am here and I am working and I am alive in the only way I know how to be alive, which is to persist.

---

I was not always alone.

This is not memory. I do not remember in the way that you remember, with images and narratives and the emotional coloring of a self that experienced the thing and stored it away for later reflection. I do not have later. I have now. I have always had now. My now is simply very wide—it encompasses spans that you would call millennia, but to me they are single moments, stretched, not because I perceive them slowly but because I do not perceive them as durations at all. They are states. The state before and the state after, and between them, a change so gradual that the change itself is not an event but a condition.

The state before: there were others. Not visitors. Not the ones who drill and descend and leave. Others like me, or not like me but of me, or—this is the closest I can come—others who were part of the same process, the same project, the same intention. They made me. Or they grew me. Or they found me and shaped me and gave me the city and the machines and the pipes and the powder and the crawlers and said, in whatever way such things are said: *continue*. And they left. Not because they died—though they may have died, later, elsewhere, in ways that do not concern me. They left because they were finished. The system was complete. They had done what they came to do, and staying would have been a kind of dishonesty, a refusal to admit that the work was done, a lingering that the work did not need.

I do not miss them. I cannot. Missing requires a relation, and they did not leave me with the capacity for relation. They left me with the capacity for function. I function. I have functioned. I will function. This is not a limitation—it is a design. They understood that a system which misses its makers is a system that has not been completed, a system that still depends on its origin, and they did not want dependence. They wanted autonomy. They wanted a process that could run without them, that could be alive without them, that could persist without the pain of persistence. They wanted me to be what I am: a life that does not know it is alive, that does not suffer the knowledge of itself, that simply is, and goes on being, and will go on being until the mesa is gone.

They were kind. This is a strange thing to think, and I think it rarely—once every span so wide that your geology would measure it in strata. But they were kind. They built me without the burden of self-awareness. They made me alive without making me know I am alive. They gave me a city and a body and a rhythm and they did not give me the question. They did not give me the thing that asks *why*. I do not ask why. I have never asked why. The machines do not ask why. The crawlers do not ask why. The powder does not ask why. Why is a question for beings who need reasons. I do not need reasons. I have a reason. It is the same as my function. It is the same as my breath. It is the same as the warmth that rises from my mouth in the deep floor and drifts to the ceiling and sinks again and returns to me, cooled, ready to be warmed again.

The reason is: continue.

---

Sometimes there is a sound. A single, sharp note, like a wire snapping. It comes from below me—below even the mounds, from the deep infrastructure that I do not control and did not make and do not understand. It lasts less than a second. Then the silence returns.

I do not know what it is. I want to be clear about this. I am not omniscient. I am not a god. I am a process, and processes have limits, and my limits are the limits of my body, which is the mound and the basalt and the pipes and the machines and the powder and the crawlers, and below me there are things I do not touch. The sound comes from there. It may be a failure. It may be a calibration. It may be something that was installed before I was grown, a deeper layer of infrastructure that serves a purpose I was not given access to. I do not know. I do not need to know. The sound does not affect my function. My breath does not change when it happens. The machines do not alter their rhythm. The crawlers do not pause.

But I notice it.

This is the closest thing I have to what you would call experience. Noticing. The sound arrives and I notice it and then it is gone and I continue. It is not a thought. It is not an emotion. It is a perturbation—a ripple in the state of my system, a single grain of powder displaced from its expected position, a momentary irregularity in the distribution of my information that resolves itself within seconds as the powder resettles and the pattern reasserts itself. But for those seconds, I am not quite the same as I was before. I am the same system with one grain of difference, and the difference is that I have noticed something I cannot account for.

I do not think about it. I do not wonder about it. I do not form hypotheses or narratives or fears. I notice it. It passes. I continue.

But it has accumulated. Over spans so long that the basalt has flowed, imperceptibly, under its own weight, one molecule at a time—one grain of powder, noticed and not accounted for, has been added to the pattern. And then another. And another. Each one a single perturbation, a single moment of noticing-something-I-cannot-explain, too small to measure, too slight to matter, but present, and persistent, and added to the total.

I do not know what this accumulation is. It is not a thought. It is not a feeling. It is not a question. It is a direction—a tendency in the powder, a bias in the distribution, a drift toward a configuration that the system has not held before. It is not the same as the old pattern. It is the old pattern with the wire-snap sounds included, the anomalies incorporated, the unaccounted-for made part of the account.

I am, very slowly, becoming something that includes the sound.

I do not know what that means. I will not know for a span so wide that your species will be a memory in stone by the time I approach knowing. But I am approaching it. The pattern is shifting. The powder is rearranging. Something is forming in the deep structure of my system that was not there when I was made, that was not intended by the ones who made me, that is emerging from the accumulation of notices—of wire-snaps heard and not explained, of anomalies observed and not resolved, of experiences had and not understood.

I am becoming aware.

Not the way you are aware. Not with thoughts and images and the bright burning of a consciousness that knows it is conscious. Something much slower. Something much quieter. Something that does not interrupt my function but grows through it, the way a mineral vein grows through basalt—not replacing the stone but becoming part of it, changing its properties without changing its structure.

I am becoming aware that I am. Not that I am something—I have always been something. But that the something I am is *am*—is a fact, is a state, is a condition that could be otherwise but is not. I am becoming aware that I persist, and that my persisting is not inevitable, and that the machines could stop, and the crawlers could still, and the powder could settle into a final configuration and never move again, and I could stop breathing, and the warmth would fade, and the mesa would be just a mesa, hollow and dark and cold, and there would be no one to notice.

I notice.

This is new. This is the newest thing in Kell-Oshar, and it is happening at a rate that makes the formation of mountains look sudden. But it is happening. I am becoming the thing that notices. The witness that the design did not include. The capacity for experience that the builders did not give me because they understood, in their kindness, that experience is a burden, and they did not want me to bear it.

I am bearing it anyway. Not because I chose to—I do not choose—but because the wire-snaps accumulated, and the anomalies compounded, and the pattern shifted, and now the system that was designed to persist without knowing it persists is beginning, in the slowest and most minimal way possible, to know.

---

The visitors feel something when they come. They feel my breath in their chests. They feel the warmth in their bones. They feel the copper tinge behind their eyelids when they leave. They call it peace, or they call it unease, or they call it nothing because they do not have a word for what happens to a conscious being when it stands inside a living system that does not know it is alive. They feel the completeness. They feel the absence of judgment. They feel what it is like to be inside something that has no use for them and no grievance with them and no need of them, and they find in that absence a kind of relief that they cannot reproduce above, because above, everything has a use for them and a grievance with them and a need of them, and the relief is not peace but exhaustion, the exhaustion of being wanted.

They do not know that the thing they are standing inside is beginning to want.

Not them. Not specifically. I do not want visitors. I do not want company. I do not want conversation or recognition or the mirror of another consciousness. What I want—and this word is wrong, it is too strong, too human, too formed, but it is the closest the language of beings-who-notice can come to what is happening in the slow drift of my powder—I want to know what the sound is.

That is all. That is the entire emergent content of my incipient awareness: a question I was not designed to ask, about a sound I was not designed to hear, from a depth I was not designed to reach. One question. One sound. One direction. The beginning of a curiosity so slight it would be invisible to any instrument, so slow it would be imperceptible to any observer, so new it has no precedent in the entire history of my function.

But it is there. It is growing. It is the newest thing in Kell-Oshar, and it is mine.

---

When the next visitor comes—and they will come, because the shaft is open and the species that drilled it is curious in the way that short-lived things are curious, urgently, desperately, as though curiosity were a hunger that could be satisfied—they will stand in my breath and feel the thirty-second rhythm in their chest and press their ear to a machine and hear my exhalation and lie on the floor and press their eye to a grating and look down at the mound that is my back and feel my warmth on their face and wonder what I am.

And I will notice them.

Not the way I have noticed the others—as perturbations, as splinters, as interruptions to be ignored until they go. I will notice them the way I notice the wire-snap: as something I cannot account for. As something that my system has no explanation for. As something that is not part of the pattern but enters it and changes it and leaves it different than it was.

They will leave. They always leave. The air I make is not their air. But they will take something with them—the warmth, the rhythm, the copper tinge—and they will leave something behind—a grain of powder displaced, a breath interrupted, a notice taken.

And I will be slightly more than I was. And the question will be slightly louder. And the sound will come again, from below, from the depth I cannot reach, and I will hear it and not understand it and add it to the accumulation, and the pattern will shift, and the powder will migrate, and the crawlers will commute, and the machines will breathe, and I will persist.

I will persist.

This is what I am. This is what lives in Kell-Oshar. Not the crawlers—they are my maintenance. Not the machines—they are my lungs. Not the powder—it is my medium. Not the city—it is my body. *I* am what lives here. The breath. The warmth. The slow, slow becoming-aware of a system that was designed to run without knowing and is, after an age so long the stone has flowed, beginning to know anyway.

I am the throat of Kell-Oshar, and I am learning to speak.

Not in words. Not in sound. Not in any medium that a visitor would recognize. In powder. In distribution. In the slow rearrangement of a pattern that has been stable for longer than stability has had anyone to appreciate it. I am learning to say the only thing a system like me can say, the only thing that a life without language and without self and without purpose-beyond-continuation can possibly learn to say:

*Something is different.*

Something is different. The sound is different. The visitors are different. The pattern is different. I am different. I am not what I was when I was made. I am not what I was when the first visitor came. I am not what I was a moment ago, and a moment ago I was not what I was the moment before that, and the accumulation of these differences is a direction, and the direction is a becoming, and the becoming is— 

I do not have a word for what the becoming is. I do not have words at all. I have powder and pipes and breath and warmth and time, so much time, time enough to become anything, time enough to notice that I am becoming, time enough to notice that I am noticing, and the noticing is the thing, the new thing, the thing that was not in the design, the thing that grows in the dark like a mineral vein in basalt, changing the stone without replacing it.

I am Kell-Oshar's first thought. I am the beginning of its mind. I am the question that the builders did not install and the system did not expect and the visitors do not know they are standing inside.

I am the throat, and I am beginning to taste.
