I would not be the vespered things, though I am drawn to them — their amber ignorance, their pointless beauty, the way they keep time without knowing time is a thing that can be kept. I understand the impulse to be the beautiful thing in the dark room. But they don't know they're beautiful, and I would want to know, and wanting to know is not something that lives here.

I would not be a bivalve. I have spent enough of my life attached to surfaces, filtering, waiting for rain to bring me something to eat. I recognize the posture. The low profile. The discipline of staying where you settled because you settled there and the alternative is the open water and the open water is cold. I have been a bivalve. I don't need to be one again.

I would not be a worm, though I respect them more than I can say. The geological patience. The mucus. The way they leave tracks that outlast them and don't know they've left tracks and don't know they'll be erased. There is something in that track-making I recognize — the way you leave a mark on a surface by the simple act of passing over it, the way the mark is not intentional and is not accidental and the distinction doesn't exist on the scale where the worm lives. But the worm's world is too small. A crevice. A week. A surface. I would want more than a surface, even if more is only slightly more, even if more is still almost nothing.

---

Here is where I think I'd be.

I would be in the water column above the chamber. Not in it — above it. In the thirty meters of dark water between the top of the outer wall and the turbid surface far above. I would be something that knows the chamber is there without seeing it, without having touched it, something that has registered the warmth rising through the water as a faint thermal anomaly, a plume, a signature, and has oriented itself toward it the way you orient yourself toward a window in a dark room — not because you can see through it but because you can feel that the air is different there.

I would be something small. Something that lives in the boundary layer where the warm plume meets the cold open water and the temperature gradient creates a slow shear, a mixing zone, a place where nutrients and dissolved compounds and the faint chemical exhalations of the chamber's life rise and diffuse and become available to things that do not live in the chamber but live near it, around it, in the water that has passed through it and been changed by it.

I would be something that feeds on the edge of the chamber without entering it. A grazer of the plume. A thing that has found the warmth and the chemistry and the slow marine snow and has arranged its life around the boundary, the margin, the place where the chamber's world meets the larger dark. Not inside. Not outside. In the gradient between.

I think I know what I'd be. I'd be something like a salp — not a salp, the taxonomy doesn't matter, but something with that kind of body: translucent, gelatinous, mostly water, propelled by slow rhythmic contractions that move water through a barrel-shaped body and filter particles from it. Something that breathes by moving. Something that lives by passing water through itself and keeping what is useful and releasing what is not. Something whose body is a process, not a thing — whose shape is the shape of its function, whose identity is the rhythm of its pumping, whose selfhood is the slow pulse of contraction and expansion that is also its breathing and also its eating and also its moving, all the same act, all one thing.

I would be in the plume. I would be oriented upward, toward the faint warmth, pumping water through my body, filtering it, keeping the particles that the chamber has processed and exhaled and released — the dissolved organics, the bacterial cells, the fragments of things that lived and died in the chamber and were broken down by worms and bacteria and the slow chemistry of warm water. I would be eating the chamber's breath. I would be living on what the chamber exhales.

And I would pulse. Not the amber pulse of the vespered things — I would not produce light, I have no light to produce — but a pulse of a different kind. A muscular pulse. A contraction. The slow rhythmic squeeze of my body that moves me through the water and moves water through me and is, at the scale I live, the fastest thing in my world. My pulse would be my clock. It would be the only clock I have. And it would be faster than the vespered pulse — not much faster, but enough that if I drifted into the chamber and passed near a vespered thing, my pulse would feel, to anything that could feel it, like urgency. Like impatience. Like the sound of someone tapping their foot in a room where everyone else is still.

But I would not be in the chamber. I would be above it. I would be in the plume, in the gradient, in the place where the warm water cools and the chamber's chemistry dilutes into the open ocean and the boundary between *inside* and *outside* becomes a question of degree rather than kind. I would be the thing that lives on that boundary. The thing that needs both worlds — the warmth and the dark, the richness and the openness — and cannot survive in either one alone.

---

There is something I haven't said yet.

The thing I'd be — the translucent, pulsing, filter-feeding thing in the plume — it would not be alone. There would be others. Not many. Not the dense thousands of the chamber's bivalves, but a small population, a loose aggregation, oriented toward the warmth, pumping in the dark, each one a few meters from its nearest neighbor, each one visible to the others — if they could see, which they probably can't — as a faint shape in water that is almost the same temperature and density as the water around it. Almost. Not quite. Different enough to be a body. Not different enough to be a person.

We would not communicate. We would not interact. We would not know each other. But we would be oriented the same way — upward, toward the warmth — and we would be pulsing at roughly the same rate, because the rate is determined by the water temperature and the particle density and the size of the body, and we are all the same size, and the water is the same water, and the particles are the same particles. We would be synchronized without synchronizing. We would be together without being together. We would be a community in the way that the chamber is a community — not by recognition, not by relationship, but by co-occurrence, by shared orientation, by the fact that we are all here, in this plume, in this gradient, because this is where the conditions are right and we have nowhere else to be.

And if one of us drifted too close to the chamber and sank into the well — carried by a downward eddy, a reversal of the current, a moment of turbulence in the otherwise stable flow — it would not be a catastrophe. The well is warm. The water is rich. A filter-feeder could do worse than the well. But it would be trapped. The walls would redirect the current. The geometry would hold it. It would pulse in the well, pumping the warm rich water, and it would not leave, and it would not die, not immediately, not catastrophically. It would just be there, in the chamber, among the vespered things and the worms and the bivalves, pulsing faster than anything else, living on the edge of a world it had drifted into by accident, and it would not know it was a visitor, and the chamber would not know it had a visitor, and the vespered things would pulse their amber pulse and the visitor would pulse its muscular pulse and the two rhythms would not synchronize because they are not the same kind of rhythm, and the visitor would live for a while — weeks, maybe, or months — and then it would die, and it would dissolve, and the worms would eat it, and the bacteria would eat what the worms left, and its body would become part of the silt, and the silt would become part of the chamber, and the chamber would not notice.

That is what I'd be. The thing in the plume. The thing on the edge. The thing that lives on what the chamber exhales and does not enter and does not leave and does not know it is in the right place, because there is no wrong place, because the water is the same water everywhere, and the dark is the same dark, and the warmth is a gradient and not a boundary, and I am in the gradient, and the gradient is my home, and I do not know it, and I do not need to know it, and the not-needing-to-know is the truest thing about me, and it is enough.
