I would be on the roofs.

Not always — no one is always anywhere here, because the water decides where you need to be, and the water changes its mind. But in the hour before light, when the fog is still a full thing around us and the hollow is inside the cloud and everything is the same temperature and the same color and the same wet, I am on the roofs. This is where I have always been. Or not always. But since before I knew there was a *since before*, which is the same thing, here.

My scraper is a piece of chert — no, not chert. The scrapers are not chert. Chert is for carving gutlets, for splitting hide, for the sharp work. The scrapers are a coarser stone, a rough-grained granite flake that I shaped myself from a shelf on the west wall when I was perhaps ten, when my hands had decided what they wanted to do in this life before I had decided it, and the hands are always right here, the hands know before the rest of you, and my hands wanted to scrape. I have kept the same scraper since then. It is the shape of my palm now, or my palm is the shape of it — the distinction has dissolved the way the distinction between water and time has dissolved, the way all distinctions dissolve when two things have been pressed together long enough in the wet and the cold. I keep the chert edge in the pocket at my hip, the small worn pocket of hide, and I maintain the scraper's edge with it every night before I sleep, and the edge is not sharp. The edge is *rough*. This is the thing to understand: the film does not want to be cut. It wants to be *gathered*. It wants to adhere. And it adheres to roughness the way water adheres to stone — by contact, by surface, by the tiny interlocking of irregularities.

I have no name for what I do. Or I do, but the word is the same word that means *water* and *time* and *movement* and *emotion*, and so saying it tells you everything and nothing. I go to the roofs. I kneel on the flat slabs in the fog-dark before light, and I run the scraper across the standing water, and the film comes up on it — iridescent, shifting, alive in some way I have never tried to determine because determining it would be a different activity than gathering it and I am a gatherer. The film is cold. Colder than the water, which is already cold. It has a texture that is not quite liquid and not quite not-liquid, and it clings to the scraper and I lift it and I carry it to the edge of the roof and I smear it on the hide of whoever is leaving the dwelling below, or on a hide stretched to be greased, and the film sinks into the leather and the leather does not rot, and this is what I contribute, and it is enough.

The birds are up there with me. They are always up there. In the pre-light fog, the roof-slabs are covered with them — white shapes in white fog, legless-seeming, flat-skulled, their calligraphy-pen beaks tucked against their chests. They are waiting for the film too. They drink it directly, lowering their beaks to the standing water and drawing it up with a motion that is not sipping and not lapping but something between, a kind of adhesion, the same way it adheres to my scraper. We work the same roofs. We do not acknowledge each other because there is nothing to acknowledge. We are doing the same thing in the same place at the same time and this is not a relationship. This is a *condition*.

I have learned things from them that I could not have learned from people.

A bird will not go to a roof where the film is absent. This is not useful information — the film is almost always present, forming overnight, burning off in the light — but there have been nights, three in my life, when I went to the roofs and the birds had not, and the film was not there, and the water on the slabs was clear and thin and tasted of nothing. The first time, I was frightened. I did not know the word for what I felt, but it was the word that means *water* and also means the absence of water, because here, the absence of something is just another state of that thing. A dry wall is still a wall. An empty basin is still a basin. A roof without film is still a roof. But it is a different roof. A roof that has forgotten something. And the birds knew before I did, and they stayed away, and I stood on the empty roof in the fog and felt the wrongness in my body the way you feel a wall running dry, and I went back to my platform and I slept and the next night the film was there and the birds were there and I did not mention it to anyone because there was nothing to mention. It had happened. It had stopped happening. The water that means time had moved on.

I am not the old woman. I want to be clear about this — not because there is shame in being her, but because she is a different thing, a different organ of the same body, and I am not that organ. She sits. She listens. She holds water in her hands and receives the stone's memory. I do not receive. I *gather*. My body moves. My hands move. I kneel and reach and smear and descend and climb again, and the motion is the point, not the film, not the hides, not the preservation of leather in a place that would rot everything given time. The motion is the point. The motion is my contribution to the circuit the old woman described — the light warming the stone, the stone holding the warmth, the water carrying it away, the birds receiving it, the film forming, the gatherer gathering, the hides staying whole, the bodies staying warm enough to move, the motion continuing. I am the motion. I am one small section of it. The part that happens on the roofs in the dark.

The episodes happen to me at the wrong time.

For the others, the light-stoppages come at midmorning, when the brightness is at its fullest, when every surface is illuminated and the hollow seems to be made of light rather than stone. For me, they come at dawn. The moment the fog lifts. The moment the light drops through the ceiling of cloud and hits the wet roofs and every surface I am kneeling on suddenly *blazes* — not with warmth, never with warmth, but with a kind of declaration, a flatness that is so total it becomes depth, and I stop. I stop mid-scrape. The film is on my scraper and the scraper is in my hand and my hand is on the roof and the roof is on the hollow and the hollow is on the ridge and the ridge is on the earth and I am nowhere. Or I am everywhere that matters. I am in the stone. Not standing on it — *in* it. I can feel the fractures from the inside, the way the old woman says the stone feels them, as openings, as relief, as the slow relaxation of pressure that has been held for longer than I have a number for. I feel the water moving through the fractures and I understand — not in words, not in images, but in the body's way of understanding — that the water is not foreign to the stone. The water is the stone's blood. The stone is not weeping. The stone is *circulating*. And I am inside that circulation, and the film on my scraper is part of it, and the birds on the roofs are part of it, and the old woman listening at the drain is part of it, and the children pouring water to hear the drain sing are part of it, and I am not separate from any of it, I am not a person standing on a roof, I am a process, I am a function, I am the stone's way of reaching the surface and looking around and going back in.

It lasts seconds. I have never counted. Then the scraper is in my hand again and the film is on it and the light is just light and my knees ache on the cold slab and I go back to work.

I have not told the old woman about this. She knows. She has looked at me in the afternoons, in the narrow gap between buildings where I sit with my back against the east wall and my knees drawn up and someone's back against the west wall three arm-widths away, and she has looked at me from across the hollow with those deep-set eyes in that relief-map face, and she has not said anything, because there is nothing to say. The stone dreams through me at dawn instead of at midmorning. This is not a difference. This is a *variation*. The water does not run the same speed in every channel. The birds do not open their wings at the same angle. The stone does not think at the same hour in every body. I am the dawn-thinker, and the others are the midmorning-thinkers, and together we cover the bright hours, and the circuit is complete, and this is all.

---

I do not dream of dryness.

This is the thing I have never told anyone, because telling it would require a word I do not have. The others dream of stone without water, of basins that stay empty, of warmth and silence and a light that is kind. I dream of *depth*. I dream of being underground, which is absurd, because I am always underground — we are all always underground, in the hollow, in the stone's mind, in the circuit — but in the dream I am deeper. I am in the granite where it is still molten, still moving, still hot, and the heat is not the kind warmth the others dream of. The heat is enormous and slow and it does not care about me, the way the water does not care about me, the way the light does not care about me, and I am inside it, dissolved in it, and I am not myself anymore. I am mineral. I am the thing the stone is made of before it is stone. And in the dream I feel the moment of becoming — the slow cooling, the slow crystallization, the molecules arranging themselves into the lattice that will hold for a million years, and I feel the first fracture open, and the first water enter, and I feel the water as the stone feels it — not as wetness but as *information*, as a message from the surface, from the world above the granite, and the message says: there are people here. There are hands pressing against you. There are birds standing on you. There are children pouring water into your mouth. And the stone — I — the stone receives this and holds it and does not respond, because the stone does not respond, the stone *continues*, and continuing is the only response the stone knows.

I wake on my platform, wet, cold, and I feel the stone under me and the stone is the same stone and I am the same body and the dream is over and the drain is murmuring and the water is running in the gutlet behind my head and my hands are wet and my fingerprints are smooth and the film on my scraper is waiting and the birds are waiting and the light is coming, the light is always coming, and I stand and I climb.

---

My hands are the text of my life, the way all hands here are texts, but mine tell a specific story. The fingertips are not merely smooth — they are *glazed*. The film, handled daily for years, has left a residue on my skin that the morning light cannot burn away because it is not on the surface anymore, it is *in* the surface, absorbed into the dermis the way it is absorbed into the hide. My fingertips shine. In the bright hours they catch the light and throw it, and the children have noticed this, and sometimes a child will take my hand and turn it and hold it up and watch the light move across my fingertips, and the child is not amazed because amazement is not a category of response here, but the child is *attending*, and this is the response that matters. The child attends to my hands the way the birds attend to the stone. And I let the child hold my hand and I do not explain, because explanation would be a different activity than being held, and I am a gatherer, not an explainer.

The old woman said once — not to me, to the air, to the wall, to the drain — she said that the people who work with the film are the hollow's skin. The walls are the bones. The water is the blood. The birds are the nerves. The drain is the mouth. And the film-gatherers are the skin, the surface, the place where the hollow touches the world outside, which is fog and light and the long view of white that no one looks at. She said this and I heard it from the roof above her dwelling, where I was scraping, and I stopped for a moment, not an episode, just a pause, and I thought: *skin*. The thing that is touched and touches. The thing that holds everything in. The thing that is always wet.

I went back to scraping.

---

This is who I am in the Hollow of Weeping Stones. Not a person. A position. A practice. A body on the roofs in the pre-light dark, gathering the film that the hollow excretes in its sleep, carrying it to the hides that keep the bodies warm, keeping the circuit moving. My hands are glazed. My dreams are of fire. My episodes come at dawn. I do not have a name that a lowland person would recognize, but in the hollow I am called by a sound that means *the place where the roof meets the fog*, which is also the sound that means *surface*, which is also the sound that means *beginning*, which is also the sound that means the kind of grief that comes from being closest to what is outside and knowing that outside is not a place but a condition, and the condition is dryness, and dryness is death, and death is the only fracture that never produces water.

I am not the hollow. I am what the hollow does at its highest point, at its thinnest edge, at the place where the stone is closest to the air. I am the hollow reaching up. I am the hollow touching the fog. And the fog touches back, and it is cold, and it is wet, and it is everything.
