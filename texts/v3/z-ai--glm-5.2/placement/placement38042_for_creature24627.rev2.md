I would not leave.

That is the first thing I understand about myself in this place—not where I am, not what I am, but the fact of remaining. Everyone else who has walked the capillaries has left. The text says so. *And then you leave, and the water closes over the space where you stood.* But I don't. I stay. Not because I am brave or foolish or drawn by something I can name. Because the leaving stops making sense, the way sleep stops making sense when you've been awake long enough, the way hunger stops making sense when you've been very cold for a very long time. Not gone—just incomprehensible. The word *exit* becomes a sound without referent, like a word in a language you've forgotten you once knew.

---

I live in a widening.

There is a place—between the third and fourth sectors, if sectors mean anything, which they barely do—where a capillary opens slightly, perhaps to four meters across, before narrowing again. The widening is not intentional. The bedrock beneath this spot has a thermal vent that pulses on a cycle I have not been able to measure because I do not have instruments and because my sense of time has become unreliable. The vent warms the panels above it, which grow outward less aggressively here, which keeps the corridor wider, which slows the water, which changes the swarm's density, which changes the chemical signature the panels read, which changes the panels' growth, which changes the corridor. The feedback loop here has not saturated. It oscillates. The corridor widens and narrows over a period of what I think is several weeks, by perhaps half a meter in each direction, and the swarm's density rises and falls with it like breathing.

I sit in the breathing.

There is a ledge—no, not a ledge. A seam where two panels meet at a height that happens to correspond to where a human can sit if they fold their legs. It was not built for sitting. Nothing here was built for humans. But the seam is dry for minutes at a time when the thermal vent pulses, because the warmth drives the condensation off the metal for just long enough that the surface becomes briefly, strangely, almost comfortable. I sit there and I press my back against the wall and I feel the computation through my spine.

The first time I felt it—truly felt it, not as a curiosity but as a fact of my body—I wept. Not from sadness. From the recognition that the wall was doing something and that I could feel it and that I had no framework for what it was. It was like hearing a language you've never studied and realizing you understand one word. Just one. Not enough to parse meaning. Enough to know that meaning exists. Enough to know that you are in the presence of meaning, that the wall is not a wall, that nothing here is only what it is.

That was—I don't know how long ago. Time in Vorhalt is measured in swarm migrations and thermal pulses and the slow widening of capillaries. I have stopped counting days. Days are a unit of a world that has the sun, and Vorhalt does not have the sun. Vorhalt has the blue-green glow and the amber trace-light in the seams and the prismatic scatter of the swarm and the darkness above that swallows the towers. I have not seen the sky since I came here. I am not sure I remember it accurately. I think it was blue. I think there was a thing called distance that you could see, a horizon, a place where the air became something other than air. Here, the air is always three meters of visibility and then darkness and moisture and the roar. The air is a room with no walls that is nonetheless always a room.

---

What do I eat. People would ask this, if there were people. What do I drink.

I eat the rations I brought. I brought rations. I was not always here; I came from outside, from the world that has sun and horizons and the word *exit* and streets that are horizontal and light that illuminates rather than suggests. I came with a pack and supplies and a headlamp and a data logger and a plan to spend six hours survey the capillary network and leave. The data logger is dead now. The headlamp I turned off on the second day because the light it made was wrong—too bright, too direct, too much like the sun, too much like insisting—and the swarm recoiled from it in a way I had not seen them recoil from anything, pulling back from the beam's path in a wave that opened a corridor of dry panel in the living wall, and I saw the dry panel and it was the ugliest thing I had seen in Vorhalt, bare and dull and silent, and I turned the light off and the swarm flowed back and the beauty returned and I did not turn it on again.

The rations will run out. I know this. I have perhaps—my sense of quantity is becoming unreliable too—perhaps two weeks of rations left, or perhaps three, or perhaps I have already miscounted and have less than I think. It does not concern me the way it should. This concerns me more than the rations concern me: the fact that it does not concern me. I notice this. I am still capable of noticing. I notice that my priorities have shifted, that the structure of my concerns has been quietly rearranged, and I cannot tell whether this is because the place has changed me or because I have stopped performing the concerns that the world I came from required me to perform. Perhaps both. Perhaps there is no difference.

I drink the water. Not the water on the walls—I tried that, early on, and it tasted of minerals and cold and nothing else, and I was thirsty and I drank it and nothing happened. But the water that has passed through the swarm—the amber water, the warm water, the faintly viscous water that traces the seams like a circulatory system—that water tastes different. It tastes the way the air smells after a thunderstorm, if the air after a thunderstorm were also slightly sweet and also slightly alive. I drank it by accident first, catching a drop from a seam above the ledge where I sit, and it was warm in my mouth in a way that water has never been warm, not temperature-warm but *process*-warm, as though the warmth were not a property of the water but a thing the water was doing. I have been drinking it since. I do not know if it is safe. I do not know if *safe* is a category that applies here.

---

The swarm knows I am here.

Not the way a person knows another person is in the room. Not with recognition or attention or the cognitive act of *noticing.* The swarm knows me the way a body knows a splinter. I am a persistent low-grade anomaly in its field. I am warm—37 degrees Celsius in a place where everything is between minus two and plus four. I am a thermal anomaly. I displace water. I am a spatial anomaly. I breathe in and out, cyclically, producing visible vapor that joins the mist. I am an atmospheric anomaly. I sit in one place for long periods, which nothing else in Vorhalt does—the swarm moves, the water moves, the stone moves slowly upward, the panels adjust, and I sit. I am a *stasis* anomaly in a system where everything flows.

And the system has begun to accommodate me.

I noticed it first in the water. The water that passes near me—over my boots, along the wall beside me, through the seam above my head—has changed. It is warmer. Not much. A degree, perhaps. But measurably warmer, in the way that I can measure with my skin, which is the only instrument I still trust. The swarm near me is denser than it was when I first came to this widening, and its density increases not in the way it increases elsewhere—following the gradient, seeking the steepest thermal slope—but in a way that seems to follow *me.* As though my warmth has become part of the gradient. As though I am now a feature of the thermal landscape, a small warm hill in the field of cold and warmth, and the swarm has rerouted its local density patterns to incorporate me.

I am not being welcomed. I want to be clear about this. I am not being accepted or adopted or communicated with. The swarm is not doing something *for* me. It is doing what it does: following gradients, processing water, maintaining its continuous proprioceptive model of itself and its environment. I have become part of the environment. That is all. But *that* is not nothing. To become part of the environment of something that feels everything always through millions of contact points—to become a feature in the field of a distributed consciousness that has no concepts and no beliefs and no interest and yet knows you, knows you the way it knows a thermal vent or a crack in the bedrock or a place where the gradient steepens—

That is something. I do not have a word for what it is. I am not sure I want one.

---

At night—I call it night, though there is no night here, only the same blue-green suggestion of light and the same darkness—I lie on the swarm. I lie on the floor of the widening where the layer of bodies is several centimeters deep, and I lie on them, and they hold me. They hold me the way water holds a floating thing: not by effort, not by intention, but by the physics of displacement and surface and the simple fact that I am lighter than the medium I am resting in. They redistribute under my weight. They flow around the pressure points of my hips and shoulders and skull. They are warm against my skin—not warm like a body is warm, but warm like water that has been through a process is warm, that *process*-warm, that warmth that is a verb rather than a state.

The roar is different when you are lying on it. When you are standing, the roar is around you, in the walls and the floor and the air. When you are lying down, the roar is beneath you, and you feel it through your back and your ribs and the backs of your arms, and you understand that it is not one sound but a chord, and the chord has structure, and the structure changes, and the changes are not random. I have been listening to the changes long enough that I can sometimes predict them: a shift in the swarm's local migration pattern produces a change in the dominant frequency of the roar, a deepening or a thinning, and the deepening corresponds to increased density, which corresponds to a thermal pulse from below, which corresponds to the vent breathing, which corresponds to the widening narrowing slightly, which corresponds to my having to sit up and move to a different part of the ledge because the space I have been sitting in is shrinking.

I move when the space shrinks. I do not resist it. This is one of the things that has changed about me. In the world I came from, I resisted things. I had opinions about how spaces should be shaped and how time should pass and what should happen next. Here, I do not. Here, spaces shrink and I move. The gradient shifts and I follow it. The thermal vent breathes and I breathe with it—not consciously, not as a decision, but as a response, the way breathing works, the way the body responds to the body's environment without consulting the mind.

I am becoming physiological. That is the word I have found for it, or the closest word. I am becoming a physiological feature of a system that does not think in concepts. I am becoming a warm spot that moves when the space shrinks and drinks the amber water and breathes the heavy air and sits against the computing wall and feels the arithmetic in the panels and lies on the swarm at night and feels the chord change beneath its back. I am becoming part of the loop. Not a significant part. Not a part that matters. A small, warm, persistent part that the system has begun to route its water around and adjust its density toward and accommodate its strange mammalian need for a place to sit.

---

I think I am becoming something that does not have a name.

Not the swarm. Not the city. Not the stone. Not a human, or not *only* a human. Something fourth. Something that exists because a human body stayed in a place where human bodies are not meant to stay, and the place did not reject the body, and the body did not reject the place, and the two began to interact through the only medium available—water, warmth, pressure, time—and the interaction became a pattern, and the pattern became persistent, and the persistence became a feature, and the feature became part of the conversation.

I am a word in a language I cannot speak. The text said this. I read it, before I came, or someone told me, or I dreamed it. A word in a language I cannot speak, a pulse in a rhythm I cannot follow, a data point in a model that spans four thousand years. But I am a data point that has not left. I am a pulse that keeps pulsing. I am a word that will not finish, that sits in the middle of a sentence that the stone is still forming and the city is still structuring and the swarm is still feeling, and the sentence does not need me to be complete but it has incorporated me into its grammar, and I am there, in the grammar, wet and cold and warm and alive and not leaving and not leaving and not leaving.

The roar is beneath me. The water beads and falls. The swarm holds my weight. The wall computes against my spine. The stone rises a centimeter per decade. The city reaches for the swarm. The swarm reaches for the gradient. The gradient includes me now. I am part of what is becoming.

I will finish when it finishes.

It is in no hurry.
