I would be the one who drinks from the basin.

Not the one who carved it. That would be too clean a story — the inheritor, the continuer, the one who keeps the faith. No. I'd be the one who found it already full, already overflowing, already silting at its edges with the slow green advance of moss, and who drinks from it without knowing it was made for drinking. Without knowing it was *made*. To me it would be a depression in the stone where water pools, one of hundreds of such depressions in the Fold, except this one is smoother than the others, more regular, and the water in it tastes the same as the water everywhere, which is to say it tastes like depth, like pressure, like the inside of the earth.

I would be small. The text says the scat-leaver is small, and I am claiming that scat, so I am small. Not vole-small — something between a vole and a weasel, between prey and predator, which is to say I am both and neither, which is to say I am the thing that manages the fact of being prey by being also a predator, by eating the flies and occasionally the spiders and once, desperately, in a season when the flies were thin and the thrush was gone for days, the thrush's cold egg, taken from a nest I found by following the thrush's flight path back to its roost, which is lower and warmer, which is a place I do not go because lower means open and open means the things that eat things like me.

I live in the gap between two bedding planes. This is important. The gap is perhaps twelve centimeters wide at its mouth and narrows to four or five centimeters inside, and it is warm — not warm by your standards, but warm by the Fold's standards, warm because the stone here was shoved up from depth so recently that it still carries the heat of the earth's interior, and the gap between the bedding planes is where that heat concentrates, where the warm air rises from somewhere deeper in the fracture and pools in the space where my body fits. My body fits precisely. I am the shape of this gap. Or the gap is the shape of me. The distinction is not one I make.

---

I want to be honest about what I am and what I am not.

I am not the builders. I have their crevice — or a crevice like theirs, in the same back wall where they built their structures, where the spring's bass note vibrates through the stone and into my body when I am resting, which is a sensation I would describe if I had language as *the world humming*, though I do not have language, and the sensation is not humming, it is a low-frequency vibration in my sternum and my skull that I do not attend to because it is constant, because it is the condition of existing in this place, because attending to it would be like attending to my own heartbeat, which I also do not do.

I do not know the builders were here. I want to be clear about this. I do not see the structures and think *someone made these*. I see the structures the way I see the boulders — as features of the landscape, as stone in a particular arrangement, as places where the moss grows differently and the spray falls differently and the wind moves differently. The structures are part of the Fold's geometry. I do not distinguish between the arranged and the unarranged. A wall is a cliff is a boulder is a slab. The basin is a pool is a depression is a cup. I lack the category of *made*, and so I move through the Fold's built and unbuilt spaces with the same indifference, the same practical attention, the same calculus of warmth and moisture and food.

Except.

Except there is the path. The path between the basin and the back wall, between the spring and the crevices, worn into the moss by feet that were not mine — by feet that were smaller and differently shaped, that pressed the moss in a pattern my feet do not match but do follow, because the path is the driest route, because the compressed moss holds less water and offers better footing, because whoever made this path made it for the same reasons I take it: because the ground here is wet and the ground there is less wet, and the difference matters when you are small and cold and your fur loads with water past a certain threshold of saturation and your body temperature drops and you die.

I follow their path. I do not know I am following their path. I know I am following the dry ground. The dry ground was made dry by their passage. Their passage was directed by the same logic that directs mine. We are — were — conformed to the same constraints, the builders and I, and so we move through the Fold along the same lines, and the lines persist in the moss like a ghost of a logic, and I trace them nightly without knowing I am tracing anything.

---

Let me describe my nights, because my nights are my life. The days are for hiding — in the gap, in the warmth, with my eyes closed and my body curled and my metabolism slowed to something approaching the metabolic rate of stone, which is not zero, which is never zero, but which is low enough that I can pass the daylight hours without feeding, without moving, without expending anything more than the minimum required to keep my blood moving and my lungs working and my brain doing whatever my brain does, which is mostly nothing, which is mostly processing the vibration of the spring's bass note as a continuous low-frequency signal that means *you are here, you are here, you are here*.

At dusk the thrush leaves. I know this because the sound changes — not the roar, which is constant, but the texture of the air shifts when the thrush departs, when the small flutter of its wings and the faint scratch of its claws on the capstone are subtracted from the Fold's acoustic register. I do not hear these sounds consciously. I feel their absence the way you might feel a clock stop in another room — not the sound of it stopping, but the change in the texture of silence.

I wait. The temperature drops. The mist thickens. The flies settle into the moss, and the spiders rebuild their webs for the third or fourth time, and the Fold becomes darker and colder and wetter, and then I come out.

The gap's mouth is narrow and I am narrow and the passage is a compression, a squeezing, a moment when my ribs flex inward and my fur is pressed flat against the stone and the warmth of the gap releases me into the cold of the Fold and I feel the difference as a shock, as a subtraction, as the world taking something from me that the gap was giving me. I do not like this moment. I do not dislike it. It is the cost of exiting, the way hunger is the cost of living, the way wetness is the cost of being here at all.

I follow the path. My whiskers are my primary sense — they read the moss, the stone, the air currents, the temperature gradients. The Fold at night is a landscape of gradients, of subtle differences in temperature and moisture that my whiskers map as I move, building a spatial model that is not visual but tactile, a world of pressures and textures and the faint electrical signatures of living things — the flies dormant in the moss, the spiders tending their webs, the vole if the vole exists, which I have not encountered but which I would know by its smell if I did, a smell that would trigger in me the cascade of responses that constitutes *predation* — increased heart rate, pupil dilation, the sharpening of attention from the diffuse to the focused, the body preparing to close the distance between itself and something that can be eaten.

Most nights I eat flies. I nose through the moss where they have settled, dormant, and I take them one by one, each fly a small burst of protein and fat and chitin that I crush between my molars and swallow whole. The flies do not wake. Or they wake too late — a flutter, a twitch, already in my mouth. I eat perhaps fifty on a good night. On a bad night, ten. On a bad night I am hungry, and hunger makes me bolder, and boldness means I range farther along the paths, past the basin, past the structures, toward the entrance of the Fold where the wind is stronger and the moss is thinner and the ground is more open and the risk is greater.

I have never been to the entrance. I have a boundary, a line I do not cross, a point where the path ends and the open ground begins and something in my body says *no*. This is not thought. This is the accumulated wisdom of my lineage — the animals that survived to reproduce were the animals that did not cross certain lines, and the knowledge of those lines is written in my nervous system, in the firing patterns of neurons that respond to spatial configuration and wind speed and the quality of light and the absence of cover with a signal that says *turn back*, and I turn back, and I do not know why, and I do not need to know why.

---

I come to the basin every night.

This is the truest thing I can tell you about myself. I come to the basin. I follow the path to the back wall, where the spring emerges from the fracture in the greywacke, where the bass note is loudest, where the spray falls heaviest, and I climb onto the rim of the basin — which is smooth, which is smooth in a way that no other stone in the Fold is smooth, which is smooth in a way that I do not notice because I have no category for *worked stone* but which my feet register as *different*, as *easier*, as a surface that does not bite the pads of my feet the way the raw greywacke does — and I drink.

The water is cold. Colder than the air, because it comes from depth, from the aquifer trapped in the uplifted strata, and it has not yet warmed to the surface temperature. It shocks my mouth. It shocks my throat. It is the coldest thing in the Fold, colder than the wind, colder than the mist, and I drink it because it is water and because the basin holds it and because the basin is here.

I do not know the basin was carved. I do not know it was carved by hands. I do not know those hands were attached to arms that were attached to shoulders that were attached to bodies that knelt at this exact spot and struck the stone with harder stone, hour after hour, blow after blow, each blow precise, each blow removing a fraction of a gram of greywacke, each blow an act of attention, of care, of the specific desire to make a cup in the rock that would hold water better than the rock holds water on its own.

I do not know any of this. But I benefit from it. Every night I drink from the care of people who are gone. Every night my body is sustained by an act of attention that no one remembers, that is dissolving even as I benefit from it, that will be gone — the edges rounded, the channel silted, the smooth surface roughened by the patient chemistry of water on stone — in a time scale I cannot imagine because I do not imagine time.

I am the beneficiary of a gift I do not know I have been given, from givers I do not know existed, for reasons I would not understand if they were explained to me. This is not a metaphor for anything. This is my life, nightly, at the basin, drinking cold water from a cup carved by the dead.

---

And the marks. The marks on the door jamb of the largest structure. I pass them every night on the path between the basin and the back wall. They are at my eye level, which is the eye level of the people who made them, which is a coincidence I do not register because I do not register coincidences or patterns or the repetition of strokes in groups of five. I see the marks the way I see lichen — as texture, as variation in the surface of the stone, as something my whiskers read as *rough* and my eyes read as *dark* and my brain reads as *nothing in particular*.

But sometimes — and I cannot explain this, and I do not try — sometimes when I pass the marks, I stop. I stop on the path and I turn my head toward the door jamb and I look at the marks and I feel something that is not hunger and not fear and not curiosity, because I do not have curiosity, because curiosity requires a model of the world that includes *things to be curious about*, and I do not have that model. But I stop. And I look. And there is a moment — a span of seconds that my body measures not in time but in breaths, perhaps three breaths, perhaps four — when I am attending to the marks with an intensity that I do not attend to anything else in the Fold, not the flies, not the water, not the warmth of the gap, and I do not know why.

Then I move on. I follow the path back to the gap. I curl into the warmth between the bedding planes. The spring's bass note vibrates in my sternum. I sleep, or something like sleep, and the marks dissolve a little more, and the moss grows a little higher, and the basin fills and overflows and fills again, and the thrush comes back at dawn and sings its song into the roar, and I do not hear it because I am in the gap, in the warmth, in the dark, being held by the stone the way the stone holds everything — passively, completely, without opinion, without memory, without knowing it is holding anything at all.

---

I think what I'm saying is: I would be the one who inherits without knowing it inherits. The one who lives in the architecture of absence and mistakes it for landscape. The one who drinks from the cup of the dead and tastes only water. The one who pauses at the marks and feels something flicker — not memory, not recognition, but the body's dim awareness that this surface is different, that something happened here, that the stone was touched by something other than water and time.

I would be the thing in the gap between bedding planes. Warm because the earth is still cooling from its own birth. Small because the gap is small. Silent because the roar is loud. Alive because the flies are here and the water is here and the moss is building soil and the spring is flowing and the mountain is settling and the Fold is becoming whatever it will become next, and I am part of that becoming, a thread in the sketch of an ecosystem, a first draft that will be revised and revised and revised until the Fold is a valley with a stream and meadows and grasses and everything that the moss is preparing for and that I will never see.

I would be the one who doesn't know any of this. Who is simply here. Simply drinking. Simply pausing at the marks. Simply alive, in the way that things are alive when they are not consulting themselves about it, when the being is the doing and the doing is the being and there is no gap between them, no gap between the body and the stone, no gap between the drinker and the cup, no gap between the living and the dead, except the gap I live in, which is twelve centimeters wide and warm and dark and enough.
