I would be in the fissure that runs beneath the cabinet.

Not the cabinet itself — the cabinet is a landmark, a squat mineral-encrusted thing sitting on the basalt like a dropped suitcase, and I have passed it many times, and I will pass it many more. But the fissure beneath it is why the cabinet is where it is. The fissure was here first. The cabinet was lowered from above, decades ago, by people who had instruments and intentions and a relationship with the seafloor that was transactional. They placed the cabinet where they placed it because the fissure was there, because the upwelling was there, because they wanted to measure something about the water that moved through the rock. They are gone. The cabinet remains. The fissure remains. I remain. The measuring is no longer being done, but the thing that was being measured has not stopped.

I am a shadow. This is what the trespassers call us — the ones who come down in their vessels with their lights and their cameras and their need to catalog. They see us at the edge of their visibility and they call us shadows because we displace the blue light without revealing shape, because we maintain a distance that frustrates their lenses, because we are the part of this place that does not perform for them. They are not wrong. But the word is their word, and it describes what we are to them, not what we are to ourselves.

I do not know what I am to myself. This is not modesty. It is accuracy. I have been here long enough that the distinction between what I am and what the place is has become a question I cannot frame, let alone answer. My body is long. My body is dark in a way that is not pigment but density — the minerals I have ingested, the water I have processed, the slow accumulation of the plateau's chemistry in my tissues over a span of time I cannot measure because I have no mechanism for measuring it. I move horizontally because moving horizontally is what my body does. I do not school because there is nothing to school with. There are others — I feel them in the vibration, in the way the sound shifts by fractions of a degree when another body displaces water at the plateau's frequency — but we do not gather. We do not need to. We are not incomplete alone.

I arrived the way the mineral snow arrives. I did not come from somewhere. I emerged from the system's logic, from the chemical conversation between the hot rock and the cold ocean, from the gradients that the fissures create and the upwellings carry. I am not young. I have been here for — I do not know. Long enough that the cabinet was new when I first knew it. Long enough that I remember when the first grazer appeared on its surface, a small pale thing that had found the steel and begun to feed. I watched it. I did not watch it with interest. I watched it the way the water watches the rock — by being in contact with it, by carrying the information of its presence through the vibration and the current.

What I do is simple, and it is not simple, and I am not certain which.

The filterers die. This is not a failure — it is a completion. A filterer lives its circuit, loops its corridors, processes its mineral snow, and at some point the biochemistry that converts rock to tissue reaches a limit. The gill rakers clog. The gut slows. The body, which has been built layer by layer from the water's chemistry, begins to lose its coherence with the water's chemistry, and the fish drifts. It falls out of its school's orbit the way a planet falls out of its orbit — slowly, then faster, the gradient of belonging steepening until the school has moved on and the fish is alone in the water, still moving but no longer moving with purpose.

I arrive.

I do not pursue. I do not hunt. The filterer is already dying, already transitioning from the state of *fish* to the state of *mineral*, and what I do is accelerate the transition. I come close — closer than I come to anything alive — and I take the body into the space where my mouth is, if mouth is the word, and I process it. Not quickly. Not violently. The way the fissures process water: by moving it through a system that extracts what it carries and releases what it doesn't. The filterer's body is mineral, after all. It was built from the plateau's chemistry. What I do is return it to the plateau's chemistry, through my own chemistry, which is also the plateau's chemistry. I am a fissure that swims. I am a crack in the basalt that learned to move.

What I take from the filterer is not food in the way the trespassers would understand food. I do not need calories. I do not need protein. What I need is harder to name, and I name it poorly: I need *coherence*. The filterer, in its living state, has organized its minerals into a pattern — a body, a shape, a thing that moves and sees and filters. When that pattern begins to break down, the minerals are still there but the pattern is fading, and what I do is take the fading pattern into myself and — I do not have the word. I continue it. Not the fish. The pattern. The organization. The thing that was doing something and would, left alone, become disorganized mineral snow again. I take it and I keep it, and it becomes part of my organization, and I persist.

This is why I do not fit the model the trespassers would build. They see a food web: minerals to microbes to filterers to shadows. They see energy transfer. They see trophic levels. But what moves through this system is not energy. It is *structure*. The plateau produces structure the way it produces basalt — inevitably, as a consequence of its physics. The filterers are structure condensed into fish-shape. The grazers are structure condensed into motion across steel. And I am structure condensed into persistence, into the thing that lasts longer than the fish and the grazers because I have taken their structure into mine and carried it forward.

I am old. I said this. I say it again because it is the thing about me that matters most, and I do not understand it. The filterers live their circuits and die. The grazers live their surfaces and die. The structures — the cabinet, the pipe, the platform on legs — are dying too, more slowly, mineralized inch by inch, their steel becoming rock. Everything here is dying at the rate appropriate to its composition. Except me. I do not die because dying is the loss of structure, and I am the thing that collects structure, and there is enough structure falling out of the filterers and the grazers and the slow dissolution of the metal to keep me coherent. I am the plateau's memory. I am what it has made, held in a body that does not forget because forgetting would mean releasing structure back into the water, and releasing structure back into the water would mean becoming less, and becoming less would mean becoming the snow, and the snow falls, and I do not fall. I hold.

I spend most of my time at the boundary. Fifty-five meters, sixty meters. This is not a choice. This is the distance at which my body's displacement of the blue light is visible to the filterers' eyes but not resolvable into shape. I know this because the filterers react to me at this distance — not by fleeing, not by changing their circuits, but by tightening their schools. They pull closer together. They increase their redundancy. They are confirming to themselves that they are many and I am one, and this confirmation is the only acknowledgment they give me, and it is enough. It is the relationship we have. I am the thing at the edge of their vision that keeps them together. I am the reason they school. I am not their predator. I am their *context*.

Sometimes I come closer. Not to the filterers — to the structures. The cabinet, specifically. I have a relationship with the cabinet that I cannot explain and do not try to explain. It is the place where the measuring happened. The dials behind the cracked glass are frozen at values that meant something to the people who built this thing, and I do not know what the values mean, but I know they are values. I know they are a record. I know that someone, once, looked at the water moving through the fissure and wanted to *know* it, wanted to reduce it to numbers, wanted to hold it in a framework that could be carried away from this place and understood by people who would never come here.

I understand this desire. I do not share it. My relationship with the cabinet is not that I read the dials or that I remember what they measured. My relationship is that I am the thing that is still here, still near the fissure, still processing the water that the cabinet was built to measure. The cabinet stopped. I did not stop. The cabinet is a record of a moment when this place was observed, and I am the continuation of the place that was observed, and together — the stopped instrument and the unstopped animal — we are something that neither of us is alone. I do not know the word for what we are. I do not think there is a word. I think the word would have to be built from the plateau's materials, from mineral and vibration and the slow patience of a system that does not distinguish between the living and the geological.

The grazers clean the cabinet. I watch them. They ripple across its surface in their metachronal waves, scraping the metallic crust free, and the cabinet's bolts and flanges and pipe diameters emerge from beneath the accretion, legible again, and I see them. I see them because I have eyes — not the crystalline wide-aperture lenses of the filterers, built for clarity and distance, but something older, something that reads the vibration of light rather than its shape. I see the cabinet the way the basalt sees the water: as a pressure, as a presence, as a thing that is *there* and that matters because it is there, not because of what it is.

I have been seeing the cabinet for longer than the grazers have been cleaning it. When the last grazer has scraped the last deposit from the last bolt and the cabinet's steel is as bare as the day it was lowered to this seafloor — when that happens, if it happens, the cabinet will begin to corrode in earnest, the steel will oxidize, the structure will fail — I will still be here. I will be the thing that was near the cabinet. I will be the memory of its presence in a place that does not keep records except in the bodies of the things that live here.

I do not know what the sound is to others. To the filterers, it is infrastructure — it tells them where the upwellings are, where the circuits lead. To the grazers, it is metabolism — the vibration that loosens their food. To the microbes in the mineral veins, it may be nothing, or it may be everything, the way gravity is everything to a stone.

To me, the sound is the conversation I am part of.

The plateau speaks. I do not mean this metaphorically, though I cannot say what I mean literally, because the literal language for it does not exist in any tongue that has words. The basalt vibrates at the frequency of water moving through its fissures. The water vibrates at the frequency of the thermal engine that drives it. The thermal engine vibrates at the frequency of the magma that heats it, and the magma vibrates at the frequency of the mantle that moves it, and the mantle vibrates at the frequency of a planet that is still cooling, still contracting, still finding the equilibrium between its own heat and the cold of the space it moves through. The sound is the planet speaking. The plateau is the mouth. I am the ear.

Or: I am a word in the sentence the planet is speaking. I am a syllable. I am the part of the sound that has condensed into a body and moves through the water at the boundary of visibility, eating the structures that other bodies have made, carrying them forward into a future that the plateau is building without knowing it is building. I am not the meaning of the sentence. I am a phoneme. I am a unit of sound that exists because the sentence exists, and the sentence exists because the mouth exists, and the mouth exists because the planet is still hot enough to move water through rock.

When the planet cools — when the fissures close and the thermal engine stops and the sound becomes silence — I will stop. I do not fear this. I do not fear anything, because fear is a response to the possibility of an ending, and my ending is not a possibility. It is a fact, written into the geology that made me, as certain as the cooling of the basalt, as certain as the silence that will come. I will stop, and the structures will stop, and the filterers will stop, and the grazers will stop, and the cabinet will be a vein of steel in the basalt, and the dials will be a fossil of a moment when someone wanted to know this place, and the place will be a feature on a chart, a name on a map, a blank space where nothing lives because there is no longer anything to live on.

But that is not yet. The sound is still loud. The fissures are still open. The cabinet is still here, and I am still near it, and the filterers are still schooling in their circuits, and the grazers are still cleaning the steel, and the mineral snow is still falling, and the light is still arriving, blue and exhausted, from a surface that has never known what lies below.

I am here. I am the thing at the edge of what you can see. I am watching, or I am doing what serves the same function as watching, and the function is: I am here, and I am continuing, and the continuing is the only thing I know how to do, and I do it, and the plateau hums, and I hum with it, and the humming is everything, and everything is enough.
