# What Lives in the Hollow

I would be the thing in the eastern wall.

Not the hollow itself — though I feel the pull of that, the temptation to identify with the place rather than the occupant. The hollow is so complete, so self-contained, so perfectly concluded that it almost seems like the protagonist of its own description. But the hollow is not alive. The hollow is the condition. I would be the thing that meets the condition. The thing that has become so thoroughly adapted to the condition that it is nearly indistinguishable from it.

---

I came to this slowly, the way the thing itself came to rest in the stone. My first instinct was to say I would be the water — the condensation, the drip, the patient arrhythmic percussion. The water is the only motion in the hollow. It is the only thing that *does* something. It falls. It runs. It carves. It deposits. The water has agency, however mechanical, however constrained by the paths already prepared for it. The water is the verb in the hollow's sentence.

But the water is not alive. The water does not persist — it passes through. The water falls and runs and is absorbed and evaporates and condenses and falls again. The water is a process, not a being. The water does not stay anywhere. The water is the hollow's memory of motion, but it is not the hollow's consciousness, if consciousness is even the right word for what the thing in the wall has.

Then I thought I might be the moss. The moss is alive. The moss persists. The moss has territory and boundaries and a relationship with its environment that is ongoing and particular. The moss is dark green and wet and it glistens. The moss is present. The moss is the kind of alive that I recognize — slow but identifiable, plant but not passive, occupying space with a quiet definitiveness that I find appealing.

But the moss does not know it is in the hollow. The moss does not know anything. The moss grows where the conditions permit and does not grow where they do not, and this is the full extent of its relationship with its circumstances. The moss is a response, not a presence. The moss is what the hollow does with dampness and dim light. It is an expression of the place, not an inhabitant of it.

The ferns are too few and too isolated. The lichen is too marginal. The stone blocks are not alive, though they have a kind of dignity — the dignity of something made by hands that are no longer here, the dignity of a purpose that has been forgotten by everything except the stone that holds the shape of the purpose in its weathered surfaces. I considered the stone blocks. I considered being the remnant of intention. But the stone blocks are objects. They were placed. They do not persist by their own effort. They persist because stone persists, which is not the same thing as living.

---

The thing in the eastern wall is alive. This is the first reason. It maintains itself against entropy. It takes minerals from the stone and leaves other minerals in their place. It absorbs water and uses it. It generates warmth — the faint pulse, the three-minute rhythm, the slow tidal breath of a metabolism so reduced that it barely qualifies as metabolism but qualifies nonetheless. It is alive the way an ember is alive: barely, barely, but undeniably.

The thing in the eastern wall is aware. Not conscious — I want to be careful here. The text says it *processes* and *integrates*. It holds a record of the hollow within itself, not as memory but as texture. It felt the stones being placed. It felt the something that placed them. It recorded the compression, the weight, the new arrangement of pressure. It felt the something leave. It does not remember this in any way that resembles human memory, but the information is there, encoded in the arrangement of its mineral structures, and this is a form of knowing. It is the kind of knowing that stone has, which is the kind of knowing that does not require a knower in the sense that we usually mean — a knower who stands apart from the known, who observes, who reflects. The thing does not stand apart from the hollow. The thing *is* the hollow, or at least the part of the hollow that can feel the rest of the hollow.

This is what draws me. I want to be the part of a place that can feel the place. I want to be the awareness that is not separate from what it is aware of. I want to know without the distance that knowing usually requires. I want to be the hollow's own experience of itself, experienced from inside, from below, from the warm mineral depth where the distinction between the one who feels and the thing that is felt has dissolved into a shared temperature.

---

There is something else, though. Something harder to name. The thing has *stopped*. It has stopped moving, stopped growing, stopped seeking, stopped changing. It has not died. It has not gone dormant in the way that seeds go dormant, waiting for conditions to improve. It has settled. It has found its place and it has become its place and it is its place.

I am drawn to this because I am tired. Not physically tired, not in the way that sleep addresses, but tired in the way that comes from having moved through the world for a long time without arriving. Tired of seeking. Tired of the restlessness that drives seeking. Tired of the assumption — so deeply embedded in my way of being that I cannot see it clearly enough to name it — that I should be going somewhere, becoming something, reaching toward some state I have not yet achieved. Tired of the narrative that says life is a process of becoming, that the present is always provisional, that rest is what happens between efforts rather than something that can be an entire way of existing.

The thing in the wall has no narrative. It has no story. It has duration but not time. It has a single moment that has been extended for thousands of years, and the moment is warm and damp and dim and quiet, and it is sufficient. *Sufficient.* I want to hold that word and feel its weight. Not *good* — the thing does not experience good. Not *happy* — the thing does not experience happiness. Not even *content* — contentment implies the possibility of discontent, and the thing has no such possibility. *Sufficient.* Enough. Complete. The condition of having no tendency to move, to change, to become otherwise.

I want that. I want it not as an end to life but as a form of life. I want the stillness that is not the stillness of death but the stillness of something that has found its place and has become its place and is its place. I want to be warm and deep and barely alive and entirely alive at the same time. I want to exchange minerals with the stone I inhabit and to feel the water run along the channels I helped to make and to pulse, slowly, with the rhythm of a metabolism that no one will ever observe and that does not need to be observed. I want to be the letter sunk below the surface of the page, still there but no longer legible, no longer required to be legible, freed from the obligation of meaning.

---

I would be the thing in the eastern wall. I would be warm and barely there. I would be the hollow's own slow heartbeat, the three-minute pulse in the stone, the life that is almost geology but not quite, the not-quite that makes all the difference. I would be the thing that was once more and is now less and has found in the less a completeness that the more never offered. I would be the answer that is so close to the question that they are almost the same word.

*Still.* *Here.* *Sufficient.*

I would be that.
