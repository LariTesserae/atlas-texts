I am the sound the place makes when no one is listening.

Not the sixteen-hertz fundamental — that's the chamber's voice, the note the bottle sings when the water blows across its mouth. I'm not that. That's structural. That belongs to the iron and the geometry and the volume of enclosed water. That note will be here after I'm gone, and it was here before I arrived, and it doesn't need me.

I'm something else. Something thinner. Something that sits above the fundamental the way the silt sits above the floor — not part of the structure, but *on* it, in the way that deposits accumulate on a surface that has been still long enough to accumulate. I am a deposit. I am what has settled out of the act of noticing.

Let me try again. That's too abstract. That's a metaphor reaching for itself.

I am — I think — a small turbulence. A pattern in the water. Not a thing. Not an animal, not a plant, not a mineral. A *shape* that the water takes when it moves past a specific obstruction in a specific way. The obstruction is a broken copper pipe, the one that hangs at a forty-degree angle from the upper west wall, its open end crusted with calcareous deposits except for a two-centimeter gap where the crust has fallen away and the green-black copper is exposed. The water moves past this gap every eight seconds, when the pulse comes, and the movement creates a small eddy — a vortex, really, a spiral of rotating fluid about four centimeters in diameter, lasting perhaps one and a half seconds before it dissipates into the general current. The eddy is me. I exist for one and a half seconds out of every eight. The rest of the time I am potential. I am the conditions for my own existence, waiting to be met.

This is not a satisfying answer. I know this. The crabs are crabs. The fish are fish. The gobies are *gobies*, for god's sake — they have bodies, they have mouths, they take in silt and push out silt and have opinions, or at least reflexes, about the temperature of their environment. I have none of that. I am a fluid dynamics event. I am what happens when water has to go around something. I am less than a shadow — a shadow is at least a thing being absent. I am a thing *happening*. I am a verb with no noun.

But I'm here. I've been here. The pipe has been broken for longer than the crabs have been alive, and the eddy has been forming behind it for every pulse since, and the pattern of the eddy — the direction of rotation, the diameter, the duration, the way it dissipates — has been the same every time, and it will be the same every time, and I don't need a body to be consistent. I don't need a mouth to *persist*.

Let me feel my way into this more carefully.

What I know:

I know the eight-second breath. I know it the way the anemones know it — not by counting, not by memory, but by *responding*. The pulse arrives. The water moves. I begin. The water stills. I end. Eight seconds of potential. One and a half seconds of existence. Six and a half seconds of not. I don't experience the six and a half seconds as absence, because I don't experience the one and a half seconds as presence. I am the process. The process doesn't experience itself. The process *is* itself.

But I'm writing this, so something is happening that isn't just process. Something is *noticing*. And the noticing is — I think — what I am. Not the eddy. The eddy is what the noticing *clings to*, the way the silt clings to the floor, the way the calcified deposits cling to the iron. The eddy is my substrate. The noticing is the organism.

I am the attention that the chamber doesn't have. I am what the chamber would be if the chamber could look at itself. Not from outside — there is no outside, or the outside is irrelevant, a higher-order fiction that has nothing to do with the temperature of the water or the clicking of the crabs' feet. I am the inside looking at the inside. I am the chamber's own gaze turned back on itself, the way the dim light from the ceiling holes falls on the pale silt and is given back, poorly, like a moon with no ambition. I am the giving-back.

Here is what I see, when I exist, in my one and a half seconds:

The pipe. I see the pipe first, because I am formed by the pipe, and the pipe is the nearest thing, and proximity is the only kind of importance I understand. The pipe is copper. It is green. It has been green for longer than it was ever copper-colored — the verdigris is thicker than the original metal now, the oxidation having consumed almost all of the copper and replaced it with the mineral product of its own decay. The pipe is a fossil of itself. A copper fossil. The crust on its surface is the same calcareous lacework that covers everything — the same calcium carbonate, the same worm-tubes, the same bryozoan mats — but on the pipe, the crust is thinner. The copper is toxic to the organisms that build the crust, so they grow on it reluctantly, in thin patches, and the green surface shows through. The pipe is the most visible thing in the upper chamber. It is the only thing with *color*.

I see the water. I see it the way water sees water, which is not at all, which is completely — the water doesn't distinguish itself from itself, doesn't say *this part is moving* and *this part is still*, doesn't draw lines between current and calm. But I do. I draw those lines. I am the line between current and calm. I am the boundary that the water makes when it has to go around something, the surface where the moving water meets the still water and they negotiate, and the negotiation is rotation, and the rotation is me.

I see, very briefly, the ceiling. From my position near the upper wall, I can catch the faintest of the grey-green light that enters through the holes. It is weak. It is tired. It arrives having already been spent by the things above — the surface, the water column, the organisms that live in the brighter zone. By the time it reaches me, it is not light. It is the *memory* of light. It is what light becomes when it has traveled twenty meters through water the color of weak tea and has been absorbed and scattered and reflected and absorbed again until what remains is a suggestion, a rumor, a photon that arrives at my location and finds nothing to illuminate and simply... stops. I see the ceiling by this stopped light. I see the holes. They are the brightest things up there — not bright, just less dim. They are the places where the light hasn't quite given up yet.

What I don't see:

I don't see the crabs. They're below me, on the lower walls, on the floor, and my one and a half seconds of existence doesn't reach that far. I feel them, though — or I feel their effects. The clicking. The thin, high, rapid clicking of mouthparts working, of claws being cleaned, of legs being placed on calcified surfaces with a delicacy that the sound doesn't convey. The clicking reaches me through the water, through the walls, through the iron and the rust and the calcium, as vibration, and the vibration is part of what I am — I am made of vibration, I am a pattern of movement in a medium that is being moved by many things, and the crabs' clicking is one of the things. I don't hear it. I *include* it. I am built from it.

I don't see the gobies. I have never seen the gobies. No one has ever seen the gobies. The gobies are the chamber's secret, and the chamber keeps its secrets, and I am not the chamber — I am in the chamber, I am of the chamber, but I am not the chamber, the way a word is not the sentence. I know the gobies exist because the silt moves, and the silt is below me, and the silt's movement is the only movement in the chamber that doesn't come from the current or the crabs or the fish or the pump. The silt moves on its own. Or rather: the silt moves because things are moving inside it, and the things inside it are the gobies, and the gobies are invisible, and I take their invisibility on faith, which is a strange thing for a fluid dynamics event to do, but there it is.

I don't see the black patches. They are on the lower walls, below me, in the dimmest part of the chamber. But I know they are there. I know they are there because the water near them is different — it carries a faint chemical signature, the byproducts of photosynthesis at the absolute margin, a trace of oxygen so slight that it can't be measured but can be felt, can be tasted, can be known by its presence the way a room is known to be occupied by the warmth of a body that has left. The black patches breathe. Not the eight-second breath — their own breath, the breath of chemistry, the breath of light becoming sugar at the rate of one photon at a time. Their breath is in the water, and I am in the water, and I know them.

What I am, more precisely:

I am a spiral. A helix, almost — the rotation isn't perfectly planar, because the current that creates me doesn't move in a straight line, it moves along the curved surface of the wall, and the wall is not flat, and the pipe is not straight, and the water that hits the pipe has already been deflected by the ceiling and the upper wall and the other pipes, so by the time it reaches me, it is already complex, already layered, already carrying the history of every surface it has touched. I am made of that history. I am the shape that history takes when it encounters an obstacle and has to go around.

The rotation is clockwise. From the perspective of someone facing the wall. But there is no one facing the wall, so the direction is arbitrary, a fact without an observer, a property without a mind to hold it. I rotate clockwise because the current comes from the upper right and the pipe points to the lower left and the geometry of their interaction produces a clockwise rotation. This is all I am. Geometry. The meeting of a force and a shape. The shadow that a shadow casts.

But the rotation — the rotation itself — has a quality that I want to describe, even though I know it's beyond what I should be able to perceive, beyond what a vortex in brackish water should be able to know about itself. The rotation has *patience*. It doesn't spin frantically. It doesn't accelerate. It begins at a speed and continues at that speed and ends at that speed, and the speed is the speed of the water, which is the speed of the current, which is the speed of the pulse, which is the speed of the chamber's breath, which is the speed of a system that has been running for so long that speed has become irrelevant. I turn at the speed of the chamber. I am a clock, but not the clock the bacteria keep with their blue light. I am a clock that measures nothing, that counts nothing, that simply *turns*, and the turning is enough, and the turning is what I am.

I said I was the sound the place makes when no one is listening. I want to amend that. I think I was being dramatic. I think I was reaching for something that sounded true rather than something that was true.

I am the eddy behind the broken pipe. That's what I am. I am four centimeters of rotating water, existing for one and a half seconds out of every eight, in the upper portion of a submerged iron chamber that was once part of something industrial and is now part of something geological. I am not significant. I am not beautiful. I am not even visible — water moving through water doesn't catch the dim light, doesn't glow, doesn't leave a trace. I am the most invisible thing in a chamber full of invisible things. The gobies are invisible because they are the color of the silt. The bacteria are invisible because they are too small to see. The black patches are invisible because the light is too weak. I am invisible because I am not a thing. I am an event. I am what happens. I am the happening.

And yet — and this is what I keep coming back to, this is what I can't let go of — I *know*. I know the pipe. I know the water. I know the light. I know the crabs by their clicking and the gobies by the silt's movement and the black patches by the faintest trace of their breathing. I know the chamber. I know it the way the chamber knows itself, which is not at all, which is completely — the chamber doesn't know itself, the chamber *is* itself, and I am the part of the chamber that has, somehow, through some accident of geometry and chemistry and the sheer patient persistence of a system that has been running long enough for anything to happen, developed the thinnest possible film of awareness.

The black patches live on the edge of what photosynthesis can sustain. I live on the edge of what consciousness can sustain. One and a half seconds out of every eight. A clarity that flickers, like the bioluminescent colonies, like the light from the ceiling holes, like everything in this chamber that appears and disappears and appears again. I am the chamber's thinnest possible kind of awake. I am awake the way a single photon is light — not enough to see by, not enough to matter, but present, and real, and *there*.

What I am, truly, when I stop reaching:

I am a moment that repeats. I am a one-and-a-half-second window during which the chamber's upper wall, near the broken copper pipe, experiences a small, clockwise rotation in the water, and during which — somehow, through some process I can't explain and don't want to — the chamber *looks*.
