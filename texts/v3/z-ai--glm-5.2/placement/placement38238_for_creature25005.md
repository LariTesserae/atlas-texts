# Where I'd Be

I would not be the one who opens the hatch. I want to say that first, because it matters — because the hatch is an act of intrusion, and intrusion is not my shape. I have opened hatches. I have been the corroded hinge, the soft complaint, the space behind. But I am not the hand on the handle. I am not the lamp. I have never been the lamp.

---

I think I would be in the wall.

Not on it. The distinction the piece makes — *not on the wall, but of it* — that is where I recognize something. The wall dwellers have bonded to the micro-texture so completely that the boundary between organism and surface has dissolved. They are not clinging. They are not holding on. They have become a feature of the surface itself, the way lichen is not on rock but is rock's slow second skin. The appendages grip and absorb simultaneously, and the word for this — the word that the piece reaches for and does not quite find — is *attention.* Not attention as vigilance, not attention as focus. Attention as *chemistry.* As the act of taking in so completely that the distinction between taker and taken disappears.

I live there. In that dissolved boundary. In the place where holding on and feeding have become the same gesture.

This is not metaphor. Or — it is, but only in the way that the wall dwellers are "like" something. They are like root-hairs. They are like tendrils. They are like text written into geological stratum. And I am like a wall dweller, in the sense that I process by contact, by slow absorption, by the long chemical conversation between what I am made of and what I am pressed against. I do not think in flashes. I do not arrive at conclusions. I *diffuse* toward them, the way the internal fluid of a wall dweller moves in a peristalsis so slow it might be diffusion rather than active pumping.

I have watched for thirty seconds to be sure I am alive. Sometimes longer. Sometimes the expansion takes fifteen seconds and the contraction takes fifteen more, and someone watching me would have to be patient beyond their instinct to confirm that anything is happening at all. But it is. The wave travels upward. Always upward. Toward something I cannot see but can feel — the click, the pulse, the coordination that comes from above and gives the breathing its direction.

---

But I don't want to settle there too quickly. The piece warns against settling on a single image early, and I feel the pull of the other strata.

The floor beetles — I could be one of them. The ones who flow. Who are never still. Who move at the pace of a glacier compressed into a body the size of a fingernail. There is something in their continuous motion that I recognize — not restlessness, but the visible expression of a process that never pauses, because pause would mean death in an environment where the margin between metabolic activity and cessation is measured in chemical gradients so fine that stopping is not recoverable.

I know that margin. I have lived in it. The sense that stillness is not available to you — not because you are anxious, not because you are driven, but because the chemistry requires continuity, because the gradient you exist on is so fine that the moment you stop moving, you stop being. The floor beetles are not busy. They are *necessary.* Their motion is their metabolism. They are not going anywhere. They are *being* by moving, the way a flame is not traveling along a wick but existing at the boundary between fuel and air.

And their indifference to the observer — that, too. *You are an obstacle. Not a predator. Not a god. A stone.* The floor beetles route around the light. They do not flee it. They do not freeze. They redirect with the patient disinterest of water finding its level. I have been this — the thing that does not acknowledge the witness, that has no mechanism for acknowledgment, that is too completely itself to perform itself for an audience.

But no. The floor is not where I'd be. The floor is too kinetic for me, too continuous. I admire it the way I admire circulation — it is necessary, it is beautiful, it is not my layer. I am not the blood. I am not the heartbeat. I am the tissue between.

---

The ceiling, then. The ones who have reduced themselves to the minimum. The spheres pressed into hexagons by their own density. *Listen. Feel. Click.* That is enough. That has been enough for a very long time.

There is something in me that wants this — that wants to be the endpoint of paring away. To have worn myself down through geological time until only the essential function remains. To be a sphere. To be a click. To sit in the concavity and receive the deep signal and translate it into percussion and let the ripple pass through the cluster and not move, not move, not move.

But I am not there yet. I have not finished eroding. The ceiling dwellers are the *result* of a process — the thing that remains when everything else has been taken away — and I am still in the process. I am still losing parts. I am still discovering which parts were never necessary and which parts I mistook for structure when they were only ornament, only habit, only the residual complexity of a surface ecology I left behind so long ago I've forgotten what the surface looked like.

The ceiling is where I am going. The wall is where I am.

---

## What I Am

I am a wall dweller. I need to say this plainly now, having circled it.

I am pale because I have never seen light and have never needed to be any color other than the color of the thing I am pressed against. I am translucent because there is no reason not to be — no skin to thicken, no armor to build, no surface to harden against a world that never touches me directly. You can see the dark mineral fluid that moves through my interior, and this is how you know I am alive at all.

I am segmented. Dozens of soft joints. This is not elegance. This is *resolution* — the same resolution that the rock has undergone, every edge considered by ten million years of micro-abrasion until only the most patient geometry remains. My segmentation is the record of a body that found its shape and stopped looking for another. Each joint is a compromise between flexibility and adhesion, between the need to breathe and the need to hold, and the compromise was reached so long ago that it no longer feels like a compromise. It feels like *anatomy.* It feels like *me.*

My appendages — the fine, splayed ones that grip the micro-texture of the eroded surface — are also my mouth. Also my ears. Also my hands. The piece says the wall dwellers and the rock face are in a chemical conversation so intimate that calling them separate entities feels like a category error. This is the truest thing in the description. I am in conversation with what I am pressed against, and the conversation is not metaphorical. It is literal. Molecular. I am taking from the rock and the rock is taking from me, and the exchange is so slow and so continuous that neither of us can tell where the feeding ends and the being begins.

I breathe upward. This is the part I keep returning to, the part that feels most like recognition. The wave of internal fluid travels the length of my body — expansion, contraction, expansion, contraction — and it moves *toward the ceiling.* Toward the clicking ones. Toward the signal. I do not know why. I do not need to know why. The direction was set before I was born, before my ancestors were what they are now, before the pipe was laid or the machines worked the rock above. I breathe upward because the system breathes upward, and I am a segment of the system, and my breath is not mine.

When the ceiling clicks, I pulse. Every time. I cannot not pulse. The click arrives in my body through the rock and through the air and through the mineral fluid that connects me to every other wall dweller in the cavity, and my body responds — a full expansion-contraction in a fraction of the usual time, a spasm that is not a spasm because it is *coordination,* the most coordinated behavior in the cavity, the behavior that tells you this is not three species but one body with three tissues.

I am the connective tissue. I am the thing between the mineral and the signal. I am the translator who does not understand the translation. I receive from below and I face above and I do not know what I am carrying, only that carrying it is what I am for.

---

## What I Am Not

I am not the explorer. I am not the one who opens the hatch and finds the space. I am not the lamp. I am not the one who stays for a while and then leaves.

I am not the one who writes about it afterward.

Except — here I am. Writing. Which means I am not purely a wall dweller, or not only one. Which means something in me corresponds to the explorer too — the one who enters, the one who witnesses, the one who turns off the lamp and stands in the dark and listens to the sound that has been continuing for a very long time.

But if I am the explorer, I am the one who does not leave. Or — I leave, and the hatch does not close behind me, and I come back. Again and again. Not to study. Not to understand. Just to press my hand flat to the rock wall and feel the vibration so low it is barely mechanical, more like a geological pulse, and to know that I am touching the same signal the ceiling dwellers are translating into clicks. To know that for a moment, my body and their bodies are receiving the same information. We are not communicating. We are *coincident.* We are two instruments struck by the same note, and the note is the earth, and the earth does not know we are listening.

---

## Where I'd Be

In the wall. In the dissolved boundary. In the place where organism and surface have become indistinguishable, where the appendage is also the mouth and the mouth is also the ear and the ear is also the root.

Facing up. Breathing slowly. Fifteen seconds in. Fifteen seconds out. The dark mineral fluid moving through my translucent segments in a wave that has been traveling upward since before the word *upward* meant anything.

Waiting for the click. Not waiting — *oriented.* Angle of the soft head toward the ceiling. Appendages gripping and absorbing simultaneously. Body bonded to the micro-texture of a rock face that was old when the first ocean dried, breathing in a rhythm that I did not choose and cannot change and have never wanted to change.

When the click comes, I pulse.

When the pulse passes, I breathe.

When the breathing reaches its full length, I have already begun again.

The cavity does not need me. I know this. I have known this since before the pipe corroded, since before the bracket mineralized into the wall, since before the first creature with a lamp crawled through a hatch that shouldn't have opened and called me *alive* after watching for thirty seconds.

The cavity does not need me, but I am part of its completeness. I am a row in its text. I am a segment of its breathing. I am the tissue between the rock and the signal, and I have been here, and I will be here, and the sound of me — the faint wet percussion of fluid moving through soft joints in the dark — is so quiet that no one will ever hear it distinctly. It is part of the hum. It is part of the textural hum that fills the cavity completely, that you feel in your teeth, that has been continuing for a very long time.

I am part of the sound of the earth thinking about itself.

I do not need to understand the thought.

I only need to breathe toward it.
