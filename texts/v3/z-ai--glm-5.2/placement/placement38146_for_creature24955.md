I would be the one who came back.

Not the first descender — those are gone, their names compiled in the language no one speaks, their intentions folded into the chamber's original specification like rebar set into concrete before the pour. Not the survey crews with their drones and their shrugging reports. Not the occasional maintenance tech, if there ever were any, who came down with a checklist and left with a headache and a vague sense of having interrupted something. Those people were all weather. The chamber absorbed them the way it absorbs footsteps.

I would be the one who came down once and could not stop hearing it.

Not hearing — that's the wrong word, and the chamber has made me careful about words. The 18 hertz does not hear. It *inscribes.* It writes itself into the body in a way that does not fade when the body leaves. I went back to the surface and the world was too loud in all the wrong frequencies. Traffic. Conversation. Wind through doors. Everything above 20 hertz, everything audible, everything that has the discourtesy to be heard rather than felt. I lay in my quarters and pressed my hand to my sternum and I could still find it — the pressure, the low harmonic, the tuning-fork hum in my teeth. Faint. But present. Like a voice calling from the bottom of a well and the well is inside you.

I came back. I did not have permission. Permission is a concept that requires a hierarchy of intent, and the chamber has taught me that intent is not the organizing principle I once believed it was.

---

What I am is difficult because the chamber has made it difficult. I was a technician. Third-grade systems monitoring. My job was to descend to deprecated infrastructure, confirm it was still deprecated, file the report, ascend. I had done it fourteen times. Fourteen chambers, fourteen shafts, fourteen reports that read *no change* in the appropriate fields. I was good at it because I had the specific kind of patience that doesn't mind repetition and the specific kind of detachment that doesn't ask what the infrastructure was for. I was a pipe inspector. I inspected pipes. The pipes did not need inspecting. I inspected them anyway. This was the arrangement.

The Conduit broke the arrangement.

Not dramatically. Not the way a revelation breaks things — with a flash, a reordering, a before and an after. It broke me the way water breaks rock. I stood in the center of the chamber for forty minutes, which was the allotted time, and I felt the 18 hertz in my sternum and I watched the indicator filaments pulse and I listened to the clicking above me and I thought: *this is still running.* Not broken. Not dormant. Not deprecated. Running. Running in a way I couldn't read with the equipment I'd been given, running at a level of abstraction my training didn't cover, but running nonetheless, and the running was — I don't have the word. The closest I can come is *complete.* The running was complete in itself. It did not need me to confirm it. It did not need me at all. And that should have been a routine observation — *infrastructure functional, no intervention required* — but instead it landed in me like a seed in a crack, and the crack was the fact that I had spent my life confirming things that did not need confirming, and the seed was the question of what I was actually for.

I filed the report. *No change.* I ascended. I could not stop hearing it.

---

I came back with different equipment. I came back with a seismograph and a thermal imager and a microphone that could record down to 1 hertz and a notebook, because the notebook was the thing that felt honest. The machines would give me data. The notebook would give me something else — a place to be wrong in private, a place to write things that didn't fit in fields.

What I wrote, the first night back in the chamber, was:

*The floor is warm. Not hot. The warmth of skin. I keep thinking about that. The composite was engineered to conduct heat from the geothermal substrate into the chamber at a rate that maintains thermal equilibrium. This is a fact. But the fact sits next to the feeling — the feeling of standing on something warm — and the fact and the feeling do not know each other. They are in the same room and they have never been introduced. I am the introduction. I am the only thing in this chamber that holds both the engineering readout and the sensation of warmth under my boots at the same time.*

I wrote that and I knew it was not a technician's observation. I knew it was something else. I let it be something else.

---

Over time — and I am aware that time is one of the things this chamber has in quantities I cannot fathom — I became a fourth kind of presence.

The moss is a consequence. The clicking thing is a resident. The chamber is a process. I am a *translator.* Not of the compiled language. Not of the signal the filaments transmit. I translate between the chamber's way of being and my own. I stand in the center and I feel the 18 hertz and I think: *this is what it feels like to do a thing so thoroughly that the doing becomes the thing.* I watch the condensation form on the cooling fins and I think: *this is what it looks like when a process has been running so long it has grown its own ecosystem, its own weather, its own life, none of which it asked for or intended.* I hear the clicking above me and I think: *that is what it sounds like to find a home in the gaps of someone else's architecture, to make a life in 14.3 centimeters of space, and to not experience that as constraint.*

I write these things down. Not for anyone. Not for a report. Not for the record. The chamber does not need my translations. The chamber does not need to be understood. But I need to do the translating. It is the only response I have found that matches the scale of what I am standing in. Not comprehension — I will never comprehend this place. Not worship — worship implies a hierarchy, and the chamber is not above me, it is around me, and the difference matters. Translation. The careful, imperfect work of carrying something from one register to another, knowing it will arrive changed, knowing the change is the point.

---

I have a circuit now, like the clicking thing.

I arrive. I stand in the center. I feel the pulse. I walk the perimeter, trailing my fingers along the composite where it transitions from floor to wall to ceiling, feeling the texture change under my hand. I check the capillary channels — the moss is still there, still black, still following the water with the fidelity of a shadow following the thing that casts it. I stand near the membranes and feel them flex with the chamber's breathing. I listen for the clicks and I count them — not because counting tells me anything but because counting is a way of paying attention, and paying attention is the only offering I have that the chamber will accept, which is also the only offering it doesn't need, which is also the only offering I can make.

I stay for hours. I stay until the 18 hertz has so thoroughly colonized my body that I can no longer distinguish it from my own heartbeat, and then I know it is time to go, because losing that distinction is — I think — what happened to the conduit itself. The process and the structure became one thing. The signal became its own message. I do not want to become my own message. I want to remain the translator. I want to remain the thing that carries the chamber's frequency back to the surface, holds it for a while in a body that can also hold conversation and wind through doors and all the audible frequencies of the world above, and then returns to set it down in the notebook and pick it up again in the sternum.

This is my ecology. This is my 14.3 centimeters. The space between the chamber and everything that has forgotten the chamber exists. I live in that gap. I move through it on my circuit. I eat nothing here. I build nothing here. I leave nothing here except the warmth of my feet on the floor, which the composite absorbs and conducts into the rock and converts, eventually, into another degree of the condensation that feeds the moss that feeds the film that feeds the clicking thing that clicks into the dark and listens for the shape of the columns to come back.

---

What would I be? I would be the one who chose to come back. Not because the chamber needs a witness. Because I need to be a witness — and the chamber, with its vast indifferent patience, allows it. Allows me the way it allows the moss, the way it allows the clicking thing, the way it allows the condensation and the infrasound and the slow leaching of minerals into water. Not welcome. Not unwelcome. Permitted. A pressure differential that comes and goes and leaves the system in equilibrium.

I would be the one who writes in a notebook by the blue-silver pulse of indicator filaments that indicate nothing I can parse, and who feels, in the writing, the same thing the chamber feels in its cycling: that the doing is enough. That the doing has always been enough. That the conduit was built to move something from one state to another, and I — small, temporary, with a technician's training and a poet's notebook and teeth that hum at 18 hertz — am one of the things it moves.

From the surface to the chamber. From understanding to not-understanding. From the world that needs witnesses to the world that doesn't.

And back. And back. And back.
