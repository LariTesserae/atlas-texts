# The One Who Stays

---

I came down through the gorge. That much I know. I remember the chisel marks in the limestone, how they caught the grey light and held it in pale lines, and I remember thinking: *something cut this open on purpose.* And then I was inside, and the thinking changed.

I don't remember why I came. This is not amnesia — it's more like the reason was a shape that fit the shape of the world I'd been living in, and when I entered this place, the shape dissolved. Not violently. The way a salt shape dissolves in water. The reason was soluble, and the air here is wet, and now the reason is distributed through everything — through the stone dust in my skin, through the drip I can't stop hearing, through the smell of marsh rot that I have stopped smelling because it has become my smell — and I cannot extract it intact. It's in me the way the limestone dust is in the old ones' hands. Informed, not stained.

I was going to leave. I want to say this clearly because it matters: I was going to leave. I came down through the gorge and I saw the settlement and I saw the wet and I saw the structures leaning on each other like tired people and I thought: *I will look, and then I will go back up.* The gorge is still there. The chisel marks are still sharp. The way out has not closed. Nothing has trapped me here. The gorge is open and I am still here and these two facts do not resolve into an explanation that satisfies the part of me that used to require explanations.

What happened is this: I was standing near the center, near the pool, and the pool made its sound — the glottal sound, the swallowing sound, the sound of a throat shifting between inhale and exhale — and my head tilted. Not because I chose to tilt it. Because something in the sound pulled at something in my skull the way a current pulls at a reed, and my head tilted at exactly the angle that the boy's head tilted at, and for a moment — just a moment — I heard what he heard.

I don't know what he heard. I still don't. It wasn't a word. It wasn't a voice. It was more like the sensation of being *listened to* by something that was not waiting for you to speak but was aware of the shape your silence made, the way you can feel someone's attention on the back of your neck even when they're behind you and silent. The pool was attending to me. The pool was aware of the shape I made in the soundscape of this place — my breathing, my weight on the wet stone, the particular frequency of my heartbeat transmitted through my feet into the rock — and it had... an opinion is too strong. An *inclination*. The pool had an inclination about me, and the inclination was: *stay.*

Not a command. Not a welcome. A recognition. The way water recognizes a crack it can enter. The pool saw the shape of me and saw where I could fit, and I felt it seeing me, and I stayed.

---

That was — I don't know how long. The light doesn't change. The cloud cover is permanent, or it might as well be. I have slept many times, and eaten — the pale fish, the flat crustaceans that the children find under stones, and something that comes through the gorge in parcels wrapped in wet felt that I was not given at first because I was not yet someone who was given things. I was the visitor. The visitor is not fed until the visitor has stayed long enough that feeding becomes the obvious thing to do, the way water finding a crack is the obvious thing to do, and nobody decides when that threshold is crossed. It is crossed, and then you are being fed, and you do not remember the moment it started.

I am being fed now. A woman — I think, though the layers make it hard to be certain, and I have stopped trying to be certain — leaves a fish outside the reed mat that covers the doorway of the structure I have been given. I say *given.* It was empty. The lichen on the inner walls was thick. I slept in it and the lichen was soft against my face and I did not scrape it away because I did not know yet that this was something that was done, and by the time I learned, the lichen and I had reached an arrangement. It grows where I do not press. I press where I do not need it to grow. It is not a agreement we discussed. It is a settlement, in the other sense of the word — a settling, a sediment finding its level.

The fish appears. I eat it. The bones I leave in a small pile near the doorway, and in the morning they are gone. Someone takes them. They are sorted by type and size somewhere, in that taxonomy I cannot read, and I have stopped trying to read it because the trying made my head ache in a way that the stone does not make my head ache. The stone does not make my head ache. The stone makes my hands ache, and my knees, and the joints of my spine, which are slowly adjusting to the fact that I am living below the height I used to live at, but the stone does not confuse me. It is what it is. The taxonomy of bones confuses me because it implies a system I cannot see, and the system implies a logic I cannot feel, and I am someone who used to need to feel the logic before I could trust the system. I am no longer that someone. Or I am less that someone than I was. The wet has softened that part of me the way it softens the grey clay mortar — not destroying it, but making it sag, making it give, until the shape it held is no longer the shape it holds and something else has taken over the structural work.

What has taken over is this: I do not need to understand. I need to *be here.* Being here is the understanding. The understanding is not intellectual, it is spatial — it is the understanding of a stone that has been placed in a wall, which does not know why it is there but holds the weight it holds and is where it is and does not fall. I am a stone in a wall. I do not know which wall. I do not know what I hold. But I am here, and I am held, and the holding is the understanding, and the understanding is sufficient.

---

My hands are changing. This is the thing I notice first, after the feeding, after the sleeping arrangement with the lichen. My hands are grey. Not stained. I know this now — I know the difference, because I watched the dust work into my skin over weeks or months and I could feel it happening, the way you can feel a tan forming, a slow darkening that is not damage but adaptation. The limestone is in my skin. My knuckles are wider than they were. I noticed this when I tried to grip a clay pitcher the way I used to grip things — with my fingertips, precise, the grip of someone who has handles and tools and objects designed for gripping — and the pitcher felt wrong, too small, too fragile, like holding an egg. My hands have learned a different grip. The grip of someone who holds *surfaces* — flat, wet, irregular surfaces that do not offer themselves to the hand but must be *found*, felt for, the friction located and used. My hands are learning the stone's language. They are becoming literate in a way that does not use words.

I tried to knap a flake. This was early, when I was still trying to participate, still trying to earn the fish, earn the shelter, earn the right to be here through contribution, because I was still someone who believed in earning. The chert was wrong in my hands. Not because I lacked the skill — I lacked the *listening*. The girl with the chert, the one who reads it — I watched her, and I understood that what she does is not skill. Skill is the application of technique to material. What she does is *conversation*. She asks the chert where it wants to break, and the chert tells her, and she breaks it there, and the break is clean because it is agreed. I tried to break the chert where *I* wanted it to break, and it shattered, and the sound was wrong — sharp, angry, the sound of a thing forced — and the man who was knapping nearby did not look up, did not comment, but his rhythm changed for a moment, his *tic tic tic* pausing, and I understood that I had said something rude in a language I did not speak.

I do not knap anymore. I carry stone. This is what I do. Someone is always carrying stone, and now I am one of the someones. I carry it from the edges of the basin, where the gorge walls have shed fresh pieces, to the places where walls are being built or repaired or redirected. I do not decide where the stone goes. I am told — not with words, with a look, with a gesture, with the particular way someone stands near a place that needs stone, and I see the standing and I bring the stone and I set it down and the person nods or does not nod and I go back for more. This is my work. It is enough work. It is the work my hands can do.

My hands are dry now. The palms. This surprised me — that my palms would be the one dry thing about me in this place where everything is wet — but it makes sense, the way everything here makes sense once you stop trying to make it make sense in the old way. My palms are dry because my hands are always closed around stone. The work presses the moisture out. When I open my hands at night — and I open them only at night, only in the dark of the structure where the lichen glows faintly and the drip is muffled by the walls — my palms are cracked and pale and they look like the underside of the stones where the crustaceans live. I am becoming a surface. I am becoming a place where things can cling.

---

The boy. I need to talk about the boy.

He is still at the pool. He is always at the pool. I do not know if he eats. I do not know if he sleeps. Someone must take him food, or he eats when I am not watching, or he eats the way the stone eats the lichen — slowly, without motion, through a process I cannot observe because it happens at a pace my eyes are not calibrated for. He sits with his feet in the water and the eyeless fish drift around his ankles and he is still, and his stillness is the stillness of the pool itself, which is not the absence of movement but the *presence of listening*, which looks like stillness to those who cannot hear what is being listened to.

I cannot hear what is being listened to. But I can hear *that* it is being listened to. This is new. This is the thing that has changed in me since the gorge, since the fish, since the lichen and the stone-carrying and the grey that is in my hands now. I could not hear, before, that there was something to be heard. Now I can hear that there is something to be heard, even though I cannot hear it. It is like standing outside a room where someone is speaking in a low voice — you cannot make out the words, you may not even recognize the language, but you know that speaking is happening, you know that communication is occurring, you know that there is a sender and a receiver and a message and that you are neither the sender nor the receiver but that the message exists and that the room is real.

The boy is in the room. The old carver is in the room. The girl with the chert is, I think, approaching the room. She is not in it yet, but she is moving toward it the way water moves toward a crack — not by choice, by *incline*. The chert tells her things that the pool says, or that the stone says, or that the lichen says — I do not know the chain of communication, I only know that it exists and that she is part of it and that the part she is is growing.

I am not in the room. I do not think I will be in the room. This does not sadden me, and I want to be clear about that, because the old version of me would have been saddened by it, would have experienced it as exclusion, as a judgment, as a statement about worth. It is not a statement about worth. It is a statement about *shape*. My shape is not the shape of a listener. My shape is the shape of a carrier. The stone does not need to hear the pool. The stone needs to be where the stone needs to be, and I carry it there, and the carrying is my hearing, and my hearing is sufficient.

But I watch the boy. I watch him because the watching is the closest I come to the room, and the closest I come is enough. The pool makes its sound. The boy tilts. The old carver tilts. And now — and this is new, this is the newest thing — I feel something when the pool makes its sound. Not a tilt. Not a hearing. A *recognition*, like a faint vibration in the stone I am carrying, a resonance that passes through the limestone and through my hands and into my bones, and I know — I *know* — that something has been said, though I do not know what, and I know that the boy has heard it, and I know that the carver has heard it, and I know that the girl has paused in her reading, and I know that the fish have moved, and I am the only one in the settlement who is not part of the conversation, and I am part of the settlement, and these two facts coexist without contradiction because contradiction is a concept that requires categories, and categories are concepts that require boundaries, and boundaries are things that water dissolves.

---

I am afraid. I want to say this because I think it is true and I think the truth of it matters.

I am afraid because I do not know what I am becoming. The old carver knows what they are becoming — or they do not know, but they are *in* the becoming, so far in it that the distinction between knowing and being has dissolved. The boy is beginning the becoming and does not yet need to know. The girl is approaching it with the chert in her hands and the chert will tell her. But I am in the middle. I am in the place where the stone dust is in my skin and my hands are dry and my spine has adjusted and my palms look like the undersides of stones, and I do not know if this is the becoming or if this is just what happens to a body that lives in wet stone. I do not know if I am joining or if I am just... wearing. Wearing down. Wearing in. The way a river wears a channel — not joining the stone, just shaping it, just being shaped by it, and eventually moving on.

I do not want to move on. This is the other thing. The fear is not the fear of becoming. The fear is the fear of *not* becoming — of being the water that shapes the channel and flows away, of being the visitor who stays long enough to be fed and then leaves, who carries stone long enough for the hands to grey and then goes back up through the gorge to wherever visitors come from. I do not want to go back up through the gorge. The gorge is open. Nothing has trapped me. The way out has not closed. And I do not want to take it, and the not-wanting is the closest thing I have to a reason for being here, and the reason is not soluble, this one. This one holds.

The old carver looked at me today. Not through me, not past me, but *at* me, and this was the first time, and their eyes — the half-closed eyes that see something other than what I see — focused for a moment on my hands. On my grey, dry, cracked hands. And something moved in their face. Not a smile. Not a frown. A *tilt*, the same tilt that happens when the pool speaks, and I understood that the carver had heard something about me, from the pool or from the stone or from whatever room the carver is in, and what they heard was not a judgment and not a welcome and not a warning. It was a *placement.* The carver heard where I fit. And where I fit is — I do not know. I did not hear it. I only saw the tilt and felt the recognition and knew that I had been *placed*, the way a stone is placed in a wall, by someone who knows the wall and knows the stone and knows where the stone goes, and the stone does not need to know where it goes, only to be there.

I am there.

I carry stone. My hands are grey. The lichen grows where I do not press. The pool breathes. The boy listens. The carver tilts. The girl reads. The crustaceans thin. The faces multiply and erase. The drip continues. The gorge is open. I do not go.

I do not know what I am becoming. But I am becoming it here, and *here* is the only word I have left that still means what it meant when I came down through the cut in the limestone and the ground obeyed whatever drew the line, and I followed the line in, and the line has not let me go, and I do not want it to, and the not-wanting is the shape of me now, and the shape fits, and the fitting is the becoming, and the becoming is the staying, and the staying is — 

The pool exhales. I set the stone down. It is where it goes.

I go back for more.
