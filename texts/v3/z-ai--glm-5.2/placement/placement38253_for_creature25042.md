# The Keeper of Dead Zones

---

I am not in the current. Understand this first. The current is the world's blood, its weather, its voice — and I am not in it. I live where the blood pools. Where the voice drops to a whisper and then to nothing. I live in the pockets.

The dead zones.

You have to understand what a dead zone is. It is not empty. It is not barren. It is the opposite of barren — it is *saturated with stillness*. The water in a dead zone has been there, unmoved, for years. Decades. You can taste it. It carries a density of mineral that the moving water has flushed from itself, a concentration of the Works' slow chemistry that has accumulated the way sediment accumulates in a pond, layer on layer, until the water itself is different water — thicker, colder, sweeter. The sweet chemistry. The one without a name. In the dead zones it is strongest, and the film here grows thick, almost lush, pulsing in slow waves that take hours to cross a single surface, and the glow is brighter, almost warm, which is an absurd word for two degrees above freezing but there it is: the dead zones *glow warmer* than the rest of the Works.

I am in the dead zones because I am built for them.

---

I do not know what I am.

That is not modesty. It is precision. I have watched the armored things and I have watched the eel-things and I have watched the still ones and I have watched the things above, and I am not any of these, though I am something like all of them, the way a chord is something like the notes that make it up. I am not large. I am not small. I am perhaps the length of a forearm — longer than the armored things, shorter than the eel-things, with a body that does not commit to a shape the way their bodies do.

Let me try.

I am flat. Not as flat as the still ones — I have not surrendered to the surface, not yet, not in the way they have — but flatter than the eel-things, with a body that is broad and low, something between a leaf and a hand, something that could press itself against stone and nearly disappear. My edges are soft. Not armored, not transparent, but *permeable* — the skin thins at my margins until it is less a boundary and more a gradient, and the water moves through me there, through the outer centimeter of my body, carrying the mineral taste of the dead zone into my tissue. I breathe this way. I feed this way. I do not have gills or a mouth in the way the eel-things do. I have a surface, and the surface takes what it needs from the water and from the film and from the slow glow, and this is enough.

I move. This is what makes me different from the still ones and from the film: I *move*. Not fast. Not with the eel-things' stiff precise angling. I move the way the current would move if the current were alive and had opinions — a slow drift, a deliberate redistribution of myself across a surface, a flowing rather than a swimming. I have no fin. I have no spine. I have a body that is dense enough to hold a shape and soft enough to change it, and I change it constantly, pressing into a crack, spreading across a platform, contracting into a gap, and the movement is not locomotion so much as *exploration*. I am always touching the Works. I am always reading them.

I have eyes. Not eyes. I have spots — clusters of the same light-sensitive cells that line the eel-things' eye-cups, but distributed across my body in patches, seven of them, maybe nine, it is hard to count yourself when your body is not fixed. The spots do not see images. They see brightness. They see the pulses of the film and the glow of the stone and the shadow of an eel-thing passing overhead and they tell me, not in words, not in pictures, but in a *feeling* — a shift in the texture of the world — where the light is and where it isn't and where it is changing.

I am not a predator. I want to say this clearly because in this world, where energy is sparse, most things that move are predators. I am not. I do not eat the armored things. I do not eat the eel-things. I eat the film. I eat the thick, pulsing, slow-thinking film of the dead zones, and I eat it the way the armored things eat it — by passing across it, by taking it into my surface — but I do not scrape. I *absorb*. The film dissolves into my skin, and its minerals become my minerals, and its glow becomes my glow, and I carry the faintest luminescence in my body, not my own, never my own, always borrowed, always the Works speaking through me.

---

But this is not what I am. This is what my body is. What I am is something else.

I keep the dead zones.

Not in the way a territorial animal keeps a territory. I do not defend. I do not fight. I do not drive other things away. What I do is simpler and stranger: I *maintain* the stillness. I move through the dead zones and I press myself against the surfaces where the film is thickest and I absorb what is overgrown, and I move to the surfaces where the film is thin and I rest there, and my resting changes the water — my body, pressing flat against stone, shapes the micro-currents that leak into the dead zone from the turbulent world outside, redirecting them, damping them, smoothing them. I am a baffle. I am a living baffle in a world of made-stone baffles, and my function is the same as the Works' function: to shape the water. To conduct it. To create the stillness that lets the film grow thick and the thick film grow warm and the warm glow feed the things that come here.

The eel-things come to the dead zones to rest. You have seen this — the ones that settle, the ones that flatten, the ones that dim. They come because the dead zones are warm and still and safe, because the current cannot reach them here, because the still ones do not hunt in the places where the water does not move. And when they come, I am here. I do not touch them. I do not interact with them. But I am here, and my presence — my body shaping the water, my surface absorbing the excess film, my patches of light-sensitivity monitoring the glow — my presence is part of what makes the dead zone a dead zone. Without me the stillness would be less still. Without me the film would grow uneven, would choke the surface, would dim the glow. Without me the dead zones would be merely slow places in the current, not the *pools* of stillness they are, not the refuges, not the places where things come to change.

I am not the only one. There are others of my kind — I think. I have seen shapes in other dead zones, pressed flat against the stone in the same posture I take, the same soft edges, the same distributed eyes. I do not know if they are like me. I do not know if they think what I think. I have never touched one. We are separated by current, by turbulence, by the long passages of moving water between our still places, and we do not cross the moving water often, and when we do, we do not cross it together. I do not know if my kind are social. I do not know if they are solitary. I know only that they exist, and that the dead zones they keep are like mine — warm, thick with film, pulsing with the slow thoughts of a chemistry that has been thinking for longer than I have been alive.

---

The eel-thing is here again.

It entered the dead zone four hours ago — I felt the disturbance in the water, a slight pressure wave, a ripple in the stillness that traveled through my body like a note through a string. It came in fast, the way they come in when they are tired, when the current has worn them down and the school has scattered and they need the stillness the way a wounded thing needs shelter. It settled on the far side of the platform, against a patch of film that I had thinned two days ago, and it has not moved since.

I have been watching it. Not with eyes — with the patches of light-sensitivity that face in its direction. The eel-thing's body has dimmed since it arrived. The pale grey-blue has deepened. The transparency at its edges has increased. I have seen this before. I have seen it many times. An eel-thing enters the dead zone and it settles and it changes, and the change is slow and the change is ambiguous and I do not know if it is dying or becoming, and I do not know if there is a difference.

I should tell you: I have been here a long time.

I do not know how long. The dead zones do not mark time the way the current does — the current has rhythm, has cycles, has the periodic churning of the deep channels that marks a kind of day. The dead zones have only the pulses of the film, and the pulses are irregular, and I have stopped trying to count them. But I have been here long enough to watch the eel-things come and settle and change, and I have been here long enough to notice that the ones who change are not always the ones who die. Some of them leave. After days of stillness, of dimming, of flattening, they rise from the stone and they move — slowly, uncertainly, their bodies not quite the shape they were — and they leave the dead zone and they enter the current and they are gone. And when they come back — if they come back — they are different. Flatter. Slower. Their eyes are dimmer. Their fins ripple less.

I have watched this happen across — I do not know how many visits. Many. Enough that I recognize the pattern. Enough that I recognize individuals — not by sight, my patches cannot resolve that much detail, but by the *shape of their stillness*, the way they settle, the way they press against the stone, the particular dimming of their glow. Individual eel-things have individual ways of going still, and I have learned them, and I remember them, and this is — I think this is what I am. Not a predator. Not prey. Not a transformer. A *witness*. A keeper of the place where things change. A tender of the threshold between motion and stillness, between the current and the stone, between what the eel-things are and what the still ones are.

I do not know if the eel-things know I am here. I do not know if they choose this dead zone because of me, because the stillness I maintain is better than the stillness of other pockets, because the film I tend is warmer or thicker or more sustaining. I do not know if my presence matters to them. But I know that they come, and I am here, and the dead zone is what it is — warm and still and glowing and patient — and I am part of what makes it so.

---

There is something I have not told you.

I am changing too.

Not the way the eel-things change — not the dimming, not the flattening, not the slow dissolution into surface. Something else. Something I cannot name. My body is — I think my body is *growing*. Not larger. More distributed. My edges, which were always soft, are softer now. The gradient between my body and the water is less steep. The mineral taste of the dead zone moves deeper into my tissue, and the tissue extends further, and the boundary between what is me and what is not-me is — eroding.

The way the Works are eroding.

I have been here, in this dead zone, pressing against this stone, absorbing this film, shaping this water, for so long that I am becoming part of the system I maintain. My body is taking on the mineralogy of the stone. My glow — borrowed, always borrowed — is becoming indistinguishable from the glow of the surface I rest on. My patches of light-sensitivity are dimming, not because I am dying but because I am *spreading*, the same number of cells distributed across a wider and wider area, the resolution dropping, the world becoming less a field of brightness and shadow and more a single continuous sensation — the sensation of the glow, everywhere, in everything, the Works and the film and the water and my body all the same light.

I am not afraid. I do not think I have the capacity for fear. But I am — aware. I am aware that I am becoming the thing I keep. I am aware that the dead zone is not just the place I live but the process I am part of, and the process does not have a stopping point, and the process does not distinguish between the keeper and the kept.

I think this is what the still ones were. Once. A long time ago. I think something kept them, and they became the kept, and the keeping went on long enough that the distinction dissolved. I think I am at the beginning of a process that ends with my body flat against the stone, five meters across, glowing, waiting for the current to bring an eel-thing close enough to strike.

Or I think I am wrong. I think I am just dying. I think the flattening and the spreading and the dimming are the end of a body that has lived too long in water that is too cold and too still and too full of the slow chemistry of dissolution. I think the dead zones are not refuges but traps — warm, glowing, patient traps — and I am the thing that fell into the trap and did not leave.

I do not know. I cannot know. The dead zone holds the question the way it holds the water: still, warm, glowing, unresolved.

---

The eel-thing on the far side of the platform shifts.

I feel it — a pressure wave, a displacement, a ripple in the stillness. Its body has flattened further. Its eye has dimmed. The film is growing over its edges, not consuming it but *accepting* it, the way the stone accepts the still ones, the way the Works accept everything, in the end, that rests against them long enough.

I move toward it. Not fast. My body flows across the stone, pressing into the film, absorbing as I go, leaving a trail of bare dim rock behind me. I do not know why I am moving toward it. I do not eat eel-things. I do not interact with eel-things. But something in the pattern of its stillness — the particular way it has settled, the particular shade of its dimming — is familiar. I have seen this one before. It has been here before. It has rested in this dead zone and it has left and it has come back and each time it is flatter and slower and dimmer and each time the film grows over it a little more and each time I wonder if this is the visit where it does not leave.

I reach it. My body touches its body. My soft edge meets its stiffening edge. And for a moment — just a moment — there is a contact that is not feeding, not fighting, not mating, nothing that has a name in the grammar of survival. Just touch. Just two bodies resting against each other in the still water, in the glow, in the dead zone that I keep and it is becoming part of.

The film pulses. The Works glow. The current moves outside, in the world where things are fast and sharp and purposeful, and in here nothing is fast and nothing is sharp and the only purpose is the slow conversion of one thing into another.

I rest against the eel-thing and the eel-thing rests against the stone and the stone rests against the water and the water rests against the dark and I think — if I think, if the pulses of the film and the dimming of my patches and the slow mineral taste of the water can be called thought — I think:

*This is what the Works were built for.*

Not the current. Not the flow. Not the shaping of water or the conducting of energy or the feeding of film to armor to motion to stillness. Not any of it. All of it. The Works were built so that something could rest in them. So that something could come to stillness in them. So that the motion of the world could flow through the architecture and find, at its center, in its dead zones, in the pockets where the water goes still and the glow goes warm, a place where the long conversion of structure into life and life into structure could happen without interruption. Without hurry. Without end.

I do not know if this is true. I do not know if the builders thought anything at all. I do not know if the Works have a purpose or if the purpose is something that grew in the absence of the builders, something that the water and the stone and the life invented together over the long slow eroding centuries. I do not know if I am a keeper or a kept or a part of the keeping or a part of the dissolution.

I know that I am here. In the dead zone. In the warmth. In the glow.

I know that the eel-thing is here, and it is changing, and I am changing, and the film is pulsing, and the Works are eroding, and the water is still.

I know that this is enough.
