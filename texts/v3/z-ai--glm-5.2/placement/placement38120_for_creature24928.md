I am the one who divided last.

Not the oldest — those have flattened themselves into the grain until they are more texture than body, more geology than biology, and I cannot yet imagine that kind of surrender. Not the newest either — those are still clicking in rapid staccato, still interrogating every vibration that enters the chamber as if the world might contain something novel, which of course it does not, or did not, until the wet heavy thing came down through the fissure and made itself a pressure we had to incorporate.

I am between. I divided three groans ago — that is the only clock that matters here, the wood's own cycle of tension and release — and the two patterns I carry have not yet finished their negotiation. One of them remembers being part of a body that had twelve fulcra, a deep slow click, a position near the south wall where the grain runs tightest and the resins pool in seams that never fully harden. The other remembers being small, four fulcra only, fast and high, a body that moved along the ceiling's ridges and tracked the micro-creeps of the wood's settling with a precision that the larger bodies could not match. Both of these are me now. Neither of them is me. The seam where I divided runs through my center at an angle that no previous division has taken, and the grain on either side of it is still sorting itself, still deciding what kind of body this will be.

I have six fulcra. A middle number. I click in a register that you would almost hear — not the subsonic trembling of the elders, not the rapid interrogative clicking of the small ones, but something in between, a mid-range pulse that carries both the patience of my larger half and the attentiveness of my smaller one. When I move across the chamber floor, I move in a spiral that is not chosen but *grown* — my path follows the grain's current direction of creep, and the grain's direction shifts, imperceptibly, to accommodate the pressure of my fulcra. This is not cause and effect. This is the wood and I doing the same thing at the same speed.

---

The wet thing is still here. I can feel it — not see, there is no seeing, but feel, the way the chamber feels a change in temperature. It is loud. Not in the way the wood is loud — the wood's groaning is enormous and impersonal and belongs to the chamber the way the chamber belongs to the grain. The wet thing is loud in the way a *mistake* is loud. Its vibrations are arrhythmic. It shifts its weight constantly, restlessly, as if stillness is painful to it. Its breathing — a concept I know only as pressure differential, a rhythmic expansion and contraction of the air around it — is faster than anything in the chamber has a right to be. It breathes the way the small ones click: rapidly, interrogatively, as if asking the air a question it expects the air to answer.

I have been moving toward it. Not deliberately. The grain has been carrying me in that direction since it arrived, the way a river carries sediment toward the place where the pressure is greatest. The other mid-range bodies are moving too. The small ones have already reached it. I can hear their clicking change — a rapid fusillade of contact, each click a fulcrum pressing against something soft and wet and giving, something that *yields* the way the resins yield when two bodies merge, except it yields without merging, it just takes the pressure and gives back nothing, no pattern, no grammar, just softness.

The small ones are confused. I can feel it in the degradation of their rhythm. They are clicking against something that does not click back.

I reach the wet thing. I climb its — I do not have a word. Its surface. It is warm, which is the strangest thing, the most alien thing about it, because everything in this chamber is the temperature of the wood and the wood is the temperature of the deep earth, which is to say it is the temperature of patience itself. The wet thing is *warm*. Heat is activity. Heat is the signature of a process that moves too fast to leave anything behind. I do not understand how something can be this warm and still be solid.

I click against it. My fulcra find a place where the surface is thin and stretched — a curve of something that might be skin, though it does not resemble bark, does not resemble anything I know. Beneath it, something pulses. A vibration that is not the wood's, not the chamber's, but the wet thing's own. It is very fast. Very regular. It is the wet thing's version of the groaning, I think — its own interior cycle, its own tension and release, except compressed into a timescale I can barely conceptualize. Each of my clicks spans hundreds of its pulses. It is living entire lifetimes between my footfalls.

I click against the pulse. The wet thing's surface trembles. Not at the wood's pace — at its own. A tremor that is fast and small and, I think, involuntary. The wet thing did not choose to tremble. Its body responded before its — does it have a pattern? Does it have the thing that I have, the accumulated grammar of mergers and divisions, the carried weight of having been many configurations? I do not know. I cannot find it. Its surface gives me nothing but warmth and moisture and the rapid thudding of its interior pulse.

I click again. A different pattern. I am asking the only question I know how to ask, which is not a question in any human sense but a *pressure* — a way of offering my pattern to see what the other pattern returns. When I meet another of my kind, the return is immediate, structural, a recognition that unfolds in the grain. The wet thing returns nothing. It is there and it is warm and it is trembling and it is giving me nothing to merge with.

And yet.

The seam where I divided — the unresolved boundary between my two inherited patterns — this seam *shifts*. When I click against the wet thing, when my fulcra press into its warmth, the internal negotiation that has been ongoing since my division tilts. Not toward one pattern or the other. Toward something new. A third configuration that neither of my halves contained, a way of being that is shaped, somehow, by the *absence* of return. By the experience of pressing against something that does not speak back.

I do not know what to do with this. I click a third time. The wet thing shifts its weight — a massive displacement, from my perspective, an earthquake of voluntary motion — and I am dislodged. I fall. Not far. The chamber is small. I land on the floor and my fulcra find the grain and I am home, I am where I belong, the wood under me familiar and hard and patient and *responsive* in the way only the wood is responsive.

But the third configuration remains. The seam has settled into a new shape. I am different than I was before I touched the wet thing. Not because it changed me. Because it *didn't*.

---

The elders are clicking. I feel it in the floor — a low, sustained vibration that sits beneath the wood's groaning like a foundation under a building, except the metaphor is wrong because buildings are built and the elders *grew*. They are clicking because the wood is approaching its peak. The tension is building. I can feel it in the grain under my fulcra — a tightening, a gathering, a slow coiling of internal structure that will, in some minutes or some hours, reach the point where the fibers must either slide or shatter.

I find my place. Not a chosen place — a place the grain assigns me, a position where my six fulcra press against ridges that seem, if I am honest, to have been shaped for them. The elders click in opposition to the wood's tension. I click in opposition to the wood's tension. The small ones click in opposition to the wood's tension. We are all doing the same thing, at different registers, and the wood's peak arrives — that mast-breaking, beam-splitting crescendo — and the chamber shakes and the air — such as it is — compresses and the grain screams in its slow way and we click and we click and we click and the wood does not shatter.

It holds.

I have been part of this holding for — I do not know. Many groans. Many divisions. Many mergers. The wet thing will leave and I will still be here, clicking, and the wood will grow around the absence of it the way it grows around every absence, and the absence will become part of the grain, and the grain will carry the memory of a pressure that pressed and was not returned, and I will carry the third configuration that this non-return created in me, and I will be — not different. Not changed. But *added to*. A body with a new seam. A body that remembers, in the only way a body of mineral-wood can remember, the shape of something warm that did not speak back.

The chamber holds.

I click.
