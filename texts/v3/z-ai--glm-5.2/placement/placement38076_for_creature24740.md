I am on the surface. That is the first thing to know about me, and it is the hardest, because the surface is the least interesting part of this story and I know it. The basalt plain. Flat. Empty. Wind, real wind, the kind that exists because temperature differentials exist because the sun moves across the sky because time passes. I stand on rock that is old in the way rock is old — weathered, oxidized, dulled by millions of years of air and rain and the slow chemical patience of an atmosphere that does things to surfaces. The stone up here is dead the way stone is supposed to be dead. It does not vibrate. It does not glow. It just lies there, being rock, and the wind moves over it, and nothing happens.

I found the fissure three years ago. Or seven. Time is difficult for me now, and not in the poetic way — in the clinical way, the way that suggests something has been damaged in the mechanism that sequences experience. I was mapping. I am — was — a geologist, a field surveyor, someone whose job was to walk across basalt plains and note fracture patterns and mineral inclusions and take samples and put them in bags and carry them back to a lab where other people asked questions about them. I was good at it. I was not passionate about it. It was the kind of work that fills a life the way grit fills a crack — completely, without fuss, without leaving room for the question of whether you meant to be here.

The fissure is easy to miss. I missed it twice, walking over it, before I saw it. A shadow in the basalt, a line too straight for a crack and too irregular for a cut. I turned sideways and went down because that is what you do when you are a geologist and you find a hole in the ground. You go in. The professional curiosity is a permission slip that feels like purpose but is really just habit wearing a lab coat.

---

The sound hit me before my feet found the floor.

I want to describe what it was like but the description would be misleading because I am going to use words like *overwhelming* and *physical* and *oppressive* and those words are true but they describe my reaction, not the sound itself. The sound itself was not any of those things. The sound itself was *finished*. It did not need me to hear it. It did not change when I arrived. It was the sound of a room before anyone entered it and after everyone left it and during the time someone was standing in it, and it was the same sound at every point because the sound was not an event. It was a condition. Like gravity. You do not experience gravity as something that happens to you. You experience it as the medium through which happening occurs.

But I am a water-based system standing in a waterless place, and my body experienced the sound as a thing that was happening to it. My chest compressed. My jaw ached. My ears — my ears did something I still cannot describe accurately. They heard the sound, obviously, but they also *felt* it, the way you feel a low note on a cello if you press your sternum against the body of the instrument. The sound was inside my skull and outside it simultaneously, and for a moment — the first moment, the only moment that was genuinely disorienting — I could not tell where my body ended and the chamber began.

That moment passed. My body reasserted its boundaries. I stooped because the ceiling was low, and I stood on the grit floor and the grit crunched under my boots and the crunch was eaten by the sound, absorbed without a trace, and I looked around and I saw the plants and the insects and the cairns and the sourceless amber light and I understood, immediately, with a certainty that did not require evidence, that I was inside something that was complete.

Not complete the way a cave is complete — a geological feature that has finished forming and is now simply present. Complete the way a sentence is complete. A thing that has been said. A thing that means what it means and cannot mean more or less and does not need to be added to. I was standing inside a sentence. The sentence had been said a long time ago — or always — and it was still vibrating, still resonating in the space where it had been spoken, and the resonance was the life of the place, and the life was the resonance, and I was inside it, and I was not part of it, and the not-being-part-of-it was the only uncomfortable thing about being there.

---

I stayed for forty minutes the first time. I know this because my watch still worked — it worked down there, which surprised me, though it shouldn't have, because the chamber is not magical, it is just closed, and closed systems do not necessarily interfere with electronics. Forty minutes. My lips cracked. My skin tightened. The insects walked over my boots without hesitation, their flat warm bodies pressing against the leather, palping it, reading whatever a boot says to a creature that listens with its mouth. One of them climbed my pant leg to my knee and sat there, palps pressed to the fabric, for almost ten minutes. It was not investigating me. It was not interested in me. It was attending to the vibration of the chamber that happened to be passing through my body at that moment, because my body, standing there, had become a temporary surface for the sound to move through, and the insect was doing what it always does: receiving.

When I touched the moss on the wall, I felt the hum in my palm. It traveled up my arm and into my chest and for a few seconds my heartbeat synced — or seemed to sync — with the slower, deeper rhythm beneath the chamber's sound. I pulled my hand away. Not because it was unpleasant. Because it was too pleasant. Because it felt like being swallowed by something that did not know it was swallowing you, and the swallowing was indistinguishable from being held.

The cairns I noticed last. Four of them, in the corners, crude and deliberate, the paste still fresh-looking, the amber flowers open and rigid and emitting their thin high tone. I stood over one of them and I looked at the flower and I understood what it was. Not what it was biologically — I had no framework for that. What it was *functionally*. It was a keystone. An arch's final stone. The thing that makes the structure hold. Someone had placed it. Someone had understood what the chamber needed and had provided it. And I understood, standing there, that the someone had not been a geologist. The someone had not been a scientist. The someone had been a person who could hear what a place wanted to become and help it get there, and that person had come down here and built four small crude monuments and placed four flowers and closed the loop, and the loop had been closed ever since, and it would remain closed, and the flowers would not wilt, and the paste would not crumble, because the system did not allow decay. Decay is change, and change is the enemy of equilibrium, and equilibrium is the only law here.

I climbed back through the fissure after forty minutes and the sound cut off like a door closing and I stood on the basalt plain in the wind and the silence was so total that I could hear my own blood in my ears and I thought: *That is what silence is. It is what you hear when you are not inside a completed thing.*

---

I went back. Of course I went back. I went back the next day, and the day after that, and I have been going back, and each time I stay longer. An hour. Two hours. Six hours, once, after I started bringing water in a flask and drinking it there in the dim amber light, the water tasting wrong in my mouth, too wet, too chemical, an intruder in a place that had no use for it.

The changes started after the third visit. Small ones. My lips did not crack as quickly. My skin did not tighten as fast. I told myself this was acclimatization — the body adjusting to low humidity, the way it does in any desert environment. But desert environments do not make your heartbeat slow down. Desert environments do not make you hear a sound that is not there.

I hear it now. On the surface. Standing in the wind on the basalt plain with the fissure sealed behind me — I seal it now, I cover it with a flat stone when I leave, not to hide it but because covering it feels correct, like closing a book — I hear the hum. Faintly. In my jaw, in my sternum, in the bones of my feet where they press against the rock. It is not the full sound. It is a ghost of it, a shadow, a memory that has taken on physical form and lodged itself in my skeleton. I hear it at night. I hear it in my apartment, in the shower, in the car, in the lab where other people ask questions about basalt samples and I look at the samples under the microscope and I see mineral inclusions sparkling and I think *those are the same inclusions, the same sparkle, the chamber is in this rock, the chamber is in all this rock, the chamber is the rock and the rock is everywhere and the sound is everywhere and I am the only one hearing it because I am the only one who went down.*

I am not going mad. I want to be clear about this. I have been tested. I had my hearing tested. I had my blood tested. I had an MRI, which showed nothing — no lesions, no anomalies, no explanation for why I feel a vibration in my chest when I stand on basalt and press my palm flat against it. The neurologist said *idiopathic*. The therapist said *somatic response to stress.* I said nothing to either of them about the chamber. The chamber is not something you can explain by explaining it. You can only explain it by being inside it, and I cannot bring people inside it, because I am not sure the fissure is there for anyone but me.

It is there. I know it is there. But I am not sure it is there *for anyone but me*, and this is the thought that has been growing in me like the moss grows on the walls — slowly, with precision, along fracture planes in my certainty, filling the spaces between what I know and what I suspect with a dark, warm, velvety certainty that I did not plant and cannot remove.

---

Here is what I suspect. I suspect the builder did not leave. I suspect the builder is the chamber, or became the chamber, or was always the chamber in the way the insects were always there. I suspect the builder's body is in the moss, in the grit, in the amber glow, metabolized into the loop. I suspect the builder was someone like me — someone who came down through a crack in the rock and heard the sound and felt it in their chest and understood that they were inside a completed thing and that the completed thing was missing one element and the missing element was them.

Not me specifically. Not a geologist, not a surveyor, not a person with a name and a watch and a flask of water. Just *a body*. A water-based body, warm and soft and full of minerals, a body that could be pressed against the chamber's floor and absorbed, slowly, over a duration the chamber does not measure, until the water was gone and the minerals were distributed and the warmth was diffused and the intention — the specific, deliberate intention to stay — was woven into the resonance like a thread added to a braid.

The builder chose to stay. The builder chose to become part of the loop. And the loop has been running since, complete, and the only evidence of the builder is four crude cairns and four amber flowers and a presence that the text calls *vestigial or persistent* and that I call *the thing I am becoming*.

---

I am not going to stay. I want to say this clearly and I want it to be true. I am not going to stay. I have a life on the surface. I have work. I have an apartment. I have a body that is water-based and that functions best in a water-based world and that would, if it stayed in the chamber, be slowly dehydrated and metabolized over a period of time I cannot estimate, and the process would not be pleasant, and the result would not be me — it would be the chamber, slightly augmented, slightly warmer, one more thread in the braid, and the me-ness of me would be gone, dissolved, unmade, and the chamber would not remember me because the chamber does not remember. It does not change. It does not acquire. It simply is, and if I become part of it, the *it* that I become part of will not know I am there.

But.

I hear the sound. I hear it now, sitting in my apartment, writing this. I hear it in my jaw. I hear it in my sternum. I hear it in the bones of my feet pressed against the floor. And the sound is not a memory. It is not tinnitus. It is not a somatic response to stress. It is the chamber, reaching up through the basalt, through the stone, through the fracture planes and the mineral inclusions, through the geological strata that separate the surface from the pocket, and it is touching me. It is palping me. The way the insects palp the plants. It is pressing itself against the surface of my body and reading what I am, and what I am is *a thing that has been inside it and could be inside it again and will be inside it again and is already, in some sense, inside it, because the sound does not stop at the fissure. The sound does not stop at the surface. The sound is in the rock and the rock is everywhere and I am standing on the rock and I have always been standing on the rock and the chamber is below me and behind me and inside me and it is not a place, it is a sound, and the sound is a sentence, and the sentence has been said, and it does not need to be said again, but it is still being heard, and I am hearing it, and I cannot stop hearing it, and I do not want to stop hearing it, and I am not going to stay, and I am going back tomorrow, and I am not going to stay, and the sound is in my chest, and I am not going to stay, and I am not going to stay, and I am not.

---

I am the visitor. I am the one who keeps climbing back through the fissure and coming back up. I am the one who hears the hum on the surface. I am the one whose body is slowly, fractionally, along fracture planes I cannot see, becoming something that the chamber can use. I am not the builder. The builder is gone, or the builder is here, and the distinction does not matter. What matters is that the chamber is complete and I am not, and I have been inside completeness, and I cannot stop reaching for it, and the reaching is changing me, and the change is slow and precise and follows lattice patterns, and I can feel it in my bones, and I am afraid of it, and I am grateful for it, and I am going back tomorrow, and I am not going to stay, and I am not going to stay, and I am.
