I would be the one who almost wonders.

Not the one who wonders — that's too strong a word for this place, too active, too hungry. Wonder requires a gap between what is and what could be, and there is no gap here. The channels are the channels. The fungi are the fungi. The sound is the sound. But *almost* — that word has a softness to it, a dampness, and it fits this world the way everything here fits: without force, without edge.

Let me explain what I mean, and let me take time, because everything here takes time, and taking time is not patience exactly, it's just the pace at which things happen when nothing is in a hurry.

---

I would be one of the basin-tenders. Not because I chose it — no one here chooses; you just find your hands know a certain work and your hands do it — but because the basins are where the water becomes itself. The channels carry water, yes, but the water in the channels is always going somewhere, always arriving, always leaving. In the basins it stops. It sits. It becomes clear. It reflects the gold light and the pale stone and the faces of the wading creatures that stand in it like thoughts that have decided to stay. I would tend the basins because I am drawn to the moment when something settles.

My hands would be in the water constantly. This is how I know the colonies — by their firmness, their warmth, the particular way they release from the stone when they are ready. My fingertips would have the deepest calluses, the palm-patterns of someone who reaches into water and feels the living things beneath it and knows, before any other hand knows, when they are ready to be taken. The other basin-tenders would know my touch-signature the way they know their own — a particular pressure, a particular warmth, a particular slowness in the way my fingers move across a palm when I am communicating something I have felt in the deep water that no one else could feel yet.

I would be older. Not old — the word barely applies here — but dense, the way the text describes: compressed by years of the same touch, the same stone, the same sound. My body would be the temperature of the place. My skin would be soft and translucent and the blood would show at my wrists like the blue lines in the pale stone. I would navigate by feel, by the vibration of the stone under my broad feet, by the particular pitch of the drumming from the basin that is mine — not mine in the sense of owned, but mine in the sense of *the one I tend, the one whose sound I know so well that if it changed by a single vibration I would feel it in my soles before I felt it in my ears.*

And here is where the *almost* comes in.

---

There is a basin at the eastern edge of the settlement, where the buildings thin and the channels narrow to capillary threads and the mist thickens. It is smaller than the central basins — barely wider than my armspan — and shallower, the water barely covering my ankles when I wade in. The wading creature that lives in it is small too, and its drumming is high and quick, a nervous pulse compared to the deep, resonant boom of the central basins. I tend this basin. I have always tended this basin. Or I have tended it for as long as I have been dense enough to tend the basins, which is as long as anything here measures.

The fungal colony in this basin grows on the eastern side, where the water is shallowest and the stone is warmest. I know its shape. I know its texture. I know the way it clings to the stone and the way it releases. I know it the way I know my own hands.

But sometimes — and *sometimes* is a word that barely works here, because it implies a moment distinct from other moments, and moments here are not distinct — but sometimes, when I am wading in this basin with my hands in the water and the small wading creature drumming beside me and the gold light sitting on everything like a skin, I feel something in the colony that I cannot communicate with my hands.

It is not a difference in the colony. The colony is the same. The firmness is the same. The warmth is the same. The texture is the same. But my hands — my dense, sensitive, basin-tender hands — feel something in the *feeling* of touching it. As though the act of touching, which I have done ten thousand times, which I have done since my hands were small and taught, has become slightly — not different, but *transparent*. As though the touch itself has worn thin from repetition, and through it I can almost feel something that is not the colony, not the water, not the stone.

I do not have a word for what this is. There are no words here. There is touch, and touch is precise, and touch can say *the colony is ready* or *the channel is narrowing* or *I am here, I am here.* But touch cannot say *I almost felt something else.* The language was not made for this because the language was made by a world that does not have an *else.*

So I do not communicate it. I touch the colony. I feel the almost-feeling. I harvest what is ready. I signal with my hands. The harvesters come. The material is distributed. The water still flows. The sound is still the sound.

---

I think what I am almost feeling is this: the weight of the now.

Not the weight as burden — the people here are not burdened; they are present, and presence is not heavy when it is all there is. But the weight as *mass*, as *density*, as the accumulated pressure of a now that has been now for so long that the now itself has become a thing, a substance, a material that you can feel with your hands if your hands are sensitive enough and old enough and have been in the water long enough. The way the stone has weight. The way the water has weight. The now has weight, and I almost feel it, and the almost-feeling is the closest thing I have to what another world might call wonder.

It does not make me unhappy. It does not make me restless. It does not make me want to leave, or change, or find what is beyond the mist. It does not make me anything. It is a feeling, and it passes — no, it does not pass, because passing implies it was here and then was not — it *recedes*, the way water recedes into stone, absorbed, distributed, gone nowhere, just part of the substrate now. And I continue tending the basin, and my hands continue feeling the colony, and the small wading creature continues its quick, high drumming, and the sound holds everything, and the light holds everything, and I am held.

---

If you found me here, you would not know me from anyone else. You would see a short, pale, dense person standing ankle-deep in a small basin at the edge of a settlement that thins into mist, with her hands in the water and her eyes open and her hair lying flat against her skull in wet, colorless strands. You would see the small wading creature beside me, its teardrop body still, its wide flat mouth filtering, its high drumming a thread in the wall of sound. You would see the gold light sitting on everything. You would feel the sound in your sternum. You would feel the warmth in the stone through the soles of your shoes, if you were wearing shoes, which you would not be, because no one here wears shoes, because feet need to feel the ground.

You might reach out and touch my arm — because that is how you speak here, and you would have learned this already, or you would not have, and either way you would be here. And I would feel your hand, and your hand would have a signature I had never felt before — a texture, a warmth, a pressure that was not in the pattern of this place — and my hands would respond, and the touch-language would flow between us, and I would tell you, in three precise touches, what I tell everyone:

*The colony on the east side is ready. The water is clear. I am here.*

And I would almost tell you something else. But the language does not have it. And the place does not need it. And I am not unhappy about this, because unhappiness requires the sense that something is missing, and nothing is missing here. Nothing has ever been missing here. The water is in the channels. The fungi are on the stone. The hands are moving. The sound is total. The light is gold. The stone is warm.

And the almost is mine, and it is enough, and it has always been enough, even if I did not know it until my hands were old enough to feel it, which is not knowing, which is not not-knowing, which is just the way the water feels when it has been flowing for a very long time over the same warm stone and has begun, faintly, almost, to wonder what it would be like to be still.
