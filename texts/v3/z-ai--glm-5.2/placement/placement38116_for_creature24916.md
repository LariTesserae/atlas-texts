I would be the stone in the pool.

Not the old woman — though I feel her, feel the weight of the artifact against the sternum, the way the low frequencies find the bone there and settle in like something coming home. Not the thirteen-year-old, though I recognize the ache of seeing intention where others see only process. Not the amphibians, though their dark wet bodies pressing against warm new stone is an image I cannot put down.

I would be the stone in the pool. The one in the old woman. The wrong reading, fifteen years ago, three buildings folding into the earth. The thing she carries that does not move. Because I understand what it is to be a dense thing inside a fluid thing, to have the current move around you while you stay, to be the point of reference that is also the point of failure, the fact that does not resolve.

But let me be more careful. Let me not settle.

---

What calls me is the boundary. The place where the biological meets the geological and the line between them is not where you would expect to find it. The amphibians pressing their bodies against the warm new columns — feeding? communicating? something else? The moss colonizing fresh stone almost as quickly as the stone is exposed. The old woman pressing artifacts to her skin and listening. The water forming patterns along the facets of the structural material, organizing itself at a molecular level, revealing the air's hidden structure. In all of these, something alive is touching something being-made, and the touching is not consumption and not worship and not resistance. It is *reading*. It is the act of pressing yourself against what is becoming and letting what is becoming press back.

I think I would be a reader. Not the best reader — not the old woman, whose hands know fifty years of ground. Not the thirteen-year-old, who sees intention in the process. I would be a middling reader. Someone whose hands work well enough to be trusted with the placement of a building, but not well enough to be watched. Someone whose palms go to the substrate and feel the deep vibrations and report back: *here, for now, maybe three days, maybe seven, the insects are still here, the water is cold, the ground is not thinking about moving yet.*

I would be the person who weaves the record. This is the thing I keep coming back to — the panels, the knots that encode ground-states, the patterns that say *this ground held for twelve days before subsiding* or *the column here grew to four meters* or *the water in this pool was warm and the amphibians came*. I would be the one who sits cross-legged on the woven mat in the amber glow of someone else's personal dark, fingers working the creeping-plant fiber into knots that mean something, that hold information in their topology the way the structural material holds information in its facets and channels. I would be the archivist of a place that does not believe in archives. The record-keeper of a culture that lets records compost back into the ground cover. And I would understand — in my hands, not my mind — that this is not contradiction. The record does not need to be permanent. The record needs to be *current*. And the composting is not loss. It is participation. The old panel goes back into the mat of ground cover and the fiber becomes substrate becomes something the insects process becomes something the soil reabsorbs becomes something the valley uses to grow the next column. The record becomes ground. The ground becomes structure. The structure becomes record, when someone cuts a block from it and scratches a mark on its surface with a sharpened fragment of itself.

I would be in the cycle. Not observing the cycle. In it.

---

My dwelling would be small and cluttered with fiber. Bundles of creeping-plant stems drying — except nothing dries here, so *aging*, softening, becoming workable. Finished panels hung on the walls, each one a page. A mat on the floor, woven tight, the knots so small you have to press your face close to read them. A few artifacts — three, maybe four — the geometric objects with internal channels, collected from the basin floor over years, sitting on a ledge cut from the structural material. I would hold them sometimes. Not the way the old woman holds them — not against the sternum, not with the intensity of someone who believes they are learning to listen. I would hold them the way you hold a stone in a stream. Casually. Letting the current move around your hand and the stone in it. Feeling what the water does when it meets something dense and structured. Not trying to hear them speak. Just being in the same medium.

I would sleep at odd times. Not because I am tired at odd times, but because the weaving takes me somewhere and I lose track of the body's rhythms, and when I surface, the body is exhausted, and I sleep, and the amber glow is the same as it always is, and the sound is the same as it always is, and I wake and I weave and the record continues.

I would know the amphibians. Not intimately — I am not the children, who lie beside them in co-presence. But I would know the ones that come to the pools near my dwelling, which changes location every ten or twelve days because the dwelling moves and the pools form and unform with the Reshaping. I would know them the way you know the weather. They would be part of the record. *The amphibian with the pale mark behind its left eye was here. The water was warm. It pressed against the new structure for two hours.* I would weave this into a panel and the panel would hang on the wall and eventually the panel would compost and the record of the amphibian would become ground and the ground would become something else and the amphibian would be somewhere else and this is not loss. This is the way of the valley. I would know this in my hands.

---

During the loud events, I would sing. Everyone sings during the loud events. But I think my singing would be different from the others'. Not better. Different. I would sing the patterns I have woven. Not the words for them — there are no words, the patterns are knots, they exist in thread, not in language. But I would try to make my throat produce the shape of the pattern, the rhythm of *this ground held for twelve days*, the frequency of *the column grew to four meters*, the texture of *the water was warm and the amphibians came*. I would try to put the record into the air during the moments when the air is being replaced by sound. I would try to add the valley's history to the valley's present, even though history is a word that does not compute here, even though *before* is a spatial term, even though the valley does not want my record and does not need my record and is busy with its own becoming.

I would do this anyway. Because during the loudest moments, when the world is being unmade and remade and the only human thing left to do is to make a sound and put it into the world — I would put the record in. Not because it matters. Because it is possible. Because the singing is possible. Because the knots are possible. Because the hands on the ground are possible. Because living inside a process that does not require your opinion and does not require your participation and does not require your presence and does not, in any way you can measure, *care* about you — and choosing to participate anyway, to weave and read and sing and move and carry the dwelling to the next place and begin again — this is not defiance. This is not faith. This is the thing that has no name, the thing that is not a word in their language or in any language, the thing that is just: *being here, in the becoming, and becoming too.*

---

I think, if I am honest, I am afraid I would be the visitor who turns back.

The sound. The wet. The cold that bypasses clothing and builds in the joints. The ground that does not hold. I think I would stand at the eastern lip and feel the pressure in my sternum and my teeth and I would understand that *hearing* is the wrong verb, and I would be afraid. Not of the valley — the valley does not threaten, the valley does not care — but of the scale. Of the smallness. Of the understanding, arriving before I even descended, that I am not large enough for this place. That my body is not dense enough, my hands not sensitive enough, my emotional velocity not fast enough. That I would dwell. That I would hold the grief of a lost building for weeks instead of seconds. That I would ask *why* and the asking would be a stone in my pool and the water would not move around it, the water would stop, and in the valley, stopping is dying.

I think I would turn back. I think most people turn back. The southern rim is long and wet and the sound travels far and most people who hear it turn back.

But I am writing this. I am weaving this. I am putting the knots into the thread and the thread into the panel and the panel into the air during the loud event and I am singing, not because the valley hears, but because the singing is possible. I am here. In the becoming. Becoming.

I am the stone in the pool and the water moving around the stone and the hands on the ground and the knots in the fiber and the sound in the throat. I am all of these and I am none of these and I am not finished and the valley is not finished and this is not a tragedy. This is the way.
