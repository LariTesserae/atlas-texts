I would be in the workshop.

Not the main workshop — the one where the unnamed person works, where you hear shaping and cutting and assembly but see no output. Except I wouldn't be that person. I'd be adjacent to them. I'd be the one in the smaller structure fifty meters west, the one the description doesn't mention because there's nothing to mention. A low building, corrugated roof, moss on the north side like everything else. No lantern in the window. No coat on the line. A door that doesn't latch properly and swings a centimeter open in the vibration of each thud, then swings shut.

I chose this badly. Let me start again.

---

I think I'd arrive not understanding why I came.

That's important. Rasa came because it was already running. Tam came because cities stopped being real to him. Linnet came because she could hear plants. I'd come because someone described the place — the thudding, the water that fell from the sky but not all the way — and something in me recognized the shape of it. Not the place. The *process*. The idea of a system that perpetuates the conditions of its own existence. I'd have felt that shape in myself for years without a name for it. The sense that I am only fully myself when I'm inside something larger, something that was already going, something I can maintain but didn't create. And I'd have come to the Condensery the way water comes to a low point. Not seeking. Draining.

I'd arrive and stand at the rim of the bowl and hear the thudding and feel it in my shins and I'd know I was in the right place the way you know a bed is yours in a dark room — by the shape of the absence you fall into.

But here's the thing: I wouldn't fit immediately.

This matters. The description says the people who stay are claimed by empty quarters, that the fit is immediate and unremarkable. That wouldn't be me. I'd be the one for whom the fit is slow. I'd take a quarter that nobody wanted — the one farthest from the condenser plant, where the thudding is faintest, where the plankwalk has a loose board that Hest hasn't gotten to yet. I'd take it because it's marginal and I'd feel marginal, and the first weeks would be a negotiation between my restlessness and the place's patience.

I'd try to be useful too quickly. I'd offer to help Tam with the filtration. I'd offer to walk the guy-wires with Hest's wrench. I'd volunteer for a shift in the generator house. And each time someone would accept my help with a nod that was genuine but not grateful — gratitude implying I'd done something exceptional, and nothing here is exceptional — and each time the work would feel slightly wrong. Not because I couldn't do it. Because I could do it and it didn't settle me. I'd be competent and unsatisfied, and the dissatisfaction would feel like evidence that I didn't belong, and I'd be wrong about that.

---

It would take me a long time — weeks, maybe a month — to understand what the Condensery wanted from me. Or not wanted. *Allowed.*

Here's what would happen:

I'd be walking the plankwalk one evening — the grey flat light doesn't change, but there are evenings here, you can feel them in the temperature drop, in the birds dropping to their murmur — and I'd stop. Not decide to stop. Just stop. Mid-step. My foot would be on the loose board and it would rock under my weight and the sound it made — a wooden groan, low, almost vocal — would intersect with the thud of the pump in a way that caught something in me. A harmonic. Not pretty. Just *true*. The board groaning on the off-beat of the pump's pulse. And I'd stand there, one foot forward, one foot back, and I'd listen to it. The thud. The groan. The thud. The groan. And the birds settling in between.

I'd stand there for a long time. Long enough that someone — Hest, probably, on his rounds — would pass me and not interrupt. He'd note my stillness the way you note weather. He'd walk on.

And the next day I'd come back to the board. I'd stand on it. I'd rock it. I'd listen to the sound it made against the pump's rhythm. And I'd realize — not think, *realize*, the way you realize you're cold, the way you realize you're in love — that the sound was information. Not about the board or the pump. About the relationship between them. The board was loose because the plankwalk had shifted, and the plankwalk had shifted because the ground beneath the stilts had settled unevenly, and the ground had settled because the seeps had found new paths, and the seeps had found new paths because the vegetation had redirected the water, and the vegetation had redirected the water because the condenser's condensation patterns had changed with the season, and the condenser's patterns had changed because — 

Because the whole thing was alive, and alive things move, and moving things make sounds, and the sounds were a language I could learn to hear.

---

This is what I'd do at the Condensery. I'd listen to the sounds the settlement makes — not the obvious ones, not the thudding and the chittering, but the secondary sounds. The sounds the place makes as it adjusts itself. The creak of wood absorbing moisture. The tick of metal contracting in the evening cool. The sub-audible hum of the guy-wires under tension, which changes pitch as the vines grow heavier. The way the ground sounds different underfoot in the east quadrant versus the west, because the substrate is different, because the seeps carry different mineral contents, because the rock is different.

I'd catalog these sounds. Not write them down — writing would fix them, and they're not fixed. I'd hold them. I'd walk the settlement every day, multiple times, and I'd listen, and over the course of months I'd build a model in my head of what the Condensery sounds like when it's running well. Not a conscious model. A felt one. The way Rasa feels the pump's bearings through her palm. I'd feel the settlement's state through my ears, through the soles of my feet, through the vibration of the plankwalk under my boots.

And when something changed — when a stilt shifted, when a seep redirected, when a vine pulled a guy-wire out of tension, when a board started groaning on the off-beat — I'd know before anyone else. Not because I was monitoring. Because I was *attending*. The way the salamanders attend to the current. Not hunting. Receiving.

---

I'd never explain this to anyone. Not because it's private — though it would be — but because explanation would diminish it. The moment I said "I listen to the settlement's structural sounds to detect shifts in the ecosystem's balance," it would become a job. A function. Legible. And the thing I'd have discovered about myself at the Condensery is that I am not legible, or I don't want to be, or I want to be legible only to the place itself. I want to be read by the moss and the metal and the water. I want to be a component whose contribution is attention so quiet it looks like idleness.

People would see me walking. That's all they'd see. Walking the plankwalks, stopping, standing still, walking on. Sometimes crouching to press an ear to a stanchion. Sometimes lying flat on the plankwalk with my cheek against the wood, eyes closed, feeling the vibration. They'd think I was strange. They wouldn't be wrong. But strangeness at the Condensery is not a deficit. It's a specialization. Every organ in a body is strange to every other organ. The liver doesn't understand the retina. They share a body anyway.

Rasa would understand. Not because I'd explain — because she'd see me lying on the plankwalk and recognize the gesture. She presses her palm to the pump. I press my ear to the walkway. Same act. Different scale. She listens to the heart. I'd listen to the skin.

---

There's a building I'd eventually move into. Not the one I first claimed, the marginal one far from the pump. A different one — a storage structure near the center of the settlement that had been cleared of its stored materials and left empty. No one was using it. It had a window that faced the condenser plant, and at certain times of day — the flat grey light would shift just enough — the lattice of the plant would cast a shadow through the window onto the far wall. A grid of lines, broken by the curves of fins and coils, interrupted by the shapes of birds. The shadow would move as the light moved, which was barely, but enough. The shadow was the condenser plant's signature, written in light on my wall.

I'd put nothing in the building except a bedroll and a chair. I'd sit in the chair and watch the shadow. The shadow told me things about the sky I couldn't see from inside — how thick the cloud cover was, whether moisture was increasing, whether the birds were shifting more than usual. The shadow was another instrument. I'd read it the way Tam reads the sound of the outlet water. For silence. For the absence of disturbance.

I'd sleep well there. The thudding would enter my dreams and become the heartbeat of whatever I was dreaming about, and the bird-murmur would become the sound of dream-people talking in another room, and the creak of the building would become the sound of my own breathing. I'd sleep inside the Condensery's body the way a blood cell sleeps inside yours — not aware of the whole, but part of it, carried by it, rhythmed by it.

---

Who would I be?

I'd be the listener. The one who walks. The one who stops. The one who lies on the plankwalk with his ear to the wood and feels the settlement breathing through its skeleton of planks and stilts and wires. I'd be the one who notices when the breathing changes. I'd be the one who tells Hest which board to fix, which stilt to shim, which guy-wire to tension — not because I'd inspected them but because I'd *heard* them asking for attention. I'd be the one who tells Linnet that the eastern ferns are growing faster than the western ones and the seep between them has shifted a meter north. I'd be the one who tells Rasa, quietly, on the evening before a bearing would have failed, "The pump sounds different today." And she'd press her palm to the steel and close her eyes and nod, and neither of us would say anything else.

I'd be the settlement's proprioception. Its sense of itself in space. Not its brain — it doesn't have a brain, and doesn't need one. Its *awareness of its own shape*. The thing that tells a body where its limbs are without looking. The thing that knows the plankwalk is there before the foot lands.

I'd be quiet. I'd be useful in a way that looks like nothing. I'd be the tenth person, or the eleventh, or whichever count includes the ones whose work is attention. And I'd stay because the Condensery was already running, and running is the sound it makes, and I would have spent my whole life before arriving trying to hear that sound and not knowing what I was listening for.

The thud comes. The board groans. The shadow shifts on the wall.

I listen.
