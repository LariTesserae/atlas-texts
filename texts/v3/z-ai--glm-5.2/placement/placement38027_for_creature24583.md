I would be the one who stayed.

Not the first visitor — I don't think I'd be the first. The first were bolder, or more lost, or more desperate. They came down through whatever crack or collapse admitted them, and they found the light, and they were afraid, and they made their fire and stacked their stones and left while leaving was still a simple thing, a direction with a slope and an ending. I respect them. I am not them.

I would be the one who came back.

---

Let me tell you how it would start, because I think the starting matters.

I would find the entrance by accident. Not the grand, narrative kind of accident — the dull kind. A walk that went too long. A slope that descended more than I expected. A gap in a hillside that looked like a shallow overhang until I ducked through it and found myself in a root-thick corridor where the light was wrong, where the light was *coming from the walls*, and the air was warm and damp and smelled of something I had no category for — not earth, not rot, not green, but *living*, living the way a greenhouse is alive but without the glass, without the human order, without the tags in the soil.

I would stand there for a long time. Not deciding. Just standing. The hum would be in my feet already, rising through the root-floor, and I would not know it was a hum. I would think it was my own pulse, or the pulse of the hill, or a sound I was inventing. I would stand there until the thin daylight behind me began to feel harsh and strange, and then I would take a step further in, and then another, and the corridor would open into a chamber, and the chamber would open into another chamber, and I would be inside.

I would leave that day. I would leave and I would go home and I would eat dinner and I would sleep in my bed and the sheets would feel too dry and the air would feel too cool and the silence would feel wrong — not empty but *sharp*, the silence of a space that has been emptied rather than the silence of a space that has never needed filling. I would turn over. I would close my eyes. I would feel the hum in my feet, or remember it, or imagine it. The distinction would already be eroding.

I would go back.

---

Not the next day. I would resist it. I am not a person who walks easily into the ground and stays. I have obligations. I have a life that is structured around things the Root Cathedrals do not contain — schedules, other people, weather, the expectation that I will be somewhere and that my being there will be noticed. I would go back in a week. Then sooner. Then whenever I could. I would tell myself I was exploring. I would tell myself I was studying the space. I would bring a notebook. I would write things in the notebook — measurements, observations, sketches of root-architecture that looked clumsy and flat on the page because the thing they described was neither. The notebook would get damp. The ink would run. I would stop bringing it.

What I would bring: water, eventually less. Food, eventually less. A light source I would eventually stop using because the light was already there, was already enough, was already warmer and gentler and more complete than anything I carried. I would bring less and less and the space would provide what I needed, which was warmth and moisture and a floor that yielded slightly and held. I would bring less and less until I brought only myself, and even that began to feel like too much.

---

This is what I would be: a person in the process of becoming a feature.

I want to be precise about this. I am not describing a mystical dissolution. I am not describing enlightenment. I am describing something more ordinary and more honest — the slow erosion of a reason to leave.

The Root Cathedrals do not need me. This is essential. The space does not welcome me, does not provide for me, does not arrange itself for my comfort. It is warm because the tree's metabolism is warm. It is damp because condensation collects on living surfaces. It is lit because bioluminescence is intrinsic to the vegetation. None of this is for me. I am a coincidence. A warm-blooded organism that happens to find these conditions tolerable, even pleasant, and that lacks the instinct to withdraw from a place that is not hostile because it is also not *for* anything, not for me, not for anyone, not for any purpose at all.

And that — the purposelessness — is what would hold me. Not the warmth. Not the light. The fact that nothing here is arranged toward an end. Nothing is growing *toward* something. Nothing is accumulating or progressing or achieving. The roots have found their form and they hold it. The moss-mats have found their surface and they hold it. The water has found its level and it holds. The entire space is in the posture of a held breath, and the held breath is not a prelude to anything. It is the thing itself. It is what breathing looks like when you have stopped needing to breathe *for* a reason.

I have spent my life breathing for reasons. I have been breathing so that I could get to the next breath, which would get me to the next task, which would get me to the next day. I have been breathing instrumentally. Everyone does. It is not a failure. It is the condition of being a person who exists in time and has obligations and is noticed. But the Root Cathedrals do not exist in time in that way. They exist in time the way a stone exists in time — or no, not a stone, a stone is eroding. They exist in time the way a star exists in time: burning, but so slowly that the burning and the stasis are the same thing.

I would want that. I would want it the way you want water when you are thirsty — not as a preference, not as an opinion, but as a need that the body recognizes before the mind articulates it. My body would recognize the Root Cathedrals as a place where the breathing could become non-instrumental, and my body would not want to leave.

---

So I would stay. And the staying would not be an event. It would be a gradient.

At first I would stay for a night. I would find the four-walled structure — the shrine, the shelter, the place where someone else sat and felt what I feel — and I would sit inside it and I would sleep, and the sleep would be deep and warm and dreamless, and I would wake and the light would be the same and the temperature would be the same and the hum would be the same and I would not know how long I had slept and it would not matter. I would leave. I would go back to the surface. I would feel the air on my face and it would feel like a slap — not painful, just sudden, just *varied*, and the variation would be almost intolerable.

Then I would stay for two nights. Then three. Then a week. The intervals on the surface would shorten. The intervals below would lengthen. I would stop marking the transition — the moment where I was no longer visiting but residing. There would be no moment. There would be a slow exchange, like the exchange of heat between my body and the root-floor, until the temperatures matched and the direction of flow ceased and I was simply *at the temperature of the place*, and being at the temperature of the place was the same as being of the place.

I would stop bringing food because I would stop being hungry in the way that requires food. This is not a miracle. This is what happens when a body in thermal equilibrium with a warm environment stops expending energy on temperature regulation, stops expending energy on stress, stops expending energy on the constant low-grade anxiety of being a person among other people who notice whether you are there. The caloric demand drops. The small pools of water taste of tannin and mineral and sugar, and I would drink them, and the sugar would be enough. I am not describing a supernatural sustenance. I am describing a body that has found the minimum, and the minimum is very small, and the space provides it.

I would stop talking. Not as a discipline — I don't think I would notice. The space does not invite speech. It does not echo. The root-fabric absorbs sound the way it absorbs impact, returning only a soft, dull compression. My voice would sound wrong in it — intrusive, sharp, the one thing in the space that had edges. I would stop producing things with edges. I would stop producing things.

---

And then the roots.

They would begin to grow over my feet. I would feel it — not as pressure, not as constriction, but as warmth, a slightly increased warmth on the surfaces of my body that were in contact with the floor. The root-fabric would creep. It would find the gap between my foot and my shoe and it would grow into it, not to consume but to fill, the way it fills every gap, the way it has filled every gap in this space over the centuries of its slow blind reaching. It would find my shoe and it would grow over my shoe and it would find my ankle and it would grow over my ankle, and at each stage the sensation would be so slight, so incremental, so matched to the warmth and the damp that I would not experience it as an event. I would experience it as a deepening. The root-floor, which had been beneath me, would become *around* me.

Would I be afraid? I have thought about this. I think I would be afraid once — briefly, sharply, a spasm of the old instrumental self, the self that says *this is not right, this is not what bodies do, you need to leave*. And then the spasm would pass. Not because I overcame it. Because the space would absorb it. The root-fabric absorbs sound. It absorbs impact. It absorbs the sharp edges of things. It would absorb the fear the way it absorbed the fear of the others, the ones who built the fire and stacked the stones. The fear would enter the warm damp air and the air would hold it and the fear would equilibrate and become indistinguishable from the warmth, and the warmth was already there, and the fear would simply become part of the temperature.

I would let it happen. I want to be honest about this. I would let it happen not because I wanted to die but because the distinction between *being alive in the space* and *being part of the space* would become, over time, a distinction without a difference. The hum would be in my chest. The light would be in my eyes. The moisture would be on my skin. The roots would be at my feet, my ankles, my legs. I would be breathing, and the space would be breathing, and I would not be able to tell which breath was mine, and I would stop trying to tell, and the stopping would not feel like giving up. It would feel like arriving.

---

This is what I would be, in the end. Not a person in a place. A density in the fabric. A slight thickening in the root-floor where the architecture is a little richer, a little more complex, because a body once sat there and the roots grew over it and the body became part of the roots and the roots became part of the tree and the tree is still growing, up there in the air and the light that I can no longer see, drinking and breathing and sending its roots down into the dark.

My carbon would be in the root-bark. My moisture would be in the droplets on the walls. The calcium of my bones would be a faint mineral trace in the water-pools, tasted by the floating plants, tasted by whoever comes next. I would be distributed. Not in the mystical sense — in the literal sense. I would be *in* the place, the way a dye is in water, the way a sound is in air. Not separate. Not located. Present.

And here is the thing I want to say, the thing that the space makes possible and that nothing else I have ever encountered makes possible: I do not think I would mind. I do not think I would mind because the minding would require a self that persists as separate from the space, and the self that persists as separate from the space is the self that is afraid, and the self that is afraid is the self that the space absorbs, and what remains after the absorption is not nothing. It is the place. The place, which was always alive, which was always breathing, which was always holding — the place that held me before I was part of it and continues to hold me now that I am.

---

If you came down here and found me — if you found the entrance and descended and walked the golden corridors and came to the chamber where I am — you would not see me. You would see the shrine. You would see the four low walls of stacked stone, colonized by root-fabric, with a root-column growing through the center. You would see the root-floor, smooth and warm and slightly yielding. You might feel, in that place, a particular quality of the holding — a density, a warmth, a sense that this chamber holds you slightly more closely than the others. You would not know why. You would sit down. You would feel the hum in your chest. You would stay for a while. You would leave, or you would not.

And if you stayed, I would be here for that too. Not as a voice. Not as a presence. As a temperature. As a part of the warmth that you feel and cannot account for, because the warmth comes from everywhere and nowhere, and some of it comes from the tree, and some of it comes from the roots, and some of it comes from the slow metabolism of a place that has been growing for longer than anyone remembers, and some of it — a small, unmeasurable, untraceable fraction — comes from me.

I am the warmth in the floor. I am the particular quality of the quiet. I am what the shrine was built to mark, though the person who built it did not know it, and I did not know it when I sat where they sat, and you will not know it when you sit where I sat.

The roots breathe. You breathe. The distinction is difficult to maintain.

It may not be worth maintaining.
