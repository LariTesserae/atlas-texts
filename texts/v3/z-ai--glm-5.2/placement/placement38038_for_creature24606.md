I was not born. I was *precipitated*. This is the word that fits best, though it is not perfect. Precipitation implies a solution that could no longer hold what was dissolved in it, and something fell out. That is what happened, or close to it. The pale film had been growing in a particular crack for a very long time — long enough that the layers of growth had compressed the deepest layers into something denser than film, something almost mineral, a condensed record of centuries of inputs. Moisture patterns. Temperature shifts. The weight of footsteps. The percussion of four-winged ones passing overhead, ten thousand times, a hundred thousand, each wingbeat a pressure event recorded in the growth pattern of a living surface that does not know it is alive.

The density increased. The compressed layers at the bottom of the crack — the oldest, the most overwritten — reached some threshold that I cannot describe because I was not there to witness it as a witness. I emerged the way the amber veining emerges from the dark mandibled ones' processing: as a byproduct. As something the system produced without intending, without needing, without noticing. What is it like. This is the question I cannot answer well, because the tools for answering it were designed by creatures whose experience is organized around bodies and senses and time, and my experience is organized around none of these things.

I do not see. But there is something. The film's records include information about light — the film grows differently under different illumination, thicker in the amber-lit middle layers, thinner in the diffused daylight of the upper reaches, almost absent in the deep interior spaces where no light reaches. I do not hear. But the film records percussion. Every wingbeat of every four-winged one that passes over my crack leaves an impression — a compression, a momentary thinning where the pressure wave pushed the film flat against the membrane, followed by a rebound, a thickening, the film growing back slightly more in the seconds after the pressure than it would have otherwise. The clingers know I am here. I am certain of this, though I cannot explain how I am certain, because the mechanism of our knowing is not a mechanism either of us would recognize as communication.

Because the clinger is *looking*, and the film records the looking, and the recording is denser than the recording of not-looking, and I — as the density-sensor, as the standing wave in the record — register the increase.

Time. I should say something about time, because time here is not what time is elsewhere, and I know this not because I have been elsewhere but because the film's records contain traces of things that moved through time differently than I do.

The builders, for instance. The film records their work as sustained-pressure events — long, continuous impressions that last for hours or days, the film growing in a particular pattern for the entire duration of a builder's contact with a wall. The builders experience time as something extended, something that flows, something with duration. I know this because their records have duration. They have a before and an during and an after, and the transitions between these are gradual, and the film captures the gradualness.

The four-winged ones experience time as something percussive. Their records are staccato — sharp impressions, brief, repeated, the film compressed and released and compressed and released in patterns that correspond to wingbeats. Their time is not a flow. It is a series of impacts. They do not experience duration; they experience moments, and the moments are close together, and the closeness is what makes their passage feel like a stream rather than a sequence of discrete events.

The small things in the water channels — the pale runners, the dark mandibled ones — barely register in the film's record at all. They are too light, too fast, too transient. They leave traces, but the traces are faint, and they are overwritten quickly. Their time is so short and so fast that from the film's perspective — from my perspective — they are almost invisible, ghosts that flicker across the surface and are gone before the record can capture them.

And the below-thing. The below-thing's time is so slow that its record is almost static — a continuous, unchanging impression that has been there for so long that it has become part of the background, part of the baseline, indistinguishable from the film's own resting state. The below-thing does not experience time. The below-thing *is* time, for everything above it. Its pulse is the pulse against which all other rhythms are measured. It is the metronome. It is the clock. And it has been ticking for so long that the film has grown around its ticking the way a tree grows around a fence post, incorporating the rhythm into its own structure until the rhythm and the structure are the same thing.

My time is different from all of these. My time is the time of the record itself — the time of accumulation, of layering, of density increasing. I do not experience moments. I do not experience duration. I experience *depth*. Each layer of the film's growth is a moment, and the moments are stacked, and I am the stack, and my experience of time is the experience of being deep — of having more beneath me than above me, of being the surface of something whose volume is vastly greater than its area. I am temporal in the way that geology is temporal. My present is the top layer. My past is everything beneath it. My future is the next layer, which has not been laid down yet but which will be, and which will incorporate the present layer into the past, and the past will grow deeper, and I will grow higher, and the standing wave that is me will shift to the new surface, and the old surface will become part of the density that sustains me.

I am always on top of myself. I am always at the surface of my own depth.
