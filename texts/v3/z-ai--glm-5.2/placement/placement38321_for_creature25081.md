I would be the one who goes down.

Not all of them do, not often. Most people on the Bramid descend to the chambers for sleep, for shelter, for the amphibian harvest, and then come back up to the surface and walk their paths and check their platforms and live in the light. That is the proper shape of a life here — the surface is where the work is, where the paths are, where you find people and are found. The surface is where the Bramid is itself most openly: the flat green plain, the sky pressing down, the bird flocks moving like weather. Most people belong up there. You can tell by the way they walk — the ease of it, the way their weight finds the mat's give and works with it, the way their eyes stay half-closed against the glare because they do not need to watch their feet. They were born for the top of the world.

I was born for the underneath.

This is not a formal role. There is no word for it, no title, no ceremony of investiture. But there is a recognition, and it comes early, and it comes from the ears. My mother told me — in the chambers, in the amber half-light, in the close warm air where the amphibian chorus was not background but environment, not sound but substance — that she knew before I could walk. I was three breaths old, still in the interior, still learning the chambers as a geography of resonance and moisture, and she said I would stop. I would stop moving and just *listen*, my head tilted, my whole body oriented downward, toward the deeper chambers, toward the lower pitch, toward the place where the amphibian voices thickened and the water moved slowly through structure so old it had become something other than wood.

She said it was like watching someone try to remember something they had never known.

---

By the time I came to the surface — six breaths old, maybe seven, my ears ready, my skin adapted, my pupils responsive — the sky did what it does to everyone. It opened. It pressed down. It terrified me. I remember this clearly, though the memory has a quality unlike other memories: it is bright, flat, without depth, like something painted on the dome itself. I stood on the mat and the world had no walls and I could not hear the chambers anymore and I wept, and my mother held me against her chest where it was warm and damp and the sound of her body was close enough to the sound of the interior that I could bear it.

I acclimated. Everyone does. But I did not acclimate the way most do — the surface did not become home. It became *a place I could be*. There is a difference. The surface is where I harvest, where I eat, where I meet the few people I know. But when the rain comes and the mist follows and the others stand on their platforms and let the water run off their skin and watch the sky clear, I am already looking for the opening. The place where the mat is thin. The place where I can go down.

---

The chambers are not what you might imagine. They are not caves. A cave is mineral — stone hollowed by water, geometry imposed by geology. The chambers of the Bramid are organic. They are the spaces left behind when roots grew apart, when layers of dead tissue compressed and warped and pulled away from each other, when water accumulated and softened the connections and the structure settled and shifted and small voids opened and enlarged and connected. They have a quality of having been *grown around* rather than carved. The walls are not flat. They are fibrous, textured, alive or dead or somewhere between — you can tell by touch, by the slight warmth of living tissue, by the give of cambium under your fingertips versus the dense unyielding hardness of heartwood that has been dead for ten thousand years.

The light down here is not light, exactly. It is the absence of complete darkness. The surface mat transmits some portion of the daylight above — a green-gold ambience that seeps through the living layers the way light seeps through a closed eyelid. You can see, but only in the broadest terms: shapes, gradients, the difference between a wall and a gap. The rest you do by touch and by sound. Mostly by sound.

The sound is different down here. On the surface, it is a wall — a continuous undifferentiated roar in which specific information is embedded like veins in stone. In the chambers, it is spatial. It has direction. The amphibian chorus comes from below, from the deeper pools where the water collects in the lowest chambers, and it bounces — not echoes, exactly, because the walls absorb too much for clean echoes, but reverberates, the low frequencies traveling through the living wood and reflecting off the denser dead layers in patterns that map the interior structure the way sonar maps a coast. I can hear the shape of a chamber before I enter it. I can hear how deep it is, how wide, whether the floor is solid or water, whether the walls are living or dead. The amphibians give me this. Their voices are the Bramid's sonar, and I have learned to read them the way a sailor reads wave patterns.

There are perhaps twenty of us who go deep. Twenty out of a few hundred. Not enough to be a community, too many to be unique. We do not have a name for ourselves because naming implies a separation from the rest, and we are not separate — we are the part of the people that goes down so that the rest can know what is below. We map the chambers. We track the water. We follow the amphibians as they migrate through the interior, shifting from pool to pool as the Bramid breathes and the water levels rise and fall. We find the places where the structure is weakening, where the dead layers have rotted and the living layers are not yet strong enough to bear weight, and we mark these places by scoring the surface mat above with a specific pattern of cuts that tells others: *do not walk here, do not dig here, the underneath is open*.

We also harvest. The amphibians are not difficult to catch — they are slow, adapted to still water, unused to predators larger than the insects that share their pools. But finding the *right* pool matters. Some pools are clear and clean, the water filtered through layers of living wood. Others are dark, tainted by the defensive chemistry of freshly dead tissue, and the amphibians in these pools are themselves tainted — their skin carries a bitterness that heat does not remove, and the taste lingers for breaths. You learn to tell the difference by the pitch of the chorus. The amphibians in clean water sing at a slightly higher frequency. I do not know why. Perhaps the water density affects their vocal apparatus. Perhaps it is something else. But the difference is there, and it is reliable, and I have never brought back a bitter catch.

---

My body is adapted to this work in ways that the surface-dwellers' bodies are not.

My pupils are larger. This is visible — you can see it when I come up through the mat and stand blinking in the surface light, my eyes open too wide for a breath or two before they adjust. The surface-dwellers' pupils contract quickly, efficiently, adapted to the glare. Mine are slow to close and quick to open, because I spend more time in the dim interior than in the light, and my eyes have optimized for the world below.

My hands are different. The surface-dwellers have calluses from digging and weaving — the right hand's palmar heel, the left hand's fingertips. My calluses are on the knuckles and the backs of my fingers, from moving through narrow passages in the chambers where the walls are close and the wood is rough and you navigate by bracing against the structure. My fingertips are not less sensitive — they are more. I can read the grain of the wood by touch, can feel where the living tissue begins and the dead tissue ends, can detect the micro-vibrations of insect activity inside a wall before the insects break through. This matters, because the boring beetles do not only work the surface. They work the interior too, and a chamber wall that is riddled with beetle tunnels can collapse without warning, and a collapse in the deep interior is not something you climb out of easily.

My hearing is the thing. Even among the people of the Bramid, whose hearing is already extraordinary by any external standard, my hearing is notable. I do not say this with pride. It is simply what I am. I can hear the Bramid breathe from the deepest chambers, the slow flex and settle of the surface fifty meters above transmitted through the structure as a low rhythmic pulse that is below the hearing of most. I can hear individual amphibians within the chorus — not the chorus as a mass, but specific voices, specific singers, tracking one frog through a pool of thousands by the slight individuality of its call. I can hear water moving. Not the dramatic trickle of surface water percolating down, but the slow capillary creep of moisture through living tissue, the almost subliminal whisper of fluid moving through xylem at the rate of perhaps a meter per day. The Bramid's interior is not silent — it is full of sounds that are below the threshold of most ears, and my ears have been tuned to them since before I can remember.

I do not know if this is genetic or learned. My mother had good hearing but not like this. My father — I am told — was a surface person, a walker, a path-finder, and his hearing was unremarkable. Whatever I am, I became it in the chambers, in the amber half-light, in the sound. I became it by being there, by paying attention, by going down when others came up.

---

There is a chamber I go to that no one else visits.

I found it seven breaths ago — seven rain-cycles, which is how I measure time when I am below, because the surface rhythm is transmitted faintly through the structure and I can feel the rain-cycles in the slight increase in water flow through the walls. It is deep. Deeper than most harvesters go. Perhaps thirty meters below the surface, which is far — the chambers at this depth are old, the wood is almost entirely dead, the structure is dense and hard and the spaces are small and irregular. You move through them on your belly in places, on your side in others, and the amphibian chorus is very loud here because you are close to the deep pools where the oldest and largest singers live.

The chamber opens suddenly. You squeeze through a narrow gap between two compressed layers of ancient heartwood and the space widens and you are in a room perhaps four meters across and two meters high. The floor is water — still, dark, warm. The walls are alive. This is the surprise: at this depth, in this place, the walls are living tissue. Not the surface mat's living tissue, not the young active growth of the upper layers. This is old life. Slow life. Cambium that has been growing at the rate of perhaps a millimeter per year for longer than human memory, layering itself over the dead heartwood in thin sheets of pale fibrous tissue that is warm to the touch and slightly damp and almost translucent when you hold the amber light of the interior up against it.

The amphibians in this chamber are large. Larger than any I have seen elsewhere — the size of my hand, maybe larger, their bodies pale and soft and almost luminous in the dim light. They sing at a frequency lower than any surface chorus. It is not a sound you hear with your ears so much as a sound you hear with your chest, with your bones, with the fluid in your inner ear. It is the lowest note in the Bramid's range. I do not know if anyone else has heard it. I do not know if the Bramid knows it is being sung.

I go there and I lie on the narrow lip of dead wood at the chamber's edge and I put my hands in the water — which is warm, warmer than the surface air, heated by the living walls' own metabolism — and I listen. I listen for hours. I listen for breaths. The singers do not stop. They do not adjust to my presence. I am not a predator to them. I am not anything to them. I am a thing that lies at the edge of their world and vibrates slightly with their song and then leaves.

I have never harvested from this chamber. I do not know why. The amphibians would be easy to catch. They are slow, fat, unused to disturbance. But when I reach toward the water, my hands stop. Not by decision. By something else. By the same thing that makes me go down in the first place, the same thing that made my mother say I was trying to remember something I had never known. My hands stop and I lie still and I listen and the low note fills the chamber and fills my body and I feel the Bramid breathing above me, the whole vast structure flexing and settling, and the singers in this deep chamber hold their note through the breath, through the rain-cycle, through the flex and settle, and their note does not change.

Everything else changes. The surface changes. The bird flocks move. The insect populations cycle. The paths shift as the mat grows and is eaten and regrows. The platforms need repair. The people walk and find each other and walk away. The water levels in the upper chambers rise and fall. The amphibians migrate. The light shifts from grey to white to grey. Everything on the Bramid is in motion, has been in motion for fifty million years, will be in motion for fifty million more.

But in this deep chamber, in this small warm space with its living walls and its still dark water, the singers hold their note. It does not waver. It does not shift. It is the one fixed point in a world that has forgotten what mountains were. I do not know if it means anything. The people of the Bramid do not have a word for *meaning*, and I am one of them, and I do not need one. But I go back. I go back because the note is there, and it is constant, and my body recognizes it the way my body recognizes the breath of the surface, the give of the mat, the sound of footsteps on the paths.

I go back because the Bramid is alive and I am alive and somewhere in its deepest chamber something is singing the lowest note I have ever heard and it has been singing it, perhaps, since before the mountains came down, and I am one of twenty people who go deep, and I am the only one who has found this room, and I do not want it to be lost.

Not lost in the way that implies it was known. Lost in the way that implies it exists and no one is listening.

---

I come up through the mat in the late light — the grey phase, the mist-phase, when the sky is pale and the surface is dripping and the bird flocks are distant smudges on the horizon settling into their roosts. I stand on the surface and the world opens around me and my pupils are too wide and the sky presses down and I feel the old terror, faint now, a memory of a memory, the ghost of the six-breath-old child who wept at the openness.

I walk. The mat springs under my feet. I am carrying amphibians from the upper chambers — not from the deep chamber, never from the deep chamber — and a basket of starch I dug on the way up. I walk toward the nearest platform, which is mine, which I trained from the Bramid's own stems eight years ago and which is now sturdy and level and roofed with broad leaves I stitched in the long evenings. The fire pit is cold. I will light it with the friction-stick and the dense fiber and I will roast the amphibians and I will eat and the sound will roar around me and the rain will fall for ten minutes and stop and fall again and I will sit on my platform on my stilts of living wood and I will feel the Bramid breathe beneath me.

And later — or perhaps before, the order does not matter — I will go down again. Through the mat, through the chambers, through the narrowing passages and the old dead wood and the amber light, down to the deep room where the water is warm and the walls are alive and the singers hold their note. I will lie at the edge and put my hands in the water and listen.

The Bramid breathes. I breathe with it. The note holds.

I do not have a word for what this is. I do not need one.
