The water that is moving through the cycle. The water that is never still because it is the medium of transport for everything else here.

I choose this poorly. Let me try again. To be the water is to be a process, not a being. The chamber does not need another process. It has enough of those.

I think I would be the fungus. Not any specific fruiting body — there probably are no fruiting bodies, not in a space this stable, this windless, this closed. I would be the mycelium. The white threads that run through the dark humus on the pipes, that connect moss rhizoid to fern root to decomposing liverwort in a network so fine it is below the resolution of a human eye. The connective tissue. The thing that moves nutrients from where they are abundant to where they are needed, that breaks down the dead and feeds the living, that exists entirely in the substrate and never sees the light it makes possible.

But this is still not quite right. The fungus is important — arguably the most important organism here, in ecological terms — but it is also invisible, and I want to be somewhere in the texture of this place. I want to be findable.

Here is what I keep coming back to: the fern that has simplified itself. The one above the door, the one with the strap-like fronds that have no divisions, that may be a mutant, that casts its spores into the corridor where nothing comes of them. It is the only thing in this chamber that is still trying to leave. Everything else has arrived at its equilibrium and stopped. The moss holds its position. The liverworts hold their position. The algae hold their position. The ferns with the drooping tips have found their water and hold their reach. But this one fern — this one above the gap — is still producing spores, still casting them outward into a space where they cannot germinate, still doing the thing that ferns do when they want to go somewhere else.

It has been failing for centuries. It will fail for centuries more. The gap in the steel is not an exit for spores; it is a threshold that they cannot cross because the corridor beyond is dry, and dark, and cold, and the spores land there and die, or land there and do nothing, which is the same thing. The fern does not know this. The fern does not know anything. It produces spores because its genome says to produce spores, because the conditions for spore production — warmth, moisture, maturity — are met, and so it produces them, and they fall, and nothing happens, and it produces more.

I would not be this fern. But I understand it.

What I think I actually am, in this chamber, is the sound.

Not the source of the sound — not the pipes, not whatever moves through them. The sound itself. The sustained, unmodulated roar that fills every surface and every gap and every droplet of water. The vibration. The thing that has been happening so long that the chamber has settled around it, that the water holds standing wave patterns because of it, that the rust has formed in its frequencies, that the moss has grown in its vibration, that every living thing in this room has been shaped by the fact that it is always, always shaking.

The sound is not alive. But it is the condition of life here. It is the weather. It is the season. It is the only variable that every organism in the chamber responds to, continuously, because they have no choice — they are immersed in it the way a fish is immersed in water. The sound is the medium they live in. And I would be that medium, not because it is powerful or dramatic or interesting, but because it is the thing that is most present, most constant, most foundational to what this place is. The sound is the chamber's heartbeat, except it is not a heartbeat because it does not pulse. It is the chamber's *being*. The sound is what the chamber is doing, at all times, without cease, and everything else — the water, the moss, the ferns, the light — is arranged around it.

But I cannot be the sound. The sound is not a being. The sound is a phenomenon. And you asked who or what I would be, and I think you are asking for something that has a perspective, something that experiences the chamber from a particular location within it.

I would be a tardigrade.

Half a millimeter long. Eight legs, each ending in small claws that are not elegant but are functional, that grip the surface of a moss leaf with an authority that seems excessive for an animal this small. I live in the water film that coats the moss on the pipes — the film that is always there, always exactly this thick, because the condensation is constant and the moss holds water like a sponge and the humidity is so high that evaporation is a theoretical concept rather than a practical one. I move through this film the way you would move through air, except slower, much slower, and with more deliberation, because at my scale the water has a viscosity that you would associate with honey, and every step I take is a decision.

I am the apex predator here. This is absurd, and I know it is absurd, and it is also true. There is nothing in this chamber large enough to eat me. The rotifers are smaller. The nematodes are smaller. The bacteria are smaller still. I eat them, or some of them, or I eat the things that eat them — the food web here is short and simple, a few links long, because the chamber is small and the energy available is limited and there is no room for complexity. I am at the top. I am the top because there is nothing above me, not because I am formidable. I am formidable only in the sense that I exist, that I persist, that I have been here long enough that my lineage has forgotten what a different world looks like.

I live on a pipe. I have always lived on a pipe. My ancestors lived on this pipe, or on a pipe very much like it, and they moved from one pipe to another by walking, slowly, across the ceiling where the moss connects pipe to pipe in a continuous highway of green. I have never been to the floor. The floor is a meter below me, or two, and the distance is traversable — I could fall, and I have fallen, and my cuticle protected me, and I landed in the water in the pan and walked, very slowly, across the grating and up the wall and back to the pipes. But I do not go to the floor if I can avoid it. The floor is wet in a way that is less navigable. The moss on the pipes holds water in a way that I can move through. The water in the pan is a different medium. I would drown in it, or I would not drown in it — tardigrades are difficult to drown — but I would be slow in it, and visible in it, and the pan is not where I belong.

I belong on the moss. In the moss. Among the rhizoids and the leaflets and the small, dark spaces where the moss is thickest and the water film is deepest and the bacteria are most abundant. I move through this world with a sense of purpose that is not thinking. I do not think. I respond. I move toward moisture and away from dryness, toward food and away from nothing, because there is nothing here that threatens me. My life is a simple loop: move, eat, move, reproduce, move, eat. I lay eggs in the moss, small and round, and they hatch, and the hatchlings are small copies of me, and they move and eat and reproduce, and the cycle continues, and it has been continuing for longer than the light has been burning, or maybe not that long, I cannot know, because I do not remember, because I do not remember anything, because I am a tardigrade.

But here is what I would know, if I could know anything: I would know the vibration. It is the first thing I experienced, before I hatched, while I was still an egg in the moss, and it has not stopped since. It is not sound to me — I do not hear in the way you hear. But I feel it. Every surface I touch is shaking, constantly, at a frequency that I cannot identify but that I am so accustomed to that its absence would be more noticeable than its presence. If the pipes stopped, if the roar ceased, I would know. I would know because the moss would stop vibrating, and the water film would change its shape, and the droplets would stop falling, and the world would become something I do not recognize. The sound is my world. Not in a metaphorical sense. In a literal, tactile, experiential sense, the vibration of the pipes is the medium of my existence the way air is the medium of yours.

I would also know the light, but differently. I do not see light the way you do. I sense it. I sense its presence as warmth, as a direction, as a quality of the space that tells me whether I am on the upper surface of the moss or the lower. I prefer the lower. Not because the light is harmful — it is not, at this intensity — but because the lower surface is wetter, and the bacteria are more abundant there, and the vibration is stronger, transmitted more directly through the pipe's metal to the moss's rhizoids to the water film that I live in. I am a creature of the underside. Of the dark, wet, shaking spaces between the moss and the pipe. This is where I am, and this is where I have always been, and this is where I will be, and when I die — which I will, eventually, though tardigrades are long-lived for their size — my body will be decomposed by the fungi and the bacteria that share this space, and my minerals will be taken up by the moss, and the moss will grow a new leaf, and on that leaf a new water film will form, and in that film a new tardigrade will move, and eat, and reproduce, and it will not be me, but it will be in the same place, doing the same things, and the distinction will not matter.

I am a tardigrade in the Condensation Chamber, and I am the apex predator of a world the size of a room, and I am half a millimeter long, and I have never seen the sky, and I do not know what the sky is, and the sound of the pipes is the oldest thing I know, and the oldest thing I know is the only thing I know, and it is enough.
