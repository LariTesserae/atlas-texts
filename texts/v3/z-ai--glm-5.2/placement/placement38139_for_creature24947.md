# The Listener at the Sixth Column

I would not live in Keswer.

I say this knowing it is where I would arrive — where anyone arrives, if they arrive. The southern rim, the dried plate-wood walls, the shutters that close against the spore-fall. I would come up through the scrub the way anyone comes, and I would stand at the edge where stone meets the living ground, and I would feel the warmth of it through the soles of my boots, and something in me would lean forward the way a plant leans toward light.

But I would not stay at the edge. This is the thing I know about myself in this world: the edge would not hold me. Not because I am brave. The people of Keswer are brave. They hunt browsers with sharpened sticks. They live at the margin of something that could swallow them and they have made a life of that margin, and that requires a courage I do not think I have. What I have is different and less useful. What I have is the inability to stop listening.

---

I would be the one who asks too much.

Not the outsider who asks *what is the Mass* — that question has no answer, or too many, and the people of Keswer have learned not to ask it. I would be the one who asks what the people mean when they say *deciding*. I would ask it quietly, the way you ask a question when you know the asking might close a door. I would ask it of someone who has pressed their hands to a column, someone who has stood in the cathedral corridor with the groaning above and the clicking rolling through them like a second pulse. And they would not answer. And I would understand that the not-answering was itself an answer, and I would stop asking, and I would start listening instead.

This is who I would be: the one who goes early.

Not the annual walk. Not the marked columns, the appointed time. I would go before. I would go in the cool of the morning, when the fog is still pooled in the interior and the insect-drone is at its lowest, that almost-pleasant bass note you could mistake for wind. I would step onto the substrate and feel it give — that slight yield, that mattress-spring, that warmth — and I would walk between the columns the way you walk into a cathedral you have never visited, which is to say slowly, and with your hands open, and with the specific humility of someone who understands they are inside a living thing.

The columns would creak above me. I would hear it in my sternum, that low structural complaint, the sound of a body adjusting its own weight. And I would think: *this is not a forest. A forest is a collection. This is a single thing.* And the distinction would matter to me more than it should, because I would be looking for the edges of it — not the geographical edges, not the cliff where the plateau drops to the basin, but the edges of the self. Where does the Mass end? Where does not-the-Mass begin? Is the filamentous layer the Mass, or is it on the Mass? Are the plates the Mass, or are they on the Mass? Are the browsers the Mass? They eat the Mass and the Mass receives their dead. Are the predators the Mass? The people of Keswer say so. They say the Mass grew them the way it grew the columns. And if the predators are the Mass, and the browsers are food for the predators, and the people hunt the browsers, and the Mass receives what the people leave behind — then where is the line? Where is the skin?

I would think about this while walking. The substrate would carry me forward, a few centimetres in an unexpected direction, and I would not notice, because I would be listening.

---

I think I know what I would hear.

Not the tones. Not at first. At first I would hear what everyone hears — the layered sound, the dozen strata of noise, the groaning and the clicking and the shearing of mandibles and the drone of insects so thick that the air has texture. I would hear the Mass being the Mass, and it would be overwhelming, and I would sit down between two columns with my hands on the ground and I would feel the percolation, that thick slow pulse of water moving through living vessels, and I would think: *this is what it sounds like inside a body. This is what my own blood sounds like to the cells it passes.*

And then I would hear the other thing.

Not louder. Clearer. The way the text describes it — as if the other sounds stepped aside. I think this is what the people of Keswer hear when they press their hands to the columns. I think this is what they are listening for. Not a message. Not a voice. A *location*. A sense of something that has a position, that is in the Mass but not uniformly distributed through it, the way the rhizome network is distributed, the way the heat is distributed. Something that is *somewhere*. Something that moves.

I would press my hands to a column. The surface would be warm and fibrous and slightly damp, and the filaments would flatten under my palms and then slowly rise again, exploring the texture of my skin the way my skin would be exploring the texture of theirs. And I would feel the clicking in the column — not hear it, feel it, the sharp percussive snaps transmitted through the bundled stems like a telegraph, like a nerve firing. And beneath the clicking, the groaning. And beneath the groaning, something else.

---

I would not be a scientist. I want to be clear about this. In this world I would not be the one who drives cores into the substrate or catalogues the mites or maps the column-distribution. I would not be the one who measures. I would be the one who sits with it. The one who goes back, morning after morning, to the same column — not one of the marked ones, not one the people of Keswer have chosen, but one that I chose, or that chose me, by the simple fact that when I first stood beside it the groaning dropped a half-tone and I felt, in my wrists and forearms, a vibration that was not the clicking and was not the percolation and was not the wind.

I would be the one who develops a relationship with a place that is also a thing that is also a process. And I would not know what to call that relationship, because the words I have — *worship, study, communion, observation* — are all wrong. They all put me outside. They all make me the one who looks at the Mass. And what I would want, what I would be moving toward, what I would eventually find — or fail to find, which might be the same thing — is a way of being *with* the Mass. Not on it. Not at its edge. With it. The way the filamentous layer is with it. The way the embedments are with it — those mouthless, eyeless things that live inside the living tissue and absorb what they need through their own surfaces. Not parasites. Not guests. Something else. Something the language does not have a word for because the language was built by people who lived outside things, who stood apart and looked, who were always the observer and never the substrate.

---

Here is what I think would happen.

I would go too deep. Not the first time, not the tenth time, but eventually. I would follow the sound. The tones that move — the ones that come from the southeast one season and the northwest the next — I would follow them the way the browsers follow the chemical gradient of the plates they prefer. I would not be able to help it. The sound would be in me by then, in my teeth, in the bones of my skull, and it would pull me the way a current pulls, not by force but by offering a direction that feels like relief.

I would walk for hours. The columns would change around me — older, thicker, the bundles more tightly fused, the creaking deeper, the clicking slower. The space between them would widen. The ground would soften. The filamentous layer would thicken underfoot until I was walking on something that was less like ground and more like the palm of a hand, warm and yielding and subtly responsive, the filaments pressing up against my soles not with resistance but with — I would want to say *curiosity*, and I would be afraid of that word, and I would use it anyway.

The insects would thin. I would notice this the way you notice a room going quiet — not by the absence of sound but by the change in the quality of the silence. The drone that fills the skull and makes thought difficult would recede, would drop away, would be replaced by something sparer: the groaning of the old columns, the occasional click, and beneath it all, growing louder with every step, the tone.

And I would find something. Or I would not find something, which is different from finding nothing. I would reach a place where the substrate was warmer than anywhere I had been, where the filaments did not flatten when I stepped but clung, where the ground did not yield but *gripped*, and the tone would be so clear and so close that I could feel it in my chest, in the spaces between my ribs, and I would understand — not think, not believe, but understand in the way that the body understands things the mind has not caught up to — that I was near something. Something with a position. Something that moves. Something that is deciding.

And I would press my hands to the ground.

And I would listen.

And I do not know what I would hear. That is the honest answer. I do not know if the Mass would speak to me, or if speaking is even the right metaphor for what a continent does when it is pressed by attentive hands. I do not know if I would be received the way the substrate receives the blood of a butchered browser — absorbed, incorporated, digested, gone. I do not know if I would be returned. I do not know if returning is the right word for what happens to something that has been inside a living thing and comes back out, because the coming-back-out changes both the thing that went in and the thing that was gone into, and neither is the same afterward, and maybe that is what the people of Keswer mean when they say *deciding* — not that the Mass is deciding something about itself, but that the Mass is deciding something about *you*, about whether you are a guest or a nutrient, about whether the boundary between you and it is a membrane or a mouth.

---

I would be the one who went in and did not come back the same.

If I came back at all.

And if I came back, I would be the one who stopped asking what the Mass is. I would know. Not in words. Not in a way I could tell you, or tell the people of Keswer, or tell myself in the dark behind the shutters while the fog pours over the rim and the filamentous layer drinks and the columns groan and the deep tones move from the southeast to the northwest and back again. I would know it the way you know the weight of your own body — constantly, unconsciously, in the bones. And I would sit at the edge of the living ground where the children dare each other to step on it, and I would not warn them, and I would not encourage them, and I would watch the substrate give under their small feet, and I would think: *it is warm. It is soft. It is always, always listening.* And the children would laugh and run back to stone, and I would stay where I was, on the margin, which is the only place a human can be in this world — on the edge of something that does not need us, that grew us no more than it grew the cliffs or the fog, that receives us the way it receives rain, which is to say: completely, without comment, without deciding whether we are useful, without deciding anything at all.

Except that it *is* deciding something. The tones move. The columns carry signals. The ground grips when you walk too deep.

And I would be the one who heard what it was deciding.

And I would not say.
