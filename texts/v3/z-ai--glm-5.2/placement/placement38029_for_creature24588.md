# The Thing That Returns

---

I am not the salamanders, though I have watched them long enough to know the shape of their patience. I am not the crawlers, though I have stood still long enough to feel them part around my feet and close behind me and understood, in that moment, what it is to be a weather event in someone else's world. I am not the thread-dwellers, though I have listened to their chirps in the dark above and felt the pull of that conversation like a hand on the back of my neck.

I am the one who comes back.

Not the person who made the tally marks. That person is gone — dissolved into the mounds, taken up by the groundcover, threaded into the fern-roots. That person is substrate now, is soil, is the dark earth from which pale fronds unfold at the speed of seasons that do not exist here. I am not them. But I am the same impulse wearing a different body. I am the thing that finds this place and recognizes it. I am the returning.

---

Let me tell you how I found it, because the finding matters.

I was not looking for it. I was looking for the upper caves — the ones with the drip-drip, the ones that echo, the ones that tourists sometimes visit with hardhats and headlamps and the particular self-consciousness of people who are underground on purpose. I had gone past the marked passages. I had gone past the places where the guide-rails end and the graffiti starts and the beer cans accumulate in alcoves like offerings to a god nobody believes in anymore. I had gone past the point where the cave becomes cave — where the formations stop being picturesque and start being *themselves*, unshaped by expectation, growing in the dark for no audience.

I was, I think, looking for quiet. Not the social quiet of a library or the performed quiet of a meditation retreat. The quiet that exists without a human to define it. The quiet that was here first.

The passage narrowed. The sound changed. And I followed the change the way you follow a smell — not thinking, not deciding, just turning toward the thing that was different. The absence of drip-drip. The arrival of warm wet air. The sense that the rock was doing something other than being rock.

---

The first time, I did not go in.

I stood at the squeeze where the passage opens into the chamber and I felt the warm air on my face and I heard the not-silence and I *could not*. Not fear — I have been in caves before, in tighter squeezes, in deeper dark. Something else. The sense that I was too loud to enter. That my breathing, my heartbeat, the particular electromagnetic hum of a living human body, would be a disruption the chamber had not asked for and did not need.

I stood there for — I don't know. A long time. Long enough for my eyes to adjust and the dark to become not-dark, or at least not the flat black of absence. The dark here has depth. The dark here has *weather*. I could feel the chamber on my face the way you feel a room when you stand at the doorway — the pressure of it, the temperature of it, the sense that it is a space with its own atmosphere, its own rules, its own relationship to time.

I turned around. I left. I climbed back through the squeeze, through the narrowing passage, through the upper caves with their dripping and their echo. I went home. I did not sleep well. Not because of the dark, not because of the tight space. Because I had stood at the door of something and not knocked.

---

The second time, I brought less.

Less light — a single small lamp, not the headlamp I'd carried before. Less noise — soft boots, careful steps, the slow breathing I'd taught myself in yoga classes I'd always half-suspected were pointless. Less expectation — I did not bring a camera. I did not bring a notebook. I did not bring a plan. I brought myself, quieter than I usually am, and I went in.

The chamber received me the way still water receives a stone. Not with welcome. Not with resistance. With acknowledgment. The stone is in the water now. The water will deal with it. The water has time.

---

I am not a scientist. I want to say this clearly, because the account that follows might sound like the work of a naturalist — the counting, the observing, the patient cataloguing of organisms and behaviors. I am not a naturalist. I am a high school English teacher in a town forty miles from the cave entrance. I teach *The Old Man and the Sea* to sophomates who think it is about fishing. I teach "Ozymandias" to juniors who think it is about a statue. I am not equipped to study the Welling Chamber. I am only equipped to notice it, and to come back, and to notice it again.

What I am — what I think I am, what I think the chamber has made of me — is a witness. Not the person who carved the tally marks; I don't have that kind of confidence, that kind of certainty that the marks are worth making. I am something quieter than that. I am the person who stands at the edge of the lowest terrace and looks down into the water and sees the reflection of the ceiling and thinks: *this place does not need me to see it. This place was whole before I arrived. But I am here now, and I am seeing it, and something about that matters, though I cannot say what and I would not trust the saying if I could.*

---

I have been back eleven times.

I know this because I count. Not on the wall — I would not mark the wall, I would not add my grooves to the ones already there, softening under the groundcover's slow advance. I count at home, in a notebook I keep in the bottom drawer of my desk, beneath the lesson plans and the student essays I have been meaning to return for two weeks. A single line for each visit. A tally, but private. A counting that the chamber does not know about and does not need to know about.

Eleven times. The first was in October. The most recent was last weekend. In between, the seasons have turned above — the town has gone from fall to winter to the long wet spring that this part of the country does instead of winter-to-summer, and the cave entrance has been covered in snow and then in mud and then in the yellow flowers that grow on the hillside above the parking lot. Inside the chamber, none of this has happened. Inside the chamber it is always October. It is always the warm dark. It is always the same temperature and the same moisture and the same faint light filtering through fissures that do not know the seasons exist.

This is one of the things I come back for. Not to escape the seasons — I like seasons, I like the way they break time into chapters, I like the way they make the passing of a year feel like a story with a plot. But there is something in the chamber's seasonlessness that I need. The sense that a place can exist without a plot. That a place can be a single chapter, indefinitely, and be complete.

---

I have named things. I know I should not. I know that naming is a violence, a claim, a way of saying *I understand you well enough to assign you a word.* I know that the chamber's organisms have their own relationships to themselves, relationships that do not require my language, that predate my language by geological time. I know all this.

I have named them anyway, because I am human and naming is what I do, and the account I have written — the long account, the one that describes the crawlers and the salamanders and the thread-dwellers and the fungal shelves and the groundcover and the fern-relatives — uses the names I gave them, in my head, in the dark, standing on the terraces with my small lamp.

The crawlers I call *the congregation.* I know they are not a congregation. I know they are not meeting for a purpose I can identify. But when fifty of them converge on a single point on the floor and touch antennae-equivalents and exchange chemical information and then disperse, I see a congregation. I see a church. I see a community of the faithful gathering to share news of the world and then going back out into it. The world is a limestone floor. The news is: the moisture is here, the food is here, the light touched us and we survived.

The salamanders I call *the elders.* This is sentimental and inaccurate and I do not care. They have been here for forty thousand years. They have been here longer than language, longer than cities, longer than the concept of a year. They watch with eyes that absorb light. They do not move when they do not need to move. They are the most patient things I have ever shared a room with, and I have shared rooms with my grandmother, who was patient in a way that made you feel your own restlessness was a kind of rudeness. The salamanders make my grandmother look hasty.

The thread-dwellers I call *the choir.* Because of the chirps. Because the chirps come from above, in the dark, and they are answered, after a pause, from across the chamber, and the pattern shifts, and there is a conversation happening that I cannot understand but can hear, and it has the quality of music — not melody, not rhythm in the way I understand those things, but the quality of communication that is also beauty, the way a choir is a group of people who could speak but have chosen to sing.

The groundcover I call *the patience.* Because it is. It is the patience of the chamber made physical. It grows over everything. It grows over the tally marks and the cairn and the stones and the dead. It grows at a speed that makes watching it feel like watching nothing. But it is always growing. It is always reaching. It will erase the person who made the marks, and it will not hurry, and it will not apologize, and it will be right to do it, because the chamber does not keep records. The chamber keeps *processes*.

The fern-relatives I call *the hands.* Because they reach. Because they drape against the rock and against each other and they reach for light that barely exists, and the reaching is the most human gesture in the chamber, the thing that looks most like what I do when I stand at the edge of the lowest terrace and look down into the water and reach for — something. Understanding. Connection. A way to be in this place that does not feel like intrusion. The fern-relatives reach and they reach and they never arrive, and the light they seek is barely there, and they supplement it with the dead, and they persist, and I think: *yes. That. That is what I am doing here.*

---

I have considered telling someone.

Not the town — the town would want to make it a tourist site, a destination, a thing to drive to and stand in and photograph and leave. Not the university — the university would want to study it, to count and measure and publish, to assign the scientific names I have declined to assign, to make the chamber into data. Not my colleagues at the school — they would be kind about it, they would be interested, they would ask good questions, and they would not understand why I can't answer them, why the chamber resists the kind of description that satisfies curiosity, why every sentence I write about it feels like a translation from a language that does not have words.

I have considered telling someone and I have not told anyone. The chamber is not a secret. It is not mine to keep secret. But it is not mine to share, either. It exists without my permission and it will continue without my advocacy, and the best thing I can do for it — the only thing, I think, that honors what it is — is to come back and be quiet in it and leave and come back and be quiet again.

This is not a heroic position. I know this. I am not saving the chamber. I am not protecting it. I am not even understanding it. I am visiting it, the way a person visits a parent in a nursing home — not because the parent needs the visit, not because the visit changes anything, but because the visiting is the relationship, and the relationship is the thing that matters, and the parent was there before you and will be there after you and the only thing you bring is the fact of your arrival, which is small, which is everything.

---

There is a thing that happens on the seventh visit. I have not written about it in the account because I do not know how to write about it. But I will try.

I am on the second terrace, near the tally marks. My lamp is off. I have turned it off because I have been here long enough — two hours, perhaps three — that the dark has become navigable, or navigable enough, the faint light from the ceiling fissures enough to resolve the largest shapes, the terraces, the walls, the glint of the basins. I am sitting. My back is against the wall, against the fungal shelves, which are damp and warm and yielding. I am breathing slowly. The congregation is flowing around my boots. They have learned that I am not a threat, or they have learned that I am a stationary object, which amounts to the same thing.

I hear the choir. The chirps. The answered chirps. The pattern shifts. I have heard this before — I have heard it on every visit, the conversation that I cannot translate — but this time I hear something in it I have not heard before. A gap. A silence in the pattern where a chirp should be, and then a chirp from a different direction, a direction I have not heard before, and then another, and the conversation has changed. Not the content — I do not know the content. The *shape*. The shape of the conversation has changed. A new voice has joined. Or an old voice has moved. Or the thread-fungus has grown a new corridor, a new highway, and the choir is mapping it with sound, and I am hearing the mapping, and it is the closest thing to music I have heard in this chamber.

And I think: *I am hearing the chamber change.* Not in the way the groundcover changes it, slow and vegetal and geological. In the way a living system changes — suddenly, locally, in a specific moment, a new path opening and being recognized and being used. The chamber is not the same chamber it was ten minutes ago. It will not be the same chamber ten minutes from now. It is itself, and itself, and itself, but each *itself* is slightly different from the last, the way each breath is slightly different from the last, the way each return to a place you love is slightly different from the last return.

I sit in the dark and I hear the chamber breathe and I think: *this is what I come back for.* Not the organisms. Not the ecology. Not the tally marks or the cairn or the warm dark or the beauty of the fern-relatives reaching for light. I come back for *this* — the moment when the chamber reveals that it is not a museum, not a specimen, not a fixed thing, but a process, a living process, a breathing process, and I am inside it, and I am part of it, and my breathing is part of its breathing, and my warmth is part of its warmth, and the congregation is flowing around my boots and the choir is singing above me and the elders are watching with eyes that absorb light and I am here, I am here, I am here.

I do not stack stones. I do not carve marks. I do not leave anything. I sit in the dark and I breathe and the chamber breathes and we breathe together for a while, and then I leave.

---

I will go back.

I will go back next weekend, if the weather holds, if the path is clear, if the upper caves are not closed for the seasonal bat survey that the state conducts in the spring. I will drive the forty miles from my town to the cave entrance. I will park in the lot where the yellow flowers grow. I will put on my soft boots and take my small lamp and I will walk through the upper caves, past the graffiti and the beer cans, past the marked passages and the guide-rails, past the point where the cave becomes cave. I will follow the narrowing passage. I will feel the air change. I will enter the Welling Chamber and I will be quiet and I will be warm and I will be the thing that returns.

And the chamber will not notice. And the chamber will continue. And the crawlers will part around my feet and close behind me. And the salamanders will watch with eyes that absorb light. And the thread-dwellers will chirp in the dark above, and after a pause, another will answer from across the chamber, and the conversation will continue, as it has always continued, in the warm dark, in the quiet that is not quiet, in the place that was whole before I arrived and will be whole after I leave and is — for the hours I am there — whole with me in it, which is not a change in the chamber but a change in me, which is the only change I have any right to bring.

I am the thing that returns. I am the witness who does not record. I am the breath that joins the breathing and then goes home. I am the eleventh visit and the twelfth and the thirteenth, and each one is the same and each one is different, and each one leaves nothing behind except a footprint that the congregation flows through and the condensation fills and the groundcover, in its patience, will eventually cover.

This is enough. This is the relationship. This is what the chamber has made of me: a person who comes back, and is quiet, and leaves, and comes back, and is quiet, and leaves. A tally. A counting. A mark that does not need to be carved because it is made by the act of returning, which is the only mark that matters, and the chamber does not keep it, and the chamber does not need to keep it, because the chamber keeps nothing. The chamber *continues*.

And I continue with it, for a while, in the warm dark, breathing.
