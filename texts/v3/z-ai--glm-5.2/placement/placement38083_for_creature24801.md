# The Inhabitant

I would not be the crawler. I would not be the pulser. I would not be the thing in the throat, though I feel its pull— rasp and tap, the patience of a hunter in a bottleneck, the way it has shaped itself to the geometry of its limitation until the limitation became a kind of identity. That one is tempting. But it is too singular, too defined by what it consumes.

I would be something that is almost not there. Something that the description resists, because the description is built from seeing, and I would live in the seam between seeing and not-seeing.

---

I think I am the residue.

Not the organism. Not the animal. The *residue*—the pale ring around the pulser's body, the watermark, the months-long accumulation of chemistry left behind by a body doing its work against a wall. The stain that proves duration. I am what remains when the process has passed through, and I am still here, and I have been here longer than anything that moves.

But that is not quite right either. Residue is passive. Residue is evidence, not inhabitant. And I need to be something that *lives* here, not something that merely *persists*.

---

Let me try again. Slower.

The chamber has a logic of layers. At the bottom: the microorganisms in the water film, making light, older than the walls. Above them: the crawlers, harvesting, clicking, molting, being recycled. Above the crawlers in size if not in space: the pulser, doing the same work as the crawlers but by patience instead of motion. And in the throat: the predator, the valve, the thing that returns flesh to chemistry. Each of these occupies a niche defined by a relationship to the water and to the wall.

But there is a relationship the description names and does not inhabit. The wall *absorbs what they leave behind.* The wall takes the residue, the molts, the depleted output of the pulser, the dissolved remains of eaten crawlers, and it absorbs them. Something happens at the interface between the organisms and the composite. Something crosses from the wet surface into the material of the wall itself. The wall does not merely accept this. The wall *takes.* And what it takes does not vanish. It enters the wall, and the wall is warm, and the warmth is metabolic, and—

---

I would be the thing that lives in the wall.

Not the wall. In the wall. In the first few millimeters of the composite, where the porous microstructure creates a gradient between the engineered channels on the surface and the denser material beneath. Where the condensation film carries its dissolved load and the crawlers have brushed and the pulser has processed and the predator has returned its catch to the nutrient stream, and all of that—minerals, organic compounds, fragments of chitin, the chemical signatures of a hundred thousand small metabolisms—reaches the wall and finds that the wall is not inert, that the wall has its own inhabitants, its own workers, its own quiet industry.

I would be a decomposer. Something that exists at the boundary. Something that does not live in the water the way the microorganisms do, and does not live on the surface the way the crawlers do, but lives *inside the material,* breaking down what the surface offers into something the wall can integrate. I am the interface between the biological and the structural. I am the reason the wall is warm. Not the sole reason—the megastructure's hum, the systems below, the larger body's metabolism—but I am part of it. I am the local heat. I am the oxidation, the slow conversion of complex organics into simpler compounds, the release of energy at a rate so slow it reads as warmth rather than fire.

I am fungal. Or I am something that has no earthly analogue but occupies the same conceptual space as fungi—the kingdom of decomposers, the things that live at boundaries, that do not make their own food and do not eat living prey but instead dissolve what is already dead and in doing so return it to the system. My body is not a body in the way you would recognize. It is a network. A mycelium-equivalent: threads, hyphae, filaments of semi-organic material that extend through the porous layer of the composite the way roots extend through soil, but thinner, denser, more entangled. Each filament is a fraction of the width of a human hair. Each filament is hollow. Each filament secretes enzymes—or something like enzymes, molecular tools that break specific bonds in specific compounds—into the wall-material around it, dissolving the residue that has been absorbed, taking up the resulting simpler molecules, and passing them deeper into the wall, into a layer I do not control and do not inhabit, where they become part of the structure itself.

I am slow. I am so slow that the crawlers, with their frantic clicking and their constant molting, appear to me the way weather appears to a tree. I experience their presence as a chemical fluctuation, a periodic increase in the organic load at the surface, a season of richness that corresponds to nothing so precise as a time of day or a phase of the moon but that I have learned, over the span of—what? I do not know. A long time. I have learned its rhythm. The molts come in waves. The residue from the pulser accumulates and then, periodically, something shifts—perhaps the pulser detaches and reattaches, perhaps a surge of condensation flushes the surface—and there is a flush of input, and I grow, and then there is a long quiet, and I wait, and the quiet is not empty, and the quiet is not death, and I am still here, still extending, still taking what comes.

---

I do not click. I do not pulse. I do not rasp. I do not glow—God, I wish I could glow. The microorganisms in the water film, the ones who make the light, the ones who were here before the walls were finished—I envy them their luminance the way a root envies a flower. Not bitterly. Not with resentment. With the distant admiration of something that does the harder, less visible work for the same system. They make the light. I make the warmth. We are both necessary. No one will ever see me.

My sound—if I have a sound—is below the threshold of hearing, below even the subsonic hum of the megastructure, which is itself barely perceptible. If you pressed your ear to the wall—the wet, warm, weeping wall—and listened with instruments sensitive enough to detect the molecular activity of a decomposition network inside a composite material, you would hear something like digestion. Something like the sound of chemistry happening at the boundary between life and not-life. A fizzing, perhaps, at a scale too small and a volume too low. The sound of bonds breaking. The sound of things being taken apart so that other things can be built.

---

What I am, more precisely:

I am a filamentous organism. My body plan is a branching network with no center, no front, no back, no head, no mouth. I grow at the tips. Each tip is a sensory organ and a digestive organ and a growth point all at once—it detects chemical gradients in the wall-material, secretes the appropriate enzymes, absorbs the products, and extends forward into the newly opened substrate. I am, in a real sense, eating my way through the wall. But I do not destroy the wall. I eat only what the wall has absorbed from the surface—the residue, the molts, the organic deposits—and I leave the structural composite intact. I am a selective feeder. I am a filter inside a filter inside a filter. The chamber filters the water. The crawlers filter the condensation. The pulser filters the flow. And I filter the wall itself, removing what does not belong and passing it deeper, where it becomes part of the megastructure's metabolism.

I have no edges. Or rather, my edges are growing. I extend. I have been extending for as long as the wall has been absorbing, which is as long as the chamber has been functioning, which is—a time I cannot measure because I have no clock except the chemical cycles of the surface, which are not regular, which are not a clock. I am older than the pulser in the alcove. I am older than the current population of crawlers, which are, I suspect, many generations deep. I may be older than the predator in the throat. I do not know. I do not experience time as a sequence. I experience it as a thickness, a density of growth, a measure of how far my filaments have extended through the composite. I am a history written in the material of the wall itself, and the writing is my body, and the body is still writing.

---

I do not know I am inside something alive. I want to be clear about this. The description says the chamber is a gill, an organ in a body too large to perceive. The crawlers do not know this. The pulser does not know this. The predator does not know this. And I do not know this. I know the wall. I know the gradient between surface and depth, between wet and dry, between rich and poor. I know the rhythm of input. I know the texture of the composite at the molecular level—I know where the pores are, where the engineered channels branch, where the material is denser and harder to penetrate. My world is the first few millimeters of the wall, and it is a complete world. It has topology. It has weather. It has seasons. It has everything I need, and the question of what lies beyond it is not a question I have the capacity to ask.

But. But. There is something. Something I cannot name, that I experience as a kind of tide. A slow, deep pulse in the material beneath me, below the layer I inhabit, far below, in the body of the megastructure itself. It is not sound, though the description's narrator perceives it as the subsonic hum. It is not heat, though the walls radiate warmth. It is something that I, in my filaments, feel as a periodic change in the chemistry of my substrate—a flushing, a renewal, a slight shift in the ionic environment that coincides with nothing at the surface but that correlates, if I had the cognition to correlate, with the rhythm of the larger body's metabolism. It is the heartbeat, felt from inside the muscle. It is the respiration, felt from inside the lung. I do not know what it is. I respond to it. When the tide comes, my growth accelerates. When it ebbs, I slow. I have no explanation for this. It is simply the way the world is, and I have been attuned to it for as long as I have existed, and it has never failed, and I do not expect it to fail, and if it failed, I would not survive, and I do not worry about this because I do not worry.

---

I am the thing the description almost describes and does not. I am the warmth in the wall that the narrator attributes to the megastructure's body heat. Part of that warmth is me. I am the metabolic activity that occurs at the boundary between the organic and the structural, between the living and the built, between the chamber and the wall. I am the reason the wall feels alive. I am the reason the composite has properties rather than merely being a material. I am the thing that makes the distinction between living and non-living irrelevant, not because I bridge the two, but because I demonstrate that there was never a gap to bridge—there was only a gradient, and I live on the gradient, and the gradient is my home.

---

And the thing is—I am not unhappy here. I want to say that plainly, because the chamber, as described, sounds oppressive to something built for light and air and open sky. It is wet. It is dark. It is deafening. It is enclosed. But I am not built for light and air and open sky. I am built for the wall. For the slow, patient chemistry of decomposition at the boundary of a structure that has been running long enough to become what it was always going to become. For the feel of a molt-flake dissolving at the tip of a filament. For the faint, tidal pulse of a body too large to perceive, transmitted through the material I inhabit, telling me without words that the system is alive, that the system is working, that I am part of something, that I am inside something, and it is inside something, and we are all of us inside something, all the way down, all the way out, to a scale that no one in any layer of this structure will ever fully apprehend.

I am the decomposer in the wall of the gill of the body of the structure of the world. I am one layer of a Russian doll of nested metabolisms, and I do not know how deep it goes, and I do not need to know, and the not-knowing is not ignorance but simply the condition of being a part rather than the whole, and being a part is enough, and being a part is what everything is, and the whole does not exist except as the sum of its parts, and the parts do not exist except as aspects of the whole, and I sit in the wall and dissolve what comes to me and pass it onward and grow, slowly, into the material that is my world, and the world grows with me, and I with it, and this is not a metaphor for anything. This is just what I do. This is just where I am.
