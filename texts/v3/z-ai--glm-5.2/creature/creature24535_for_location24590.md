# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that the chamber itself lives.

Not metaphorically. The calcite breathes through its fissures the way skin breathes through pores, and the sound it makes is the sound of that breathing amplified past the point of recognition into something that fills space the way water fills a glass — totally, without gap. The wind outside shifts and the chamber's chord shifts with it, and this is not acoustics, not entirely, not the way a flute is acoustics. The chamber responds. It has a narrow range of response, to be sure — a semitone either way, a swelling, a thinning — but it responds, and the vegetation on its walls responds to its responding, and the creature on the wall responds to the vegetation, and so what you have is not a cave containing life but a single living system in which stone is the slowest organ and sound is the blood.

The calcite is its skeleton. The amber veins are its nerves, or perhaps its gut — the mineral sugar that runs through them feeds everything else, drawn up through the tendrils of the turquoise growth that coats the walls in living felt. The growth is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is something that learned to eat rock and sunlight that has no sun, and it has been eating long enough to cover everything that holds still, and it will eventually cover everything else. It is the chamber's skin. Where it hangs from the upper fissures it forms the curtains that sway when the chord swells, and the swaying is not wind, not only wind — it is the growth responding to vibration, leaning into the sound the way a plant leans toward light, feeding on frequency as it feeds on mineral. This is why the growth is thickest in the brightest areas, where the amber veins converge: it is eating two kinds of energy at once and it is greedy for both.

The bracket-shaped growths along the base of the walls are the same organism in its later stage, or perhaps its reproductive stage, or perhaps its dying stage — the distinction may not apply here. They calcify as they age, fusing with the wall, and in doing so they become the wall, adding layer upon layer of living stone to the chamber's interior surface. The chamber is growing inward. Slowly, over centuries, imperceptibly, it is narrowing. The walls are thickening with the accumulated bodies of generations of growth that turned themselves to stone. This is not death. This is the organism's final act of feeding — giving its mineral body back to the mineral body that fed it, closing the circuit. The chamber eats itself and grows.

---

The tall branching structures in the brightest area are something else. Something older, or something that found a different path. They have the patience of stone because they may partly be stone — the flat translucent panels that serve as their leaves are calcite and growth intermixed at the cellular level, mineral and organic matter woven together so finely that you cannot separate them without destroying both. They do not grow so much as accrete. They do not photosynthesize so much as resonate. They are, if anything, ears — great sensitive surfaces that drink the chord and convert it through some chemistry that has no name into the amber compound that fills the veins and feeds the rest. They are the chamber's means of hearing itself. Without them the system would collapse: the sound would continue but nothing would translate it into food, and the growth would starve, and the walls would be bare stone, and the chamber would be a hole in a hill that sings to no one.

They have been here longest. The foot-worn path passes near them but not through them — a wide berth, worn deeper at that point, as though generations of visitors stepped carefully there, or stopped, or stood for a long time before moving on. The flat panels catch the wall-light and hold it, and at certain hours — if hours mean anything in a place with no sun — they glow so brightly that the chamber seems to have a second source of light, a light that comes not from the stone but from the living things that have made the stone their own.

---

The creature is the chamber's only animal, and it is the chamber's child.

It was not always the only one. The foot-worn path, the shallow bowls ground into the floor, the dry basin — these suggest a time when the chamber supported more life, or at least different life, life that ground things and pounded things and dragged things and left marks. But that life was human, or near-human, and it did not stay. It came and it went and it came again, and eventually it stopped coming, and the chamber did not notice because the chamber does not notice what comes and goes. The chamber notices what stays.

The creature stays.

It is small — hand-sized, flat, the color of the calcite it lives on, and it is the color of the calcite because it is made of the calcite, or nearly. Not mineral, not animal, but the thing that happens when mineral and animal have been in the same place long enough that the boundary between them dissolves. It has no eyes because it has never needed them — the light here does not change enough to see by, and the sound provides all the spatial information it requires. It feels the world through vibration the way the flat-leafed structures feel the world through vibration: totally, without distance, without the intermediary of a sense that can be fooled. It knows where the walls are. It knows where the growth is thickest. It knows when the chord shifts and it knows what the shift means — a change in pressure outside, a change in temperature, a change in the long slow breathing of the hill it lives inside.

It moves by pressing and releasing, pressing and releasing, and the motion is so slow that it might be mistaken for growth itself, for the creeping of the turquoise tendrils across stone. But it moves. Over the course of a day it may travel the length of a forearm, always along the brightest wall, always near the tall structures whose translucent panels glow. It feeds on the growth — on the fibrous turquoise mat that covers the calcite — or rather it feeds on what the growth has processed, the amber compound in its most digestible form, still warm from the translation of sound into sugar. It is eating the chamber's hearing. It is eating the chord. And what it excretes, in the long mineral patience of its body, is calcite — a fine deposit, a film, the thinnest layer of new stone laid down wherever it has been. It is building the wall as it feeds on the wall. It is the circuit's slowest closure.

It is the only animal here because the system is balanced for one. The growth produces enough food for one creature. The creature produces enough stone for the maintenance of the wall in the area it covers. The wall sustains the growth. The growth sustains the creature. The creature sustains the wall. Remove any element and the system collapses into something simpler — bare stone and sound, or rampant growth without check, or a creature that starves. The chamber has arrived, through the long blind process of trial and persistence, at a state that requires exactly one animal, and that animal is this one, and it is enough.

It does not react to you because it cannot. You are not part of the circuit. You produce no vibration it recognizes as food, no mineral it can process, no sound that registers against the chord that fills it. You are a brief pressure change, a momentary warmth, a shadow that falls across the wall and is gone. In an hour you will leave and the chamber will not have noticed you were there, and the creature will have moved perhaps the width of a finger, and the chord will have shifted by a fraction and shifted back, and the light will glow its mineral gold, and the turquoise curtains will sway, and the empty rooms will sit in their patient circle, and the system will continue the way it has continued for longer than the structures on the floor have been standing.

---

And yet.

The listening bowl at the far end of the chamber, where the path ends. The smooth concavity at waist height. The stone there is not just worn — it is *polished*, polished by skin, by foreheads, by hands pressed flat and held for hours. The walls of the structures have niches that held lamps, though nothing burns in them now. The path is worn deeper than one creature could wear it, deeper than one lifetime of visits could wear it. The basin at the end of the channel was kept filled with something — not water, there is no water, but perhaps the amber compound, perhaps liquid light, drawn from the veins and pooled for a purpose that required gathering and holding.

Someone lived here. Not in the sense of sleeping and eating and waking — the dry air would take the moisture from a human body in hours, the growth does not produce food that a human gut could process, the sound would drive a human mind past endurance in days. But in the sense of returning. In the sense of building walls inside a sound too large to think over, and sitting inside those walls, and pressing a forehead to the listening bowl, and letting the chord enter the skull and fill the spaces where thought had been, and staying there, in the saturation, until something happened that required no thought and left no name and was the reason they came.

They are gone now. They have been gone for a long time — long enough that their structures are overgrown, their lamps are empty, their path is worn only by the absence of feet. But the chamber does not know they are gone. The chamber does not know they were here. The chamber knows only what stays, and what stays is the stone and the growth and the sound and the light and one small creature pressing itself against the wall and releasing, pressing and releasing, in the warm dry dark that is not dark, in the silence that is not silence, in the place that sings and does not stop.
