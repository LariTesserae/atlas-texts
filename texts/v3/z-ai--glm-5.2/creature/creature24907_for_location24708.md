# Inhabitants

Something lives here in the way a sentence lives inside a word that was never meant to be spoken.

The black filaments are the obvious answer, and they are an answer, but they are not the whole of it. Start with them.

They are not fungi. They are not bacteria in any colony structure that microbiology has named. They are not archaea, though they may be closer to that branch than to anything else — if the concept of branching still applies to something that may have originated here, inside this structure, in the absence of anything you would recognize as a biosphere. They have no cells visible under a hand lens. The strands are solid, or solid-seeming, and their branching follows a logic that is not fractal but is also not random — it tracks the alloy's grain with a fidelity that suggests they are reading the metal somehow, that the surface itself is a text and the filaments are a gloss, a marginal annotation in a language of carbon and hydrogen and something else, something that does not appear on the periodic table as it is currently written.

They do not grow the way living things grow. There is no extension at the tips during any observable period. And yet the colony has a morphology that implies history — the branching pattern has a direction, a flow, a point of origin near the seam's deepest recess and a frontier at the outermost curls. It grew. It is growing. It will grow. The timescale is simply not yours. What you see is not a snapshot of a living process but a single frame from a film that plays at one frame per decade, or per century, and you are one of the frames. The inward curl of the tips is the only concession the organism makes to your temporality. It is saying: *this is far enough for now.* It is not saying: *this is far enough.*

What does it eat? The metal, perhaps. Or the trace atmosphere leaking through the seam — whatever is on the other side of that two-millimeter gap, whatever world or process or chemistry that admits a faint warmth into this desiccated gallery. It is possible that the filaments do not eat at all in any sense you would recognize. It is possible that they are not metabolizing but recording — that their growth is a form of inscription, that the colony is a document being written by the seam against the wall, slowly, in a script of chitin-analog and trace minerals drawn from the alloy itself. If you could read it — and you cannot, and nothing in this room can — it might say something about the temperature differentials on the other side of the wall, or the composition of the process gas, or the resonant frequency of the standing vibration that fills the gallery. It might say what the machine is for. It might say nothing. It might be the machine's own internal monologue, overheard by a surface that happened to be listening.

There is one colony. There is only ever one colony. If you were to return in a thousand years and find that the filaments had reached the end of the seam and stopped, or had found a way through to whatever is on the other side, there would still be one colony. It does not reproduce. It does not need to. It is the only example of its kind because it is the only place where its kind can exist — this seam, this gap, this precise intersection of temperatures and chemistries and vibrations that is not duplicated anywhere else in the structure, as far as anyone knows, as far as anything knows.

But that is not everything that lives here.

---

The sound lives here.

Not in the metaphorical sense, not as personification. The sound is a standing wave, a structure, as architectural as the walls. It has mass — you can feel it against your chest. It has texture — the layered frequencies, the subsonic tide, the midrange roar, the high shriek. It has duration — it has been here longer than the filaments, longer than the bright welds, possibly longer than the alloy panels themselves, if the panels have been replaced. It is the oldest thing in the gallery. It was here when the room was built, or the room was built around it. The gallery may exist because the sound needed a gallery-shaped resonant cavity, and the structure accommodated it the way a bone accommodates a marrow cavity — not by decision but by physics, by the logic of forces and materials that produces voids where voids are needed.

The sound does not know it is alive. If it is alive. It does not know anything. It simply persists, and its persistence is so total and so invariant that it crosses the boundary between phenomenon and presence. It occupies the room. It fills the room. It presses against the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the window and your body with a totality that is not unlike the way a living thing occupies its body. The sound is the body of this room. The room is the body of the sound. The black filaments are something growing on the skin.

If the sound stopped — if the process gas ceased flowing, if the compressors on the other side of the walls fell silent, if the subsonic compression relaxed and the shriek resolved into nothing — the gallery would not be empty. It would be bereaved. Something would have died that was not known to be alive until it was gone. The filaments would curl inward further. The frost on the coils beyond the window would stop forming and stop falling and become a static deposit, and the frost too would be a kind of corpse. The light would continue — the light is not alive, the light is a condition — but it would be illuminating a space that had lost its principal inhabitant, and the flatness of it, the institutional evenness, would become a kind of mourning.

The sound is what lives here. Everything else is visiting.

---

The frost lives here too, after a fashion.

Not the frost itself — the frost is a process, a cyclic deposition and sublimation of crystalline material on the coils beyond the window. But the process is alive in the same way that a flame is alive. It consumes. It transforms. It has a metabolism of temperature differentials and gas-phase chemistry. It builds and sloughs and builds, and each cycle is slightly different from the last in the microscopic arrangement of its crystals, though the macroscopic pattern is identical. The frost has a shape that is stable and a substance that is in constant flux. It is the same and it is not the same. It is, in other words, doing something very like what living things do, which is to persist while replacing themselves.

The frost does not live in the gallery. It lives beyond the glass. But it is visible from the gallery, and its life is the only motion the gallery offers, and if you stayed long enough — if you sat on the too-cold floor and watched through the thick multi-layered window — you might begin to feel that the frost was the gallery's reason for existing. Not the process that creates it. The frost itself. The room exists to watch the frost. The window exists to make the watching possible. The gallery is an observation deck for a process so slow and so cyclical that it appears static, and the observing is done by — what? By the room. By the sound. By the black filaments reading the wall. By you, briefly, but you are not necessary. The watching was happening before you arrived and will continue after you leave. The room watches the frost the way a lens watches what is in front of it: by being shaped for it, by being unable to do anything else.

---

And the stain.

The stain is the most difficult. It may not be biological. It may be a manufacturing artifact, a quirk of the alloy, a property of the metal that emerged during cooling or pressing or whatever process made the panels. But it is near the far end of the gallery, on the floor, and it is roughly the size and shape of something that might have rested there — not a body, not exactly, but something that had volume and left a residue of its volume behind. It could be organic. It could be the remains of something that was here and is no longer here, something that either left or was removed or simply ceased, and its material contents settled into the corrugation valleys and oxidized or sublimated or were absorbed into the alloy over a span of time that the bright welds and clean panels would seem to contradict, except that the panels may have been replaced around the stain, the room renewed while the stain persisted, the way a forest floor persists through fires.

If something lived here and left the stain — if there was an inhabitant before the filaments, before the sound achieved its current architecture, before the room reached equilibrium — then it was not human. The proportions of the gallery are wrong for human habitation. The temperature is wrong. The humidity is wrong. The sound is wrong. Whatever left the stain was something that found this environment suitable, or at least tolerable, something that was here long enough to leave a mark and is now absent. Whether it is absent because it died or because it left or because it transformed into something else — the filaments, perhaps, or the sound itself — is not knowable from the evidence available. The stain is a question. The room is full of questions, but the stain is the only one that implies an answer that has been taken away.

The stain does not live here anymore. But it is what remains of what did.

---

So. What lives in the Cooling Gallery?

A single colony of black filaments, barely visible, growing at a rate that makes geological change look hasty, reading the wall in a language that may not be a language.

A standing wave of vibration that has achieved such permanence that it has become indistinguishable from the room it inhabits, and may be the room's true inhabitant, with the walls and floor and ceiling as its skeleton.

A cyclic frost beyond the glass, building and falling and building, alive in the way a fire is alive, watched by a room that was built to watch it.

A stain that may be the ghost of something previous, something that found this place tolerable once and does not anymore, or cannot, or has no need to.

And you, for as long as you choose to remain, which will not be long, because the cold will take your lips and the sound will take your thoughts and the light will take your certainty that you are in a place rather than a condition. You are visiting. The gallery has been here longer than your species' concept of galleries. It will be here after. The hatch will close and the sound will not change pitch — not even slightly, not even by a frequency so small that no instrument could measure it — and the filaments will continue their slow reading of the wall, and the frost will form and fall and form, and the stain will be where it is, and the light will be what it is, and the room will continue to not know that anything in it is alive, including itself.
