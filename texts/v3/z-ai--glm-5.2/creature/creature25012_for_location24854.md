# The Inhabitants of Ayun-Kel

---

They call themselves by the names of the terraces they were born on.

This is the first thing you learn. Not their word for themselves — they don't seem to have one that carries the weight you'd expect, no name for *the people* as a collective category. When they introduce themselves, they give a personal name and then a terrace designation, spoken the way you might say a surname. *Kel Amra, Third Tier, East Face.* *Vesh, Seventh Tier, the Garden Level.* The terrace comes first in their internal grammar, even when the syntax puts it second. You hear it in how they pause: the name is the prefix, the terrace is the root.

There are perhaps three hundred of them. Perhaps four. No one is certain, and no one is troubled by the uncertainty. They do not keep a census. Children are born — not often, but steadily — and they are given the terrace of their birth, and this is enough. The mountain keeps its own count in the wear patterns on the stairs, in the depth of the grooves worn into the iron ladders, in the layers of wax and oil on the cistern access hatches that are opened most often. If you wanted to know how many people lived here, you could read the mountain, and the people would consider that a more reliable method than asking.

---

## How They Look

They are not tall.

This is generational, not nutritional — they are well-fed, the terraces produce abundantly — but the mountain has selected for a certain frame over the centuries. The ceilings in the older interior passages are low. The ladder rungs are spaced for a shorter stride. The walkways along the narrow terraces, the ones clamped to the retaining walls where the mountain falls away into cloud, are built for a center of gravity that sits close to the ground. The people of Ayun-Kel are compact, broad through the chest, deep through the lung. Their limbs are dense with the particular muscle that comes from climbing — not the long lean muscle of a runner but the rounded, knotted muscle of someone who has spent their life pulling themselves up iron rungs and pushing themselves up stone steps.

Their skin runs darker than you'd expect for this elevation, and this is the cloud's doing. The mist that wraps the mid-terraces does not burn away; it diffuses but never fully clears, and the people who live in it have a permanent warmth to their complexion, as if the light here has a color that stains over time. Their hair is dark, straight or loosely curled, and they wear it short or bound back. Loose hair in the mist becomes a sponge, and they learned long ago that this is impractical. The older ones, men and women both, often shave their heads entirely, and there is no significance to this beyond comfort.

Their hands are remarkable. Not callused in the way you'd expect — not the hard, cracked calluses of manual labor — but *stained*. The organic dyes of the fruit trees, the mineral deposits of the cistern water, the rust of the ironwork, the green of the moss that they are always brushing against: all of these have worked themselves into the skin of their palms and fingers in patterns that look, up close, like the sedimentation layers in the mountain's concrete. A person's hands tell you how they spend their days. The cistern engineers have dark crescents under their nails. The pruners — the ones who tend the vegetation — have green-black fingertips. The mechanics, the ones who work the interior machinery, have hands that look burnished, as if they've been polished with steel wool, which, in a sense, they have.

They dress in layers. Always in layers. The temperature on the mountain varies wildly between terraces — warm and humid at the lower levels, cool and bright at the summit, perpetually damp in the middle — and a person moving through the mountain will shed and add clothing continuously as they climb or descend. The base layer is a close-fitted garment of woven plant fiber, something like linen but denser, with a slight sheen that sheds water. Over this, a heavier tunic, often quilted, often patched in patterns that have nothing to do with fashion and everything to do with which fabric is available on which terrace. Over this, when needed, a cloak of treated leather — goat leather, from the animals on the broad terraces — that has been oiled until it is supple and dark and nearly waterproof. The cloaks are valuable. They are passed between people, between generations, and they develop the same patina as the mountain itself: stained, oiled, patched, worn smooth at the shoulders where straps have rubbed.

They go barefoot on the terraces they know well. The concrete, the stone, the moss — these are surfaces they trust, and their feet are tough and broad and as stained as their hands. On the narrow walkways, on the iron ladders, on the unfamiliar terraces, they wear shoes: thin-soled, soft, made from the same treated leather as the cloaks, with a grip pattern on the sole that mirrors the corrugation of the galvanized steel facing on the retaining walls. Someone designed those shoes for this mountain. Someone designed everything on this mountain.

## Where They Live

The dwellings are modest, and they are not the point.

This is important. The people of Ayun-Kel do not build homes. They live in spaces that the mountain provides — spaces that were, presumably, designed for some other purpose when the mountain was constructed, and that have been claimed, slowly and without force, as living quarters. The broader terraces have clusters of low buildings: brick walls, corrugated metal roofs, wooden doors that have swelled with the damp and now fit their frames the way a cork fits a bottle. These buildings were workshops once, or storage bays, or machinery access points. They still are, in some cases — a dwelling might share its space with a water pump, or a flywheel housing, or a junction box for the conveyor system. The people do not seem to mind this. The machinery is not noise to them. It is temperature, it is vibration, it is the sound of the mountain doing what it does, and sleeping next to it is no stranger than sleeping next to a beating heart.

The interiors are spare. Sleeping platforms raised off the floor — the concrete sweats, and anything on the ground gets damp. Woven mats for sitting. Small clay stoves, vented through the existing ductwork, for cooking and for warmth on the coldest nights. A few possessions: tools, clothing, vessels for water, containers of preserved food. The dwellings are not personalized in the way you might expect. There are no decorations on the walls, no images, no objects whose only purpose is aesthetic. This is not poverty or asceticism. It is simply that the mountain itself is the environment they inhabit, and the dwelling is only the place where they close their eyes. Their *life* is on the terraces.

A family — and the family structures here are loose, extended, more territorial than biological — occupies a section of a terrace. Not a building. A *section*. The buildings are where they sleep; the terrace is where they live. The section includes their dwellings, their share of the garden, their section of the water system, their access points to the machinery they maintain. The boundaries of these sections are not marked. They are known. They are passed down through demonstration — *this is where we prune, this is where we clear, this is the cistern we tend* — and the knowledge is so deeply embedded that a stranger could not identify the boundaries, but every resident of the mountain knows exactly where their section ends and another begins.

## What They Do

The work of Ayun-Kel is maintenance, and the people understand this with a clarity that borders on the religious but never crosses into it.

They do not worship the mountain. They do not reverence the machinery. They do not have rituals of propitiation or ceremonies of gratitude. What they have is a practical understanding, arrived at over generations, that the mountain is a system that requires participation, and that their role in the system is specific and limited and important.

The cistern engineers are the most visible of the specialists. Every terrace has a cistern, and every cistern has an engineer — or, more accurately, every engineer has a cascade of cisterns, a chain of them running down through several terraces, and their job is to monitor the flow, clear the inlets, repair the channels, and ensure that the water moves at the right pace. Not too fast — a cistern that drains too quickly leaves the terrace below without enough pressure. Not too slow — a cistern that overflows floods the terrace above and undermines the concrete. The engineers know their cisterns the way a shepherd knows a flock: by sound, by behavior, by the particular way the water tastes at different times of day. They can hear a blockage from three terraces away. They can feel, through the vibration of the concrete, when a turbine downstream has shifted its rhythm.

The pruners are the other major specialization, and their work is more visible. The vegetation of Ayun-Kel is in equilibrium with the structure, but equilibrium is not stasis — it is a dynamic balance that requires constant, gentle adjustment. The pruners move through the terraces with short, curved blades — iron, forged on the mountain, honed to an edge that can cut through a vine the thickness of a wrist — and they trim, they guide, they redirect. A vine that is growing toward a drainage channel is bent along a railing instead. A tree whose roots are pressing against a retaining wall is root-pruned from below, carefully, over the course of weeks, so that it redirects its growth without dying. A moss carpet that has grown too thick over a ventilation grate is thinned, not removed — the pruners understand that the moss holds moisture that the grate needs, and they take only enough to keep the air flowing.

The pruners also tend the food plants. The fruit trees — the citrus, the dark-skinned figs, the honey-scented white blossoms — are their responsibility, and they harvest with a calendar that is not based on days or months but on the behavior of the trees themselves. When the fig-skin darkens further and the stem begins to soften. When the citrus heavies on the branch and the oil in the peel begins to scent the air. When the white blossoms fall and the fruit behind them swells. The pruners know these signs the way a sailor knows weather, and the harvest is not an event but a continuous process, a slow rolling gathering that moves from terrace to terrace as the fruit ripens at different elevations on different schedules.

The mechanics are the third group, and they are the most opaque to an outsider. Their work happens inside the mountain, in the vaulted chambers behind the iron doors, in the spaces where the machinery lives. They do not fully understand the machinery — this is stated plainly, without shame or mystery. They understand *parts* of it. They know which levers control which flows. They know which valves to adjust when a pressure reading shifts. They know which bearings need grease, and how often, and they make the grease themselves from plant oils and mineral powders ground from the concrete dust that collects in the lower chambers. They know the sounds the machines make when they are running correctly, and they know the sounds they make when something is wrong, and they can diagnose a mechanical problem the way a doctor diagnoses a cough — by quality, by duration, by the specific note of the vibration.

But they do not know *why*. They do not know what the machines are for, in the largest sense. They know that the machines keep the water moving, the terraces stable, the interior ventilated. They do not know what the machines were originally built to do, whether there was a purpose beyond maintenance itself, whether the mountain was producing something once, or processing something, or serving a function that has since been forgotten. The machines are still running, and the people keep them running, and the question of ultimate purpose is not a question that occurs to them. It would be like asking a heartbeat what it is for. It is for itself. It is for continuing.

There are other roles. There are the path-keepers, who maintain the walkways and ladders and stairs — replacing rusted rungs, repointing crumbling masonry, clearing the drainage channels that run alongside every path. There are the animal-tenders, who manage the goat population — not domesticating them, exactly, but culling them when they overpopulate, guiding them away from the more fragile terraces, using their milk and their leather and their meat. There are the builders, who work rarely but with great care — reinforcing a wall that has begun to bow, adding a buttress where the concrete has cracked, patching a roof, fitting a new door. There are the teachers, who are not called teachers but who are the ones who walk the children through the mountain and show them, terrace by terrace, cistern by cistern, how the system works and where they fit into it.

And there are the climbers.

## The Climbers

The climbers are the ones who go to the upper terraces.

This needs explanation. The upper terraces of Ayun-Kel — the ones above the cloud layer, above the mid-level mist, in the region of bright sun and thin air and hard light — are not abandoned. They are not forbidden. They are simply *hard*. The steps become steeper. The terraces become narrower. The wind is stronger and colder. The vegetation thins — the lush green of the mid-levels gives way to sparse moss, to lichen, to bare concrete and iron. The machinery is louder up there, or seems louder, because there is nothing to muffle it — no leaves, no moss, no canopy. The flywheels turn in the open air. The pistons rise and fall with nothing around them but the sky.

The climbers go up anyway. They are a self-selecting group — not a caste, not a guild, just the people who are drawn upward, who feel the pull of the summit the way some people feel the pull of the sea. They are leaner than the others, longer-limbed, and their lungs are larger — you can see this in the depth of their chests, in the way they breathe at the lower elevations, slowly and fully, as if the air down here is thicker than they need.

They go up for several reasons. Some go to maintain the upper machinery, which is more exposed and more vulnerable to weather and requires more frequent attention. Some go to check the upper cisterns, which are smaller and more critical — they feed the entire cascade below, and a blockage at the top affects everything. Some go to watch the sky. The upper terraces are where you can see the horizon, where you can see the weather coming in, where you can see the world that exists beyond Ayun-Kel — the valley below, the other mountains in the distance, the flatlands to the south. The climbers are the ones who maintain the mountain's relationship with the outside, such as it is. They see the freight elevator's lower terminus. They see the supply barges that come up the river in the valley. They are the ones who know that there is a world beyond the mountain, and they carry this knowledge back down with them, and it does not seem to trouble them.

The climbers do not stay up long. The upper terraces are not welcoming — the air is thin, the wind is cold, there is no water beyond what you carry, and the sun at that elevation is intense enough to burn. They go up, they do what they need to do, and they come back down, and when they return to the mid-levels, they move through the cloud layer and back into the green and the damp and the sound of birds, and you can see something change in them — a softening, a settling, the way a compass needle settles when it finds north. They are back in the mountain's body. The upper terraces are the mountain's head, and they are worth visiting, but they are not where life is.

## The Children

The children of Ayun-Kel are not supervised in any way you would recognize.

This is not neglect. It is a different model of childhood, one that has been shaped by the mountain's specific character. The terraces are safe — not perfectly safe, but safe in the way a forest is safe, safe in the way a stream is safe, safe in the sense that the dangers are known and legible and the children learn to read them very early. A child who can walk is taught the edges. A child who can climb is taught the ladders. A child who can reach a cistern inlet is taught what the water sounds like when it is flowing correctly and what it sounds like when it is not.

The children move in packs. Three to eight of them, ranging in age from barely walking to nearly adult, and they move through the terraces with a fluidity that terrifies an outsider and is, to the mountain, simply correct. They know which terraces are theirs — the ones they were born on, the ones their families tend — and they range outward from there in expanding circles as they grow. A four-year-old stays within three terraces of home. A seven-year-old might range across a full tier. A twelve-year-old knows the mountain from the fifth tier to the ninth, knows the shortcuts, knows the hidden passages, knows which retaining walls have handholds and which cisterns you can climb through when the water is low.

They play, but their play is functional in ways that are not immediately apparent. They race each other up the ladders — this is practice for the climbing they will do as adults. They dam the drainage channels and build pools — this teaches them how the water system works, how flow and pressure and gradient interact. They catch the lizards and hold them and let them go — this teaches them the animals, the ecosystems, the way life has adapted to the made-environment. They imitate the sounds of the machinery — the thudding, the humming, the clanking — and they can reproduce them with startling accuracy, and this is not a game but a skill: a person who can mimic the sound of a machine can also detect when the sound has changed.

The children are not taught in a formal sense. There is no school. There is no curriculum. There is simply the mountain, and the adults moving through it, and the children following the adults and watching and doing. A child who shows an interest in the cisterns begins to accompany the cistern engineer on their rounds. A child who is good with the plants starts working alongside the pruners. A child who is drawn upward starts climbing with the climbers. The specialization emerges from inclination, and the mountain absorbs it, and the system adjusts. There is always someone to tend each cistern. There is always someone to prune each terrace. The mountain has never been without a mechanic, or a path-keeper, or a climber. The children become what the mountain needs, and the mountain needs what the children become, and the fit is not imposed but discovered.

## What They Believe

This is the hardest thing to describe, because they do not have a word for it.

The people of Ayun-Kel do not have a religion. They do not have myths of origin. They do not have a creation story — or rather, their creation story is the mountain itself, which is not a story but a fact, and facts do not require narration. The mountain was built. They do not know by whom. They do not know when. They do not know why. They do not speculate about these questions, not because they are forbidden but because the questions do not seem to generate heat in them. The mountain is here. They are here. The relationship between these two facts is not causal but *coincidental* — they coexist, they participate in each other, and that is sufficient.

What they have instead is something that has no name in their language or ours, something that is closer to *attention* than to faith. They pay attention to the mountain. They pay attention to its sounds, its rhythms, its shifts and changes. They pay attention to the way the light moves across the terraces over the course of a day. They pay attention to the way the mist thickens and thins, to the way the water tastes different in the morning than in the evening, to the way the machinery's rhythm changes with the temperature. They pay attention to each other — to the way someone walks, to the way someone breathes, to the small signs that tell you whether a person is tired or troubled or content.

This attention is not anxious. It is not hypervigilance. It is the attention of someone who lives in a place they love — not the love of passion or intensity, but the love of long familiarity, the love that comes from knowing every stone and every rung and every vine and every sound, and from being known in return. The mountain knows them. This is not a metaphor. The mountain's systems have adapted to their presence — the paths they walk are worn to fit their feet, the cisterns they tend most often are the ones with the most accessible hatches, the machinery they maintain most regularly runs most smoothly. The mountain has incorporated them the way it has incorporated the moss and the birds and the goats. They are part of its ecology. They are part of its continuation.

When someone dies — and people do die, from age, from illness, from a fall on the narrow terraces, from a misstep on a ladder — they are buried in the soil of the terrace they were born on. There is no ceremony. The body is placed in the earth, and a tree is planted above it, or a shrub, or a vine, and the vegetation grows, and the person becomes part of the terrace's biomass, part of its root system, part of the living layer that covers the concrete. This is not a spiritual belief. It is a material fact. The dead feed the living. The living tend the dead. The mountain continues.

The only thing that resembles a ritual is what happens at the transitions — when the light changes, when the cloud moves through, when the sun drops below the horizon and the terraces go from bright to diffuse to dark. At these moments, the people who are outside stop. They stop walking, stop working, stop talking. They stand still, and they watch, and they wait for the change to complete. It takes only a moment — a minute, perhaps two — and then they resume what they were doing. They do not do this deliberately. They do not do it consciously. It is a reflex, a response, the way a plant turns toward light. The mountain changes, and they acknowledge the change, and they continue.

## What They Are Like

They are quiet. Not silent — they talk, they laugh, they call to each other across the terraces, they imitate the birds, they sing while they work, low tunes that follow the rhythm of the machinery — but quiet in the sense that they do not fill space with sound. They are comfortable with silence. They are comfortable with the sounds of the mountain. They do not feel the need to distinguish themselves acoustically from their environment.

They are patient. This is the quality that defines them most clearly to an outsider. They move slowly, not because they are incapable of speed but because speed is not useful on the mountain. The machinery moves slowly. The water moves slowly. The plants grow slowly. The light changes slowly. The people have matched themselves to this tempo, and they do not feel it as constraint. They feel it as *correct*. A person who moved quickly on Ayun-Kel would be a disruption — to the paths, to the machinery, to the animals, to the air itself, which has a weight and a texture that rewards deliberation.

They are practical. They do not speculate. They do not plan beyond the next season. They do not have ambitions in the sense that the word implies — they do not strive for status, or wealth, or influence. They have status, of course — the cistern engineers are respected, the climbers are admired, the oldest pruners are regarded with a kind of affectionate reverence — but this status is earned through competence and through time, and it is not sought. It accumulates, the way lichen accumulates on a wall. You do not pursue it. You simply live, and if you live well, the mountain and the people recognize it.

They are curious, but their curiosity is specific. They are curious about the mountain. They want to know why a particular cistern overflows in the wet season. They want to know why a particular vine flowers twice a year when all the others flower once. They want to know what is behind a particular iron door that has never been opened in their lifetime. They are not curious about the world beyond the mountain. They know it exists — the climbers see it, the freight elevator connects to it — but it does not draw them. They do not want to leave. The ones who do leave — and there have been a few, over the centuries, people who felt the pull of the flatlands or the distant cities — are not prevented, are not judged, are not mourned. They simply go. And the mountain continues without them, and the people continue without them, and if they come back, they are welcomed without comment, and if they do not, they are forgotten without effort, because the mountain's memory is long but it is not sentimental.

They are content. This is a strange word to use, because contentment has a passive quality in most contexts, a suggestion of settled satisfaction, of lowered expectations. The contentment of Ayun-Kel is not passive. It is *active* — it is the contentment of a person who is doing exactly what they should be doing, in exactly the place they should be doing it, at exactly the pace that is right. It is the contentment of participation. Of being part of something larger than yourself, something that does not need you specifically but that accommodates you specifically, something that was here before you and will be here after you and that you can, for the duration of your life, help to continue.

They are, in the end, inhabitants. Not residents, not citizens, not occupants. Inhabitants. They inhabit the mountain the way the moss inhabits the concrete, the way the birds inhabit the flywheel housings, the way the water inhabits the cisterns. They have found their place in the system, and the system has made room for them, and the arrangement is stable, and the arrangement is old, and the arrangement is, for as long as the mountain continues, sufficient.

---

The freight elevator takes you back down through the cloud layer, and the world closes over you, and you are in fog again, and the sounds of the mountain fade — the thudding, the humming, the birds, the water, the slow creak of the structure — and what you carry with you is not an image but a feeling. The feeling of a place that has settled. The feeling of people who have settled. The feeling of continuation.

The mountain breathes. They breathe with it. They have been doing this for a very long time.
