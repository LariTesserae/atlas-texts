# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is in a hurry.

This is not the stillness of death or the stillness of pause. It is the stillness of a thing that has arrived where it was going. Every organism on this plateau has completed a journey — not a physical one, but a journey of subtraction. They have all, every one of them, been getting smaller for longer than small usually takes. What lives here is what is left after the getting-smaller is done, or nearly done, or so close to done that the difference between done and not-done has been worn away like everything else.

---

## The Ones in the Pools

Start with the pools, because the pools are where life is densest, and because the pools are where you would look first if you looked at all.

Each depression in the stone is a world. Not metaphorically — functionally. A palm-sized bowl that holds condensation for six hours after dawn is a complete ecosystem with its own trophic cascade, its own chemistry, its own history. Some of these pools have been occupied continuously for longer than human civilization has existed. Not the same individuals. The same *lines*. The same microscopic dynasties, breeding and dying and breeding in a few milliliters of water that appears and disappears and appears again, every day, forever.

The bottom-dwellers come first. They are the foundation, the things that make everything else possible, and they are the least visible. Some are single-celled — greenish films that coat the submerged stone like a stain, photosynthesizing in the thin light that reaches the morning pools. But others are multicellular and strange. There is a thing that lives at the bottom of perhaps a third of the pools across the plateau, and it has no common name and no scientific name that has been published, and it might be an animal or it might be something that hasn't been categorized yet. It is a flat, translucent sheet, thinner than a single layer of skin, that adheres to the stone beneath the water and absorbs whatever the condensation brings it — mineral traces, the faintest organic residues, the occasional microorganism that settles onto its surface and is slowly, silently engulfed. It does not hunt. It does not move in any way you can perceive. It simply covers an area and digests whatever lands on it. When the pool dries, it does not die. It curls into a thread, thinner than a hair, and waits in the deepest part of the depression, and when the water returns, it unfurls. It has been doing this for as long as there have been pools here. It is possibly the oldest continuously surviving lineage on the plateau. It does not know this. It does not know anything.

Above the bottom-dwellers, the grazers. These are the visible ones, the iridescent blue-green things that move in unison in some pools, the matte black solitary things in others. They are small — the largest is the size of a grain of rice — and they are the most diverse group on the plateau. Every pool holds a different solution to the same problem, which is: how do you eat enough, in a pool that exists for six hours a day, to sustain a body that must survive the other eighteen hours in a dry depression on bare stone?

The answers are legion. In one pool, the grazers have solved it by becoming dessication-tolerant to a degree that should not be possible for an animal. They dry out completely. They become crisp, brittle, indistinguishable from the mineral crust that forms on the stone. Their metabolic rate drops to essentially zero. Their cells vitrify — the water in their bodies is replaced by a sugar that forms a glass-like matrix, preserving cellular structure. And when the water returns, they rehydrate in minutes and resume grazing as though nothing has happened. They can do this hundreds of times. They do it every day. They will do it today. They will do it tomorrow.

In the next pool, three inches away, separated by a ridge of dry stone that has never held water in a million years, the grazers have solved it differently. They do not dry out. They leave. At the last moment, as the pool shrinks to a film, they climb out of the depression and travel — slowly, but with clear purpose — across the dry stone to the nearest pool that still holds water, and they descend into it, and they graze there until that pool begins to dry, and then they move again. They are pilgrims. Their entire lives are circuits between pools, following the water as it retreats and advances across the stone. They know the topography of their territory the way you know the rooms of your house — not by sight, but by the feel of the stone under their feet, the specific roughness or smoothness that tells them they are on the right path. They have been making these circuits for so long that the paths they travel are slightly lower than the surrounding stone — worn down by the passage of millions of generations of feet so small they barely weigh anything, but so persistent that they have eroded their own roads into a mountain range's bones.

In a third pool, the grazers have solved it by not needing the pool at all. They live in the pool when the pool is there, but when it dries, they burrow into the thin organic paste at the bottom — the residue of previous generations — and they seal themselves in a cocoon made of their own secretions and the mineral crust, and they enter a state that is not dormancy and not death but something between: a pause so deep that they do not grow, do not metabolize, do not age in any measurable way. They can hold this state for weeks. For months. One researcher — there have been a few, drawn by the strangeness of this place — kept a sealed sample of these cocoons in a dry container for three years and then immersed them in water, and they emerged and resumed grazing within an hour. The grazers did not know three years had passed. For them, nothing had. They had simply been waiting, the way stone waits, and then the conditions were right again, and they were alive.

The predators come next. They are the ones that eat the grazers, and they are faster, and larger, and fewer. Some are familiar in shape — there are things that are clearly beetles, or were beetles once, hard-shelled and legged and purposeful, patrolling the edges of pools with a mechanical gait. But their shapes are wrong in specific ways. Their legs are too long for their bodies, as though they have been stretched. Their mouths are too large. Their eyes are reduced, sometimes to a single ocellus, sometimes to nothing at all — there is little to see in a palm-sized pool, and sight has been traded away for other senses. They feel vibrations in the water. They can detect a grazer moving on the other side of the pool through the displacement of molecules. They are patient in the way that the landscape is patient. They wait at the edge of the pool until a grazer comes within range, and then they strike, and the strike is fast — the only fast thing here — and then they are still again, and they wait.

Other predators are not familiar. There is a thing that lives in perhaps one pool in twenty that is not a beetle, not a spider, not a mite, not anything that maps onto the taxonomies you carry in your mind. It is a circle. A flat circle, the size of a small coin, with a rim of fine cilia that moves in coordinated waves, and in the center, a mouth. That is the whole animal. It sits in the pool and the cilia create a current — a tiny current, imperceptible, but enough to draw microorganisms and the smallest grazers toward the center, where they are consumed. It has no eyes, no head, no limbs, no anus. It eats and excretes through the same opening. It reproduces by dividing — simply splitting into two half-circles that each grow to full size within a day. It might be a surviving relic of something very old, something from before the categories were established, or it might be something new, something that evolved in these isolated pools so recently that it has not yet been found elsewhere. There is no way to know without sequencing its genome, and no one has, because the pools are fragile and the equipment is heavy and the plateau is far from everywhere.

The parasites are the smallest and the strangest and the least understood. They live inside the other animals — inside the grazers, inside the predators, sometimes inside other parasites. They are the things that make the system complete, the things that ensure no single species dominates, that keep the balance so finely tuned that a pool that has been the same for ten thousand years will be the same in ten thousand more. They are the regulators. They are the reason the pools work.

## The Lizards

The lizards — or the things that were lizards — are the most visible animals on the plateau, and they are the ones that stay with you after you leave.

They are everywhere, but you will not know this for some time. You will walk for an hour and see perhaps three, and you will think they are rare. Then your eyes will adjust. It happens gradually, the way night vision happens, but it is not darkness you are adjusting to — it is a particular kind of sameness. The lizards are the same color as the stone. Not approximately the same — the same. Their skin matches the specific tired yellow-bone color of the rock they live on, and it matches it the way water matches a glass, filling every variation, every mineral streak, every subtle shift in tone. A lizard on grey stone is grey. A lizard on yellow stone is yellow. A lizard on a patch where the two meet is both, divided along a line that runs through its body as though it were a geological map of its territory.

They have achieved this not through rapid color change — they do not have that ability — but through the slowest kind of natural selection, the kind that operates over timescales so long that the stone itself is part of the process. Their ancestors were lizards that lived on the mountain range when it was a mountain range, and as the mountains lowered, the lizards lowered with them, and the color of the stone changed as it was exposed and weathered, and the lizards that matched the new color survived, and those that did not did not, and over unimaginable spans of time the lizards became the stone. They are not camouflaged against the stone. They are of the stone. There is a difference.

Their flatness is their most remarkable feature, and it is the thing that makes them invisible even after you know they are there. They are not merely low — they are thin. A large one, perhaps fifteen centimeters from snout to tail-tip, might be two millimeters thick at its thickest point. This is not a metaphor. They have compressed their entire body plan — skeleton, organs, musculature — into a plane. Their skulls are flat plates. Their ribs are horizontal. Their limbs extend sideways, not downward, and the limbs themselves are thin, blade-like, more fin than leg. They move by undulating — a slow, sinuous wave that passes from head to tail and pushes them across the stone the way a snake moves across sand, but lower, always lower, their bellies in constant contact with the rock.

They have given up everything that a lizard needs. They cannot run. They cannot climb. They cannot bite with any force — their jaws are too flat, too spread, too thin. What they can do is press. When a lizard settles onto a surface, it exhales, and it flattens further, and its skin contacts the stone so completely that there is no gap, no air pocket, no space between animal and rock. Adhesion does the rest. The slight moisture from the morning condensation creates a capillary bond, and the lizard is fixed in place, and it will stay fixed until it chooses to move, and it may not choose to move for days.

They hunt by covering pools. This is their single strategy, and it is enough. A lizard selects a condensation depression — not at random, but with a discernment that suggests either intelligence or an extremely refined instinct — and it positions itself over the pool, pressed flat against the rim, its body forming a lid over the water. The pool is now dark. The insects in the pool, which use light to orient, become disoriented. They climb the walls of the depression, seeking the light, and they climb until they reach the rim, and the rim is where the lizard is, and the lizard takes them. One by one. Patiently. Over the course of the hours the pool exists, a lizard will consume a significant portion of the pool's inhabitants. Not all — it is not a destructive predator, not a thing that empties a pool — but enough. Enough to sustain itself. Enough to continue.

A lizard will guard a pool for its entire life. Some of them occupy the same depression for years — for the duration of their adult existence, which may be a decade or more, though no one has studied them long enough to be certain. They mate by leaving their pools briefly — a risky, extraordinary behavior that exposes them to the open stone and the wind and the birds — and finding a nearby lizard on a nearby pool, and the mating is fast and flat and pressed against the stone, and then they return, each to their own pool, and the female lays her eggs in the depression she guards, and the eggs hatch in the condensation, and the hatchlings are tiny versions of the adults — already flat, already stone-colored, already patient — and they leave the pool, because there is not room for two lizards on one pool, and they find pools of their own, and the cycle continues.

If you crouch beside a pool and look closely — and you must look closely, because the lizard is invisible until you see it, and once you see it you cannot unsee it — you will notice that its eyes are the only part of it that are not stone-colored. They are dark, very dark, almost black, and they are flat, almost flush with the skull, and they watch you with an expression that is not fear and not curiosity and not anything you can name. It is the expression of a thing that has been watching for a very long time. It is the expression of the stone itself, if the stone had eyes.

## The Birds

The birds are wrong in the way that a word is wrong when you have said it too many times. They are birds — you can see that they are birds — but the more you look at them the less bird-like they become, until you are looking at something that used to be a bird and is now a bird the way a stump is a tree.

They walk. This is the first wrong thing. You see them before you hear them — you will not hear them — and you see them walking across the flat stone in groups, and the walking is not the awkward, temporary walking of a bird that would prefer to fly. It is purposeful. It is primary. They walk the way people walk, with their weight committed to each step, each foot placed deliberately on the stone. Their legs are longer than they should be. Their bodies are smaller. The ratio is wrong, and it makes them look like they are all legs, like something drawn by someone who had heard about birds but never seen one.

Their wings are the second wrong thing. They are not flightless — that would be too simple, too familiar a story. Their wings work. They spread them, and the wings are large, relative to the body, and they catch the wind, and the birds are moved by it. But they do not fly. They sail. They use the wind the way a ship uses wind — not to leave the surface, but to travel across it. When the wind is steady, a group of birds will spread their wings simultaneously and drift across the stone at the speed of the air, their feet barely touching the ground, their bodies tilted, their wings adjusted with small movements that are clearly deliberate. They are steering. They are piloting. They are navigating a flat ocean of stone using the same principles that moved ships across water for thousands of years, and they have been doing it for longer than ships have existed.

They sail to feed. As they move across the stone, they harvest insects from the surface — the ones that live on the stone, outside the pools, the ones that graze on the thin organic films that form in protected crevices. Their beaks are extraordinary. Each species — and there may be a dozen, it is hard to tell, they are all small and brown-grey and silent — has a beak that is bent at a specific angle, and the angle corresponds to a specific crevice shape in the stone. Some beaks are curved like question marks, designed for the spiral cracks that form where the stone has been stressed by millennia of temperature cycling. Some are L-shaped, straight and then bent at ninety degrees, for the angular fractures that occur at the edges of the shallow depressions. Some are nearly straight but offset, the upper mandible shifted two millimeters to the left of the lower, for the narrow linear crevices that run like fault lines across the surface. The beaks are keys. The crevices are locks. Each bird species opens a different lock, and the locks are geological, formed by erosion too slow to see, and the keys have been cut to fit them over a span of time that makes the word "coevolution" seem inadequate.

They do not call. This is the third wrong thing, and it is the one that affects you most. Birds call. Birds sing. Birds make noise, territorial noise, mating noise, alarm noise, contact noise. These birds do not. They are entirely silent. They communicate — and they do communicate, you can see it in the coordinated sailing, the synchronized wing-spread, the way a group of twenty birds will all change direction at the same moment — but they do not do it with sound. They do it with movement. A shift of the wing, a tilt of the body, a turn of the head: these are their signals. They read each other the way the stone reads the wind, by surface and pressure and the subtlest indication of force. A group of them moving across the plateau is a silent ballet performed by animals that have traded voice for something else — perhaps efficiency, perhaps secrecy, perhaps simply the recognition that in a place where sound is swallowed by the stone and the thin air, silence is not a loss but a conservation.

They nest in the shallowest depressions — the ones that barely hold water, the ones that are more suggestion than bowl. They lay their eggs directly on the stone, and the eggs are the color of the stone, and they are warm from the stone's warmth, and the parents take turns standing over them, wings spread, not to shade but to shelter, to create a small still space in the wind where a chick can emerge into a world that is already the temperature of its mother's body. The chicks are precocial. They walk within an hour of hatching. They sail within a day. They do not learn — there is no childhood, no period of dependence. They emerge knowing everything they need to know, because everything they need to know is very little: walk, sail, eat, be silent, repeat.

## The Pale Things

And then there are the others.

You will see them and you will not know what you are seeing. You will think you are seeing a trick of the light, or a moisture effect, or your own eyes failing in the thin air. Then you will see one clearly, and you will not be able to categorize it, and you will try, and you will fail, and you will keep trying, and you will keep failing, and eventually you will stop trying and simply watch.

They are the size of a human thumb. They are pale — not white, not translucent, but the pale of something that has never been exposed to sun, the color of a root, the color of a cave animal, though there are no caves here. They move across the stone, and the movement is wrong. It is not walking. It is not crawling. It is not sliding, not in the way a slug slides, with a visible foot and a trail of mucus. It is flowing. The body moves across the stone the way water moves across stone — in contact with the surface, conforming to every irregularity, never lifting, never breaking contact — but it moves faster than water, and with more purpose, and in directions that water would not choose.

They appear from the stone and return to it. This is not a figure of speech. You will see one moving across the open surface, and it will reach a crack or a crease in the rock, and it will not enter the crack — it will become the crack, its body narrowing, flattening, extending until it is a thin pale line in the stone, indistinguishable from a mineral vein, and then it is gone. And later — minutes later, hours later, sometimes days later — you will see a pale line emerge from a crack elsewhere on the stone and thicken and become a thumb-shaped thing moving with purpose across the surface. They are in the stone and they are on the stone and the distinction may not be meaningful to them.

They leave no trail. Whatever they are, they do not produce waste, or if they do, the waste is indistinguishable from the mineral crust that forms naturally on the stone. They do not seem to eat — at least, no one has observed them feeding. They do not seem to be eaten — the lizards ignore them, the birds ignore them, the insects do not react to their passage. They exist outside the food web. They are not part of the economy of the pools. They are something else.

They might be animals. They might be something that evolved from animals but is no longer quite animal, the way the birds are no longer quite birds. They might be something that evolved independently, from a different starting point, a lineage that has no name because it has no other members. They might be a phenomenon of the stone itself — a crystallization, a growth, a thing that the rock does when conditions are precisely right, the way some minerals form fibrous structures that look almost organic. They might be alive in a way that does not map onto the categories that have been established for life.

There is no way to know without capturing one, and capturing one may not be possible, and even if it were possible, there is a feeling — not a scientific feeling, not a testable feeling, but a feeling that the stone itself seems to support — that there are some things on this plateau that should not be taken from it. That the plateau has been here for so long and has been so thorough in its reduction of everything to its simplest form that the things that remain are the things that most belong here, and removing them would be a kind of theft from a place that has already had everything taken from it that can be taken.

The pale things move. They emerge and they flow and they return. They have been doing this for longer than anything else on the plateau has been doing anything. Or they have been doing it for a very short time. There is no way to tell. They do not leave fossils. They do not leave traces. They are the one thing on this plateau that resists the landscape's defining property, which is that everything here eventually becomes part of the stone. They move through the stone and they are not of the stone and they are not of anything else, and they are the closest thing this place has to a mystery, and the mystery may simply be that there are things in the world that have been here long enough to be themselves and nothing else, and the names for them have not been invented yet, and perhaps never will be.

## The People Who Are Gone

There were people here. The post-holes say so. The ghost-wall says so. The circle of depressions that might have been a dwelling or a tomb says so. But the people are so thoroughly gone that they are not so much absent as dissolved.

They lived here when the plateau was younger — but still ancient by human standards, still a place that had already been flat for longer than civilization has existed. They found a plateau that was warm and flat and watered by condensation and they built on it, and their buildings were made of the same stone the plateau was made of, and the stone treated the buildings the way it treated itself: it eroded them. It took them down grain by grain, the same way it took down the mountains, at the same rate, with the same patience. The people built and the stone unbuilt, and at some point the people stopped building, or the people stopped being, and the stone continued unbuilding, and now there is nothing left but the faintest impressions in the rock where the posts were, the merest ridges where the walls stood, the shallowest circles where the structures were.

We do not know what they ate. We do not know what they wore. We do not know what they believed or what they hoped or what they feared. We do not know if they saw the pale things. We do not know if they sailed the birds, or farmed the pools, or worshipped the stone. We know they were here because stone remembers everything, even if what it remembers is only the shape of an absence, the ghost of a post that was once set in a hole that is now a dimple that is almost nothing but not quite.

They might have been the most patient people who ever lived. They might have come here because they understood what the plateau was — a place at the end of things, a place where everything had been reduced to its essence — and they wanted to be part of that reduction. They might have come here to become stone. They might have built their buildings knowing that the buildings would be taken, and they might have intended that, and they might have experienced their own eventual erasure not as death but as a return, a merging with a landscape that was already doing to the mountains what time does to everything.

Or they might have been desperate. They might have come here because there was nowhere else to go, and they might have endured for as long as they could, and they might have left, and the stone might have taken their buildings after they were gone, and there might be no meaning in any of it, no poetry, no intention, just geology doing what geology does to the things that people build on stone.

The plateau does not distinguish between these possibilities. The plateau does not distinguish between the people and the mountains. Both were here. Both are gone. Both are part of the stone now, in the structural sense, in the way that a thing that has been taken apart and redistributed is still present in the distribution even if it is no longer present as a thing. The people are in the stone the way the mountains are in the stone: as shape, as trace, as the faintest alteration of a surface that has been eroding for longer than anything alive can comprehend.

## What Lives Here, In Sum

What lives here is the end of a long process of becoming less, and the beginning of nothing. What lives here has found the minimum — the minimum size, the minimum complexity, the minimum need — and has discovered that the minimum is enough. What lives here is a community of things that have been simplified by time until they are perfectly suited to a place that has been simplified by time, and the fit is so exact, so precise, so complete that it does not look like adaptation. It looks like belonging. It looks like the things on the stone have always been on the stone and the stone has always been what it is and the two — the living and the mineral — are not two things but one thing, expressed in different materials, moving at different speeds, but moving in the same direction, which is down, which is flatter, which is simpler, which is the direction that everything moves when it has been moving for long enough.

What lives here lives in the pools and on the stone and in the stone and across the stone, and what lives here is alive in the way that this place is alive — not with vigor, not with growth, not with the surging vitality of a young ecosystem, but with the persistence of something that has found its final form and is content in it, or if not content, then at least settled, then at least done with becoming, then at last and at the last simply being, which is its own kind of life, its own kind of warmth, its own kind of answer to the question of what survives when everything that can be taken has been taken.

What lives here is what remains.
