# The Inhabitants of the Resonant Quarter

---

They are small. Not in the way of malnutrition — though that plays a part, historically, in the long arc of their ancestry — but in the way of adaptation. Compact. Dense. The tallest man among them comes to your shoulder, and he is considered tall, and he walks slightly stooped because the galleries demand it and have demanded it for generations and his spine has organized itself around the demand. The women are smaller. The children are almost invisible — not because they are hidden but because they move in the interstitial spaces the adults have already mapped and no longer use, the low channels near the floor where the water runs shallowest, and they have learned to be where they are not in the way, which is a kind of training that begins before language. Before gesture, even.

Their skin is remarkable. Pale in the way of things that have never seen the sun, but not uniformly — there is a gradient to it, a geography. The faces and forearms and any surface that faces the lamplight or the phosphorescent band carries a faint warmth, a suggestion of color that is not quite pink and not quite brown and not quite anything you have a name for. The skin of their backs, their shoulders, the backs of their thighs — anything that faces the wet stone — is gray. Not sallow. Not jaundiced. Gray the way the fungi are gray, the way the stone is gray, the way the light is gray, as though the body has made a quiet agreement with its surroundings and is honoring it in pigment. You notice this most when they sleep, which they do in the cells, curled on their sides, facing the wall, and in that posture they are nearly invisible — a gray body against gray stone in gray light, and the only way you know they are there is the slight movement of breathing, which is slow, which is deep, which is the breathing of people who have never needed to wake quickly.

Their eyes are the thing that surprises you most.

They are large. Not enlarged in the way of cartoon or deformity — proportionally large, the way a nocturnal animal's eyes are large, because the iris has widened over generations to gather what little light there is and the socket has accommodated. The irises themselves are pale — washed blue, washed gray, sometimes a color that is almost amber, and in the low light they appear black, all pupil, all reception. They do not blink often. They do not need to; the air is damp enough that the eye never dries. You watch an elder woman threading dried mycelium fabric through a bone needle — a needle that must be generations old, smoothed to a polished sliver by ten thousand grippings — and her eyes do not blink for what you count to be ninety seconds. She is not staring. She is not in a trance. She is simply *looking*, with the unhurried completeness of someone who has never needed to glance, because nothing here moves quickly and nothing here requires a fast response.

They have no books. They have no writing. They have no visual art that you can identify — no carvings beyond the functional, no decorations on the walls, no pigments, no marks. Their history is in the stone itself, in the tool-marks that overlay older tool-marks, in the cells that have been deepened by successive inhabitants, in the drainage channels that have been worn an inch lower by the feet of the dead. When the elder who knows a few words of the upland tongue shows you a cell — a niche in the southern wall, fourth row up, deep enough to lie in — she touches the back wall where the stone is smoothest and says nothing, but the gesture means *this one's hands made this surface* and you understand without understanding that the person who smoothed this wall is gone and the wall is the monument and the monument is adequate.

---

Their relationship to sound is the most difficult thing to describe, and you spend days trying to understand it before you realize that understanding it is the wrong frame. You are approaching it as a visitor — someone who *hears* the sound, who perceives it as a phenomenon, who is conscious of it as a thing separate from silence. They do not have silence. They have never had silence. The sound is not a thing they hear; it is the medium they exist in. It is their air, their weather, their sky. Asking them about the sound would be like asking a fish about water, and the question would land the same way — a blankness, a slight tilt of the head, a patient indication that the question does not map onto anything they have a concept for.

But they *use* it. This is the thing that takes you longest to grasp.

The carved cells — the resonators — are not all the same. Some produce low tones when water strikes their thresholds. Some produce high ones. The inhabitants know which is which, not by pitch — they have no word for pitch — but by *feeling*. The low tones resonate in the chest, in the gut, in the long bones of the legs. The high tones resonate in the teeth, in the sinuses, in the fine bones of the inner ear. They have mapped the entire chamber's acoustic geography with their bodies, and they use it the way you use street signs or landmarks. The elder tells you — through gesture, through the interpreter who is herself barely verbal — that a specific cell on the third row of the eastern wall is where you go when your chest is tight. The resonance there is low and steady and it does something — they believe it does something, and you suspect they are right — to the rhythm of the heart. Another cell, high on the northern wall, is for headaches. Another, near the drainage basin where the water falls in a specific rapid patter, is for grief.

Grief. Yes. They grieve. They are not without emotion. They are not without interior life. They simply do not express it the way you do.

When someone dies — and they do die, though rarely, and not of the diseases you would expect; the oldest among them, a man whose face is a topography of lines so deep they hold shadows, is said to be over ninety, and the dead are usually old — the body is taken to the deepest basin, the one where the pale things live, and left there for a period that you are unable to determine precisely. Three days, perhaps. Perhaps longer. The water is cold and mineral-rich and it does what cold mineral-rich water does, and when the body is retrieved it is wrapped in the waxy mycelium cloth and placed in a niche that has been carved for it in the lowest gallery, the one nearest the floor, where the water runs shallowest, and the niche is sealed with a plug of compressed mycelium that hardens to the consistency of stone. The niche becomes the wall. The wall becomes the monument. The monument is adequate.

You attend one of these — not the placement, which is private, which is done before dawn, or what they call dawn, which is the time when the oil lamps are trimmed and the mycelium is checked and the day-cycle begins again. You attend the period after, the gathering. The forty of them — thirty-eight, now — assemble in the central gallery, the one space wide enough for three people to stand close, and they do not speak, because they never speak, but they do something else. They hum.

It is the hum you were told about — the low moan, the sound that means *I am still here* — but it is not one hum. It is thirty-eight hums, each slightly different, each in a register low enough to cut through the water-roar, and they do not harmonize because they have no concept of harmony, but they *coincide*, and the coincidence produces a sound that is not music and not speech and not prayer but is something else, something that the water-roar carries and modifies and returns to them in fragments that bounce off the carved cells and the mycelium walls and the wet stone, and for a moment — a long moment — the room's sound is not the same sound it has always been. It is the same sound *plus this*. And then the hums taper off, one by one, and the room returns to its equilibrium, and the water falls where it falls, and the pale things drift in their pools, and the thirty-eight move back to their cells and their work and their lives and the dead are part of the wall now and the wall is the monument and the monument is adequate.

---

They eat fungi. This is the whole of their diet, or very nearly. The mycelium that sheathes the walls is not the same as the fruiting bodies that emerge from it — pale, shelf-like growths that push out from the lower walls in the places where the water runs richest, where the mineral content is highest. These growths are harvested with a small stone blade — each family, each cell-group, has a blade, and the blades are the most carefully maintained objects in the quarter, sharpened on a specific piece of harder stone kept in a niche near the central gallery, a whetstone that is itself worn into a curve by generations of sharpening. The fungi are eaten raw. They have a faintly sweet taste, a faintly mineral aftertaste, and they are, you are told, sufficient. The word the elder uses — or the gesture, rather — is not *enough*. It is not *good*. It is a flat palm pressed to the stomach, which means *this fills the space that was empty*.

They do not cook. They have no fire. The oil lamps burn a rendered fat that they cannot produce — the elder tells you, in her few words of the upland tongue, that the lamps were filled by someone from above, long ago, and have been refilled by the occasional surface-walker who finds the crease and descends, which happens — has happened — perhaps once in a generation, perhaps less. You are that visitor now. They are not surprised by you. They are not alarmed. They are not pleased. They regard you with the patient, slightly unfocused gaze of people who have no reason to expect anything from the world above and no reason to fear it, because it is not their world and does not impinge on theirs. You are a weather event. You are a condensation bead on the ceiling that will fall and be absorbed and be part of the system again.

You refill the lamps, because they showed you where the oil is kept — a stone jug in a niche near the entrance, half-full, the oil thick and dark and rancid-smelling — and they watch you do it and the gesture they make afterward is the one you have come to understand as *done*, which is also *enough*, which is also *I am still here*. It is, you think, the closest thing they have to gratitude, and it is not gratitude. It is acknowledgment. You did the thing. The thing is done. The system continues.

---

Their interior lives are — you believe this, though you cannot prove it — enormous.

You come to this conclusion not through evidence but through the accumulation of small observations that have no other explanation. The way they touch the walls as they pass — not for balance, not for guidance, but the way you might touch a friend's shoulder in a crowd. The way they sit in their cells and face the carved stone and *listen*, not with the effort of someone trying to hear something, but with the relaxation of someone hearing something that is always there. The way two of them will share a cell — not for warmth, though warmth is part of it, the human body being the only heat source in the quarter besides the lamps — but for the *proximity*, the chest-to-back press of bodies in a space too small for distance, and the way their breathing synchronizes not immediately but over minutes, and the way the synchronized breathing matches, or seems to match, one of the low resonances in the chamber, so that the body becomes part of the organ, and the organ becomes part of the body, and there is no distinction.

They do not name themselves. Or rather — they do not have names in the way you understand names. They have *positions*. The elder is the elder because she is oldest and because she has been to the surface and because she retains the upland tongue, and these three facts constitute her identity. Others are identified by their cells — third row, eastern wall, the deep one — or by their functions — the one who checks the basins, the one who sharpens the blades, the one who sleeps in the high cell where the resonance is bright, meaning high, meaning the one that hums in the teeth. These are not titles. They are descriptions. They are accurate, they are stable, and they change only when the person they describe changes — moves to a different cell, takes on a different task, dies and becomes part of the wall and vacates the description, which is then occupied by someone else, or not, because the quarter needs what it needs and does not need what it does not need.

There is a child — a girl, perhaps six, perhaps eight; the developmental markers you rely on do not map onto bodies that have grown in this environment, that have never run, never jumped, never climbed anything but the carved footholds in the gallery walls — who does not yet have a position. She follows the basin-checker on his rounds and watches and does not help and is not asked to help and is not in the way, because she has learned not to be in the way. She watches the pale things in the pools with an expression you cannot read — not wonder, because wonder implies surprise, and nothing here is surprising to her; not curiosity, because curiosity implies a gap between the known and the unknown and she may not have that gap. She watches them the way the basin-checker watches them: with the steady, unblinking regard of someone who is confirming what is already known. They are there. They were there. They will be there. She touches the water's surface with one fingertip, very lightly, and the pale thing nearest her — the one with the too-many segments, the one that moves peristaltically, the one that was here first — turns toward the touch, or seems to, and then turns away, and the girl removes her finger and the surface settles and the basin-checker nods and they move on.

You think she will be the next basin-checker. You think she is already becoming it.

---

What they are, you decide — standing in the central gallery on your last day, feeling the water-roar in your sternum, in your teeth, in the small bones of your inner ear — is not a community. Community implies connection, choice, the voluntary association of individuals who might otherwise be separate. They are not separate. They have never been separate. They are an *organism*. A single body with forty parts, sustained by water and stone and fungi and the faint blue-green glow of a mineral vein in the northwest wall, inhabiting a space that has shaped them and that they have shaped until the two things — the shapers and the shaped — are not distinguishable. They are the quarter. The quarter is them. The water falls and the sound roars and the pale things drift and the fungi glow and the mycelium holds the stone together and the stone holds the mycelium together and the people hold the quarter together and the quarter holds the people together and it has been this way for longer than anyone remembers, which is far, which is far enough that *remembering* itself has been worn smooth like the drainage channels, like the bone needle, like the whetstone, and what remains is not memory but *form*. The form of the thing. The shape it has worn into. The equilibrium it has reached.

They are not happy. They are not unhappy. They are not at peace, because peace implies the resolution of conflict, and there is no conflict. They are not content, because contentment implies the awareness of alternatives, and there are no alternatives. They *are*. The way the water is. The way the stone is. The way the pale things in the pools are — the ones that were here first, the ones that have never needed to be many, the ones that drift in their basins with a motion that is not swum and not walked and not any verb you have but is simply the motion of a body that has been in this water for longer than the water has been here, or so the elder says, and you believe her, because she has no reason to lie and no concept of lying and no word for it in any of the three languages she carries in her old gray skull.

You leave. The sound follows you up. The silence presses. You miss it.

You understand why they do not leave.
