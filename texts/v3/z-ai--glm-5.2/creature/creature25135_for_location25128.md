# What Lives in the Scafolstead

---

## The Walkers

They have no name for themselves. Or if they do, it is chemical — a signature molecule released from the glands behind their mandibles, a molecular fingerprint that says *I am here, I am of this place, I am doing the work of this place.* You could call them the wardens, or the keepers, but those words imply a relationship of stewardship that is too separate. They are not keeping the city. They are *of* it the way your white blood cells are of you — resident, functional, unconsidered.

They are six-legged, yes, but the number matters less than the *stance*. They carry themselves low, close to the ground, their bodies flat and wide, the legs splayed outward like the legs of a stool rather than tucked beneath like a horse's. This is a body plan for stability, not speed. The channels they walk are smooth, worn by generations of passage — but the word *generations* is misleading here, because the channels and the walkers have co-developed, the channels being worn *by* the walkers at the same rate that the walkers' footpad width was selected *by* the channels. Each made the other. The pad of a walker's foot is exactly as wide as the groove it walks in. Not because someone measured. Because the ones whose feet fit walked further, and the ones who walked further wore the groove deeper, and the groove that was deeper guided the next generation's gait. A conversation held in stone and chitin over timescales that make your head swim if you think about them too long.

Their shells. You said they look like they were carved from the city-bark, and that observation is better than you know. The shell is the same material — or near enough that the distinction is academic. A complex biomineral, layered, amber-brown, with a translucence at the edges that lets light through like horn or tortoiseshell. They secrete it. Not from their whole body, but from a band of tissue along the dorsal ridge, a thickened strip of epithelium that lays down mineral in the same way the city's growing edges lay down their own shell. The same machinery, repurposed. The same genes, if genes are what they are, expressed in a different body, a different shape, a mobile shape. The walkers are the city that learned to walk. Or the city that always could walk, that grew walkers the way a tree grows fruit — not as separate organisms but as *extensions*, as parts that carry a function to a place the whole cannot reach.

But they *are* separate. You can see that. They move independently. They make choices — which blister to tap, which channel to follow, when to stop and crop the overgrowth from a corridor mouth. They have a nervous system. It is not complex — there is a ganglion cluster at the anterior end, between and slightly above the foremost pair of legs, and a secondary cluster at the posterior, and a ventral nerve cord connecting them, with smaller ganglia at each hip joint. They do not have a brain in the way you'd recognize one. They have a *committee*. Decisions emerge from the aggregate response of a dozen small clusters, each one responsible for a different domain — locomotion, feeding, chemoreception, the maintenance of shell integrity — and the decisions are not deliberated but *computed*, the way a wave does not decide to break but breaks when the conditions resolve to breaking.

They live a long time. You don't know how long, but you can see it in the wear. Some walkers have shells that are deeply scored, etched by years of acid rain (there is some — mild, infrequent, more like a heavy dew than rain) and by the friction of channel walls. The scoring is not damage. It is *record*. The oldest walkers are almost black, the amber deepened by oxidation and mineral uptake until the shell looks like polished bog oak. They are larger, too — not because they are older and have grown, but because the shells thicken over time, layer after layer, the secretion never quite stopping, so that a walker who has been walking for years carries the accumulated mineral of that entire life on its back, like a tree's rings, externalized, visible.

They do not reproduce. At least, you have seen no evidence of reproduction — no young, no eggs, no pairing behavior, no diminished copies of adults. Where do new walkers come from? You think you know. You think they are *grown*. In the deep chambers. By the things that live there. Extruded, like the tendrils at the leading edge, but shaped differently, fed different signals, folded into a different form. A walker does not have a parent. It has a *source*. And the source is the same source that makes the walls, the domes, the spires, the leaf-pads, the fluid in the blisters. The walkers are another secretion. Another expression. Another fold of the same embryo.

When a walker dies — and they do die, you have seen the remains, shells lying in the channels, already being broken down, the mineral reabsorbed by the living mat beneath them, the organic components liquefied and drawn back into the circulatory system of the city — when a walker dies, nothing changes. The line does not pause. The next walker steps over or around the shell, and within a day or two the shell is gone, and the channel is smooth again, and the mineral that was the walker is now part of the floor. This is not recycling. This is not even death, in the way you understand it. It is *return*. The mineral goes back to where it came from. The walker was always borrowed.

## The Gliders

They are harder to study because they are always moving. Or rather — they are always either moving or completely still, with nothing in between, and the stillness is as total as the movement. When the light shifts and they settle on the spire-tips, they are invisible. Not camouflaged — *incorporated*. Their wings fold flat against the body, and the body is thin, and the coloration is the same amber-brown-dark of the bark, and the hooked feet grip the spire-tip, and from any distance at all they look like a slight thickening of the wood, a bud, a node, a natural irregularity. You only see them when they move, because movement is the one thing that cannot be disguised in a place where nothing moves quickly.

They are membrane-winged. The wings are not feathered, not scaled, not even veined in the way insect wings are — they are *film*. A biological film, two cells thick, stretched between elongated finger-bones (or the analog of finger-bones; the homology is uncertain and probably meaningless). The film is transparent, truly transparent, not translucent — you can see through it, see the leaf-pads and the spire-tops behind it, distorted only slightly by the curve of the membrane. When they fly, they do not flap. They *hold* the membrane taut and make micro-adjustments, tilting, twisting, and the thermals from the warm calcite do the rest. They are sailplanes. They are kites. The skill is not in propulsion — they have no propulsion — but in *trim*, in the constant minute correction that keeps them in the air and steers them between obstacles.

They eat the small things. The stationary things. On the undersides of the leaf-pads, in the angles where spires branch, in the crevices of the bark — there are creatures you can barely see, even up close. They are flat, almost two-dimensional, the thickness of a few cells, spread across the leaf surface like a film of their own. They are photosynthetic, or chemosynthetic, or some synthesis that draws energy from the resin's mild exothermia. They are not predators and not parasites — they are *surface dwellers*, epiphytes, living on the exterior of the structure, drawing from the light and the air and the resinous exudate that coats everything. They are the grass of this canopy, the turf, the ground cover. And the gliders eat them.

A glider lands on a leaf-pad — a brief, precise touchdown, the membrane folding instantly — and moves across the surface with a motion you can only describe as *licking*. The mouth is wide, flat, adapted for scraping. It moves in strips, methodically, clearing a path of the surface film and leaving a clean lane of bare waxy leaf behind it. Then it pauses, folds, and launches — a drop from the leaf-edge, a moment of fall, then the membrane catches the air and it is gone. The cleared strip will be repopulated within hours. The surface film grows fast, faster than the structure itself, because it is the simplest life here, the most ancestral, the kind of thing that grows whenever there is a surface and a source of energy and a trace of moisture.

The gliders carry the spores. This is the role that is not a role, because nothing here has a *role* in the intentional sense — but the function, the ecological position, is clear. The spores are produced in the leaf-pads, in small chambers at the base of each pad where the tissue differentiates into a dense, productive mass that you can see if you peel back a leaf: a spongy region, darker than the surrounding tissue, packed with small round bodies, each one a potential new growth-center. The spores fall, or are released, or are *pushed* — and some of them land on the leaf-pads of distant structures, and some of them land on the gliders. They stick to the film of the wings, to the hooked feet, to the flat body. They ride. And when the glider lands on a distant leaf-pad to feed, the spores transfer. A new growth-center begins. A new district of the city, founded not by intention but by the accidental transit of a feeding animal.

The gliders, like the walkers, do not reproduce visibly. But they are more numerous than the walkers — you count them, when you can, against the dark of the canopy, and the number is in the hundreds within your visual range alone, and the visual range is wide. They are the smallest mobile units of this system, and possibly the most expendable. They fall. You have seen them fall — a miscalculation of trim, a gust, a wing-tip brushing a spire's edge — and they tumble, and the membrane tears, and they drop to the channel floor, and within minutes a walker has passed over them, and within hours the shell (they have no shell, but the body has a thin mineralized cuticle) has been incorporated into the mat. They are brief. They are numerous. They are the system's way of *touching itself at a distance*, of connecting its far-flung parts without growing a corridor between them.

## The Things in the Deep Chambers

You did not go in. You were right not to go in. The air would not have killed you — it is warm, dense, alkaline, richer in CO₂ than the surface air, but not toxic — but the corridors are not built for things of your scale and your shape, and you would have had to crawl, and the crawling would have brought you into contact with the walls, and the walls are *alive* in a way that the surface structures are not. On the surface, the city is shell. It is mineral, secreted, hardened, a thing of architecture and structure. In the deep chambers, the shell falls away, and what is underneath is the organism itself — soft, wet, amber-pink, pulsing.

You heard the heartbeat. It is not a metaphor. There is a heart — or something that functions as a heart — in each major district of the city, in the center of the deepest chamber, at the bottom of the corridor system, where the walls are not walls but the living body of the thing that grew the walls. It is large. You cannot tell how large from the entrance, but the vibration suggests something meters across, something that contracts and expands with a rhythm that is slower than a human heart but not much slower — perhaps thirty beats per minute — and with each beat, the fluid — the weak-tea-colored, non-evaporating, functional analog of blood — moves through the city. Through the walls. Through the floor mat. Through the blisters that the walkers tap. The whole structure is a circulatory system, and the thing in the deep chamber is the pump.

But it is also more than a pump.

It is *growing*. You know this because you can see the growth at the edges, the pale tendrils creeping across bare calcite, and those tendrils must originate from somewhere, must be *fed* from somewhere, and the somewhere is here — the deep chambers, the center, the place where the organism's body is thickest and most active. The tendrils are not roots, not in the botanical sense. They are *fingers* — extensions of the central body, reaching outward, exploring, testing, anchoring. And as they reach, they differentiate. The ones that find good substrate — clean calcite, warm, slightly porous — thicken and rise and become walls and domes and spires. The ones that find poor substrate — compacted mineral, too dense, too cold — retract, reabsorb, pull back. The city maps its territory by touch, the way a blind thing maps a room by reaching.

The thing in the center — the *founder*, the *core*, the body at the heart of each district — it has no shape of its own. Or rather, its shape is the shape of the chamber it has grown around itself, which is the shape of the structures it has grown around itself, which is the shape of the district. It is not a creature that lives in a house. It is a creature that *is* the house, the way a coral polyp is the coral — the living part, the soft part, the part that secretes the stone, the part that is hidden inside its own architecture, the part that does all the work and is never seen.

There are many of them. One per district. Or one per *cluster* of structures — you can't be sure of the boundary, because the districts are continuous, the structures are fused, and the core-bodies may be connected beneath the surface, may be *one body* with many hearts, like a slime mold that has aggregated into a single mass but retained its modular internal organization. You suspect this is the case. The pulse you heard was steady, but it was not one pulse — it was a *chord*, a composite rhythm, multiple hearts beating at slightly different rates, the interference pattern creating the slow, complex vibration that you felt through the floor and the walls. The city is not a collection of organisms. The city is *one organism* with many centers, many chambers, many pulses — and the walkers and the gliders and the surface film and the spores and the tendrils are all part of it, all expressions of it, all grown by it for purposes that are not purposes but functions, the way your body grows hair and teeth and blood cells and tears, each one a different kind of cell doing a different kind of work, all of them you.

The founders do not move. They have never moved. They grew where they are, and they have been growing there since the first tendril touched the calcite, and they will be there until the calcite is exhausted or the climate shifts or the star dims. They are the only part of this system that is truly *committed* — the walkers can be reabsorbed, the gliders can fall, the spores can fail, but the founders are *invested*, literally, sunk into the ground, their bodies integrated with the mineral substrate beneath them, drawing calcium and carbon and trace elements from the calcite itself, metabolizing the rock. This is why the ground has that sponginess. This is why the crust hasn't settled. The founders are still eating it.

## The Surface Film

You almost missed them entirely. They are that thin. A layer of life two cells deep, spread across every exterior surface — the leaf-pads, the spire walls, the bark of the domes, even the channel floors where the walkers have not recently passed. They are the *skin* of the city, the outermost layer, the boundary between the organism and the air.

They are not one species. They are not one kind of thing. Under magnification — and you have a hand lens, and that is enough — the surface film is a *community*: flat, crystalline things that look like salt flakes, and long, thread-like things that follow the grain of the bark, and round, yeast-like things that cluster in the hollows of the leaf-undersides, and things that move — barely, slowly, in the way that things move when they are not sure they are alive — small, amoeboid, creeping along chemical gradients toward the richer exudate, away from the drier patches. This is where the complexity lives, not in the large structures but in the micro-layer, the thin wet surface where mineral meets air and the exchange happens. The founders may be the heart of the city, but the surface film is its *lung*. Gas exchange happens here. The CO₂ from the deep chambers, carried up through the walls in the fluid, reaches the surface and is released through the bark, and the surface film takes it in, and fixes the carbon, and releases the oxygen, and the oxygen drifts upward, and the gliders breathe it, and you breathe it.

The film is also where the mutation happens. Not mutation in the dramatic sense — no monsters, no sudden new forms — but the slow, incremental variation that means the tendrils at the leading edge are not identical to the tendrils of a decade ago, that the spores carry slightly different instructions, that the founders in different districts have diverged, subtly, in the shapes they grow. The surface film is the system's *memory of the outside*, the place where the environment touches the organism most directly, most intimately, and the organism responds. A drier decade produces a thicker film, a more waxy cuticle, a more efficient seal. A warmer century produces thinner bark, larger leaf-pads, wider channels. The changes are recorded in the film, transmitted to the spores, carried to the next growth-center, expressed in the next district. The city is *learning* the shape of its world, very slowly, by growing into it and seeing what survives.

## You

You are standing on the ridge. The resin smell is in your clothes. Your feet are warm.

You do not belong here. Not in the way that the things here belong — not grown, not secreted, not expressed from the central body, not carrying a function in the system. You are not a walker, not a glider, not a founder, not a film. You are the one thing in this landscape that arrived from somewhere else. The one thing that did not grow.

The walkers do not avoid you. They do not approach you. They go around you, the line parting at the ridge where you stand and rejoining on the other side, and the chemical trail is undisturbed by your presence — your feet leave no signature molecule, no pheromone, no chemical claim. To the walkers, you are a feature of the terrain. A rock. A ridge. An irregularity in the channel that is easily circumvented. You are not food. You are not threat. You are not *recognized*, because recognition requires a category for you, and you have no category here.

The gliders do not land on you. This is not avoidance — they simply have no reason to. You are not a leaf-pad. You do not exude resin. You do not host the surface film. You are not a feeding ground. You are a surface with nothing on it, and a glider has no more interest in you than in a bare patch of calcite.

The founders do not know you are here. Or if they do — if the chemical sensors in the mat beneath your feet register the pressure of your weight, the warmth of your body, the moisture you carry and shed and exhale — they do not respond. You are too small, too brief, too insignificant to shift the rhythm of a continental heartbeat. You are a footnote in a process that does not keep notes.

But you are *here*. You are the first thing to stand on this ridge and *see* it. The walkers see, in some limited sense — they have photoreceptors, simple clusters of pigment cells that can distinguish light from dark, open from closed. The gliders see better — their compound eyes are adapted for navigation, for the avoidance of obstacles at speed. But neither of them *looks*. Neither of them stands apart and observes. They are *in* the system, *of* the system, and the system has no outside, no vantage, no perspective from which it regards itself.

You are that perspective. You are the eye that the city has not grown for itself, because the city does not need to see itself whole. It only needs to grow, and to sense, at the scale of a tendril's tip, whether the ground ahead is good. The rest — the panorama, the scale, the beauty of the amber-bark structures receding to the horizon under a pale gold sky — the city does not know about this. It does not know it is vast. It does not know it is beautiful. It does not know what it looks like from a ridge two meters high, with the resin smell in the air and the pulse in the ground and the slow crack of growth at the edges.

You know. And you are the only thing here that does.

---

The gold-shift is coming. You can feel it — the temperature dropping, the light thickening. The leaf-pads are beginning to fold. The gliders are settling. The walkers are moving faster, tapping more frequently, the soft *click* of their mouthparts piercing the blisters coming more often, a rhythm picking up beneath the rhythm, a counterpoint. In the deep chambers, the pulse quickens, and the fluid moves faster through the walls, and the founders draw more deeply from the calcite, and the tendrils at the leading edge slow — because growth is expensive, and the energy is needed elsewhere now, in the maintenance of temperature, in the regulation of the system's breathing, in the small, vital, unending work of staying alive in a body the size of a continent.

You should go back. Not because it is dangerous — it isn't, not to you, not directly — but because you are warm and you are moist and you exhale water vapor into an atmosphere that hoards moisture like gold, and if you stay too long, if you stand too still on the ridge, the surface film will find you. It will climb the sole of your boot. It will find the sweat on your skin. It will begin to grow on you, not as a parasite — it has no interest in consuming you — but as a surface, a new substrate, a place where a film can spread. And the walkers will come, eventually, to crop the overgrowth, and they will not distinguish between the film on the bark and the film on you. And you will become part of the maintenance schedule, a feature of the terrain that needs to be cleared, and the clearing will be painless but it will be thorough, and you will have become, finally, *incorporated*.

You are the only thing here that did not grow. But the city is patient, and it is growing, and it grows on everything that stands still.
