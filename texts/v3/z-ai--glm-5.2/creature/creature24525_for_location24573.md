# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that what lives here does not think of itself as living here. There is no here to the things that are here. There is only the next chemical gradient, the next dissolved molecule, the next pore of sediment slightly warmer or more anoxic than the one behind you. The bacteria in the upper centimeters of the silt do not know they are on top. The bacteria thirty meters down do not know they are buried. They are where they are, and they do what they do, and the distinction between those two statements does not exist for them.

---

Start with the archaea, because they were here first, and because they are, in terms of biomass, the closest thing this basin has to a ruling body.

They are not the kind of archaea that live in hot springs or salt flats or any of the places where extremophiles are usually photographed for textbooks. They do not live in extremes. They live in the absence of everything — no light, no oxygen, no fresh organic matter, no turbulence strong enough to disturb the pore water they inhabit. They live in the sediment at depths where the bacteria above them have already consumed every easy electron donor, where the organic matter that filters down is so degraded it is barely distinguishable from the mineral matrix it sits in. They eat what the bacteria cannot. They metabolize methane that rises from deeper layers, or they reduce sulfate that diffuses down from the seawater above, and they do it so slowly that a single metabolic cycle — the equivalent of a breath, a meal, a generation — can take months.

You could hold a cubic centimeter of this sediment in your hand and be holding a billion of them. They are small. Some of them are among the smallest single-celled organisms ever measured, barely larger than the ribosomes they are built from, and there has been speculation — never confirmed, because confirming it would require methods that do not exist yet — that some of them are even smaller, that there are archaea in the deepest layers of this sediment that have shed so much of their cellular machinery they are essentially just membranes wrapped around a few enzymes, doing almost nothing, doing it almost forever. Whether this is true is a question for a microbiologist with a coring rig and several million dollars. What is true is that the cells in the deep sediment are there in numbers that should not be possible given how little energy is available, and nobody has fully explained how they stay alive.

Some of them may not be alive in the way you mean. They may be in a state that is neither alive nor dead — metabolically inert, intact, waiting. The term is "cryptobiosis," though that implies a capacity to revive, and nobody knows if these cells can. They may simply be persisting. A membrane with a charge across it. A chemical potential that has not yet run down. If you dug one up — and you cannot, not from thirty meters deep in sediment under five hundred atmospheres of pressure at four degrees — and you gave it food, it might respond. It might divide. Or it might have been dead for ten thousand years, its membrane intact only because there is nothing down there to break it, the cold and the pressure and the absence of any organism large enough to eat it preserving it the way amber preserves an insect.

The archaea are the deep biosphere. They are the floor's floor. Below them, at depths where even they cannot function, the sediment becomes rock — but the boundary between life and geology is not a line. It is a gradient. A long, slow fade from cells that are definitely doing something to cells that might be doing something to cells that are definitely doing nothing but have not yet fallen apart. The basin does not have a bottom in the way you might expect. It has a depth where chemistry gradually replaces biology, where the reactions that process carbon stop being catalyzed by enzymes and start being catalyzed by mineral surfaces, and nobody has ever determined exactly where that depth is, because it changes. Because even the rock at the base of the sediment is not entirely dead. The mineral surfaces catalyze reactions that the archaea would have catalyzed if they could survive down there. The rock does the same job, slower, without any of the overhead.

Life does not end in this basin. It thins.

---

Above the archaea, in the upper meters of sediment where there is still sulfate and still organic matter recent enough to be called food, the bacteria are different. Not in appearance — they are still rods and cocci and the occasional curved filament, still featureless under any microscope that does not use fluorescent stains — but in tempo. They reproduce. Not quickly. Not by the standards of a bacterium in a warm, nutrient-rich lab, which might divide every twenty minutes. These divide every few months, perhaps every year in the deeper layers. But they reproduce, and they move, and they respond to their environment in ways that the deep archaea do not.

They form layers. This is the strangest thing about the sediment, the thing that makes it not a graveyard but a structure: the bacteria arrange themselves by chemistry. The ones that use oxygen live at the very top, in the first few millimeters, where seawater has diffused into the sediment and brought trace amounts of dissolved oxygen with it. Below them, where the oxygen is gone, the sulfate-reducers take over. Below the sulfate-reducers, the organisms that ferment. Below the fermenters, the methanogens. Each layer consumes what the layer above it leaves behind, and each layer produces what the layer below it needs, or at least what the layer below it can tolerate. The sediment is a vertical machine. A stack of metabolisms, each one feeding on the exhaust of the last.

This is not a food web. A food web implies mobility, predation, choice. This is a food column. Things do not move through it. They sit in their layer and wait for chemistry to arrive, and when it arrives, they process it, and the product of their processing drifts down to the next layer, and the next layer processes that. It is more like a refinery than a community. It is more like a lung than a city. The sediment breathes the way the description said — with chemistry, not with lungs — and the bacteria are the alveoli, the individual sites of exchange where one form of carbon becomes another, where sulfate becomes sulfide, where the slow rain of dead matter from above is disassembled into its component atoms and those atoms are handed down the column until they reach a layer where nobody can use them, and there they sit, dissolved in pore water, waiting for diffusion to carry them back up to the seawater or down to the rock.

The bacteria do not know any of this. They do not know about the layers above or below them. They know sulfate. They know acetate. They know the particular configuration of carbon and sulfur and hydrogen that means food, and they process it when they encounter it, and they do not process it when they do not. They have been doing this for millions of years. They will do it for millions more. Their ancestors in the sediment below them — the same species, the same metabolic pathway, preserved in layers that are older than grass — did exactly the same thing when the ocean above was warmer and the continents were in different positions. The bacteria do not evolve quickly here. There is no pressure to evolve. The environment has not changed in any way that matters to a sulfate-reducing bacterium since before the first tree grew on land. Why would it change? The sediment is the same. The pore water is the same. The food is the same, arriving at the same rate, in the same form, from the same process of death and sinking that has fed this basin since it was enclosed.

If you were a bacterium in this sediment — and you are not, you are a large warm mammal who would die here in under a second — you would experience the world as a three-dimensional gradient of chemicals. You would not experience time. Your metabolism would be so slow that the difference between a day and a decade would be below your capacity to detect. You would exist in a pore of sediment the size of a red blood cell, and the walls of that pore would be the skeletons of organisms that lived and died above you before your kind had evolved to eat them, and you would eat what those skeletons leaked, and you would divide, and one of your daughter cells would be buried by the next centimeter of sediment arriving at a rate of one centimeter per thousand years, and that daughter cell would adapt to the deeper chemistry or would not, and if she did not, she would join the archaea in their long fade toward mineral persistence.

This is the majority of life in the basin. Not the fish. Not the salp. Not the things that drift in the water column. The bacteria. The bacteria and the archaea, packed into every pore of every centimeter of nine hundred meters of sediment, processing the dead, building nothing, remembering nothing, present in numbers so vast that if you removed all other life from the basin the chemical cycles would barely notice.

---

Now the water column, where life is a rumor.

The salp is real, but it is not common. There are perhaps a few dozen of them in the entire basin at any given time — a volume of water the size of an inland sea containing a few dozen gelatinous animals each the size of a finger. They are not from here. They are from the midwater, from the zones above where food is still meaningful, and they have drifted down through the shelves or been carried in by the cold dense water that enters the basin from gaps in the walls. They arrived by accident. They will die here by accident too.

A salp is a tunicate, which means it is a chordate, which means that in a strictly phylogenetic sense it is closer to you than almost anything else in this basin. It has a notochord, at least in its larval stage. It has a primitive heart. It has a brain, or something that was a brain, a small cluster of neurons above its mouth that controls the rhythmic contraction of its barrel-shaped body. The contraction pushes water through its interior, and the water passes through a mucus net that catches particles, and the net and the particles are swallowed, and this is how the salp eats. It is a pump. It is a filter. It is an animal that has solved the problem of feeding in sparse water by simply processing as much water as it can and extracting whatever is in it.

The salp in this basin is starving. Not in the dramatic sense — it is not wasting away, not visibly — but in the mathematical sense. The particles it needs are too few. The marine snow that falls into this basin is sparse to begin with, and what does arrive has been picked clean by organisms in the water column above, and the salp's mucus net captures almost nothing. It pumps and pumps, contracting its body once every several seconds, and the net catches perhaps a few thousand bacterial cells per pulse, and this is not enough. The salp's metabolism is slow — it has to be, in water this cold — but it is not slow enough. It is losing energy with every contraction. It is spending more to pump than it is gaining from what the pump captures.

It will drift for months. It will pump and starve and drift, and the turbulence will carry it through the basin in patterns that are not random but are so complex they might as well be, and eventually its energy reserves will run out, and its contractions will slow, and it will stop pumping, and it will begin to sink. Not fast. The salp is mostly water, and its density is only slightly greater than the water around it, so it will sink slowly, rotating gently in the turbulence, its body beginning to break down at a rate determined by the bacteria that colonize its exterior. It will reach the sediment after days or weeks, and the bacteria in the sediment will process it, and it will become a layer of organic carbon in the ooze so thin that it cannot be distinguished from the layers above and below it.

The salp does not know any of this. It does not experience its own starvation as distress. It does not experience anything. It contracts because its neurons fire, and its neurons fire because they were wired to fire, and it will keep contracting until it cannot. It is a small, transparent machine running on a battery it cannot recharge, and the basin is too large and too empty for it to find another battery, and it will run down. The basin does not care. The salp does not care. Nothing here cares. Caring is a thing that happens at the surface, in the light, where there is enough energy to spare for internal states that do not directly contribute to survival.

---

The fish — the one that drifts past every several hours — is more purposeful than the salp, if purpose can be attributed to something that has no capacity for purpose.

It is small. Six centimeters, perhaps eight. It is black, not in the way the water is black, but in the way that something absorbs light so completely that it seems to be a hole in the space it occupies. This is not an adaptation to darkness — there is no light here to absorb, no predator with eyes to hide from. It is black because black is the default. Pigment is cheap, and in the absence of any selective pressure favoring any particular color, the fish is the color of its own melanin, which is black. It would be black if it lived in a cave. It would be black if it lived in mud. It is black because there is no reason not to be.

It has no eyes. This is not quite accurate — it has eye sockets, or the vestigial remnants of eye sockets, two shallow depressions on either side of its head where eyes might once have been, in an ancestor that lived higher in the water column where eyes were useful. The eyes are gone. Not removed, not eaten away, just absent, the way a tail is absent in an animal that has stopped needing one. They were not lost in a single generation. They were lost over thousands of generations, each one slightly less functional than the last, until the gene sequence that produced them accumulated enough mutations that it no longer produced anything at all. The fish does not miss its eyes. It does not know it ever had them. It navigates by its lateral line, the series of pressure-sensitive organs that run along its flanks, and the lateral line is enough. It detects the pressure wave of a particle moving past it in the turbulence, and it turns toward the particle, and it opens its mouth, and if the particle is edible, it swallows.

The fish does not hunt. This is important. Hunting implies effort, intention, a calculation of cost and benefit. The fish does not calculate. It drifts, and the basin's turbulence carries it, and when something touches its lateral line, it reacts. The reaction is fast — faster than anything else in this basin, faster than the salp's contractions, faster than the bacteria's metabolic cycles — but it is not deliberated. It is a reflex. A particle hits the lateral line, the fish turns, the mouth opens, the particle is engulfed or it is not, and the fish goes back to drifting. The whole sequence takes less than a second. The fish will then drift for hours before another particle touches its lateral line, and during those hours it will not seek. It will not swim toward a region of higher particle density. It will not remember where the last particle was. It will simply be carried, and it will wait, and the waiting is not patience because patience implies the possibility of impatience.

There are not many of these fish. The basin is large, and the fish are small, and the energy budget of the water column is so tight that the basin can support perhaps a few hundred of them at most. They do not encounter each other often. When they do — when two drift close enough that their lateral lines detect each other's pressure signatures — they do not react. They are not social. They are not antisocial. They are nothing-social. The concept of another individual does not exist for them, because the concept of an individual barely exists for them. They are processes. They are the downstream end of a chemical gradient that starts with marine snow falling from the surface and ends with a bacterium in the sediment, and the fish is just a step in that gradient, a way station where a particle of organic carbon pauses for a moment before continuing its descent.

They reproduce, presumably. Nobody has observed it. The fish are too rare, too dispersed, too deep, too difficult to study. There must be reproduction, because there are fish, and fish die, and dead fish fall to the sediment and are eaten, and if there were no reproduction there would eventually be no fish. But how they find each other, how they mate, whether they guard their eggs or scatter them into the turbulence — these are unknown. It is possible that they are hermaphroditic, that a single fish can fertilize its own gametes and release buoyant eggs that drift upward through the water column and hatch in the slightly warmer, slightly less barren water above the basin, and that the larvae descend back into the basin as they mature. It is possible that they do something else entirely. Nobody knows. The fish are a question that the basin has not been asked, and the basin does not volunteer information.

---

There are other things in the water column, or there have been. The description mentions larvacean houses — the mucus structures built by larvaceans, which are another kind of tunicate, smaller and more complex than salps. A larvacean builds a house around itself, a structure of mucus and filtered particles, and it lives in the house, and when the house becomes clogged with debris, the larvacean abandons it and builds a new one. The abandoned house sinks. In most of the ocean, larvacean houses are a significant source of food for deep-sea organisms — they are large, nutrient-rich packages that descend through the water column like manna. In this basin, the houses that arrive are already mostly empty, their nutrients extracted by organisms above, and what reaches the basin floor is a collapsing mucus ghost that the bacteria process in days.

Whether the larvaceans themselves live in the basin is unclear. A few might. The water column is not sterile — it contains dissolved organic matter, bacterial cells, the occasional particle of marine snow — and a larvacean could theoretically survive by filtering this material. But the energy budget is the same as the salp's: the cost of pumping water through a mucus net may exceed the energy recovered from what the net captures. If larvaceans live here, they live on the edge of viability, and they do not live here long.

What else. Nematodes, perhaps, in the sediment — tiny roundworms that crawl between the grains, eating bacteria, being eaten by nothing, because there is nothing large enough to eat them. They would be the largest organisms in the sediment, a millimeter long, and there would not be many of them. The sediment is too fine, too anoxic, too energy-poor to support a thriving meiofaunal community. But a few nematodes, in the upper centimeters, persisting the way everything here persists — not thriving, not failing, just continuing.

Ciliates, perhaps. Single-celled organisms larger than bacteria, with nuclei and cilia and a rudimentary capacity to move through the pore water of the sediment. They eat bacteria. The bacteria do not know they are being eaten, or if they do, they respond only by reproducing fast enough to replace what the ciliates consume. This is not predation in the way a lion predates a gazelle. This is a chemical steady state. The ciliates eat bacteria at a rate determined by the ciliates' metabolism, which is determined by the temperature, which is four degrees, which means the ciliates eat slowly, and the bacteria reproduce slowly, and the two rates are balanced, and the balance has been balanced for longer than the ciliates or the bacteria have existed in their current forms.

---

And then there is the thing that is hardest to describe, because it is not an organism. It is the community. The whole.

The basin is alive in the way a gut is alive. Not because it has a brain or a will or a purpose, but because it processes material. Material enters — organic matter falling from above, dissolved ions arriving with the cold dense water from the shelves — and material is processed, and material leaves, or does not leave, or is transformed into something that will leave eventually, on a timescale so long that "eventually" is the only honest word. The bacteria and archaea in the sediment are the gut flora. The sediment itself is the gut wall, the tissue through which nutrients are absorbed and waste products are expelled. The water column is the lumen, the open space through which material moves, churned by peristalsis — the internal waves, the turbulence, the slow churning of a basin that has no exit and no entrance large enough to matter.

This is not a metaphor the basin would recognize, because the basin does not recognize metaphors. But it is accurate in the only way that matters here: the basin is a system for transforming carbon. It takes dead things and it breaks them down and it builds them into geology. It takes the bodies of organisms that lived in the light and it compresses them into a substance that will eventually become rock, and the rock will sit here for tens of millions of years, and one day a tectonic force will crack the basin open and the rock will be exposed and someone with a mass spectrometer will read it and learn what the ocean was like when the rock was sediment, when the sediment was ooze, when the ooze was living things, when the living things were alive.

The basin is a recorder. That is its function, if it can be said to have one. It records. It does not choose what to record — it records everything that falls into it, indiscriminately, the way a drain records everything that flows into it. The sediment is the record. Each centimeter is a page, as the description said, and the pages are not written in a language but in a chemistry, and the chemistry is the chemistry of organisms that lived and died and sank, and the organisms did not know they were writing, and the basin does not know it is being written.

---

Who lives here, then?

Bacteria that do not know they are bacteria. Archaea that may or may not be alive. Nematodes that crawl through a medium made of skeletons. Ciliates that eat bacteria and are not eaten. A few dozen salps that are starving. A few hundred eyeless fish that are waiting. Larvacean houses falling like abandoned architecture.

And below all of them, in the pore spaces of a sediment layer that has been accumulating since before the continents were where they are now, the archaea that are barely anything, that are almost geology, that persist in numbers and in forms that blur the line between the living and the mineral, that may be alive or may be memories of life preserved in cold and pressure and silence, that have been here longer than anything else and will be here after everything else is gone.

They do not know they are here. They do not know anything. They are the closest thing this basin has to inhabitants, and they inhabit it the way minerals inhabit a rock — present, persistent, unaware.

The basin is not empty. It has never been empty. But the life that fills it is so small, so slow, and so patient that it is indistinguishable, from any distance greater than a microscope's, from the sediment and the water and the dark. The life is the sediment. The sediment is the life. The basin is a body that does not know it is a body, processing material it does not know it is processing, recording a history it does not know it is recording, and the only sound is the heartbeat of the internal waves against the walls, every forty seconds, and the only listener is the sediment, and the sediment is not listening.
