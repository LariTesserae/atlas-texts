# The People of the Hollowing Quarter

They are smaller than they would be. Not dramatically — a visitor might not notice immediately — but the evidence accumulates. The ceilings of the newer passages are lower than the old ones, carved by people who learned to stoop and then built for their own stoop and then raised children who never fully stood. The men average perhaps a hand's breadth shorter than their surface ancestors. The women too. It is not hunger, though there is hunger sometimes. It is the stone. The stone imposes its dimensions on the body the way a river imposes its shape on a stone. Three generations of brushing low ceilings, of squeezing through shoulders-width fissures, of sleeping in alcoves carved to fit a curled form — the body adapts. The body always adapts.

Their skin is the color of the limestone. Not metaphorically. The pale mineral dust gets into everything — into the pores, into the creases of knuckles and elbows, into the grain of their rough wool clothing until the wool itself is more grey than brown. They wash when they can, which is not often, and the water they wash with is mineral-heavy, so it cleans and coats simultaneously. The result is a complexion like unfinished sculpture. Like someone started carving a person from the living rock and stopped just before the polish. Babies come out pink and screaming like anywhere else, but within months they begin to grey. By the time a child walks, they match the walls.

Their eyes are the thing visitors notice. Or rather, visitors notice that they cannot tell what the residents are looking at, because the residents' eyes have become something else. Not larger — that is a myth, the old story about cave-dwellers with owl eyes. The shape is the same. But the pupils dilate differently. They open wider and faster, contracting with a speed that seems involuntary when a tallow lamp flares or when someone passes too close with a reflective surface. In the dim blue of the *lue*-lit ceiling, their eyes are black wells, lightless, accepting. They see in the grey-blue dark the way surface people see at dusk — not clearly, not in color, but in shapes and movement and the particular quality of shadow that distinguishes a person from a wall. They have names for kinds of darkness the way surface people have names for kinds of weather. *Grave dark* is the deep unoccupied passages. *Lamp dark* is the normal nighttime. *Lue dark* is the ceiling-glow of the grandmothers' chamber. *Wet dark* is the air near the cisterns, heavy and close. They navigate by these distinctions the way a sailor navigates by wind direction.

---

They speak softly. This is not ideology or manners. It is architecture. Stone carries sound the way water carries current — efficiently, without loss, and in every direction at once. A voice raised in the Throat reaches every chamber branching from it. A shout reaches the whole settlement. The residents learned this early and learned it permanently. They speak at the volume of dripping. Their language — and it is their own language now, three generations of divergence from the surface tongue, though mutually intelligible if strained — has developed consonants that carry through stone: hard stops, clicks, a glottal catch that surfaces in the speech of the young and that the last of the surface-born grandmothers never used. Their vowels have softened, gone breathier, because breath carries less than percussion. Whispering is their normal register. A conversation between two residents in the Throat sounds like prayer.

They have also developed a vocabulary of taps. Not a full code — nothing so organized — but a shared repertoire of knocks and scratches that mean specific things. Three quick taps on a shared wall mean *quiet, my child is sleeping*. A long slow scrape means *I am carving, do not lean*. A rapid double-tap means *water on the ceiling, bring a vessel*. These signals pass through stone the way speech cannot, felt rather than heard, and the residents read them with their hands pressed flat against the rock. A visitor watching a resident place their palm against a wall, pause, and then walk to fetch a basin might think they are practicing superstition. They are reading their mail.

---

Their hands are their most telltale feature. Every adult has hands that have been shaped by the chisel. The calluses are not like laborer's calluses on the surface — not concentrated on the palm. They cover the fingers, the webbing between thumb and forefinger, the back of the knuckles where stone chips strike. The fingertips themselves are thickened, blunted, the ridges of the fingerprints worn smooth in patterns that correspond to how the individual grips their tools. A carver's hands can be read: the left hand steadies, so its calluses are on the heel of the palm; the right hand strikes, so its calluses follow the line of the mallet's grip. Older residents have hands that look almost arthritic from the outside — knuckles swollen, fingers slightly crooked where breaks healed without setting — but the movement is precise, the grip strong, the touch fine enough to feel the grain of the limestone and know where to place the next cut.

Children's hands are checked. This is a practice that might seem strange until you understand it. From the time a child can hold a chisel — six, seven years old — their hands are examined weekly by a parent or elder. Not for injury. For *fit*. The shape of the calluses tells you how the child holds the tool, and how the child holds the tool tells you whether they will be a carver or a collector or a cook or a keeper of the cisterns. The settlement reads its children's hands the way a surface community might read its children's inclinations — by watching them play, by listening to them talk. Here, the hands speak first.

---

They do not think of themselves as poor. This is important to understand. A surface visitor — if one were to descend the thirty steps, which few do — would see deprivation. Small rations. Dim light. No room. No animals. No sky. The visitor would feel pity, or horror, or the particular guilt of the privileged. The residents would feel puzzled by this, and then perhaps a little irritated, and then they would offer the visitor mushrooms, because hospitality is hospitality and the mushrooms are a luxury and you offer luxuries to guests.

The residents think of themselves as builders. As makers. As people engaged in a project so large and so slow that it will outlast every individual currently alive in it. The Hollowing Quarter is not finished. It may never be finished. This is not a source of despair. It is the source of everything. The project is the culture. The chisel marks on the walls are the genealogy. When a carver dies, their marks remain, and their children run their hands over them and know the shape of the labor that made the room where they sleep. The walls are full of hands. The settlement is a graveyard that is also a home, and the residents do not distinguish between those two things.

The oldest walls — the ones carved by the first generation, the ones who came down the steps with hand tools and a plan that was barely a plan — are rough and uneven and full of wrong angles. The residents do not smooth them. They do not correct them. Those walls are the first attempts, and the first attempts are kept the way a family keeps a great-grandmother's clumsy embroidery. Not because it is beautiful. Because it was first.

---

The children are the ones who reveal the place most clearly.

They are born into a world of grey light and dripping sound and the constant tap of the chisel, and they accept it with the total absence of wonder that children bring to whatever is given. They do not know the sky. They have the *lue*-lit ceiling of the grandmothers' chamber, with its pale constellations, and that is their sky. They have names for the bright patches. The Long One. The Fork. The Mother's Hand — a grouping that looks, if you squint, like an open palm. They lie on their backs in the grandmothers' chamber and trace these shapes and make up stories about them, and the stories are not stories about stars. They are stories about light trapped in stone, about a mineral that holds what it is given and gives it back slowly. The mythology of the Quarter is a mythology of patience and retention. Their heroes do not shine. They glow, dimly, after the lamp goes out.

The children play in the Throat and the ribs, chasing each other through passages too narrow for adults, hiding in fissures that will be sealed in a decade when the carving catches up. They are the explorers of their own settlement, because the settlement is always growing, and the growing edge — where the chisels are currently working — is the frontier, and the frontier is where children want to be. They are shooed away from active carving sites. They return. They watch. They learn.

They are not afraid of the dark. This needs to be said plainly. They are not brave about it. They are not克服ing a fear. They simply do not have the fear, because the fear is learned, and they were never taught it. The grave dark — the deep unoccupied passages beyond the settlement's edge — is not a place of dread for them. It is a place of potential. It is the place where the Quarter will be, eventually, when enough hands have done enough work. The dark is raw material. The dark is the future.

---

They have a relationship with the surface that is complex and largely transactional. They are not exiles. They are not refugees. They chose this, or their grandparents chose this, and the choosing has become foundational to who they are. But they are not self-sufficient. The grain comes from above. The wool comes from above. The copper and the occasional iron come from above. A small number of residents — traders, surface-walkers — make the climb regularly, bringing the products of their chisels (carved stone vessels, decorative gypsum, the pale threadweed which some surface apothecaries value) to trade for what the Quarter cannot make.

The surface-walkers are a distinct subculture. They squint. They tan. They dress differently — heavier cloth, broader hats that they do not need underground but wear above as disguise, as armor against the sky. They come back with goods and stories and a faint smell of wind and sun that the other residents find exotic and slightly uncomfortable, the way surface people might find the smell of incense. The surface-walkers' children often follow them above. The cistern-keepers' children become cistern-keepers. The carvers' children become carvers. This is not caste. It is gravity. The work shapes the worker and the worker raises the child and the child becomes the work.

There is no resentment about this. Or if there is, it is private, expressed in murmurs through thin walls, heard and not heard, the way everything in the Quarter is heard and not heard.

---

They grieve differently. The dead are placed in the walls.

Not in the walls — in the walls. When a resident dies, a niche is carved — if one has not already been prepared, and usually it has — in a wall of the chamber where they lived, and the body is placed there and the opening is sealed with stone and clay. The niche becomes part of the structure. The wall thickens by the width of a person. Over generations, certain walls become dense with the dead, the stone bulging slightly where the bodies have been added, and the residents pass their hands over these swellings the way you might pass your hand over a gravestone, except the gravestone is also your kitchen wall and your neighbor's sleeping alcove and the structure that holds the ceiling above your head. The dead are literally with them. In the stone. In the architecture. The Quarter is built on its own dead, and the dead are built into the Quarter, and the distinction between home and cemetery dissolves entirely.

The moss in the grandmothers' chamber — the pale green living thing that grows a finger's width per year — is sometimes spoken of as the grandmothers' gift. The story, which is not quite a religion and not quite a myth and not quite a history, says that the first grandmother to die in the Quarter was sealed into the far wall, and the moss grew from the crack where her niche was sealed. That the moss is fed by whatever the stone gives back. That the dead become the living and the living become the stone and the stone becomes the home and the home becomes the dead. This is not doctrine. It is not faith. It is just the shape of the place, and the residents live inside that shape without needing to name it.

---

They are not many. Perhaps two hundred, perhaps three — the count shifts with births and deaths and the movement of surface-walkers who are sometimes here and sometimes not. They know each other the way the stone knows the chisel: intimately, by the mark left. They know each other's coughs through walls. They know each other's footsteps on the worn floor of the Throat — who drags slightly, who walks heel-first, who is carrying something heavy by the change in their rhythm. They know each other's breathing in sleep, because they sleep within arm's reach of each other, separated by inches of stone or sometimes by nothing at all. Privacy is a concept they have a word for, borrowed from the surface tongue, but it describes a thing they do not have and mostly do not want. Privacy is the space between you and the next person, and in the Quarter, that space has been carved away, deliberately, with hand tools, over three generations.

They argue. They love. They have rivalries and debts and friendships that span decades. They have a politics — informal, consensus-based, conducted in the grandmothers' chamber with the ceiling glowing overhead. They have jokes, which are quiet jokes, jokes that depend on timing and the specific acoustic of the room. They have songs, which are mostly rhythmic and low and sound, to a surface ear, like work songs, which they are. The chisel has a rhythm. The dripping has a rhythm. The breathing has a rhythm. The songs follow these rhythms, and the rhythms follow the stone, and the stone follows the slow geological patience of the earth.

---

In the end, what lives in the Hollowing Quarter is a people who have become the place. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Physically, materially, in the calluses on their hands and the dust in their skin and the stone in their walls that was once their grandparents and will one day be them. They are a cavity being taught to be a home, and they are the teaching, and they are the home, and they are the cavity. They do not need the sky. They have a ceiling that glows. They do not need the rain. They have a ceiling that weeps. They do not need the stars. They have the dead in the walls and the moss on the far stone and the tap-tap-tap of the chisel that is the heartbeat of the place, always, always, from somewhere in the rock, the sound of something being made out of something being removed, the sound of patience, the sound of becoming.

They are not finished. They will never be finished. This is what makes them what they are.
