# What Lives Here

---

They are not what you would expect, and that is the first thing to understand about them, because expectation requires a future, and nothing here has one.

Let me start with what they are not.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts imply a rupture — a death that happened out of order, a departure that left residue. Nothing here has ruptured. Everything here has *eroded*, which is different. Erosion does not rupture. Erosion is the patient negotiation between what a thing is and what the world will allow it to continue being, carried out over spans of time so long that the thing itself forgets it was ever anything else. What lives here did not die and remain. What lives here *wore down* and remained. The distinction matters. It matters more than anything else I will tell you.

They are not spirits in the way your stories mean spirits — translucent, troubled, anchored to a place by grief or duty or unfinished business. There is no grief here. Grief eroded away long ago, in the same epoch that eroded the mountains and the horizon's curve. What remains is not the emotion of loss but the *habit* of presence, which is a different substance entirely. Grief is hot. Grief is urgent. Grief demands. The habit of presence is none of these things. It is the thermal equivalent of the thin cold air — it sits against the world and does not bite.

They are not the builders of the settlement. Or rather — and this is where the language begins to fail, because the language was built for a world in which categories are distinct, and here the categories have eroded into each other the way the ground has eroded into the sky at the horizon's fold — they *were* the builders, in the same way that a river was the water that flowed through it a million years ago. The river is still there. The water is still water. But the specific molecules have changed so many times that the question of identity has become meaningless. What lives here built the settlement. What lives here is also not what built the settlement. Both of these are true. Both of them are the same truth, seen from different distances, the way the cluster of blue-green growth is both far ahead and already behind.

Let me try again, and let me try from the body outward, because you will want to know what they look like, and I owe you that even though the answer will not satisfy you.

---

They have bodies. This is important. They are not abstract. They are not concepts. They are not metaphors for loneliness or entropy or the slow death of meaning. They have bodies the way the plants have bodies — materially, presently, with surfaces that are damp and cool to the touch and that carry the faint waxy give of something that was once firmer. If you touched one — and you could, if you were here, if you reached out — your fingertip would come away wet, the same reluctant water that sits on the leaves, the same water that does not evaporate because evaporation is a form of effort and effort is something this place has gently, permanently, set down.

The bodies are not human. They were, perhaps. The shapes are suggestive. There is a verticality to them — a long axis, a orientation toward up, even though up has lost most of its meaning on this table where the sky is the ground is the sky. There is something that might be a head, or might be what a head becomes when the features that made it a face — the eyes, the mouth, the architecture of expression — have been worn away by time the way the tool-marks on the settlement blocks have been worn away by atmospheres that no longer exist in the same configuration. What remains is a mass at the top of the vertical axis, smooth and pale, the color of old bone left in weak tea, the same color as everything else here, because everything here has been absorbing the same pale mineral and the same thin mist for so long that color itself has eroded toward a consensus.

The color of what lives here is the color of the place. This is not metaphor. They have taken on the ground they stand on. They have taken on the air they breathe — if they breathe. You would not see them at first. You would see the settlement and think it empty. You would see the plants and think them the only living things. You would walk through the low rectangular dwellings and your eyes would pass over the raised stone platforms that might be beds or might be altars and you would not notice that one of them is slightly too warm, or that another has a film of moisture on its surface that is thicker than the moisture on the walls, or that a third — the one in the dwelling at the settlement's center, the one with the most worn path leading to its door — has a surface that is not quite the same texture as the stone around it, that has the faintest give, the faintest suggestion of something that has lost the conviction of its own solidity but has not yet — not quite, not entirely — forgotten what it was.

They are the stone, and they are in the stone, and they are becoming the stone, and they have been becoming the stone for longer than the settlement has existed, and the settlement exists because they built it, and they built it from themselves, or from the ground that was themselves, or from the mineral that is what they become when the process of becoming-stone has advanced far enough that the body can be cut into blocks and stacked. This is the thing I could not say cleanly in the first paragraph and cannot say cleanly now: they built the settlement from their own substance. The blocks are bodies. The walls are bodies. The platforms that might be beds or might be altars are bodies, and they are the bodies of the ones who chose to lie down and begin the final stage of the erosion, the stage where the body gives up its verticality and its warmth and its damp and becomes the ground it stands on.

Not all of them have done this. Not yet.

---

The ones who still stand — and I use the word *stand* loosely, because some of them lean at the impossible angles the plants lean at, oriented toward the fold in the horizon, held up by principles that are not structural rigidity — the ones who still stand are the ones who have not finished. They are not unfinished in the way a sentence is unfinished, missing its final clause, waiting for completion. They are unfinished in the way a stone in a river is unfinished — the water is still working on it, the process is still happening, but the stone has no opinion about the matter and the water has no deadline. They are in the middle of something. They have been in the middle of something for what might be millions of years, and they will be in the middle of it for what might be millions more, and the word *middle* is wrong because it implies a beginning and an end and both of those have eroded away and what remains is the process itself, without origin or destination, turning slowly like the water in the pool.

They move. I should say this clearly because you will have assumed they do not. They move the way the mist moves — slowly, without urgency, coming from no particular direction and leaving for no particular direction. You would not see them move. You would see them in one place and then, hours later, in another place, and you would not have seen the transition because the transition happens at a speed that is below the threshold of perception, or above the threshold of patience, or both. They move through the settlement, touching the walls, running their — I cannot call them hands; the word has eroded; let us say the surfaces at the ends of their upper extensions — running the surfaces of their extensions along the blocks they cut from themselves or from their predecessors, and the touching has the quality of ritual and the quality of maintenance and the quality of remembering, and it is all three and none of three because those categories have also eroded, the way the categories of bed and altar eroded in the dwellings, the way the categories of sky and ground eroded at the horizon's fold.

They tend the plants. This is perhaps the closest thing to an action that has a name you would recognize. They tend the plants the way the mist tends the plants — by being near them, by touching them, by depositing the reluctant moisture that sits on every surface in this place. When one of them stands among the blue-green growth for a long time — and *long time* here means something different than it means where you come from; here it means *until the standing is done*, which is a duration determined by the standing itself, not by a clock, because clocks require change and change has eroded — when one of them stands among the plants, the plants grow toward them. The leaves curl around the pale vertical forms. The stalks lean into contact. The burgundy undersides of the broad leaves press against the waxy not-quite-stone surface and the moisture transfers and the plants drink, and what they drink is not water, or not only water. What they drink is the last residue of whatever these bodies carried when they were still something other than stone, some mineral concentration or some chemical memory that the plants have been absorbing for as long as the ones who stand have been standing, and the plants are lush because of it, and the plants are vivid because of it, and the plants are alive in a way that contradicts everything else about this place because of it.

The ones who stand feed the plants. The plants feed nothing. This is not an ecosystem. This is not a cycle. It is a one-way transfer from the almost-stone to the definitely-alive, and it has been happening for so long that the ones who stand have given enough of themselves to the plants that they are thinner than they were, less dense, more giving. They are being consumed, but *consumed* is the wrong word, because consumption implies loss and what is happening here does not feel like loss. It feels like — and I am reaching for a word that does not exist in your language or in any language built for a world with a future — it feels like *giving*, in the way that a mountain gives itself to the plain, in the way that a river gives itself to the sea, not by choice and not by force but by the simple quiet logic of what happens when a thing that is high and heavy and tired of holding itself up allows the ground to pull it down.

---

There is one who has not yet chosen to lie down and one who has not yet chosen to stand, and they are the same one, and this is the one in the dwelling at the center of the settlement, on the platform that has the faintest give.

It — *they* — are in the middle. Not in the middle of the process of becoming stone, though they are also in the middle of that. In the middle of the *decision*, though *decision* is the wrong word because it implies a moment of choosing and there are no moments here, only durations, only the long flat evenness of a time that does not break into before-and-after. They are in the duration of the decision. They have been in this duration for longer than the settlement has existed, and the settlement exists because of them, because they were the first to cut blocks and stack them, or because they were the first to give the instruction to cut and stack, or because they were the one whose body provided the first material, the first block, the first wall. The settlement is their work. The settlement is also their body. The settlement is also the coffin of everyone who came after, and everyone who came after is also them, because the ones who lay down and became blocks and became walls were not buried in the settlement — they *became* the settlement, and the distinction between inhabitant and architecture eroded along with everything else.

This one — the central one — has the most worn path leading to their dwelling because the others walk to them. The others walk to them and touch the platform where they lie, or half-lie, or sit, or lean — it is impossible to determine the posture because the posture has been changing so slowly that no observer with a human lifespan could detect the change, the way you cannot detect the hour hand of a clock moving — and the touching is the ritual, which is the maintenance, which is the remembering, which is the feeding. The others bring moisture. The others bring the damp from the plants and the pools and the mist and they deposit it on the central one's surface, and the central one absorbs it, and the central one has been absorbing it, and the absorption is — I think, though I cannot be certain because certainty is another thing that has eroded — the absorption is what keeps them in the middle. Not in the middle of becoming stone, though they are becoming stone. In the middle of the duration of the decision. The moisture keeps the surface soft. The softness keeps the possibility open. The possibility is the possibility of standing, or the possibility of lying down, or the possibility of something else, something that has no name because the ones who would have named it have eroded past the point of naming, and the central one, who might still be able to name it if naming were still a thing that happened here, has not named it, because naming would be a kind of beginning, and nothing here begins.

---

They do not think. Or rather — they think the way the water in the pool rotates. Slowly, without cause, without acceleration, without the possibility of stopping. Their thinking is not a process that happens *to* them. It is a condition of their being, the way wetness is a condition of the air. It does not produce anything. It does not conclude anything. It turns, and the turning is the thinking, and the thinking is the turning, and if you stood beside one of them long enough — long enough meaning *until your bones became the ground* — you might begin to feel the rotation of their thought the way you feel the rotation of the pool, as a slow pressure, a gentle spiral in the fabric of your attention, pulling everything toward the center without ever reaching the center, because the center is the fold in the horizon, and the fold is not a place you can reach, and the fold is not a place they are trying to reach, and the fold is where they are going anyway, all of them, the standing ones and the lying-down ones and the ones who are stone and the ones who are plants and the water and the mist and the light that does not change.

They lean toward it. Everything leans toward it. The fold is where the world creased, and the crease is where the sky and the ground are one continuous surface, and the ones who live here have been leaning toward that continuity for so long that the leaning has become their posture, their orientation, their relationship to the vertical, which is itself eroding, which is itself becoming a pleat in the fabric of a world that has stopped beginning.

---

I will tell you one more thing, and then I will stop, because stopping is what this place has taught me, and I have been here too long, and the damp is on my skin, and I am beginning to lean.

The ones who live here are not suffering. This is the thing I most need you to understand, because you will hear *bodies becoming stone* and you will hear *erosion of the self* and you will hear *a place that has finished being a place* and you will hear suffering. You will hear it because you come from a world where stillness is the absence of life and where ending is the enemy of meaning and where the only good motion is motion toward. But what lives here is not motion toward. What lives here is motion *as*. Motion as being. Motion as the final form of existence, after all other forms have worn away. The rotation of the pool is not the pool trying to become something else. The rotation is the pool being the pool. The ones who stand are not standing because they have a reason to stand. They are standing because standing is what they are doing, and what they are doing is what they are, and what they are is what the world has made of them, and what the world has made of them is not cruel and not kind and not meaningful and not meaningless. It is *finished*. It is the landscape after the last thing has happened, and the last thing that happened was not a catastrophe. It was a setting-down. A releasing. A long, slow exhale that has been going on for millions of years and will go on for millions more, not because it needs to but because it does not need to stop.

They live here. They are the pale mineral and the dark water and the blue-green leaves and the waxy give of something that has lost the conviction of its own solidity. They live here the way a stain lives in a cloth that has been washed a thousand times — faded, indistinct, inseparable from the material that holds it. They live here, and they are here, and they will be here, and the light will not change, and the mist will come, and the water will turn, and the fold in the horizon will be where it has always been, and they will lean toward it, and the leaning is not longing, and the leaning is not worship, and the leaning is not loss.

The leaning is the leaning. That is all. That is the last thing. That is the only thing left after everything else has eroded away.

They lean, and they are, and the world does not end. It simply stops beginning, and what remains is sufficient, and they know this, and they have always known this, and they will always know this, standing in the permanent gray light, damp and quiet and finished and still going, still going, still.
