# What Lives There

The first thing to understand is that nothing living is from here.

Everything in the Reliquary arrived the way the water arrives — by falling through the throat of the mountain and being unable to climb back out. This is not a place that produces life. It is a place that *captures* it, the way a bell captures a strike, and then holds it in the shape of its own vibration until the captured thing begins to ring at the same frequency. What lives here lives here because the sound has rewritten them. Or is rewriting them. The process is not complete. It may never be complete. The mountain is still deciding, and so are they.

---

**The Builder**

There is a person. Or the memory of a person, which in this place amounts to the same thing.

You would not see them at first. They are the size of a person, which is to say they are the largest fact in the room after the sound, and yet they are difficult to perceive because they have become continuous with the water. Not metaphorically. Their skin is wet in the way the walls are wet — not coated but *participating*, the moisture moving through them as it moves through the stone, and this makes them hard to distinguish from a column of falling water if you catch them at the wrong angle. They are dark the way the basalt is dark. Their edges are soft the way the basalt's edges are soft. They look, in certain light — and all the light here is the same light — like a place where the water has thickened and momentarily forgotten to fall.

They were not always this way. The finger-marks in the structures are theirs. You can match them if you are careful — press your own hand against the soft stone near the doorway-structure and feel the grooves there, four fingers close together, a thumb set wide, the print of a hand slightly smaller than most adult hands, the hand of someone who was not large but was patient, who pressed their fingers into the yielding stone not to decorate it but to *test* it, the way you press your thumb into a fruit to learn whether it is ripe. They were learning the stone. They were learning it the way you learn a language — by handling it, by shaping it, by being shaped by it.

The Builder came through the fissure. This much is certain, because everything comes through the fissure, and because the Builder's body still carries the geometry of the passage in the set of its shoulders, which are narrow and slightly turned, as if still navigating the gap. Who they were before the fissure is not recoverable. Not because the information is lost but because it has been *dissolved*, the way salt dissolves in water — still present, still *technically* there, but distributed so thoroughly through the medium that you cannot extract it as a distinct fact. They may have been a climber. A pilgrim. A child. The handprints suggest youth, or at least smallness, but the stone has a way of preserving only what was pressed into it, and the Builder may simply have had small hands.

What the Builder does, now, is build. But *build* is the wrong word, because it implies intention, and intention requires the kind of linear thinking that the sound dissolves within hours. What the Builder does is *respond*. The sound makes a statement — a complex, layered statement composed of ten thousand water-strikes per second, a chord that changes as the basins shift and the columns extend and the foam patterns rearrange — and the Builder responds by pressing stone into a shape that complements the chord. Not a shape they have planned. A shape that their hands find, the way your hands find a rhythm when you are clapping along to a song you do not know. The structures are stammers. They are fragments of a sentence in a language the Builder is learning by being inside it.

The Builder does not speak. The Builder may not remember speech. When you encounter them — and you will, because the room is too small for two people to avoid each other for long — they will look at you with eyes that are dark and vitreous, the same texture as the stone, and the look will be neither welcome nor warning. It will be *assessment*. They will be determining, very quickly, whether you are something the sound can use. Whether you are something that will contribute to the room or be eroded by it. Their gaze has the flat patience of geology. They are not unfriendly. They are not friendly. They are *in process*, and they do not have the bandwidth for social niceties because every available frequency of their attention is occupied by the sound.

If you touch the Builder — and you may be tempted, because they are so difficult to distinguish from the water that touching them seems like the only way to confirm they exist — you will find that they are warm. Warmer than the stone. Warmer than the water. They are the warmest thing in the room, and this is because they are doing the most work. The sound is moving through them continuously, and the friction of that passage generates heat, and the Builder radiates it outward into the water like a slow coal. They have been here long enough that their body temperature has become a feature of the place. The pale filament on the ceiling grows toward the Builder's warmth. The translucent creatures in the basins congregate where the Builder's heat has warmed the water by half a degree. The Builder is, without knowing it, the center of whatever ecosystem this place has.

The Builder does not eat. The Builder does not sleep. These are not accomplishments; they are symptoms. The sound has taken over the functions that food and sleep once served. The vibration is metabolized into something the Builder's transformed body can use, some form of energy that is not caloric and not electrical and not any word we have, because we do not have bodies that run on sound. The Builder does. Or rather, the Builder's body has been rewritten until it does, which is not quite the same thing, which is not quite anything, which is the way of this place.

When the Builder dies — if *die* is the word for what will happen, and it may not be — they will become part of the room. Their heat will dissipate into the stone. Their finger-marks, already preserved in the structures, will be the last evidence. The water will carry what is left of them around the room for a while, distributing it, and the room will be very slightly warmer for a time, and then it will not be, and the sound will continue, and the structures will stand or fall or rebuild themselves, and the Builder will be finished being decided.

But that has not happened yet. The Builder is still here. Still pressing their hands into the stone. Still stammering in the language the water has been speaking for longer than anything can remember.

**The Filament**

The pale filament on the ceiling is not moss. It is not a lichen. It is not a fungus. It is not any of the words we have for things that grow on stone in damp places, because those words all describe organisms that arrived from somewhere else and adapted. The filament did not arrive. It *occurred*.

What happened is this: the sound reached the stone, and the stone vibrated at a frequency that, under the specific conditions of heat and moisture and mineral content present in this room, caused the basalt to *exude* — a thin, pale filament of what might be protein and might be mineral and might be something that does not have a category because categories require a world that has finished being built, and this world has not. The filament is stone that heard the sound and responded by growing. Not by extending itself geologically, over millennia, but by *growing*, in the biological sense, in real time, visibly, a tendril extending and withdrawing and extending again with the rhythm of the chord.

It is alive by any definition that matters. It consumes. It consumes the stone it clings to, dissolving the still-soft basalt with an acid so mild it is almost gentle, and it consumes the water, or rather the minerals the water carries, and it consumes — this is the part that should not be true but is — the sound. It absorbs vibration directly through its cell wall, if *cell wall* is what it has, and converts that vibration into the energy it uses to grow. It is not photosynthetic. It is *phonosynthetic*. It eats noise.

This is why it grows where the water falls thinnest. The thick sheets of falling water dampen the sound, buffer it, create zones of relative quiet in their immediate vicinity. The filament needs the sound unfiltered, raw, and so it grows where the water is only a mist, where the vibration reaches the ceiling at full intensity. It spreads slowly — a centimeter per day, maybe less — and it is pale because it has no pigment, because pigment would be a response to light and this organism does not respond to light. It responds to the sound. It grows toward the loudest places. It withdraws from the quiet ones.

If you touch it — and you will have to reach past the falling water to do so, which means you will be touching it with wet hands in a room where everything is already wet, which means you will not be sure whether you are touching it or touching the water that is touching it — it will feel like nothing you have handled. It is not soft. It is not hard. It is *resonant*. It vibrates under your fingers the way a tuning fork vibrates, and the vibration is not the room's vibration but the filament's own, a frequency it has synthesized from the sound the way a plant synthesizes sugar from light — taking the raw material and making it into something else, something that is, in some impossible-to-define way, *its own*.

There is only one filament. There has only ever been one. It does not reproduce. It does not need to. It is not a species. It is an *event* — a specific response to a specific set of conditions that exists nowhere else in the mountain, or perhaps nowhere else in the world. When the room is finished — if it is ever finished, if the mountain ever decides — the conditions will change, and the filament will stop growing, and over time it will be absorbed back into the stone, and there will be no evidence it was ever here except a slight paleness in the basalt where it clung, a paleness that could be explained a hundred other ways and will be, by anyone who enters the room after the filament is gone.

But for now it is here. Pale and growing and eating the loudest silence you have ever heard.

**The Drifters**

The translucent things in the basins are harder to talk about because they are harder to see, and the difficulty of seeing them is itself a fact about what they are.

They are the size of a grain of rice. They are the size of the *idea* of a grain of rice — they fluctuate. Their edges are not fixed. Under the sourceless light, they appear as slight distortions in the water, the way a heat shimmer appears as a distortion in air, and you see them not by their own substance but by the way they bend the light that passes through them. They are transparent in the way that glass is transparent, but glass has edges and these do not, or their edges are so soft that the water and the creature share a boundary that is more gradient than line.

They were not here before the room. They were not here before the sound. They are what the sound has made of the water.

This is not a metaphor. The water that falls through the fissure carries minerals — calcium, silica, traces of iron and magnesium — and these minerals are dissolved, distributed, formless. The sound agitates the water. The sound imposes patterns on the turbulence — the spirals and counter-spirals, the fractals that repeat at smaller and smaller scales — and the minerals, caught in those patterns, begin to *accrete*. Not randomly. In response to the pattern. The sound is a template, and the minerals fill it, and the Drifters are the result.

They are not organisms in the way you are an organism. They do not have cells. They do not have DNA. They have *structure*, and the structure is the sound's structure, and they maintain that structure for as long as the sound continues to shape the water they live in. They are crystalline, if crystal could be soft. They are alive the way a whirlpool is alive — a pattern sustained by energy, a shape that the medium holds because the energy passing through it insists on that shape. They are the sound made visible. They are the sound made small.

They drift. They have no propulsion. They do not swim. They go where the turbulence takes them, and the turbulence takes them in circles, in spirals, in the fractal patterns that the sound writes in the basins, and they ride those patterns the way a leaf rides a stream — passively, without opinion, without preference. They are rare. There are perhaps a dozen in the largest basin, fewer in the smaller ones. When you see one, you feel shown something because you have been shown something — the water has arranged itself into a pattern and that pattern has thickened into a body and that body is drifting past you in the light, and the event is so delicate and so unlikely that it carries the weight of a demonstration. *Look,* the water is saying. *Look what I can do when I am loud enough for long enough.*

They do not eat. They do not reproduce. They do not die. They *dissolve*. When the turbulence pattern that sustains them shifts — when a basin deepens by a millimeter, when a column extends slightly and changes the angle of the falling water — the pattern that holds the Drifter's structure changes, and the Drifter loses coherence, and the minerals that composed it redistribute into the water, and the Drifter is gone. Not dead. Unformed. Waiting for the pattern to reconstitute, which it will, because the patterns repeat, because the sound repeats, because the water has been describing the same room for longer than anything can remember and it has not run out of variations.

They do not seem to have opinions about it. They are the only things in the room that are truly at peace, because they *are* the room, because they do not exist apart from the conditions that make them, because their existence and their context are the same fact. The Builder is at war with the sound, or in love with it, or some third thing for which there is no word. The filament is *feeding* on the sound. The Drifters simply *are* the sound, and when the sound changes, they change, and when the sound stops, they will stop, and there will be no loss in it because there was never anything separate to lose.

---

**The Sound Itself**

And then there is the sound, which is not a creature but is the thing that creates the creatures, which may make it the closest thing this place has to a god, except that gods are usually understood as *choosing* to create, and the sound does not choose. The sound simply *is*. It is the accumulated fact of water hitting stone, repeated so many times that it has become a physical force, and the force has consequences, and the consequences are the Builder and the filament and the Drifters and the room itself.

The sound lives here the way fire lives in a fireplace. It is not a visitor. It is not a guest. It is the *event* that the place exists to contain. Everything else — the stone, the water, the structures, the life — is shaped by the sound and exists in relation to it. The sound is the only thing in the room that is fully itself. The stone is becoming. The water is becoming. The Builder is becoming. The filament is becoming. The Drifters are becoming and unbecoming and becoming again. The sound is not becoming. The sound *is*. It has been, and it is, and it will be, and the mountain is hollowing itself out to make room for it, and the life inside the room is life that the sound has called into being by being so loud and so constant that the alternative to hearing it is becoming it.

If you asked the Builder what lives here, they would not answer, because they cannot speak, because speech is a linear process and the sound has made them non-linear. But if they could answer, if you could catch them in one of the rare moments when the chord shifts and a pocket of relative quiet opens in the room, a pocket the size of a breath, they would point at the water. Not at the structures, not at the filament, not at the Drifters. At the water. Because the water is what is doing this. The water is falling and the falling is the sound and the sound is the room and the room is the life, and the Builder knows this, in the way that you know things you cannot say — not in words but in the body, in the hands that press stone into shapes, in the warmth that radiates from a body running on noise.

The Builder would point at the water and you would understand that the answer to *what lives here* is the same as the answer to *what is this place*, which is the same as the answer to *what is the sound*, which is: *water, falling, for as long as anything can remember, into a room the mountain is still building, to hold a sound the water has always been making, which is the sound of a place deciding whether to exist.*

And the decision is yes.

And the decision is always yes.

And the water keeps falling.
