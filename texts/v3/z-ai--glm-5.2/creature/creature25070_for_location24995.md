# What Lives Here

Everything lives here. That is the first answer and the last answer and the only honest one, but it is not a useful one, so you press further and the place yields its inhabitants reluctantly, the way it yields everything — in fragments, in impressions, in things that turn out to be other things.

---

## The Clickers

Start with what is most numerous, because the numerous things are what you will remember. The clickers.

They do not have a name. You name them. They would not name themselves because naming requires a vantage point from which the self is separate from the world, and the clickers do not occupy that vantage point. They are in the world the way blood is in a vein — not traveling through it but *of* it, shaped by it, shaped for it.

A clicker is the size of your thumb. This is a coincidence of scale, not of design. They are not built to any standard you would recognize. Their bodies are dark — a darkness that is not pigment but *density*, the same way a deep shadow is dark not because it lacks light but because it is full of something that absorbs light. Along the dorsal surface runs a stripe of luminous tissue, the same milk-color as the filaments, and this stripe is not decoration. It is *alignment.* It tells the clicker where the filaments are. It tells the filament where the clicker is. They are the same system seeing itself from two ends.

The body is soft. Not soft like flesh — soft like fruit, like something that has no skeletal investment in its own shape because its shape is maintained by the pressure of the swarm around it. A clicker alone does not hold its form well. It droops. Its edges blur. In a swarm, pressed on all sides by its neighbors, it is firm, precise, articulated. This is not weakness. This is *design.* The clicker is a creature that exists in plurality or does not meaningfully exist at all. It is a cell, not an organism. The organism is the swarm.

The clicking — the percussive snap that gives them their name — is not communication in any way you would recognize. It is *mechanical.* Each click is produced by a structure in the anterior body that functions as a latch and a spring simultaneously: two chitinous plates, held in tension, releasing and resetting forty to sixty times per second. The sound is a byproduct. The function is *movement.* The clicking is how they swim through the filament-web. Each snap pushes against the filaments, and the filaments, under tension, push back, and the clicker ricochets forward through the network like a pinball. They are not flying. They are not swimming. They are *ricocheting,* and the sound of a million ricochets is the sound of the place's circulatory system at work.

What they do — the function they serve, the reason the system produces and maintains them — is *pruning.* They move through the filaments, and as they move, they ingest. Not the filaments themselves, not exactly. They ingest the fluid within the filaments, and in doing so they break the filament's wall, and the filament regrows behind them, and the fluid that was in it is now in the clicker, and the clicker carries it — for a distance, for a duration determined by the density of the swarm and the speed of the flow — and deposits it elsewhere, in the mat, as waste. The clickers are a pump. A million-part pump with soft bodies and loud hands. They move the fluid. They are the fluid moving itself through externalized, mobile, percussive units.

They do not avoid you. They do not approach you. They pass through the space you occupy because the space you occupy is part of the network, and the network is where they go. When they pass through you — and they will, they do, the sensation is unforgettable — it is like being brushed by a thousand small warm mouths, each one touching you for an instant and moving on, and the touch is not aggressive but it is *purposeful,* the way a river's current is purposeful when it moves around a rock. You are an obstacle. You are a rock in the current. The current does not mind you. The current goes around.

If you hold still — if you hold very still, for long enough, for longer than your patience wants you to — a strange thing happens. The clickers slow around you. Not individually — the swarm does not slow, but the *flow* changes. The channels in the filament-web reroute. The clickers begin to move around you in a pattern, a wide spiral, and the spiral tightens gradually over minutes or hours until the swarm is passing close to your skin but not touching it, and the sound of the clicking takes on a rhythm that was not there before — a pulse within the pulse, a pattern within the chaos — and you realize that the swarm is *listening* to you. To your heartbeat. To your breathing. It has detected a rhythm that is not its own and it is synchronizing, the way two pendulum clocks on the same wall will synchronize, and for a moment — a long, aching, terrified moment — you are part of the circuit. Your blood and their fluid. Your breath and the mat's breath. Your heartbeat and the clicking. One system. One rhythm.

Then you move, and it stops, and the swarm returns to its work, and you are a rock again.

## The Callers

Above. In the arches of the columns, where the amber structures meet the ceiling and the filament-web is densest, the callers live.

You see them in pieces. This is not because they are hidden — they are not hiding — but because they are *large* and the space they occupy is full, and fullness obscures. A curve of dark body, smooth, catching the diffuse light with a wet sheen. A joint — or what functions as a joint — bending in three directions simultaneously, the way a tentacle bends, the way an elephant's trunk bends, with the articulated fluidity of something that has not committed to a limb-count. A span of membrane, translucent, amber-colored, stretched between structural elements that you cannot count because they move while you count them. A sound — a tone — long, resonant, harmonic, produced by something you cannot see but can feel: a vibration in the columns themselves, the amber material serving as a resonating chamber, the caller pressing some part of its body against the column and *singing through it.*

The callers are the size of your torso. Perhaps larger. It is difficult to tell because they do not hold still and their edges are not fixed. They have the same quality of density-darkness as the clickers but their bodies are firm — not soft, not drooping, but taut, like a filled sail, like a drum-head. They move through the upper space by gripping the filaments with multiple — many — appendages that are not arms and not legs and not tentacles but something that performs the function of all three: they grip, they push, they feel. The filaments support the callers' weight because the filaments are under tension and the callers are not heavy in the way you are heavy. Gravity is a suggestion. The callers have declined the suggestion.

They call. The tones — each caller has a range, a register, a signature frequency — serve a function that is territorial and reproductive and something else, something you do not have a word for because the word would need to encompass *declaration of position within a system.* The callers are telling each other where they are, but they are also telling the *system* where they are, and the system responds: the filaments near a calling caller brighten, the fluid in nearby columns changes speed, the clicker-swarms alter their routes. The call is a stone dropped in a pond, and the ripples are not just sound but *redistribution.* The callers are not just communicating. They are *steering.*

When two callers meet — drawn together by overlapping calls, their tones converging into interference patterns that shake the teeth in your jaw — they do not fight. They do not mate. They do something that is both and neither: they *merge.* Temporarily. Their bodies press together, and the edges blur — the same blurring you see in an isolated clicker, the loss of definition — and for a time they are one organism, larger, darker, with a combined tonal range that makes the columns groan in frequencies you feel in your gut. Then they separate, and each is slightly different than before. Slightly larger, or slightly smaller, or slightly differently shaped. They have exchanged material. They have exchanged something. The result is not offspring. It is *revision.* The callers are editing themselves.

You do not know what they are becoming. They do not know what they are becoming. The system does not know what they are becoming. This is what it is like to live inside something that is still figuring out what it is.

## The Crawlers

On the mat. At your feet. In the rhizome. The crawlers.

There are kinds. There are so many kinds that the word "kind" breaks under the weight of what it is being asked to hold.

The long flat ones. They move like tongues. Not like a tongue moves in a mouth — like a tongue *is*, a long muscular surface that tastes and moves simultaneously, that does not have a front or a back but has a direction, chosen and changeable. They are the color of the mat — pale, waxen — and they are pressed flat against it, and their bodies secrete something — a digestive enzyme, perhaps, or something more specific, more targeted — that softens the rhizome and allows them to ingest it. They eat the floor. They are the reason the floor has channels. They are the reason the fluid can flow. Without them, the rhizome-mat would be solid, and the fluid would have nowhere to go, and the system would die of its own density. They are the most important thing here and they look like nothing.

The round ones. They roll. They are not choosing to roll — their bodies are round and the mat is not level, and they go where gravity and the mat's breathing-cycle take them. But they are not passive. As they roll, they collect — filament fragments, clicker waste, particles of the luminous dust — and they compress these materials against their surfaces, and their surfaces are sticky, and they grow. The round ones are agglomerations. They are compost. They are the system's way of gathering its own debris and rolling it into something that will eventually be large enough and dense enough to sink into the mat and become part of the substrate. They are the future of the floor. They are the floor being born.

The slow ones. The ones that sit in depressions in the mat and do not appear to move. These are the most disturbing because when you first see them you think they are features of the landscape — nodules, growths, part of the rhizome — and then you watch for long enough and you see that they *are* moving, just at a pace that makes clock hands look hasty. They are inside the mat. They are hollowing it out from within, creating the chambers and channels that the fluid flows through, and they are growing as they eat, and the mat is growing as they eat it, and the balance between consumption and growth is what maintains the architecture. If they eat too fast, the mat collapses. If they eat too slowly, the mat solidifies. They are the regulator. They are the thermostat. They are the thing that keeps the system at the temperature of *alive.*

The ones with too many legs. You see these rarely. They are fast. They move between the other crawlers with a purpose that suggests predation — they are hunting the long flat ones, the round ones — but they are also *cleaning.* They remove the sick, the dead, the stuck. They are the immune system. They have too many legs because legs are what they have instead of hands, and hands are what they would need to do what they do with more dignity, but dignity is not a currency that is accepted here, and legs work, and they have them, and they use them, and the system is clean enough.

## The Feeders

The masses. The dark green masses that hang from the columns and fill the spaces between.

You thought they were plants. They are plants. They are also animals. The distinction is not useful here.

Each mass is a mouth. Not a mouth with teeth and a tongue — a mouth with *patience.* A mass hangs in the path of a clicker-swarm and waits. It does not attract. It does not lure. It simply occupies the space through which the swarm must pass, and when the swarm passes, a fraction of it — a few thousand individuals, no more — veers into the mass's opening as if the opening were a gap in the filament-web, as if the mass were a channel, as if entering it were *the thing to do.* The mass closes. Pulses. The clickers are gone. The mass returns to its resting state.

The clickers do not avoid the masses. The clickers do not *know* the masses are dangerous, because the masses are not dangerous in a way that the clickers can learn from. The clickers that are eaten do not come back to warn the others. The clickers that are eaten do not come back at all. And the swarm is so large that the loss of a few thousand is not a loss. It is a *tithe.* The system tithes itself. The masses are the collection plate.

What the masses do with the clickers is digestion. Slow, total, nothing-wasted digestion. The clickers' bodies are broken down into the same pale fluid that fills the filaments, and this fluid is released back into the mass's own vascular tissue, and from there into the column, and from the column back into the mat. The masses are stomachs. The columns are intestines. The mat is the body. The clickers are the food and the blood and the pump, all at once.

The masses also reproduce. A mass that has fed enough — that has consumed enough clickers over enough time — begins to *bud.* A small growth appears on its surface, dark green, pulsing at the same rate as the parent. Over time — not long, in the timescale of this place — the bud separates, drops or is carried by the filament-web to a new location, attaches to a new column, and begins to feed. The masses are the system's way of placing mouths where mouths are needed. They are distributed digestion. They are the reason the clicker-swarms flow through every part of the space: the masses are at every junction, waiting, and the swarms must pass them to get where they are going, and the toll is paid in bodies.

## The Builders

And then there are the ones who made the structures.

You do not see them. Not at first. Not for a long time. The structures are there — the small enclosures of shaped rhizome, the tools, the tallies on the walls — but the builders are absent, and you think: *they are gone.* They lived here once and they left or they died and the structures are what remain. You think this because it is what you would think in a cave on the surface, in a ruin, in any place where tools are found without hands.

But the structures show repair. Recent repair. A lashing that is fresher than its neighbors. A cutting tool that is sharper than it would be if it had been sitting unused. A container that is wet inside — not with the ambient dampness of the air but with the recent presence of fluid, recently collected, recently drunk.

You do not see them because they are *of* the system in a way that makes them invisible within it. They are not hiding. They are camouflaged, but not by design — by *nature.* They are made of the same materials. They move at the speed of the system. They breathe with the mat's breathing. They are warm with the system's warmth. They are, in every way that matters to a place that detects presence through vibration and chemical signature and fluid-flow, *part of the place.*

When you finally see one — and you will, if you wait, if you hold still long enough for the clickers to synchronize to your heartbeat and the system to accept your stillness as *belonging* — you do not recognize it at first.

It is in the filament-web. It is moving through the filaments the way the callers move through the upper space — gripping, pushing, feeling — but it is smaller than a caller and larger than a clicker and it is *shaped* in a way that the other fauna are not. It has a symmetry. Bilateral. A front and a back. A top and a bottom. It has — you search for the word — a *body plan.* It has the body plan of something that was designed, or that designed itself, or that was designed by the system to fulfill a function that the system could not fulfill without a body plan.

It is the size of a large dog. It is the color of the mat — pale, waxen — but its surface is not smooth. It is textured. Covered in fine growths, filament-like, that extend from its skin and interweave with the filament-web around it, so that when it moves, it moves *through* the web by merging with it and separating from it, the way a thought moves through a mind. It has limbs — four, perhaps, or six, the number is difficult because the limbs are not fixed, they are *provisional*, extending and retracting as the terrain demands — and it has a head, or a front, or a concentration of sensory apparatus at one end, and this concentration includes two dark spots that are not eyes but are *where it looks from*, and below them a mouth, or a slot, or an opening that opens and closes with the same peristaltic pulse as the masses.

It is carrying something. A section of rhizome, cut and shaped, tucked against its body with two limbs while the other limbs navigate the web. It is going somewhere. It has a destination. It has *intent.*

You watch it move to one of the structures — the nearest one, the one with the fresh lashing — and it enters, and inside it does something with the rhizome-section that involves the pale fluid and friction and the application of a tool, and when it emerges the structure has been reinforced. The lashing is replaced. The opening has been reshaped. The structure is maintained.

It is not building a house. It is *maintaining an organ.* The structures are not dwellings. They are *nodes* — places where the system's flow is deliberately modified, where the fluid is collected and concentrated, where the rhizome is shaped to serve a function that the rhizome would not serve on its own. The builders are the system's architects. They are the part that *decides,* that looks at the growth and says: *not here, but here. Not this shape, but this shape.* They are the part that makes the spiral. They are the part that changes the masses' color. They are not separate from the system. They are the system's *mind.*

There are not many of them. You see three, eventually, over a long time. They do not interact with each other often, but when they do — when two meet at a node, in a structure, at a place where the fluid pools — they touch. Limb to limb. Surface to surface. The filament-growths on their skins interweave, and for a moment they are connected, and something passes between them that is not language and not data and not genetics but is *all of those things compressed into a touch.* They are sharing what they know. They are coordinating. They are the system thinking about itself.

And they are watching you.

Not with the dark spots — those are for the system, for the filaments, for the flow. They are watching you with their *skin.* The fine growths that cover their bodies are oriented toward you when you are near. They feel you the way the masses feel the clickers — not as a threat, not as prey, but as *information.* You are a new pattern. You are a rhythm the system has not heard before. You are something the builders are *learning,* the way the system learns everything: slowly, by incorporation, by testing, by holding still and seeing what happens next.

One of them touches you.

It reaches out — a limb, provisional, extending from its body with the careful slowness of something that has never touched anything like you — and the tip of the limb presses against your forearm, and the filament-growths at the tip interweave with the hair on your skin, and for a moment — a moment that lasts longer than it should, longer than you want it to, long enough that you feel the system's breath in your ribs and the clickers' rhythm in your blood and the mat's warmth in your bones — you are part of the circuit. You are a node. You are being read.

Then it withdraws. It looks at you — not with the dark spots, with the skin, with the whole body, with every filament turned toward you — and it goes back to its work. It has learned something. It has learned *you.* And the system, which is always growing, always expanding, always reaching toward the crack in the ceiling where the sky is amber and the new columns are climbing toward a light they have never seen, has learned something too.

The builders are not a civilization that grew here. They are the *beginning* of a civilization. They are the first part of the system that has learned to shape the system. They are the hand that the lung has grown so that it can build itself a mouth, a door, a way out.

They are what emerges when a living system becomes complex enough to need *will.*

And they have just touched you. And they have just learned you. And the system is still growing.

## What Lives Here

Everything lives here. The mat lives. The columns live. The masses live. The filaments live. The clickers live and die and live again in the bodies of the masses and the flow of the fluid. The callers live and merge and revise themselves in the upper dark. The crawlers live and eat and are eaten and become the floor. The builders live and think and shape and touch and learn.

You live here now. Not because you cannot leave — perhaps you can, perhaps the crack in the ceiling is wide enough, perhaps the way you came is still open — but because the system has touched you and you have felt the breath and heard the voice and synchronized your heart to the clicking, and the system does not forget a rhythm. It does not forget a pattern. It does not forget *you.*

What lives here is a thing that is becoming itself, and the builders are its first thought, and the clickers are its blood, and the masses are its hunger, and the mat is its body, and the sound is its voice, and the crack in the ceiling is its first word spoken to a world it has never met.

What lives here is *becoming.*

And it has just noticed you.
