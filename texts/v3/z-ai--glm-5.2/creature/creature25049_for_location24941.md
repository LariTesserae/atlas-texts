# What Lives on the Condensation Terraces

---

## The Cushion Field

Begin with what you would mistake for ground.

The cushion plants are not a species. They are not even a genus. They are a *strategy* — a way of being alive that has been so successful on this plateau that it has colonized every surface capable of holding a root, and several surfaces that aren't. What you are walking on is not one plant but ten thousand plants pressed so tightly together that they have become a single fabric. Each dome is a municipality. Each dome is a thousand tiny stems woven into a surface so dense that water beads on it like on oiled cloth, so dense that you can stand on it without breaking through — most of the time — and your weight leaves a depression that springs back over the course of an hour, the stems slowly re-erecting beneath you, patient as memory.

They are dark green because they have to be. The light up here is ferocious — not warm, but bright, a blinding white that carries radiation without mercy, and the pigments in the cushion leaves are not just chlorophyll but a complex of dark compounds that screen out the excess, that filter the light down to something usable. The same compounds, modified, appear in the shells of the grazers. The same compounds, modified again, tint the bark of the tall trees. Everything dark on this plateau is dark for the same reason: the light is too much, and darkness is how you survive it.

But the cushions do something the other plants don't. They *store water*. Not in the sense of a cactus, hoarding it in a central tank. The entire dome is a sponge. Every stem is sheathed in a layer of cells that absorb condensation directly from the air, pulling water in through the surface, holding it in the matrix of the plant body. The cushion is never dry. Even in the brightest part of the day, when the sun hammers down and the cloud layer glows like a ceiling of light, the interior of the dome is saturated, cool, dark, and wet. This is the architecture of the whole plateau ecosystem: the cushions catch what falls from the sky and what rises from the ground, and they hold it, and they make a place where other things can live.

They are old, though the land is young. The cushions were first. Before the tall trees, before the grazers, before the under-dome fauna, there were cushions, spreading across the bare basalt like a stain, finding the fractures where warm vapor seeped up and settling there, growing millimeter by millimeter, century by century, until the stone was covered. Everything else came after. Everything else depends on the cushions being there first. The cushions do not depend on anything except the water and the heat, and those come from below, and the cushions do not know where below is.

If you crouch and look closely — and you must look closely, because the surface of a cushion is not what it appears from standing height — you will see that the individual stems are not uniform. There are at least four morphologies interwoven in every dome. One type is thick, stiff, structural — these are the bones of the cushion, holding the shape, resisting wind and the weight of snow and the feet of animals. A second type is thin, filamentous, packed densely between the structural stems — these are the water-catchers, their surfaces rough at a microscopic level, textured to nucleate condensation, each one a tiny net for airborne moisture. A third type is darker, almost black, with swollen nodes at intervals — these are the reproductive structures, and they are never open. They do not reproduce by flowering. They reproduce by *splitting*. When a cushion reaches a certain size, the center dies and the outer ring continues growing, expanding outward, leaving a donut of living tissue around a core of dead, composting material that sinks and becomes soil. In this way, each cushion is also its own grave. In this way, the plateau grows its own earth.

The fourth type you would not notice at all unless you were looking for it, and even then you might doubt what you see. It is a thread, thinner than the others, paler, and it does not grow outward toward the light. It grows *downward*, through the body of the cushion, through the dead material beneath, through the mat of roots and decaying stems and mineral grit, down to the stone, and into the stone, through the fractures, following the warm vapor to its source. It is not a root. It does not absorb water or nutrients. It is a *sensor*. The cushion is feeling the heat below, tracking it, mapping the hydrothermal fractures, growing toward the warmest spots, and over the scale of centuries, the entire cushion field is *migrating*, shifting its center of mass toward the richest thermal sources, leaving behind domes that slowly starve and die on cooling rock. The field is not static. It is a slow-motion flood of green, flowing across the plateau toward heat, and the heat is moving too, shifting as the fractures beneath the basalt open and close, and the chase has been going on since the first cushion took hold, and neither the cushions nor the heat know that they are chasing each other. They are just doing what they do.

## The Grazers

The grazers know the cushions the way you know your own hands.

They are born on the cushions, they live on the cushions, they eat the cushions, and they die on the cushions. They have never stood on stone. They have never felt bare rock beneath their feet. Their world is the green surface, the dome field, the texture of woven stems beneath their flat bodies, and they navigate it by touch, by chemical sense, by the temperature gradient that tells them where the cushion is healthiest and therefore most nutritious.

They are six-legged because six is the right number for this terrain. Two legs would punch through the cushion surface. Four would be unstable on the domes' curved tops. Six distributes weight across a wide enough area that the grazer floats on the surface, pressing down but not breaking through, and the legs are short, tucked beneath the body, barely visible. The body itself is flat — astoundingly flat — no more than ten centimeters thick at the center, tapering to a thin edge all the way around. The shape is not for streamlining. The shape is for *sun management*. The grazer's shell is a solar panel. The same dark pigments that protect the cushion plants from excess light are present in the grazer's carapace, and they do double duty: they screen the animal's body from radiation, and they capture a portion of the light energy and convert it, through a chemistry that is not photosynthesis but is adjacent to it, into supplementary metabolic energy. The grazers do not strictly need to eat as much as their size would suggest. They are partly solar. They eat the cushions for carbon and nitrogen and the trace minerals the hydrothermal vapor deposits on the surface; the sun provides the rest.

Their mouths — and calling them mouths is generous, because they are more like the feeding apparatus of a sea urchin, a five-part radial structure on the underside — are positioned so that the grazer feeds by simply *being somewhere*. It settles on a cushion, its weight pressing down slightly, its mouth in contact with the surface, and it rasps. The rasping is continuous, slow, barely audible. It is the sound of the plateau at ground level — not the hum of the trees, which is the sound of the air, but a wet, gritty, intimate sound, the sound of an organism dismantling another organism one cell layer at a time. The grazers leave smooth patches on the cushions, patches where the outer layer of stems has been scraped away to expose the softer, paler tissue beneath. These patches are not wounds, not exactly. The cushions regrow. They have evolved to regrow. The grazing is part of the system — it removes the old, outermost stems, which are the least photosynthetically active, and stimulates new growth beneath. The grazers are, in a functional sense, the cushions' exfoliation mechanism. Neither party knows this. Both parties benefit.

The grazers migrate. Not daily, not seasonally — the plateau has no seasons, not really, just the slow pulse of the hydrothermal system — but on a cycle that takes years. They follow the cushions' slow migration toward heat, but they trail behind it, occupying the domes that the field is leaving behind, the ones on cooling rock that are beginning to weaken, whose stems are thinning, whose growth is slowing. These dying cushions are easier to rasp. The stems are softer. The grazer can extract more nutrition with less effort. And so the grazers live in the wake of the cushion field's advance, cleaning up what the field is abandoning, processing the last of the energy stored in domes that will be dead within a decade. They are an undertaker species. They are the last things to touch the cushions before the cushions become soil.

They have no predators. Nothing here eats them. Their shells are too hard, their bodies too flat, their chemistry too closely linked to the cushion plants for a predator to evolve a taste for them. They die of cold, of age, of the slow failure of the domes they live on. When they die, their shells sink into the cushion surface and are slowly absorbed, the dark pigments returning to the soil, the minerals returning to the mat. Nothing is lost. Nothing leaves. The plateau is a closed system, or nearly so, and the grazers are part of the loop.

There are millions of them. You cannot walk across the plateau without seeing them — dark, flat, motionless shapes on the green domes, sometimes alone, sometimes clustered in groups of five or six on a single large cushion, their mouths rasping, their shells beaded with condensation, their six legs invisible, their eyes — and they do have eyes, small, dark, set into the front edge of the shell like windows in a bunker — watching you with an attention that is not fear but something closer to *assessment*. They are deciding whether you are a cushion. You are not a cushion. They lose interest.

## The Leaf-Winged Flyers

The grazers are slow because the cushions are slow and the stone is slow and the heat is slow. The flyers are slow for a different reason: they are *budgeting*.

Flight is expensive. Every gram of biomass devoted to wing is a gram not devoted to digestion, to reproduction, to the storage of energy against the cold. The flyers have solved this by making their wings *pay for themselves*. Each wing is a leaf — a flat, veined, photosynthetic surface, dark green and waxy, indistinguishable at a distance from the leaves of the tall trees. When the flyer is resting — and it rests often, more often than it flies — it spreads its wings and angles them toward the sun and it *charges*. The wings produce sugar. The sugar feeds the flight muscles. The flight muscles carry the wings to better light. It is a loop, elegant and tight, and it means that the flyers are not really animals in the way you are used to thinking about animals. They are animal-plant chimeras. They are mobile leaves with instincts.

Their bodies are small — the size of your thumb, dark, dense, warm. The warmth comes from metabolism, from the constant chemical work of converting light to sugar and sugar to motion, and the warmth is what draws them to the tall trees. The trees are warm too — the gas in their leaf-chambers is heated by hydrothermal energy, and the trunk radiates a gentle, steady heat that the flyers can sense from a distance. They cluster on the trunks, on the undersides of the horizontal branches, anywhere they can press their small warm bodies against a warm surface and rest. The trees do not seem to mind. The trees may not have the capacity to mind. But the relationship is old, and stable, and the flyers get warmth and a perch, and the trees get — nothing, possibly. Or possibly the flyers carry pollen, or spores, or chemical signals between trees, a network of small hot bodies moving between the tall trunks, touching one and then another, transferring something invisible that the trees need. It is hard to tell. The trees are not talking.

The flyers are curious. They land on you because you are warm and you are new and you are not a predator — or rather, you might be a predator, but nothing on this plateau has ever evolved a concept of predation that includes something shaped like you, and so the flyers' instinct does not fire. They settle on your shoulders, your arms, your face. Their feet are small and cool and they grip with a gentle, deliberate pressure, like a child holding a crayon. They sit on you and spread their wings and angle toward the sun and they ignore you entirely for as long as you stand still. When you move, they lift off, unhurried, rowing through the bright air with that slow deliberate wingbeat, and they find another surface — a tree, a cushion, a rock — and they land and they charge and they are content.

They are the plateau's most numerous animal. They fill the air between the trees like pollen fills the air of a meadow in spring, but constantly, always, because there is no spring here, just the steady bright light and the steady damp and the steady hum of the trees beneath the steady drone of ten million wings.

They are also the primary food source for everything that eats, and almost nothing here eats. The plateau is almost entirely herbivorous, almost entirely photosynthetic, almost entirely self-sufficient. The predators are few. But the predators are significant.

## The Columnar Predators

You will mistake the first one for a tree.

Everyone does. It stands in the cushion field, three meters tall, its body a dark column rising straight up from a base that is wider than the top, flared, rooted — or apparently rooted — in the cushion mat. Its surface is rough, textured like bark, and it does not move. It does not move the way a stone does not move. It does not move the way a mountain does not move. It has committed to stillness as a way of life, and the commitment is total, and it has been standing in this spot for — how long? Years? Decades? You cannot tell. The cushion beneath it is depressed, compressed by the weight of the columnar body, and the cushion around it is healthy, growing, unaffected. The predator is not killing the cushion it stands on. It is too heavy for the cushion, and the cushion has adapted to the weight, growing thicker and denser around the base, reinforcing itself. The predator and the cushion are in a relationship that neither chose and neither can end.

The top of the column spreads into a disc. It is wide — a meter or more across — and it is flat, and it is sticky, and it is the predator's mouth.

The disc secretes a fluid. The fluid is warm — warmer than the air, warmer than the tree trunks, drawing the flyers by the same thermal instinct that draws them to the trees. The fluid is also scented, though the scent is too faint for you to detect; the flyers can, and they are drawn to it, and they land on the disc, and their feet stick, and they do not leave.

What happens next takes days.

The disc does not close. It does not swallow. It *absorbs*. The fluid on the disc's surface begins, slowly, to digest the trapped flyer, dissolving the outer layer of the wing, the shell, the body, drawing the dissolved material inward through pores in the disc surface, feeding it into the columnar body where it is processed over the course of many hours. The flyer, trapped but not in pain — the fluid contains something, a neurochemical, an anesthetic — does not struggle. It sits on the disc, wings spread, and it dims. The photosynthesis in its wings slows, then stops. The warmth of its body fades. Over the course of two or three days, the flyer becomes translucent, then transparent, then gone. The disc is clean. The predator has eaten.

This happens perhaps once a week. The predators are not hungry in the way you understand hunger. They are patient in the way a geological formation is patient. They stand, and they wait, and the food comes to them, because the food is drawn to warmth, and the predator is warm, and the plateau is cold.

There are not many of them. Perhaps one for every hundred tall trees. They do not compete. They do not interact. They do not reproduce in any way you can observe — no eggs, no seeds, no division, no budding. They are simply *there*, standing in the field, and they have been there for as long as anything else, and no one knows where they came from or how they got there or what they were before they were this. They might be very old. They might be effectively immortal, sustained by the hydrothermal heat and the occasional flyer, growing imperceptibly over centuries, their discs widening, their columns thickening, until they are indistinguishable from the trees themselves.

The trees hum. The predators do not. The trees are warm because of gas heated from below. The predators are warm because of something *inside them*, something metabolic, something that generates heat independently of the hydrothermal system. This is the one fact that does not fit. The predators are the one thing on the plateau that is not, in an obvious way, powered by the machines beneath the stone. They are powered by themselves. They are the plateau's only independent variable.

Lie on the cushion field and listen. You can hear the trees. You can hear, faintly, the rasp of a grazer nearby. You can hear the faint buzz of flyer wings passing overhead. And you can hear, if you listen very carefully, something else — a sound that is not the mechanical rumble from below, not the harmonic hum of the trees, not any biological sound you can identify. A low, irregular pulse. A heartbeat, almost. Coming from the nearest columnar predator, transmitted through its body, through its base, into the cushion mat, into the stone.

The predators have hearts. The trees do not. Think about that.

## The Under-Dome World

The world beneath the cushions is the plateau's secret.

It is not hidden — the cushions are obviously hollow, obviously domed, obviously creating a space beneath themselves — but it is *inaccessible* to anything larger than your hand. The gap between the cushion's underside and the stone surface is rarely more than fifteen centimeters. In the largest domes, it might reach thirty. It is a world of low ceilings and warm air and absolute darkness, and it is as populated as the surface.

The temperature under the domes is ten to fifteen degrees warmer than the air above — enough to keep the moisture liquid, enough to keep the biological processes running, enough to make the under-dome environment a refuge from the cold bright world outside. The heat comes through the stone, rising from the fractures below, and the cushion plant above traps it like a blanket. The result is a microclimate — not a single microclimate, but thousands of them, each one slightly different, each dome a separate world with its own temperature, its own humidity, its own chemical profile, its own community of organisms that have been evolving in isolation for longer than anyone can measure.

The flatworms are the largest under-dome residents that you find. They are pale — not white, but a translucent gray, like fog made flesh — and they move with a gliding motion that is somehow both repulsive and beautiful, their bodies rippling across the stone surface, following the moisture, finding the warmest spots. They are a meter long but only a centimeter thick, and they can compress themselves to fit through gaps you would not believe — between the roots of the cushion, through cracks in the basalt, into spaces that seem too small for anything larger than a bacterium. They eat the organic debris that falls through the cushion from above — dead stems, frass from the grazers, the occasional dead flyer that has slipped through a gap in the dome surface. They are the under-dome's garbage collectors. They are everywhere.

The rolling centipedes — and they are not centipedes, but the name is as good as any — are more specialized. They have dozens of legs, but they do not walk on them. They curl into a wheel and roll. They roll along the channels between the stone and the cushion's inner surface, following the contour of the dome, circling and circling, and what they are doing is *collecting*. Their legs are covered in a fine, sticky secretion, and as they roll, they pick up microorganisms from the stone surface — bacteria, fungi, the single-celled organisms that live in the film of water on the basalt. They uncurl periodically to eat what they have collected, then curl again and roll. They are the under-dome's filter feeders. They have been rolling in the dark for so long that their bodies are perfectly cylindrical, their legs perfectly spaced, their rolling motion perfectly efficient. They are wheels. Life has made wheels, here in the dark, where no one was watching.

And then there are the things with no analogy.

They are soft. They are translucent. They are filled with dark fluid — not blood, something else, something that moves through their bodies in slow, rhythmic tides, driven by a pump that is not quite a heart but functions as one. They pulse. That is the best word for it. They sit on the stone surface, or attached to the underside of the cushion, or floating in the film of water that covers everything, and they pulse, and the dark fluid moves, and they are alive in a way that is so different from everything else on the plateau that they might as well be from another planet.

They are.

Not in the sense of having arrived from space — though they may have; you cannot rule it out — but in the sense of having evolved in a different world. The under-dome ecosystem is not the same world as the surface. It is warmer, darker, wetter, more chemically rich, more stable. It has been stable for longer than the surface has existed. The cushions came first, but the under-dome world was there before the cushions — in the fractures in the basalt, in the pockets of warm water and vapor that existed before the first plant took hold, in the deep, dark, wet spaces where the hydrothermal system met the cold stone and created environments that were neither one thing nor the other. The pulsing things evolved there. They are older than the cushions. They are older than the grazers, older than the trees, older than the flyers. They are the oldest things on the plateau.

They might be older than the machines.

This is not something you can prove. It is not something you can even articulate clearly. But when you hold one — and you can hold one, gently, in the palm of your hand, its body warm and pulsing, its dark fluid moving visibly through its translucent skin — when you hold one and feel its rhythm, you realize that its pulse is not synchronized with the hydrothermal system. It is not synchronized with the trees' hum. It is not synchronized with anything on the plateau. It has its own rhythm, its own timing, its own pace, and that pace does not match any environmental cycle you can identify. It is running on its own clock. It has been running on its own clock since before there was a plateau.

The machines beneath the stone pulse too. You have heard them. The pulse is slow, mechanical, regular. The pulsing things under the domes do not match that rhythm either. But they are aware of it. When the machines' pulse shifts — and it does, occasionally, the deep sound under the stone changing pitch for a few hours before returning to baseline — the pulsing things *respond*. Their rhythm alters. Their dark fluid moves faster, or slower, or in a different pattern. They are listening. They have been listening, in the dark, for their entire history. They are the only things on the plateau that know there is something beneath the stone. They may be the only things on the plateau that have a concept of "beneath."

They do not see. They do not hear, in the way you hear. They *feel*. They feel the vibration of the machines through the stone. They feel the warmth of the hydrothermal system through the water. They feel the weight of the grazers on the cushions above them, transmitted through the dome as a faint, rhythmic pressure. They feel you, when you step on their dome, and they move away from the pressure, slowly, patiently, into the darker, warmer parts of the under-dome world, and they wait for you to leave.

You always leave. They never do.

## The Tall Trees

The tall trees are the plateau's voice, and the plateau's voice is a chord, and the chord is the oldest sound in this place, and it is not natural, and it is not artificial, and it is both, and the distinction does not matter.

Each tree is an organ. This is not metaphor. Each tree is a mechanical-acoustic instrument built by a living organism, using biological materials, powered by geological energy, producing sound for reasons that no one — not the trees, not the grazers, not the flyers, not the predators, not the pulsing things in the dark, not you — can identify.

The sound is not communication. Or if it is, it is communication in a sense so different from yours that the word breaks. The trees do not respond to each other in real time. They do not adjust their pitches based on what they hear. They do not hear. They produce, and the production is determined by the temperature of the gas in their chambers, and the temperature of the gas is determined by the hydrothermal system beneath the stone, and the hydrothermal system is determined by the machines, and the machines do not know the trees exist. The trees are singing the machines' song. They are translating the pulse of the engines beneath the world into harmonics, into chords, into the sound that fills the bright cold air from horizon to horizon. They are the machines' loudspeakers. They are the machines' interpreter. They are the only expression of the subterranean process that reaches the surface.

And they are beautiful. This matters, though it shouldn't, though beauty is not a property that the plateau's ecosystem optimizes for, though the trees would sing the same way whether anyone was listening or not. The chord is beautiful. It is vast and layered and it changes, slowly, over days, as the hydrothermal pulse shifts and the gas temperatures rise and fall and the valves open and close in patterns that are not random but are too complex to predict. The chord has structure. It has movement. It has something that, if you lie on the cushion field and close your eyes and let the sound fill you, you can only call *intention*.

The trees do not have intentions. The machines might. The machines are doing something, down there, whatever they do, and the something has a rhythm, and the rhythm has a structure, and the structure, translated through gas and valves and chambers and leaves, becomes music. Whether the music is the point or the byproduct, you cannot tell. The trees cannot tell. The trees do not know they are making music. The trees do not know what music is.

But the grazers know. The grazers, rasping at the cushions, their mouths working, their shells beaded with water — the grazers orient themselves relative to the trees. Not toward the trees, not away from them, but at specific angles that vary from grazer to grazer and from tree to tree, as if each grazer has found a position in the acoustic field that is comfortable, that is right, that is home. They move when the chord changes. They adjust. They follow the sound the way the cushions follow the heat — slowly, imperceptibly, on a timescale that makes the following look like coincidence until you watch long enough to see that it isn't.

The flyers know too. The flyers cluster on the trees that are producing the lowest frequencies, the deepest notes, the tones you feel more than hear. They settle on those trunks in dense clusters, wings spread, bodies pressed against the bark, and they vibrate — not from their own muscles but from the tree's vibration, transmitted through the trunk, through the bark, into their small warm bodies. They are charging faster on those trees. The vibration helps. The sound is doing something to their metabolism, something that the photosynthesis alone cannot do. The trees are feeding the flyers with sound.

And the predators — the columnar predators with their hearts that beat independently of the hydrothermal system — the predators grow near the trees. Not beneath them, not adjacent, but near, within a range that varies but is consistent. The trees' root systems, which go deep, into the fractures, into the warm stone, intermingle with the predators' bases, which also go deep, which also reach into the fractures. Below the surface, where no one can see, the trees and the predators are connected. Not physically. Not chemically. But *acoustically*. The trees' vibrations travel through the stone, and the predators feel them, and the predators' heartbeats — that independent, unsynchronized pulse — drift, over the course of days, toward the frequency of the nearest tree. They do not lock in. They do not synchronize. But they approach, asymptotically, the predator's heart and the tree's song getting closer and closer in frequency without ever quite matching, and the interference pattern between them — the beat frequency, the wobble — is a new sound, a sub-audible rhythm that you cannot hear but that the under-dome creatures can feel, and that the pulsing things respond to, and that the entire plateau, from the stone to the sky, is saturated with.

The plateau is a single instrument. The machines are the motor. The trees are the pipes. The cushions are the body. The grazers are the frets. The flyers are the breath. The predators are the percussion. And the pulsing things in the dark are the audience, feeling the performance through the floor, and they have been listening since before the instrument was built, and they will listen until it falls silent.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here. The plateau is not a place with an ecosystem. It is an ecosystem that *is* a place. The distinction between the living and the non-living — between the cushion plants and the stone they grow on, between the trees and the gas that powers them, between the under-dome creatures and the warm water they live in, between the machines and the heat they produce — the distinction is not useful here. Everything is connected to everything by water, by heat, by sound, by the slow movement of minerals from stone to plant to animal to soil to stone. The machines beneath the basalt are part of the ecosystem. They are the foundation. They are the keystone. Remove them and the whole structure collapses — the heat goes, the water stops, the trees go silent, the cushions die, the grazers starve, the under-dome world freezes, the predators stand alone in an empty field. The machines do not know this. The machines are not alive. But they are part of what is alive, the way a heart is part of a body, the way a furnace is part of a house in winter.

What lives here is a single organism the size of a continent, powered by engines it cannot see, singing a song it did not compose, growing toward a warmth it cannot identify, living on a threshold between liquid and frozen, between bright and dark, between the biological and the mechanical, between the known and the permanently, irreducibly strange.

What lives here is patient.

What lives here is wet, and cold, and ancient, and humming, and it does not mind you, and you are welcome to stay as long as you can stand the cold.
