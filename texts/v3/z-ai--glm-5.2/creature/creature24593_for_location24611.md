# The People of the Sapping Yards

They are fewer than you would expect. A dozen, perhaps, sometimes thirteen or fourteen — the number shifts, but never dramatically, and never in a way that seems to disrupt the work. You do not see all of them at once. The Yards are dense with structure, and the people move through that structure the way blood moves through tissue — present in the whole system, visible only at certain points, elsewhere implied by sound and effect. You become aware of them gradually. A figure passing between two chambers along a root-canal path you had not noticed was a path. A hand reaching from a darkened doorway to check a gauge. The smell of someone's lamp smoke, different from the tannin-and-sap ambient, drifting from a sleeping-nook where someone is reading or resting.

---

They are not tall. This is the first thing you notice when you are close enough to notice anything, and it makes sense — the chambers are low, the corridors between them lower, and the ceiling of the Yards is a living canopy that has been trained close. The workers have grown into the proportions of their place over time, or were selected for them, or simply became this way. They move under branches that would catch a taller person's head without ducking, and they do not duck, because the space was made for them or they were made for the space, and the distinction has stopped mattering. Their bodies are compact, dense-seeming, with a lower center of gravity than you are used to in people — built for stability on a floor that gives, that breathes, that is never quite still. They walk with a particular gait, weight slightly forward, feet placed flat and deliberate, the way you walk on a boat or a surface that might move. The floor does move, slightly, with the tidal patience described, and they are calibrated to it the way a sailor is calibrated to the sea. A visitor stumbles. They never do.

Their skin is dark. Not uniformly — it varies among them as skin varies among any group of people — but the baseline is dark, and the specific quality of it is something you do not see in people who live under open sky. It is a darkness that has a green undertone, or a brown that has deepened past brown into something almost like the color of wet bark. Some of this is the light. The green-gold filtration of the canopy does strange things to color perception, and after an hour in the Yards your own hands look different too. But some of it is real. They live in permanent shade, in humid warmth, in air that is chemically saturated with terpenes and tannins and the byproducts of anaerobic decomposition. Their skin has responded. Or their skin has always been this way. You cannot tell, and when you ask — if you ask — the question seems not to land properly, as though you have asked whether the bark of a tree is the color it is because of the forest or because of itself.

Their hands are remarkable. You see this most clearly at the central processing area, where the work requires fine control. The sap-liquor is drawn from the tap-lines into collection vessels, then transferred to the copper hearth for boiling, and the boiling is a precise process — too hot and the liquor caramelizes and is ruined, too cool and it does not reduce properly, and the window between these states is narrow. The workers who tend the hearth manage this with their hands. They do not use thermometers. They dip a finger into the liquor at intervals — a quick, practiced gesture, in and out in less than a second, because the liquor is hot enough to burn — and they judge the temperature and the viscosity and the sugar-caramel threshold by feel. Their fingertips are thickened, calloused in a specific pattern, and they are stained. The sap-liquor leaves a residue that builds up over time, a dark amber varnish that coats the skin of the hands and the creases of the knuckles and the lines of the palms. The workers' hands are stained this color — the color of the product, the color of the place, a deep, translucent brown that catches the green-gold light and looks almost like wood itself. After enough years a worker's hands begin to look like they are made of the same material as everything else in the Yards.

---

They do not all do the same work, and you can see this in their bodies. The hearth-workers are the most visible — they work in the brightest part of the Yards, in the heat, in the steam, and their forearms are bare and scarred with small burn marks from years of proximity to the copper vessel and the occasional splash of hot liquor. They are the most stained. Their hair, if they have any, is dark and damp and pressed flat from the humidity. They wear less clothing than the others — not out of style but out of temperature. The area around the hearth is tropical. Further out in the ring of chambers the air is cooler, merely warm, merely wet, and the workers there are more covered.

The line-workers — those who maintain the tap-line network, who walk the miles of copper and ceramic tube threaded through the root lattice, who clear blockages and replace sections and check pressure gauges — are leaner, more angular. They spend their days moving through the root mass, sometimes in spaces so tight they must lie flat and pull themselves forward with their elbows, sometimes up in the canopy where the tap-lines connect to the tree trunks at the point of tapping. They are the ones who use the domesticated beetles, releasing colonies of coppery larvae into sections of tube and collecting them again when the cleaning is done. Their hands are less stained but more cut — the copper tubes have sharp edges where they have been cut and re-fitted, and the root lattice has its own roughness. Their forearms show a particular pattern of fine scratch-marks, diagonal, as though from reaching into narrow spaces between woody surfaces.

The digester-workers are the ones you see least. They tend the sealed root-hollows where methane is captured from decomposition, adjusting valves, checking gas pressure, adding organic material to the digesters and removing the spent residue. This work takes them to the outer edges of the basin, where the chambers are smallest and the light is dimmest, and where the smell of decomposition is strongest — a sweet, sour, heavy smell that clings to them even when they come to the central area for meals. They carry it with them. The other workers do not seem to notice it, or have stopped noticing it, or share it to some degree themselves. You notice it. The digester-workers have a particular quality — a stillness, a patience, that is greater even than the general patience of the Yards. They sit with the digesters the way a person sits with a sleeping animal, attentive to breath and movement, ready to act but mostly just present.

---

There is a woman — or someone you read as a woman, in the green-gold light, in the damp air, with the engine-throb in your sternum — who seems to be the eldest. You do not know if this is true. Age is hard to read here. The conditions preserve a kind of smoothness in the skin, and the dim light is forgiving, and the general unhurried quality of movement means you cannot distinguish the caution of age from the calm of long habit. But her hands are the most stained you have seen — not just the fingertips but the whole hand, the wrist, partway up the forearm, a continuous dark amber varnish that has the texture of old wood. She works at the hearth. She is the one who dips her finger and judges the liquor, and she does this with a rhythm and a certainty that suggests she has been doing it for longer than the engines have been running, which is impossible, but the impression is there. She does not speak much. None of them speak much, but she speaks least. When she does speak it is in a low voice that sits just under the engine noise and is hard to separate from it, so that her words seem to come from the same source as the throbbing — from the floor, from the roots, from the ground itself.

There is a younger one — a line-worker, angular and quick, with the diagonal scratch-marks on his forearms and a specific alertness in his eyes that the others do not have, or do not show. He moves through the Yards with more speed than the general pace, not rushing but purposeful, with the energy of someone who has not yet settled into the tidal patience of the place. He has been here long enough to know the work but not long enough to have been shaped by it. His hands are still his own. His skin is still his own. He climbs into the canopy to check the upper tap-lines, and from below you see his legs disappear into the green-gold darkness among the branches, and you hear him call down a reading — pressure, flow rate — in a voice that is clear and specific and louder than any other voice in the Yards. The others tolerate this. Some of them almost smile, the way older people tolerate the volume of the young.

---

They eat together. The communal chamber with its single-root table is the one place in the Yards where all of them gather, though not all at once, because the work does not stop. They eat in shifts, and the table is never empty. The food is not from the Yards — it is brought in from outside, across the levee, from gardens or settlements elsewhere in the forest — but it is prepared here, in a chamber with a small hearth of its own, and the preparation involves the sap-liquor itself, a small amount of it, used as a flavoring, a sweetener, a glaze. The smell of cooking sap-liquor is different from the smell of reducing it — warmer, less sharp, with a caramel note. They eat slowly. They talk, but the talk is low and sparse and practical — valve adjustments, line pressures, the behavior of a particular digester, the health of a particular tree. Personal talk exists but is hard to distinguish from work-talk, because for them the distinction may not be meaningful. A tree that is tapping poorly is a personal concern. A digester that is underproducing is a personal concern. The work is the substance of their lives and the conversation reflects this.

They do not seem unhappy. This is a thing you find yourself wanting to say, and then wanting to qualify, and then wanting to leave unqualified because the qualification is more revealing of your own assumptions than of their state. They are not happy either, in the way that word implies a lightness or a contrast. They are present. They are continuous with their work and their place. They have the quality of people who have been doing one thing for a long time and have become the thing they do, and the place they do it in, and the people they do it with, and this has not happened suddenly or dramatically but slowly, the way the architecture happened — grown, not built, over decades, with constant maintenance. You do not see joy. You do not see misery. You see the expression of a system in a human face, and the system is functioning.

---

At night — and the Yards do have a night, though it is only a darkening of the green-gold to green-silver as the light above the canopy fades and the filtered light dims — the engines do not stop. The Yards never stop. The workers sleep in shifts, as they eat in shifts. The sleeping-nooks are warm, cradled in living fiber, and the workers who sleep in them sleep deeply despite the engine-throb, or because of it. There is something about the sound — the layered pulsation, the never-resolving rhythm — that seems to function for them the way a heartbeat functions for an infant. It is the sound of the system that holds them. They have known it since they arrived. Some of them, you think, may have been born to it. The youngest workers have the stain in their hands already, at an age where it should not yet have built up, and this suggests either that they started very young or that the stain is not entirely from the work but from the place itself, from the water, from the air, from the slow saturation of a life lived inside a living system.

They keep to themselves. They are not unfriendly, but they are not curious about you, or about the world outside the Yards, in any way that compels them to ask questions. They answer yours, when you ask, in the low voices, with the economy of people who have organized their speech the way they have organized their work — nothing wasted, nothing extra, nothing that does not serve the function of the moment. They would let you stay. They would feed you. They would give you a sleeping-nook and a lamp and they would not treat you differently from anyone else, and you would be expected to work, and the work would be shown to you once, slowly, and then you would be left to do it, and the doing of it would be the only further instruction you would receive. This is how they teach. This is how they were taught. The knowledge lives in the doing and is not separable from it.

When you leave — climbing back up through the alder-willow, ascending through the root-floor that becomes ground, through the band of thicket that is not a thicket but a threshold — you look back down into the basin and you see the steam from the copper hearth rising through the canopy, and you hear the engines, fainter now but still present, still pulsing, and you see the green-gold light dimly through the leaves, and you realize that the people down there will still be there when you are gone, doing the work, tending the boil, checking the lines, sleeping in their living cradles, and the Yards will be running, and the trees will be tapping, and the sap will be flowing, and the engines will be throbbing, and it will all be exactly as it was when you were in it, because it does not need you, and it never did, and the people who are part of it do not need to be anything other than what they are, which is the part of it that moves.
