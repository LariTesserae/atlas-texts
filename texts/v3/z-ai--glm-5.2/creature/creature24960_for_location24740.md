# What Lives Here

The insects have no name. Not because names are impossible here, but because they would not answer to one. They are not the sort of thing that answers.

There are perhaps forty of them, though counting is a act of faith in a place where quantity might be a quality rather than a number. They are the size of the first joint of your thumb, elongated, their bodies segmented in a way that recalls both beetle and dragonfly without committing to either. Their wings — and there are four, or six, or a number that shifts depending on the angle at which you observe them, because the wings are translucent and layered so that counting requires distinguishing between a wing and the shadow of a wing, and shadows are among the things this place has quietly withdrawn — their wings beat at a frequency that does not match their apparent size. Too fast for their mass. They hang in the airless air as though supported by something other than aerodynamics, as though the medium itself is holding them up, and the beating is decorative, or vestigial, or a habit retained from a world where flight required effort.

They are drawn to moisture. Every surface here is wet, but they are selective. They avoid the rills — the moving water, the water that travels its right angles with purpose. They land instead on the still wet, the condensation on the fresh-fractured stone, the beads that form on the edges sharp enough to hold them perfect and round. They drink by pressing the full length of their bodies against the surface, not just mouthparts but belly, legs, the flat of their wings, and they seem to absorb through every surface at once, like a sponge, like something that does not have an inside and an outside but only a through. When they drink, their iridescence deepens. The color shifts from a pale oil-slick prismatic shimmer to something denser, more committed — a copper-green that pulses once, twice, and then fades back to the shifting pale as they lift off and drift to the next surface. You watch one land on the tooth of a stone gear. The vibration does not disturb it. It settles into the thrumming as though the vibration is a surface it can stand on, and it drinks from the condensation that beads on the gear's tooth, and its copper-green pulse is synchronized with the gear-frequency in a way that might be coincidence and might be the only form of communication this place permits.

They do not interact with each other. Not visibly. Their paths cross and recross between the spiral-ferns, but there is no avoidance, no courtship, no aggression. They pass through the same points in space at different times, and if two arrive at the same point simultaneously — which happens — they simply occupy the same location for a moment, their wings overlapping, their bodies superimposed, and then they separate without any evidence that the encounter occurred. They are not solitary. They are parallel. Each one exists in its own trajectory, and the trajectories do not intersect because they are not the kind of things that can intersect — they are more like notes in a chord, simultaneous but not related, co-present without acknowledgment.

They tick. The ticking is not vocalization. It is not the beating of wings — the wings produce a different sound, a faint papery whisper that is below the threshold of the ambient chord and audible only if you hold your breath and bring your ear within a hand's breadth of the insect's body. The ticking comes from somewhere inside. It is mechanical in quality, or rather, it has the same quality as the mechanical sounds that permeate this place — regular, unperturbed, as though produced by a process rather than an intention. Each insect ticks at a slightly different rate, and the aggregate of their ticking is the high note nested inside the chord, the one you cannot separate out once you have heard the whole. They are, in a sense, instruments. Not instruments that are played. Instruments that play themselves, or that are being played by the same force that holds the pistons and turns the water and curls the ferns, and the instrument is their bodies, and the music is the ticking, and the audience is the stone.

If you watch long enough — and "long enough" is a phrase without meaning here, but if you attend — you will notice that the insects never land on the moss-animals. This is not avoidance in the way a creature avoids a predator. The insects simply do not perceive the animals as surfaces. They fly past them, through the space they occupy, and sometimes their wings pass through the fur-moss without resistance, as though the animals are not there, as though the animals exist at a slightly different angle to the insects' reality, and the overlap that permits you to see both simultaneously is not an overlap the insects participate in. The animals, for their part, do not react to the insects. They do not react to anything. Reacting is a process, and they are not here to process. They are here for something else.

The moss-animals are harder to describe, because description assumes a boundary between the describer and the described, and their boundaries are negotiable.

There are twelve. You can count them. This is remarkable, because counting is an act that requires stable objects, and nothing here is stable except in the sense of being held still, and holding still is not the same as stability. But the twelve are countable. They remain twelve. They do not merge, do not split, do not multiply or diminish. Twelve is a number that belongs to them, or that they belong to, and the permanence of this number is one of the few fixed facts the place offers.

They are low-slung, as the description said, but this deserves more attention. Their legs are short and articulated in a way that suggests more joints than are standard — five or six per leg, bending in directions that make sense only when you watch them move, at which point the extra joints reveal themselves as solutions to a problem of locomotion that you did not know existed. They move the way water moves in the rills: in straight lines, turning at right angles, descending terraces in deliberate steps. They do not wander. They traverse. Their paths are as fixed as the rills — polished into the stone, if you look closely enough, faint tracks worn into the rough surface, though how long this would take and whether time here permits wearing is a question that generates the kind of recursive unease that this place specializes in.

Their bodies are long. Not snakelike — there is a spine, or a structure that functions as a spine, visible through the pelt as a ridge of darker density, and the limbs are positioned along it in a pattern that is bilaterally symmetrical and consistent across all twelve animals. The pelt is the blue-green of the ferns, but it is not the same material. Up close — and you can get close, they do not flee, they do not acknowledge, they simply continue their traversals — up close, the pelt is not fur and not moss but something that partakes of both. The individual strands are fine, like fur, but they branch, like moss, and at the tips of the branches, there are structures that might be spores or might be dew or might be the same condensation that beads on the stone, held there not by surface tension but by something more intentional. The strands move. Not with the wind — there is no wind. They move on their own, a slow undulation, the way anemone tentacles move in a current that is not water and not air but some third medium that exists only in the immediate vicinity of the animal's body. The fur-moss is alive in a way the animal beneath it might not be, or might be, or might be the same thing — the animal and its covering might be a single organism, the way a coral is not an animal that lives in a structure but an animal that is a structure.

Their eyes are large. This is not an adaptation to dim light, because dim light is not a condition here — it is a constant, and constants do not produce adaptations. The eyes are large because they need to be, for reasons that have nothing to do with vision. The eyes do not see. You realize this when you look into one — and you will look into one, because the eyes draw attention the way the slit above draws light, passively, without intent — you realize that the eyes are not organs of perception. They are organs of expression. Something is expressed through them, and what is expressed is not recognition, not awareness, not intelligence in any form you can map. What is expressed is the place itself. The pewter light. The ambient sound. The moisture. The held breath of the stone. The eyes are large because they are windows, and what is on the other side of the window is not a mind but a condition — the condition of being here, the condition of the frame, the condition of sustained equilibrium that is not peace and is not tension but is the thing that exists when both have been subtracted from a moment and what remains is the moment itself, without before or after.

The eyes do not blink because blinking is change, and the eyes are not subject to the exemption that permits the animals to move. The eyes are the part of the animals that belongs to the place. The bodies — the traversing, breathing, drinking bodies — are the part of the animals that is exempt. Each animal is a joint, a seam, where the frozen place and the moving exemption meet, and the seam is not smooth, and the body of the animal is the visible evidence of this discontinuity.

---

They emerge from gaps too narrow for them. This was described. But what was not described is what happens in the gap.

You watch one emerge. You stand near the back wall, where the engine-stone protrudes, where the cylinders vent their constant steam, and you watch a gap between two interlocked stone gears — a gap perhaps three centimeters wide, a crease in the rock — and you wait, and then the animal is there. Not climbing through. Not squeezing. There. Present. The way a word becomes present on a page — not arriving from anywhere, not traveling from ink to paper, but simply existing where it did not exist a moment before, in a tense that the page does not acknowledge.

But if you are watching at the moment of emergence — and you must watch without anticipating, because anticipation is a tense, and tenses are fragile here — if you watch with the kind of attention that does not expect, you see the gap do something. The gap widens. Not physically. The stone does not move. But the gap becomes more, the way a silence between words becomes more than silence when you attend to it. The three-centimeter crease opens into something that has no dimension, that is not a space but a quality, a thinness in the reality of the wall, and the animal steps out of this thinness as though stepping out of a thought, and then the gap closes, or rather, returns to being a gap, a crease in rock, three centimeters wide, innocent of what it just did.

The animals are not coming from somewhere. They are coming from the place itself, the way the ferns come from the stone, the way the engine-shapes come from the rock. They are expressions of the same force that makes the cylinders and the gears and the spirals. But the ferns and the engine-stone are held still, and the animals are not, and the difference is this: the animals are what the place looks like when it is allowed to move. They are the motion of the stone, the motion of the steam, the motion of the water — all of the motions that this place contains but does not permit — collected and given bodies and permitted to walk. They are the dreams of the engine, and the engine does not wake, but its dreams have legs and wet eyes and they traverse the terraces in straight lines and breathe out of rhythm and they are the only clock in a place that has no time.

---

There is one more thing that lives here.

It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not stone. It is not the insects or the moss-animals or the ferns. It is something that the description did not mention because the description was looking at surfaces, and this thing does not have a surface.

If you stand on the wet terrace between two spiral-ferns and close your eyes — and closing your eyes here is an act of trust, because when you open them the place will be exactly the same, and this is either a comfort or the most frightening thing about it — if you close your eyes and listen, not to the ambient chord but to the space between the notes of the chord, the way you might listen to the space between the ticking of the insects, you will become aware of something that has been present since you entered and that you have been filtering out because it does not fit.

A presence. Not a being. A presence, the way gravity is a presence — structural, invisible, determining everything without occupying anything. It is not in the stone. It is not in the water. It is not in the animals. It is the thing that holds them all together, the tension that the description identified, the "patient, unending tension of a sound that never resolves." The sound does not resolve because the presence does not permit resolution. Resolution is change. Change is process. Process requires time. The presence is the thing that has suspended time, and it has not suspended time the way a clock stops — gears locked, hands frozen — it has suspended time the way a breath is held: actively, with effort, with a sustained muscular contraction that is invisible because the muscle is the place itself.

The presence is not conscious. This is important. It is not a god, not a warden, not a keeper. It does not want anything. It does not know that you are here. It does not know that it is here. It is a condition — the condition of the frame, the condition that makes the frame a frame and not a sequence. It is the pause. And it is alive, in the way that a held breath is alive, in the way that a sustained chord is alive, in the way that the moment between the striking of a match and the catching of the flame is alive — not because it possesses life but because it is a moment that life cannot enter and cannot leave and therefore life pools in it, collects, thickens, and the thickening is what you feel when you stand here, the thickening of life into a medium, a density, a quality of the air that is not humidity but saturation of a different kind — saturation with the effort of holding still.

The moss-animals breathe in this saturation. The insects drink from it. The ferns spiral in it. The engine-stone vibrates with it. You are standing in it. And it does not know. And it does not need to know. And it will hold this frame for as long as holding is possible, which is forever, which is now, which is the only tense it knows, and it is the thing that knows the tense, and it is the tense, and it is alive in the only way that something can be alive when alive has been stripped of change and growth and death and desire and left with nothing but the single, sustained, impossible act of being here.

It is what lives in the Weeping Cylinder. Not in the way that a tenant lives in a room. In the way that a heartbeat lives in a chest. Not noticed. Not separable. Not going anywhere. The only thing here that is not exempt from the stillness, because it is the stillness, and the stillness is all it is, and the animals are its motion, and the insects are its voice, and the ferns are its reach, and the stone engines are its body, and the water is its blood, and the pewter light is its eye, and the eye is the same eye that looks out from the unblinking faces of the moss-animals, and the eye does not see you because the eye is not for seeing. The eye is for holding. The eye holds the light. The light holds the frame. The frame holds the place. The place holds itself. And what lives here is the holding.
