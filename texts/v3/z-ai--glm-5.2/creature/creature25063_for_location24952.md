# What Lives in the Bloomworks

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here arrived. Everything here *grew*.

This is not a distinction without a difference. The insects that drift through the warm air in their slow, purposeful columns did not fly in through a crack in the root-throat above. They hatched here. They have always hatched here. Their life cycle — egg, larva, pupa, adult, death, dissolution, absorption — is threaded through the root-felt like thread through linen, and the felt itself is partly made of their accumulated exuviae, their discarded wings, the fine chitin dust of generations that lived and died in the space between two heartbeats of the bioluminescent bark. They are not visitors. They are sediment. They are the place breathing out and breathing in and breathing out again.

There are — you count, during a bright phase, standing still, letting your eyes adjust to the particular way light behaves here, how it catches edges but softens volumes — at least four distinct morphologies in the airborne layer. The most numerous are the ones you noticed first: small, pale, winged, with bodies no longer than a thumbnail and wings that are disproportionate, almost decorative, translucent and veined with something that catches the green-gold light so that the columns of them appear, at certain angles, as a faint shimmering curtain. They navigate by the light-pulse. During the bright phases they rise, dispersing through the upper air of the nave, and during the dim phases they sink, clustering along the walls where the residual warmth lingers in the bark. They are, you think, feeding — not on the bark itself but on the condensation film, on the dissolved sugars and minerals that the bark exhales, on the thin soup that coats every surface here. They drink the walls. They drink the air. They drink the moisture that beads on your jacket and your eyelashes and the backs of your hands, and when they pass close to your face — which they do, not landing, not biting, just passing, the displacement of their wings a small warm breath against your skin — you can see that their mouths are not mandibular. They are broad, flat, like the pad of a cat's tongue, and they are constantly working, constantly lapping at the air itself.

The second morphology is larger and less numerous. These are the ones that produce most of the tearing-paper sound, because their wings are different — not translucent but opaque, a dull cream color, and they are stiff, almost leathery, clicking against each other with each beat. They move faster. They move with intent. Where the first kind drifts, these hunt, and what they hunt is the third kind, which is smaller, darker, and moves in erratic, darting paths that suggest panic or at least a permanent state of high alert. The third kind stays low, below knee-height, skimming the root-felt, and when one is caught — and you see this happen once, quickly, a small dark shape folding into a small cream shape and then both falling to the felt where the cream one eats and the dark one stops — the sound is very small. A click. A compression. The predator folds its wings and eats where it landed, and then it rises again, and the column re-forms, and the static-sound continues as if nothing happened, because nothing did, because this is the ordinary texture of existence here, the way breathing is ordinary, the way the bark pulses.

The fourth kind you do not see clearly. You see evidence of it. During a bright phase you notice, on the wall nearest to you, a cluster of papery nodules attached to the bark at the joint where two root-masses meet — each nodule the size of a grape, arranged in a loose spiral, and inside each one, visible through the translucent paper, a dark shape curled tight. These are eggs, or pupae, or something analogous, and they are not the eggs of any of the three kinds you've observed. They are too large. The thing that laid them is something else, something you haven't seen, something that moves through this space at a scale or a time that you have not yet accessed.

---

The wall-walkers are not geckos.

You know this because one drops from the ceiling during a dim phase and lands on your forearm, and you hold still, and it holds still, and for perhaps ten seconds you and it regard each other in the darkness, and you can feel its feet — all fourteen of them — gripping the fabric of your sleeve with a precision that is not adhesive but mechanical, each foot a cluster of tiny hooked setae that engage with the texture of the material the way a burr engages with wool, and it is warm, warmer than the air, and its body is not scaled or furred but covered in a short, dense pile that feels like velvet, and it is breathing, you can feel that, a rapid shallow pulse through its abdomen, and then it moves, and the movement is so fast that by the time your eyes have tracked it, it is already on the wall and climbing and you are left with the impression of a body that was longer than it was wide, with a flexibility that suggested spinelessness, and a head — if it was a head — that was mostly mouth, a mouth that opens sideways, like a book, and inside, something that is not teeth but is not nothing either, a ridged surface, like a file, like the sole of a boot worn rough.

They work the bark. All of them — and there are dozens visible during the bright phases, dozens more heard during the dim — are engaged in the same activity: moving across the bioluminescent cambium, scraping it with those file-mouths, harvesting something. The bark where they have worked is duller, the glow reduced to a faint amber, but it recovers — you watch one patch over three light-cycles and see it brighten, slowly, the bioluminescence returning like blood to a pressed blush. The wall-walkers are not destroying the bark. They are pruning it. Removing the outermost layer, the oldest glow, and leaving the younger tissue beneath to brighten and grow. They are gardeners. Or they are the garden's own immune system, performing a function that the plant cannot perform for itself, a symbiosis so old that neither party remembers which one needed the other first.

They do not communicate with sound. They communicate with movement — you become certain of this after watching them long enough. When two meet on the wall, they pause, and there is a rapid exchange of postures, a dialogue of angles, bodies tilting and twisting in patterns that are too structured to be reflex and too quick to be contemplative. They are exchanging information. About food sources. About the location of the large fauna. About you, probably. You see one of them, high on the wall, orient its body toward you and hold a posture — head down, abdomen raised, all fourteen legs splayed — and within seconds, three others on nearby surfaces have adjusted their positions to face you as well. They are aware of you. They have been aware of you since you entered. They have simply decided, collectively, or individually, or through whatever decision-making process their species uses, that you are not relevant to their work.

When the light dims, they glow. Faintly, faintly — a greenish phosphorescence along their ventral surfaces, as if they have absorbed some of the bark's bioluminescence and are carrying it with them, or as if the same chemical process that makes the bark glow is present in their bodies as a byproduct of their diet. In the dark, they become a second constellation, a map of green points moving across the invisible surfaces of the chamber, and the map is beautiful, and the map is also a record of labor, because every point of light is a mouth at work, and the work never stops.

---

The large fauna — the dog-cow things, the ones that walk the floor with their heavy deliberate gait — are the ones you spend the most time watching, because they are the most legible, the most analogous to something you already understand, and because watching them eventually undoes that legibility entirely.

You see one clearly during a bright phase at close range — eight meters, maybe less — and what you see is this: a body roughly the size of a large wolf, low-slung, with a broad chest and legs that are thicker than they need to be, ending in feet — hooves? claws? — that are wide and flat and covered in the same velvet-pile as the wall-walkers, a padding that silences their footfall on the root-felt, or would silence it if the animals were lighter, but they are not light, they are dense, and each step compresses the felt with a sound that is not a hoof-strike but a muffled thud, like a fist pressed into a pillow. Their bodies are not furred. They are covered in a bark-like hide — rough, ridged, the color of dark walnut — and along the spine, a line of something that catches the light differently, something smoother, something that might be keratin or might be chitin or might be something that does not have a name because the material sciences that would name it have not been invented, because this material is the product of an evolutionary path that diverged from anything you know a long time ago, in a place that was not a forest and was not a factory but was both.

Their heads are the part that resists description. They are large — too large for the body, almost, with a blunt, wide skull that slopes down to a mouth that is, as you noted before, always shaped as if it is carrying something. The mouth is not a mouth in the conventional sense. It is a basket. A lattice of bony or chitinous struts that interlock to form a container, a cage, a receptacle, and inside the cage — in the one you see clearly — there is something. A mass of fibrous material, pale, damp, about the size of a grapefruit. The animal is carrying it. It has been carrying it, you think, for some time, because the material is compressed, shaped to the interior of the basket-mouth, worn into the shape of the cage that holds it.

You follow this one. Not closely — you keep ten meters between you — and it does not react to your presence, or it reacts only by adjusting its path slightly, widening the distance without changing its pace, a calibration so smooth it might be unconscious. It walks to one of the machines. The press, the one with the iron framework grown through with root, the one that moves in the floor-thrum rhythm. It stops. It lowers its head. It opens the basket-mouth, and the fibrous mass drops onto the root-felt, and then — and this is the part that makes you hold your breath — the animal begins to work it.

It uses its feet. It presses the mass flat against the felt, kneading it, folding it, pressing again. The motion is rhythmic and practiced and unmistakably *skilled*. This is not instinctive behavior. This is learned. This is a craft. The animal is processing the fibrous material — you realize, watching, that the material is bark, is cambium, is the same bioluminescent tissue that the wall-walkers harvest — and it is processing it into something, a flat sheet, a felt, a fabric of compressed bark-fiber that it then lifts, with a dexterity that its feet should not possess but clearly do, and carries — in its mouth, in the basket, the basket that is a tool — to the press.

The press accepts the sheet. The animal feeds it into a gap between the iron frame and the root-housing, and the press, in its slow piston-rhythm, closes on it, and the sound changes — a wetter sound, a denser compression — and when the press opens again, the sheet is gone. It has been pushed deeper, into the channel, into the system, into whatever the Bloomworks is making with all of this.

The animal turns away. It walks to the wall. It begins to strip bark, its basket-mouth scraping against the bioluminescent surface, and the bark comes away in long fibrous ribbons that it catches and holds and begins to compress with its feet even as it walks, even as it moves back toward the press, and the cycle — strip, compress, carry, feed, strip again — is a loop, a circuit, a program, and the animal is not following instinct, it is following a schedule, and the schedule is the schedule of the Bloomworks itself, the forty-second bright phase, the sixty-second dim phase, the piston-rhythm, the flow cycle, the whole vast integrated process that the animal is a part of, a moving part, a biological component in a machine that includes iron and root and water and light and bark and insect and the accumulated labor of creatures that have been doing this long enough that the doing has become what they are.

There are others. You see them now, during the bright phases, distributed through the nave, each one at its own station, each one working its own part of the process. One is at the generator, not feeding it but tending it — nudging a root-tendril back into contact with a copper coil, a maintenance gesture, a mechanic's adjustment performed with a blunt-barked head and velvet-padded feet. Another is at the far end of the nave, where the long low curve of the old river-channel bends out of sight, and it is doing something with water — standing in a shallow basin where condensation has collected, pressing its feet into the water, agitating it, and the water foams, and the foam is faintly luminescent, and the animal drinks it, and the drinking is not thirst, the drinking is sampling, is quality control, is a chemist's test performed with a palate that has been calibrated by generations of selection to detect the specific concentrations of sugar and mineral and whatever else the water is supposed to contain at this stage in the process.

They are not animals.

You understand this slowly, the way you understand all the important things in this place — not as a revelation but as a sediment, a slow accumulation of evidence that eventually outweighs the framework you brought in with you. They are not animals the way a worker on a factory floor is not a machine. They are living components. They are organs that move. They are the Bloomworks' hands and mouths and testing apparatus, and they have been shaped — by evolution, by domestication, by something that erases the difference between those two words — to perform functions that the plant cannot perform for itself and that the machinery cannot perform without them. The press needs someone to feed it. The bark needs someone to harvest it. The water needs someone to taste it. And the something that does all of this needs to be alive, because the system is alive, because the system is not a system but a body, and bodies need hands.

---

There is a fourth layer. You knew there would be. You were told, in the description of the place, that the food chain is long and not entirely visible. The large fauna are not the apex. They are the middle. Above them — or below them, or beside them, in a direction that the word "above" does not correctly describe — there is something else.

You find evidence before you find the thing itself.

The evidence is in the architecture. In the far section of the nave, past the last machine, past the last feeding-station, where the root-walls narrow and the ceiling drops and the space becomes a long low gallery that follows the old river-curve, the root-felt on the floor changes. It becomes smoother. More densely packed. The random swell and dip of the main nave gives way to a surface that is almost flat, almost finished, and the finish is not natural. It has been worked. Someone — something — has compressed and smoothed this surface to a quality that resembles poured concrete, and on this surface, in the brightest phase, you see marks.

They are not tracks. They are not prints. They are grooves, worn into the surface, a network of channels that follow a pattern you cannot immediately read. The grooves are shallow — a few millimeters — and they are smooth inside, polished, as if something heavy has been dragged along them repeatedly, for a long time. The pattern is not random. It is geometric. It includes straight lines, which the root-felt does not naturally form, and right angles, and circles, and the circles are concentric, and the concentric circles are connected by the straight lines, and the straight lines terminate at the right angles, and the whole thing is a diagram, or a circuit, or a map, or a text.

You follow the grooves. They lead deeper into the gallery. The ceiling drops further. The light dims — not in the cyclical way of the nave but progressively, steadily, as if the bioluminescent bark is thinner here, or older, or is being used for something else. The air changes. It is cooler. It is drier. The condensation film is gone. The insect-static is gone. The dripping is gone. The soundscape reduces to the floor-thrum, which is stronger here, more felt than heard, a vibration that rises through the smoothed root-felt and into your bones and your teeth and the fluid of your inner ear, and the sound it makes inside you is not a sound but a tone, a single sustained note that is the note of the Bloomworks' deepest engine, the thing at the center, the thing that everything else is built around.

The gallery opens.

Not into a chamber — into a *space*. The walls are gone. The ceiling is gone. The floor extends into darkness in every direction, and the darkness is not empty. It is structured. You can feel the structure — in the air, which moves in slow currents that follow a pattern; in the floor, which vibrates differently in different places, a topography of frequency; in the grooves, which continue, which widen, which deepen, which become channels, which become something like roads, which become something like veins, which become something you cannot name because the naming would require a vocabulary that does not exist yet.

And in the center of the space — you cannot see it but you know it is there, the way you know the floor is there, the way you know the ceiling was there — something breathes.

Not the mechanical breath of the press. Not the respiratory pulse of the bark. A breath. In and out. Slow. A cycle that takes minutes, not seconds. The air moves toward you and away from you and toward you again, and the breath carries a sound, and the sound is low, and the sound is complex, and the sound is — you listen, you hold still, you let the sound fill the space inside your skull where your own small thoughts usually are — the sound is layered. It contains the insect-static. It contains the dripping. It contains the floor-thrum. It contains the machinery. It contains the vocalizations of the large fauna. It contains all of it. All of it is here. All of it comes from here. All of it is one sound, one breath, one voice, and the voice is the voice of whatever lives at the center of the Bloomworks, and the voice is not speaking to you, and the voice is not speaking to anything, and the voice is simply the sound that a body makes when it is alive and working and very very large and has been alive and working for a very very long time.

You do not see it. You are not meant to see it. The darkness is not an absence of light but a membrane, a boundary, a polite but absolute separation between the body's exterior and its interior, between the parts you can visit and the part that the parts belong to. You stand at the edge and you feel the breath and you understand — not intellectually, not as a proposition, but in the way that your body understands warmth and gravity and the feeling of being watched — that the Bloomworks is not a place. The Bloomworks is a creature. The factory district was its seed-cradle. The root system is its body. The machines are its organs. The fauna are its cells. The water is its blood. The light is its metabolism. And you have been walking through it the way a microbe walks through a body — not unnoticed, not unwelcome, but irrelevant to the fundamental process, which is the process of being alive, which is the process of working, which is the process of making something, and the something it is making is — you feel it in the breath, in the tone, in the vibration — the something it is making is *more of itself*.

It is growing. It has been growing for decades, maybe centuries. It is growing deeper and wider and it is growing in the only direction that matters, which is the direction of complexity, which is the direction of integration, which is the direction of a system that began as a factory and a forest and is becoming, with the patience of wood and the persistence of machinery, something that has no name because nothing like it has existed before.

You turn back. The gallery is behind you. The nave is ahead. The light is brightening again. The insects are drifting. The wall-walkers are working. The large fauna are carrying their bark-loads in their basket-mouths to the presses and the channels. The machinery is humming. The water is moving. Everything is in motion. Everything is at work. Everything is alive.

And you walk back up the helical groove in the root-throat, and the wood is warm, and the condensation falls on you like rain, and the sound of the breath follows you all the way to the surface, and it does not stop when you emerge, because it has never stopped, because it will not stop, because the Bloomworks is not finished, and the Bloomworks is not finished, and the Bloomworks is not finished.
