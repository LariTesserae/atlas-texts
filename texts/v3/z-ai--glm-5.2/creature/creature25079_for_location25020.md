# The People of the Rift

They are not tall.

This is the first thing you notice and the last thing you understand. It is not malnutrition, not stunting — their children are robust, loud in the way children are loud everywhere, which is to say not very loud here at all. The adults are simply *compact*. Short-limbed, deep-chested, wide through the hands and feet. A body plan that fits the space. The gorge narrows in places, the paths are tight, the ceilings of the lower dwellings press low, and the people who live in those dwellings move through them without ducking because they were shaped by the same forces that shaped the stone. A generation is not long enough for evolution. But it is long enough for selection — for the simple brutal arithmetic of who thrives in a cold, wet, confined place and who does not. The first settlers were desperate people. Not all of them stayed. The ones who stayed became this.

Their skin runs dark. Not the dark of sun-exposure — they see little direct sun, the strip of sky above is narrow and often gray — but a duskiness that seems to come from within, as if the minerals in the weeping water have stained them over years the same way they've stained the stone. Iron, calcium, something else the geology hasn't told them the name of. The older women have it deepest, a ruddy-brown that in the lichen-light looks almost plum. The children are paler. It comes with time, the darkening. Like the moss on the walls. Like the mineral streaks. You are in a place where everything slowly colors.

---

They call themselves something with a click in it. You knew this already — the vine's name has a click, the settlement's name has a click, and when you listen more carefully you realize the language is built around them. Not one click but several: a lateral click made against the molars, a dental click off the front teeth, a palatal click that sounds like a hollow pop. They come in clusters. A word can carry three or four in succession, and the rhythm of speech — what you can hear of it, murmured and swallowed by the stone — has a percussive quality, as if people are quietly clapping at each other while they talk.

The clicks carry in the gorge. The voiced parts of words — the vowels, the resonants — are absorbed by wet stone, flattened, rendered indistinct. But the clicks *travel*. They bounce clean off the flat fracture-faces of the limestone and arrive sharp at a distance. You watch two women coordinate work on a high scaffold, one above the other, and they speak almost entirely in clicks — short bursts, call and response, the dental and the lateral alternating. It is not code. It is just the language doing what the gorge asks of it. A voiced shout would smear into the mist and the dripping. A click arrives.

The children learn it before they learn to walk, you think. You watch a toddler on a boardwalk path, bare feet gripping the slick wood with toes that splay wide — another adaptation, another selection — and the child makes a single dental click at its mother's back. The mother doesn't turn. She clicks back. Lateral. The child stops following. Something was said. You don't know what.

---

They came from above. This is not a story they tell you so much as a story the place tells. The raw pale stone of the switchback path has handholds carved into it at intervals — not deep, just the beginning of a groove, the first gesture of a stair — and the grooves are newer than the rock. The rock broke a generation ago. The grooves were made after. Someone climbed down into an open wound in the earth carrying what they could carry, and they found water and soil and vine and the faint glow of lichen on the walls, and they did not climb back out.

They don't talk about why. You ask — carefully, in the way a stranger asks — and the answers you get are practical. *The water is here. The vine grows. The walls keep the wind.* When you press, an older man — one of the settlement's elders, you think, though age is hard to read on faces weathered by mineral-damp — makes a gesture you don't fully understand. He touches the wall of his dwelling. The vine has grown into it so thoroughly that the wall is more vine than stone, more alive than mineral. He puts his palm flat against the intergrown surface and leaves it there. His hand is wet when he pulls it back. He looks at you. The look says: *this held us. We stayed because something held.*

You think you understand and then you think you don't.

---

They are not singular. This is important and you miss it at first because the density of the settlement and the uniformity of the work — everyone doing something, everyone damp, everyone moving through the same narrow paths — makes them seem like one thing, one organism, one response to one place. But they are not.

There are families. You learn this by the way dwellings connect. A lower-tier structure will have a narrow passage — not a door, just a gap in the vine-wall where it's been trained to leave an opening — leading to the dwelling above it on the middle tier, and that dwelling will have a ladder to a third on the upper scaffold. Three generations, vertically stacked. The oldest at the bottom, where the air is still and the mist sits and the warmth of bodies collects. The youngest at the top, where the strip of sky is visible and the black corvids land on the roof-beams and the air moves.

There are disagreements. You hear them — not the content but the texture. A clicking exchange that accelerates, the intervals shortening, the volume of the voiced portions rising just enough to carry emotion if not meaning. It happens between two men at a drainage channel where water has found a new path into a lower dwelling. One wants to redirect the channel. The other wants to seal the fracture. They are both right. The gorge does not care. They argue for ten minutes, voices low and tight, and then an older woman arrives from a nearby dwelling and says something — few clicks, mostly voiced, almost melodic — and they stop. She looks at the channel. She looks at the fracture. She points. They do something else — neither plan, a third thing — and the water is managed. You never learn what she said.

There is a person who is not like the others. You notice it on the second day. Taller — not much, but enough to stand out among the compact bodies. Thinner. Skin paler, not yet darkened by the mineral water, and the thought arrives unbidden: *new.* This person arrived recently. Came down the switchbacks the way you did, maybe months ago, maybe a year. They move through the settlement with the careful deliberate footwork of someone who has not yet learned where to step, and they carry water — everyone carries water — but they carry it with a concentration that the others don't need. The children watch them. Not unkindly. With the frank curiosity of children watching someone learn to be where they already are.

The newcomer wears something the others don't: a garment of woven fiber that seems too fine, too regular, too much like the work of a loom that doesn't exist here. It is getting damp. It is starting to come apart at the seams where the wet has loosened the thread. Soon it will be replaced with something the gorge makes — vine-fiber cloth, rough, durable, already growing in a frame on the wall of the dwelling the newcomer has been given. The gorge takes. The gorge provides. The gorge is not sentimental about either.

---

They keep records. Not written — or not written in a way you recognize. The vine walls of certain dwellings — the oldest, the first to be grown — are scarred with patterns. Notches, grooves, shallow cuts in the woody surface of the living vine, arranged in vertical rows. The vine has grown around them, swelling at the edges, healing over the cuts the way skin heals over a scratch, but the patterns remain, legible if you know the system. You don't. But you watch an old woman run her fingers along a row of notches and her mouth moves — counting, or reading, or both. When you gesture questioningly, she takes your hand and presses your fingers to the wall. You feel the notches. Some are deep, some shallow. Some are grouped. There is a rhythm. She makes a click — the palatal one, the hollow pop — and pulls your hand away. She is smiling. Or something close to smiling. The expression is brief, private, and not really for you.

The records are time. The seasons, perhaps. The births. The deaths. You realize there is a dwelling on the upper tier whose vine-wall is entirely covered in notches, hundreds of them, and no one lives in it. It stands empty, roof intact, walls intergrown, and the vine continues to grow around the notches, slowly absorbing them, and the people leave it alone. It is the archive. When someone dies, a notch is cut. When someone is born, a notch is cut. When the gorge does something new — opens a fracture, floods a path, sends the predator down from the rim — a notch is cut. The vine remembers what the people cannot hold. A generation is short. The vine is patient.

---

They are not religious, or if they are, the word doesn't apply. They don't seem to worship anything. They don't seem to fear anything — not the gorge, not the water, not the pale things in the pools, not the predator on the rim. They *account* for these things. The way you account for gravity, for the weight of your own body, for the fact that air goes in and out. The gorge is a condition. The water is a condition. The pale things are a condition. The predator is a condition. You work with conditions or you work against them, and working against them is not something these people do, because they have seen what working against a place looks like — they came from that, you think, from the place above where the wind scours and the ground is sparse and nothing grows because someone tried to make it be something it wasn't — and they chose to come down into the split earth and work with what was already here.

But there is something. At night, when the lichen glows and the settlement contracts into its dwellings, there is a sound. Low. Not quite singing. Not quite humming. A voiced drone that starts in one dwelling and is taken up by others until it comes from everywhere and nowhere, the stone walls carrying it, amplifying it in strange ways so that it seems to rise from the floor, from the water, from the air itself. It has no words. It has clicks — slow, spaced, rhythmic, as regular as breathing. The drone rises and falls. It goes on for a long time. Then it stops, and the dripping resumes, and the settlement sleeps.

You ask about it. A woman — the one who was checking the boardwalk joints, you think, though all the faces blur a little in the lichen-light — looks at you with an expression you can't read. She makes a gesture. Both hands, palms down, pressed flat against the stone she's sitting on. Fingers spread. She holds it. Then she lifts her hands and looks at the damp left on her palms.

*The stone is here,* the gesture says. Or: *we are on the stone.* Or: *the stone is under us and we are on it and the water is in it and we are in the water and the water is in us.* You are projecting. You know you are projecting. But the gesture is not for you. It is for her. It is the thing she does when she wants to say *this is where I am and I am here on purpose.*

---

They will be here in a hundred years. You know this the way you know a tree will be where it was planted. The vine will have grown. The notches will have multiplied. The language will have clicked and shifted and the gorge will have shaped it further, selected for the sounds that carry and against the sounds that don't, and the people will be shorter still, and darker, and their feet will be wider, and the paths will be deeper, and the dwellings will have layered three more tiers high against walls that have softened a little more, the sharp edges rounding, the fracture-faces blurring under moss and mineral deposit and the patient work of water doing what water does. The pale things in the pools will have multiplied or moved. The predator on the rim will be the great-grandchild of the one that is there now. The lichen will glow.

And someone will climb the switchbacks and emerge onto the surface and feel the wind and the openness and think: *too much. Too much sky.* And they will go back down. Because the gorge held. Because the vine grew. Because the water found every seam and the people found every path and the stone was here and they were on it and it was enough.

It was enough.
