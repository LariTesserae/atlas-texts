# What Lives Here

---

## The Fish (First, Because They Are First)

They do not remember the dry time. There is no reason they should — the dry time was not theirs. But the shape of the place remembers for them. They move through doorways because the doorways are there, and they move through them in the direction the doorways face, which is the direction the builders faced when they set the stones. The fish do not know this. They swim through the doors the way water flows through a channel cut by something that understood water, and the fact that they swim through doors rather than around walls is a kind of inherited memory that lives in the architecture and not in them.

They are not one population. They are at least three, possibly four, and the boundaries between them are not spatial but temporal — one group occupies the north wall in the morning and the south wall in the afternoon, and another group does the reverse, and they pass each other in the courtyard at midday in a slow exchange that looks like courtesy and is probably just temperature. The third group, smaller, paler, lives deeper in the wall structures, in the gaps between frond layers where the water is still and slightly warmer, and they almost never enter the courtyard. The fourth, if it exists — and the evidence is ambiguous — is nocturnal, or rather it responds to the dimming of surface light as a signal to move upward, and it is larger and more solitary and the other three give it space without appearing to notice they are doing so. You would only see the fourth if you were here at night, and even then you would see it as a shadow before you saw it as a fish, and by the time you understood what you were looking at it would have returned to whatever alcove it holds in the deeper wall.

What they do — all of them, all day, the thousand small mouths — is eat. Not hunt. Not graze. Eat. The walls produce a film, almost invisible, a biofilm of bacteria and algal cells and the fine particulate matter that settles out of the water column and adheres to every surface in the settlement the way dust adheres to surfaces above water. The film grows. The fish consume it. They do this with the pharyngeal teeth — a second set of jaws in the throat, modified for grinding, and what they are grinding is the film and whatever has colonized the film and whatever has settled on what has colonized the film, a layered ecosystem compressed to the thickness of a few cells that the fish reduce to nothing in a line, a stripe, a path, and then move on. By the time they return, the film has regrown. This has been happening for longer than the walls have been plant. The film was here before the plants. The film may have been here before the village. The fish were almost certainly here before the village. They were eating the film off the stones when the stones were dry and the film was a different kind of film, and when the water came they did not leave. They did not need to.

The sound — the full, textured, constant rasp — is ten thousand mouths eating the place that holds them, and the place does not mind, and the place grows back, and this is the oldest relationship here.

## The Grazers

They are not shrimp. They are closer to isopods but the term is wrong too; they are something that flattened itself over time, over generations, pressing itself against the surfaces it lived on until its body became a surface itself — thin, translucent, the outline of organs visible through the dorsal plate like shadows through frosted glass. They have too many legs because they need too many legs. Each pair operates independently, in a ripple that runs front to back, and they can grip the moss, the stone, the algal bark, the calcareous mortar, all of it, because each leg ends in a cluster of fine hooked setae that can find purchase on anything that is not perfectly smooth, and nothing here is perfectly smooth. They move in groups of six to twelve, though "groups" implies coordination and what they actually do is follow a chemical trail that the lead animal lays down and the others detect and adhere to, so that they move as a single drifting body that can fragment and reform around obstacles — a wall corner, a fish, a patch of bare stone — and reconverge on the other side without any apparent loss of composure.

They eat the moss. Not the whole moss — they eat the top layer, the living fronds, the part that breathes. They scrape it clean and the pale trails they leave are the underlayer, the rhizoids and the calcareous base, and this underlayer is white and exposed and looks wounded, and within minutes the moss has grown back over it, the fronds expanding in their slow breathing rhythm to cover the scrape, and by the time the grazers have circled back the surface is whole again. Whether the moss minds is a question that anthropomorphizes a plant, but the moss has been here and the grazers have been here and neither has eliminated the other, which means something. In ecological terms it means they have reached a balance. In the terms of this place, which are slower and less interested in terms, it means they are together in the way that things that share a surface are together — not symbiotic, not parasitic, just co-present, each one doing what it does on the same stone at the same time.

The fish follow the grazers. Not to eat them — the grazers are not food, they are too flat, too armored, too many legs. The fish follow because the grazers stir up the moss, and in the moss is the film, and in the film is the food, and the grazers expose what the fish cannot reach on their own. So the fish hover behind the grazing groups at a distance of a few centimeters, picking at the wake, and the grazers do not acknowledge the fish and the fish do not acknowledge the grazers, and this is also a relationship, though it is a relationship of indifference rather than cooperation.

When the grazers sleep — and they do sleep, pressing themselves into the gaps between cobblestones where the mortar has softened, going still, their legs folding flat against their bodies so they look like seeds or scales or nothing at all — the fish move through the courtyard in their full number and the sound of their eating fills the space completely and the moss is ungrazed for hours and the moss breathes without interruption and the rhythm of the place shifts, not faster or slower but fuller, and if you were here at that hour you would feel the difference as a kind of warmth that is not temperature.

## What Lives in the East and West Buildings

They are the same kind of thing. This is known because the pulse is the same — the same interval, the same pressure profile, the same faint displacement of water from the building openings. But they are not the same individual. The east one's pulse arrives perhaps half a second later than the west one's, and this half-second lag creates an interference pattern in the courtyard, a slow wave that builds and breaks and builds again every twenty or thirty seconds, felt in the chest rather than heard, a pressure that rises and falls and that the fish ignore completely and the grazers ignore completely and the moss, being moss, also ignores.

They are large. How large is impossible to determine without entering the buildings, and entering the buildings is something that the fish do not do and the grazers do not do and the water, which enters freely, does not report back. The plant growth has sealed the openings except for narrow gaps at the base of each doorway — gaps wide enough for water to pass through in either direction, driven by the pulse, but too narrow and too low for anything larger than the invertebrates to enter. The invertebrates do enter. They go in and they come out and they do not appear different. Whatever is in there does not eat them, or eats so few that the population is unaffected, or eats them and they are replaced by others who enter unknowing.

What can be inferred: the thing in each building has been there long enough for the plants to have grown around it and with it and to have shaped themselves into a chamber that accommodates its respiration. The pulse is not mechanical. It is not the flexing of a wall or the expansion of a root. It is biological. It has the quality of a heartbeat — not the sound of a heartbeat but the *pattern* of one, the inhale-hold-exhale-pause of an animal moving water through a body. The building is not a lung. The animal in the building is a lung, and the building has become its body, and the plant is the skin, and the stone is the skeleton, and this is the closest thing the settlement has to a resident.

The fish know it is there. They give the buildings a margin of two to three meters, which is wide for a fish that lives in walls and narrows and tight spaces. They do not feed on the building walls. They do not enter the doorways. They do not sleep in the shadow of the building. This is not fear — fish do not fear in the way that is useful to describe with the word "fear." It is something older, a routing, a knowledge in the body that says *not there, not there*, passed through the population by proximity and by the accumulated memory of what happens when you go where the pulse comes from. Nothing happens. That is what happens. The fish that go in do not come out. The fish that do not go in do not go in. This has been true for long enough that it is simply true.

If the thing in the buildings is a fish, it is a fish that grew and did not stop growing, that found the building and fit inside it and stayed, and the plants grew over it and with it and the building became its shell. If it is not a fish, it is something that was here when the water came, something that was already large and already slow and already breathing in the way that large slow things breathe, and the water did not drown it because the water does not drown things that are water, and it has been breathing here for however long the water has been here, which is long enough that no one remembers the dry time, which is long enough that the dry time may be a story and not a memory.

It does not come out. It does not need to come out. The water brings it what it needs — the particulate, the film, the small things that drift in through the doorway gaps in the current that its own pulse creates, drawing water in, pushing water out, drawing in, pushing out, a cycle so slow that it looks like stillness from the outside. The fish that enter the gap are not pulled in by force. They enter because the gap is there and the gap is the size of a gap they would enter, and inside the gap is the building and inside the building is the mouth, or the filter, or the net, or whatever it is that the thing uses to take what the water offers, which is everything that fits and nothing that doesn't.

It is not malevolent. It is not anything. It is alive and it is large and it is in there and it has been in there and it will be in there, and the building holds it and the plants seal it and the water feeds it and this is the arrangement and the arrangement works.

## The Moss

It breathes. This is not metaphor. The fronds — thousands per square centimeter, each one a filament finer than hair, translucent, dark green because there are so many of them packed so close that their individual color compounds into density — expand and contract. The expansion is not growth. Growth happens too, but more slowly, over days and weeks, the fronds lengthening, branching, the network thickening. The expansion that sets the tempo is something else. It is a hydraulic motion, water moving in and out of the cells in a rhythm driven by — what? Light. The shifting light on the courtyard floor. When the surface brightens, the fronds expand, the cells fill, the moss rises, the texture of the carpet deepens. When the surface dims, the fronds contract, the moss settles, the carpet flattens. The cycle is two minutes because the light cycle at this depth — the refraction bands sweeping overhead — is approximately two minutes, and the moss has synchronized to it, or the light has synchronized to the moss, or they have synchronized to each other, or synchronization is the wrong word for two things that have been doing the same thing at the same time for so long that the question of who followed whom is meaningless.

The moss is on everything. Not by choice — moss does not choose. But the spores are in the water and the water touches every surface and every surface that can hold the moss holds the moss, and what cannot hold it — bare sand, open water, the smooth tops of the flat courtyard stones before the moss colonized them — has been colonized anyway, because the moss builds its own substrate, laying down the calcareous deposit that fills the joints between stones and creates a surface on which more moss can grow, and this has been happening for so long that the courtyard stones are not stones anymore, they are moss-covered mineral platforms that happen to have stone cores, and the moss does not know this and does not care.

It is the slowest thing here and it is the foundation and everything moves at the speed it sets.

## The Nubbin

In the center, in the sediment held by the pale plant's pad, the knot grows.

It is not a plant. Or it is a plant. Or it is a plant becoming something that was not a plant, and the becoming is not finished and may not finish and this is the only genuinely uncertain thing in the settlement, the only point where the equilibrium has not yet resolved.

It has been there for weeks or months. It is small — the size of a walnut, maybe smaller, a dense cluster of tissue that is darker than the sediment around it and denser and slightly warm, though "warm" at this depth means a fraction of a degree and the measurement is not one that instruments would confirm but the fish confirm it, because the fish visit the pad. They do not eat from it — there is nothing to eat, the pad is smooth, the sediment is fine, the nubbin is not food. But they visit. They circle. They approach to within a centimeter and then withdraw, and they do this in a pattern that is not feeding and not schooling and not territorial. It is something else. It is the behavior of animals in the presence of a thing that is not yet decided.

What it will become: a flower. A fruit. A spore body. A new stem. A mouth. An eye. A nothing — a growth that grows and then stops and then dissolves back into the sediment, and the pad holds the empty space where it was and the fish stop visiting and the center of the courtyard is just a stone again, for a while, until the next thing.

Or it will become something that has not been here before. Something that the settlement does not have a shape for. Something that will require the channels to shift and the walls to rearrange and the equilibrium to break and reform around a new center, a new tempo, a new sound added to the grinding and the knocking and the pulse.

It is not waiting. It is growing. The distinction matters. Waiting implies a future that is already known. Growing implies a future that is being made, cell by cell, in the cool water, in the slow light, at the pace the moss sets, in the place that does not need anything but holds everything and has held it and will hold it until the thing in the center becomes what it is and the place responds, or doesn't, and the water keeps moving, and the fish keep eating, and the moss keeps breathing, and the sound goes on.
