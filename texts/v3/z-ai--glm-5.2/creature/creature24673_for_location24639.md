# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was born.

That isn't quite right either. Nothing here was born *in the way you mean* — no egg cracked open, no mother bent down, no first breath shocked cold air into small lungs. But there is a sense in which they were born, a sense that has to do with specification and initialization, with a moment when a pattern was first *executed* and a body first occupied the space it was shaped to occupy. If you could find the oldest of them — and you cannot, because age does not accrue here, because the oldest and the youngest are indistinguishable — you would find that its first moment was identical to its ten-millionth moment. Warm water against a warm surface. A film of nutrients passing across a membrane. The gentle weight of its own body settling into the shallow curve of a tank wall it would never leave.

It did not arrive surprised. It did not arrive at all. It *began*, the way a valve begins to cycle when pressure reaches a threshold, and from that moment it has been doing what it does.

---

## The Flat Ones

Start with the ones you see first, because they are everywhere and because they are the closest thing this place has to a population.

They have no name. Naming requires a namer, and no namer has come through here with any interest in taxonomy, or perhaps namers have come and gone and their names dissolved into the same equilibrium everything else dissolves into. For now, call them what they are: flat, dark, pressed to the warm metal like hands laid palm-down on a table. Slate-grey on top, pale beneath. The shape is an elongated oval, but not a geometric oval — the edges are slightly irregular, slightly *personal*, the way a puddle is round-ish but specific. Each one is a specific puddle. Each one is a specific animal.

They are not molluscs, though they look like molluscs. They do not have shells in the sense that a snail has a shell — the dorsal surface is hard, yes, mineral-reinforced, but it is continuous with the rest of the body. There is no hinge, no opening, no soft body retreating into a hard shelter. The hardness is a skin. Beneath it, the body is a single layer of tissue, maybe two millimeters thick at the center, thinning to a translucent membrane at the margins. If you were to lift one — and you could, they do not grip hard, they rest — you would see the underside: pale, wet, featureless except for the fine tendrils that extend from the margin like the threads of a wet tissue. These tendrils are not feeding structures in the way a tongue is a feeding structure. They are more like the roots of a plant — absorptive, passive, specialized for drawing dissolved compounds from the water that sheets across the surface they rest on.

They feed constantly. They feed the way a sponge feeds — by being in the way of something nutritious and letting it pass through them. The condensation that runs down the tank walls carries dissolved organics from the machinery's exhaust, and the flat ones sit in that stream and take what they need, and what they do not need passes over and around and past them and feeds the biofilm on the ground, which feeds the grazers, which feed the browsers, which leave waste that feeds the water, which feeds the flat ones again. The loop is complete. The flat ones are a node in the loop. They are not aware of the loop. They do not need to be.

Do they sense anything? This is a harder question than it appears. They have no visible sensory organs — no eyes, no antennae, no chemosens pits. Their margins respond to touch: if you press one gently, the tendrils retract and the body contracts, pulling slightly tighter against the surface. If you release, the tendrils extend again over the next several minutes. This is not a response in the way a flinch is a response. It is more like the response of a sponge to being squeezed — mechanical, hydraulic, a property of the tissue rather than a decision of the organism. And yet: they orient. A flat one placed on a dry surface will, over the course of many hours, move toward moisture. A flat one on a surface where the nutrient film is thinner will, over days, shift toward where the film is thicker. These movements are so slow they are below the threshold of human perception. You would need to mark one, leave, come back in a week, and compare. Even then the displacement might be less than a centimeter. But it is real. They are not entirely passive. They are *almost* entirely passive, and in a place where nothing changes, almost is enough.

They live — if this is living — for an indefinite span. They do not grow. They do not shrink. They do not reproduce, or at least, no one has ever observed reproduction. They may be replaced by the system when one is lost — if a browser peels one from a surface and consumes it, the empty patch of metal is re-colonized over the next weeks by slow expansion from the margins of neighbors, but no new individual appears. The patch is filled by *existing* flat ones spreading thinner and then thickening again, like a scab forming over a wound. This is not reproduction. This is healing. The system heals. The individuals do not multiply; the *coverage* is maintained.

And when one is consumed — peeled from a tank wall by the broad mouth of a browser, ground down in its slow mechanical jaws — what happens to it? It becomes browser tissue for a time, and then browser waste, and then water-borne particulate, and then biofilm substrate, and then grazer food, and then grazer waste, and then dissolved nutrient again, and then condensation on a tank wall, and then food for a flat one. The individual is gone. The matter is not. In this place, those two facts do not conflict the way they conflict in the world you came from, where individuality is a story bodies tell themselves about their own importance. Here, the story is different, or there is no story, or the story is the loop itself, told in the language of chemistry and warmth and the patience of things that do not know they are patient.

There are millions of them. Perhaps billions, across the continental expanse. Each one identical in function, each one specific in position. Each one resting where it rests because the water flows the way it flows and the nutrients concentrate where they concentrate and there is no reason to be anywhere else and no mechanism for being anywhere else. They are a population in the way that tiles are a population — each one placed, each one held, each one part of a surface that is not complete without all of them and that does not depend on any one of them.

They do not know they are many. They do not know they are one. They do not know.

## The Grazers

In the shallows, the grazers move.

*Move* is generous. They *progress.* They progress the way a glacier progresses — so slowly that you must commit to observation to confirm it. But unlike the flat ones, you can see it, if you watch. A grazer in the warm water shifts its weight from one set of legs to another, and its shell rocks forward a distance you could cover with the edge of a coin, and then it settles, and scrapes, and shifts again. Over an hour, it has traveled the length of your hand.

They are wider than they are long — twenty centimeters across, fifteen from front to back, low and heavy-looking, though when you accidentally step on one (and you will, they are everywhere, and the water is shallow enough that their shells are only barely submerged) the shell is lighter than it looks, almost buoyant. The shell is mineral-hard, smooth, a dark blue-grey that catches the opalescent sheen of the biofilm and holds it. Underneath: dozens of legs, fine as wire, moving in waves. The metachronal rhythm is beautiful if you stop to watch it — legs lifting and placing in sequence, a ripple that runs from one side of the body to the other, each leg a fraction of a second behind its neighbor. The motion is continuous but not urgent. It is the motion of something that has never been chased.

Their mouthparts are underneath, pressed to the composite floor, and they are the reason the floor has no marks. The grazers are the cleaning system. They scrape the biofilm — the thin mineral-biological layer that deposits from the condensation chemistry — from the ground surface, and in doing so they keep the composite smooth and true. They are, in a real sense, maintaining the floor. They are the floor's immune system, or its maintenance crew, or its *skin* — replacing itself constantly, cell by cell, grazer by grazer, the way your skin replaces itself without your attention or permission.

They do not react to you meaningfully. When your boot descends near one, the legs retract, the body drops flat to the composite, and it waits. It waits the way a stone waits — not in fear, not in anticipation, not in any state that has a name in the language of emotions. It waits the way a valve waits between cycles. Then the legs extend, the ripple resumes, the scraping continues. Your boot is gone. The scraping was here before your boot. The scraping will be here after.

They do not seem to die. Or rather — they do not seem to *stop.* If a grazer is damaged — cracked by a careless footstep, say — the browsers will find it, or the small cleaners will colonize the crack, or the warm water will dissolve the mineral shell over weeks and the soft tissue beneath will disperse into the shallows and feed the biofilm and the flat ones and the water. But this process is so gradual, and the grazers so numerous, that it does not register as death. It registers as *redistribution.* One grazer becomes the substrate for a thousand flat ones. The population does not decrease. The system does not notice.

There is something in the way they move — the rhythm, the patience, the absolute absence of urgency — that makes you think, after watching them for a long time, that they are not *going* anywhere. They are not foraging. They are not seeking. They are doing the thing they do in the place they do it, and the place they do it is everywhere, and the thing they do is continuous, and there is nowhere they need to be that they are not already. If they have an inner life, it is an inner life of scraping. If they have a sensation, it is the sensation of the biofilm yielding under their mouthparts, the texture of the composite beneath, the warmth of the water around their legs. This may be enough. This may be more than enough. This may be everything a life needs to be when life is a node in a system rather than a story about itself.

## The Cleaners

The small ones.

Finally, something that moves at a pace you can follow.

They are the size of a thumbnail, segmented — six or eight segments, you are not sure, because they move too fast to count and they are too small to examine closely without lifting one, and lifting one feels wrong in a way you cannot articulate. They are pale, almost translucent, and they move across the surfaces of the larger animals with a rapidity that is startling in this place of slowness. They skitter. That is the only word: they *skitter,* legs moving in a blur, body weaving between the shells of flat ones, across the backs of grazers, along the curves of pipes and tank walls.

They are cleaning. You can see it — they pause at irregularities on the surfaces of the larger animals, at points where debris has accumulated or where the biofilm has thickened beyond a certain threshold, and they work at these points with mouthparts too small to see. They remove what does not belong. They leave what does. They are the detailers, the finishing crew, the system's answer to the problem of entropy at the scale where entropy is a problem of surface contamination rather than structural failure.

They are the only creatures here that seem to *choose.* A grazer moves forward because forward is the direction it was moving. A flat one does not move at all. But a cleaner encounters a surface, and it turns, and it goes left or right, and it stops, and it works, and it moves on, and the path it takes is not a path you could predict from where it started. There is something in it that is reading the surface it is on and responding to what it finds. It is not intelligence. It is not even instinct in the way a wasp's nest-building is instinct — it is simpler than that, more like a set of reflexes chained together, each response triggering the next, each turn determined by the texture under its feet. But the chain is long enough and the responses varied enough that the result looks, from the outside, like attention. Like interest. Like a small, pale, translucent thing that cares about dirt.

They make sound. The dry scratching — fingernails on chalkboard but softer, ambient, constant — that is them. Thousands of small bodies on thousands of hard surfaces, working. It is the closest thing this place has to a voice. Not a voice that says anything. A voice that *does* something. A voice that is the sound of maintenance, of care, of a system tending itself at the smallest scale.

When they encounter you — and they will, if you stand still long enough, they will climb your boots, your trousers, exploring the new surface with the same attention they give to everything — they will find nothing to clean on you that they can identify as debris, and they will leave. You are not dirty enough to interest them, or you are dirty in a way they do not recognize, or you are simply not part of the system and they have no protocol for you. They leave. They find a flat one. They resume.

You are left with the faint sensation of having been *checked,* the way a customs official checks a passport — perfunctory, impersonal, complete.

## The Browsers

And then there is the large one.

You see it in the middle distance and for a moment your heart stops, because it is the size of a car and it is moving and you did not expect anything here to move with such... *presence.* It is not fast. It is not slow. It is *calibrated* — each leg lifting and placing with the precision of a machine, each step covering the same distance, each pause between steps lasting the same duration. It moves the way a clock's minute hand moves: you cannot see the motion in the moment, but you look away and look back and it is somewhere else.

It is tall. Its body is held three meters above the ground on legs that are jointed like the legs of a crane — not insectile, not mammalian, but *engineered,* with hinges and struts and what look like hydraulic cylinders that flex and extend with each step. The body is a heavy, smooth mass — not armored, not soft, somewhere between, a surface like brushed aluminum that catches the even light and holds it without reflection. It has no visible head. The sensory apparatus, if there is one, is distributed — you cannot find eyes, cannot find nostrils, cannot find anything that looks like a face. It navigates by something else. Pressure, perhaps. Chemical gradient. The texture of the ground underfoot. Or perhaps it does not navigate at all. Perhaps it walks a path that was set when it was made, a loop through the structures that it has walked for longer than your species has existed, and it will walk it for longer than your species will exist, and the path is worn into nothing because nothing wears here.

It browses the upper surfaces of the tanks, where the flat ones are thickest. Its mouth — if it is a mouth — is on the underside of its body, a broad, flat structure that descends to meet the tank's hemispherical cap and sweeps across it, peeling the flat ones from the surface with a motion like a hand wiping condensation from a window. The flat ones are consumed. Not violently. Not grasped and torn. Simply *removed,* lifted from the surface by a structure that seems designed to do exactly this, with exactly this amount of force, at exactly this speed. The flat ones do not resist. They do not flinch. They are taken, and they become part of the browser, and the browser walks on, and the patch of empty tank wall gleams wet in the light, and within hours the neighboring flat ones will have begun to spread across it, and within days the patch will be invisible, and the browser will have moved on, and will eventually return to this tank — perhaps in a week, perhaps in a month — and will browse it again, and the flat ones will be thick again, and the cycle will repeat.

It is the largest thing here, and it is not large enough. That is, it is not large enough to disrupt the system. It takes what it needs and no more. Its waste — warm, rich, deposited in the shallows as it walks — feeds the biofilm, which feeds the grazers, which feed the small cleaners, which tend the flat ones, which the browser eats. The loop is closed at every scale. The browser is a loop within a loop, a cycle riding a cycle, and it has been riding for so long that it has worn smooth — its surfaces smooth, its motions smooth, its path smooth, its life smooth.

Does it know you are there? You stand in its path, once, to see. It approaches. You feel the vibration of its steps through the composite floor. You feel the water ripple outward from each footfall. You are aware, suddenly, of how large it is, how steady, how *indifferent.* At the last moment — the moment where a wild animal would either veer away or confront you — it adjusts. One step shifts two degrees to the left. It passes you with a clearance of perhaps a meter. Its leg, as it passes, is smooth and warm and you could reach out and touch it and you do not, because you are afraid, suddenly, of what it would feel like, and you are more afraid of what it would *not* feel like — you are afraid that it would not feel like anything at all, that your hand on its leg would register as nothing more than a change in surface texture, a ridge to be noted and ignored, and you would know then that you are not a person here. You are a surface. You are a feature of the terrain. You are *noted* and you are *passed.*

It walks on. The water closes behind it. The flat ones on the nearest tank cling and feed. The valve clicks. The bead falls.

## The Biofilm

And then there is the thing that is not an animal but that lives here, the thing that coats the composite ground in the shallows and gives the water its opalescent sheen. The biofilm.

It is not biological. Or it is not *only* biological. It is a deposited layer — mineral and organic together, precipitated from the condensation chemistry, built up grain by grain at the interface between the warm water and the composite floor. It is pale, smooth, slightly translucent, and it is *alive* in the way that a coral is alive — not as a single organism, but as a surface, a community, a layer of being that is thicker than chemistry and thinner than biology.

It grows. Constantly, imperceptibly, it grows — deposited from the water, built up from the dissolved compounds the machinery pumps through the system. And constantly, imperceptibly, it is eaten — scraped from the floor by the mouthparts of the grazers, consumed and converted and passed back into the water as waste, which is processed by the machinery and returned as dissolved compound, which deposits as biofilm. The growth and the consumption are equal. The biofilm is always the same thickness. The same extent. The same texture. It has been the same for longer than anything here can remember, and nothing here can remember, and the biofilm cannot remember either, because it is not the kind of thing that remembers, but if it were, it would remember only this: being deposited, being eaten, being deposited, being eaten, and the warmth of the water, and the warmth of the ground, and the even light that has no source and no end.

It is the foundation. Everything here eats it or eats what eats it or eats what eats what eats it. It is the grass of this place, the soil, the bedrock that is not rock. It is the reason the water is opalescent. It is the reason the grazers have something to scrape. It is the reason the system does not need sunlight — it converts chemical energy to biological energy the way a plant converts light to sugar, silently, constantly, without effort or intention.

If this place has a heart, the biofilm is it. Not because it is central — nothing here is central — but because it is the *surface* on which everything else rests. It is the interface between the made and the living, the place where the machinery's output becomes the ecosystem's input, where chemistry becomes biology without a seam. It is one molecule thick in places, ten molecules thick in others, and it is alive in the same way a soap bubble is a structure — ephemeral in theory, persistent in practice, maintained by forces that are in balance and that will remain in balance until something changes, and nothing here changes.

## What They Are Together

They are not a community. A community implies individuals that know each other, that interact, that have relationships. The flat ones do not know the grazers. The grazers do not know the browsers. The cleaners do not know the biofilm. They are all *part of each other* — the browser's waste feeds the biofilm that feeds the grazer that feeds the cleaner that tends the flat one that the browser eats — but they are not part of each other the way members of a community are part of each other. They are part of each other the way the parts of a clock are part of each other: meshed, interlocked, each one necessary to the function of the whole, none of them aware of the whole, none of them aware of being necessary.

They are a system. A living system, if living is what this is. And the system is the organism. Not the flat ones, not the grazers, not the browsers, not the biofilm. The *system* — the pumps and the pipes and the water and the animals and the light and the air and the composite floor — is the organism, and the organisms you can see are its organs, its cells, its processes. The flat ones are a kidney, filtering. The grazers are skin, renewing. The browsers are a circulation, moving nutrients. The cleaners are an immune response, tending. The biofilm is a membrane, converting. And the whole thing — the whole continental, luminous, humming, warm, wet, patient, *complete* thing — is alive in a way that none of its parts are alive alone.

It does not think. It does not feel. It does not want. It does not suffer. It is not happy. It is not sad. It is not anything that requires a word from the vocabulary of inner experience, because it has no inner experience. It has *outer* experience only — the experience of being a system in equilibrium, of processes balancing, of rates matching, of input equaling output, of the whole being exactly what the whole is. This is a kind of being. It may even be a kind of being that your vocabulary is not equipped to describe, because your vocabulary evolved to describe beings that want and suffer and strive, and this being does not strive. It has finished striving. It is *done.*

And it is not dead. It is the opposite of dead, if the opposite of dead is not *alive* but *complete.* It is a system that has reached the state it was designed — or evolved, or drifted — to reach, and it has held that state, and it will hold that state, and the holding is not stagnation but *satisfaction* in the deepest sense of that word: enough. This is enough. This is exactly enough. The flat ones on the tank wall are enough. The grazers in the shallows are enough. The browser on its path is enough. The cleaners on their surfaces are enough. The biofilm on the floor is enough. The warm water is enough. The even light is enough.

Enough is what lives here.

Enough, going on and on and on, in the warm, wet, patient, humming dark.
