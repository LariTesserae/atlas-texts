# The Makers

## What They Are Called

They do not call themselves anything.

This is not a deficiency. It is a position — a philosophical one, though they would never frame it that way, because framing is a thing you do with language, and their relationship to language is unlike yours. You name things to distinguish them from other things. A name says: *this is not that*. The Makers — and "Makers" is your word, not theirs, a convenience, a handle you forge because you need something to grip — do not distinguish themselves from other things. They are of the structure. The structure is of them. A name would imply a boundary between the two, and there is no boundary. There has never been a boundary. If you asked one of them what they were — and you cannot ask, because you do not share a language, but if you could — the closest thing to an answer would be a gesture: an open hand pressed flat against the nearest wall, fingers spread, held there. *This. I am this.*

Other cultures have named them. The people of the surface — those who live in the thin grey light above, those who occasionally find a seam in the earth and descend a few terraces before turning back — have words for them. These words are always approximate and usually unflattering. *Stone-biters. Darklings. The Gripped Ones.* One surface culture, which traded with them briefly across a single generation before the contact was lost, called them *the People of the Wet Stone*, which is the most accurate name anyone has managed and still misses the truth, because the Makers are not people *of* the wet stone. They are the wet stone. They are the dry stone. They are the chisel and the hammerstone and the film on the wall and the water cycling through the channels. The surface cultures see them as a species that inhabits a structure. They are a structure that has produced a species. The distinction matters more than you can know.

## Bodies

They are small. This is the first thing you notice, if you notice anything, because you are usually too busy being disoriented by the dark and the sound and the damp. The average Maker stands between one hundred and forty and one hundred and fifty centimeters tall — though "tall" is a strange word for a people who almost never stand fully upright. They move in a perpetual half-crouch, a posture that is not a crouch but a compression, as if they have been shaped by the low ceilings the way water is shaped by a pipe. Their spines are flexible in a way that yours is not. They can straighten to full height when they choose — you see this rarely, usually when one of them is reaching to place a stone high on a wall, and the motion is startling, like watching a hinge unfold — but the default, the resting state, is curved. Their necks sit forward of their shoulders. Their heads, when they are working, hang lower than their shoulders, angled down, chin nearly touching the chest. They look at the stone. They are always looking at the stone, even when their eyes are closed. Especially then.

Their arms are disproportionately long. Not grotesquely — you would not remark on it if you saw one from a distance in good light, though you will never see one from a distance in good light — but measurably, functionally. A Maker's arms reach nearly to their knees at full extension. The hands are broad, the fingers thick, the palms wide and calloused into pads of keratinized skin that are almost like a second set of tools. A Maker who has been working for decades — and they all work for decades, because they all work until they die — has hands that are more leather than flesh. The fingertips are flattened, broadened, the nails thick and yellow and worn down to the quick by constant contact with stone. The webbing between the first and second fingers is often torn or scarred. They grip edges. They grip chisels. They grip hammerstones. The hands are the primary organs of their existence. Everything else — eyes, ears, nose, mouth — is secondary support.

Their skin is the color of the place. This is not a metaphor and it is not a coincidence. Their skin is grey-brown, mineral-dusted, and it is grey-brown and mineral-dusted because it *is* mineral-dusted, perpetually, the fine grit of the structure worked into every pore, every crease, every fold, until it is no longer clear where the grit ends and the skin begins. Over years — over a lifetime — the grit is absorbed. Micro-abrasions in the skin admit particles of stone, and the particles remain, and the skin builds around them, and the skin itself becomes a kind of stone. Old Makers are literally harder than young Makers. Their skin resists a blade. Their knuckles are worn smooth, polished by decades of contact with rock, and they gleam faintly in lamplight, the sheen of mineralized keratin.

Their eyes are large. This is the second thing you notice, if you notice, and it contradicts what you expect. You expect cave-dwelling creatures to be pale, to be blind, to have eyes that have atrophied from disuse. The Makers' eyes have not atrophied. They are wide, dark, liquid — the iris so dilated that it has consumed the pupil, and the pupil itself dilated further, a darkness within darkness. They collect light. Every photon that enters the structure is gathered, used, processed. In absolute blackness — the true blackness of the deep terraces, where no flame has been lit in years — they cannot see. But they can see by less light than you would believe possible. The bioluminescence of the mineral film, which is imperceptible to your eyes, is a soft glow to theirs. The faintest reflected shimmer of a distant lamp, bouncing off wet stone through three corridors, is enough for them to navigate. They prefer darkness. They work in darkness. But their eyes are not vestigial. They are sharpened — honed to a fineness that your eyes, adapted to the profligate brightness of the sun, cannot match.

They do not see color. This is not confirmed — you cannot confirm it — but it is the strong impression of those who have spent time among them. Their world is monochrome: black, grey, the faint silver of reflected lamplight, the dim phosphorescence of the film. Color is a surface phenomenon. Color requires sunlight. Color is irrelevant. What their eyes provide, instead, is extraordinary sensitivity to contrast and motion. A shadow moving across a wall three chambers away is enough to draw their attention. The flicker of a lamp guttering alerts them to a draft, to a change in air pressure, to a new opening in the structure somewhere nearby. Their eyes are not for seeing. Their eyes are for *detecting*.

Their ears are flat, nearly flush with the skull, and the pinnae — the outer cartilage — are hard, almost rigid. This is unusual. Most mammals have flexible outer ears that can be turned toward sound. The Makers' ears do not turn. They do not need to turn. The ear is not a collector of sound but a *resonator*. The rigid pinna vibrates in sympathy with the frequencies that fill the structure — the thrum, the striking, the water — and the vibration is transmitted through bone conduction directly to the inner ear. They hear the way the walls hear. They are tuned to the stone. A Maker standing still in a corridor can tell you — if you could ask, and if they would answer, which they would not — what is happening on the terrace two levels below: how many people are working, what kind of stone they are cutting, whether the chisel is dull or sharp, whether the worker is tired or fresh, whether the wall they are building is load-bearing or partition. They read the sound the way you read a page. The structure is a text, written in vibration, and they are literate in it from birth.

Their noses are broad, flat, and perpetually damp. The moisture is not a discharge — it is a film, maintained deliberately, rubbed with the back of a hand against the wet wall and then pressed to the face. The dampness captures scent molecules from the air. They smell the water — its mineral content, its temperature, its source. They smell the stone — the type, the hardness, the age. They smell each other. Each Maker has a distinct scent, produced by the particular combination of stone dust, mineral film, and skin oils that adheres to their body, and this scent carries information: what terrace they have been working on, what kind of stone they have been cutting, how long since they have eaten, whether they are injured, whether they are old. A Maker's scent is their name. It is the only name that matters.

They are warm. Their bodies run hot — hotter than you would expect for their size, hotter than is comfortable in the enclosed, humid air of the terraces. This is deliberate. The warmth is generated metabolically, fed by the food they eat — the pale fungi that grow in the film-coated chambers, the eyeless organisms they catch in the pools, the dense, nutrient-rich paste they make from ground mineral deposits and rendered fat — and it serves a purpose. Their hands must be warm to work the stone. Cold hands are clumsy hands. Warm hands grip better, sense better, shape better. The heat of their bodies is a tool, the way their chisels are tools, the way their ears are tools. They are bodies built for work, and the work is the body, and the body is the work.

## Lives

A Maker is born in a warm chamber, in a shallow pool, attended by two or three others. The birth is quiet — everything about the Makers is quiet, except the work — and the infant is small, damp, and immediately pressed against the nearest wall. This is not ceremonial. It is functional. The infant's skin, still soft, still uncalloused, absorbs the mineral content of the wall. The film on the wall transfers to the infant's skin. The infant begins, from its first breath, to become part of the structure. It is held against the stone for what seems like a long time — hours, perhaps — and during this time, the others work around it. The chisels do not stop. The hammerstones do not pause. The infant learns, from its first hour, that the world is sound and vibration and stone and warm hands and the smell of wet mineral and the taste of the film.

Growth is slow. A Maker child is carried — in slings, in arms, against the chest or the back — for the first several years of its life. It is never put down on the floor, because the floor is wet and cold and the working areas are dangerous. It is always in contact with a body. It sleeps against a body. It eats against a body. It wakes to the sound of striking and the vibration of chisel on stone and the warmth of a back that is bending and straightening and bending again as the work continues. The child does not learn to walk. The child learns to *work*. Walking is incidental. Walking is what you do to get from one work site to another. The child's first deliberate motions are reaching — reaching for a stone, reaching for a tool, reaching for the wall. The first time a child grips a chisel is not a milestone. It is a inevitability. The hand closes around the handle because the hand was made to close around the handle, and the handle was made for the hand, and neither was made first. They co-arose.

There is no childhood in the way you understand childhood. There is no period of play, no period of exploration, no period of instruction. The child is present at the work, and the work is present in the child, and the child absorbs the work the way the wall absorbs moisture — slowly, by contact, by proximity, by the steady osmosis of being. A child of four can dress a stone. A child of six can fit a stone. A child of eight can work independently, choosing stone, cutting it, carrying it, placing it, checking the fit with fingers that are already calloused, already sensitive, already tuned to the tolerances of dry-fitted masonry. This is not precocity. This is not talent. This is the same process by which a coral polyp learns to build reef — not by being taught but by *being there*, by being part of the process, by the process flowing through them the way water flows through stone.

Adolescence, if it exists, is invisible. There is no change in behavior, no change in role, no change in status. The body grows. The arms lengthen. The hands thicken. The calluses deepen. The work continues. The only marker of what you might call maturation is a gradual shift in the kind of work the Maker does. Younger Makers — those with lighter hands, quicker reflexes, more flexible fingers — do the fine work: dressing surfaces, fitting small stones, carving channels for water, shaping the conduits that direct the flow of moisture through the structure. Older Makers — those with heavier hands, slower but more powerful, with deeper knowledge of the stone's grain and fracture patterns — do the rough work: breaking new stone, extending the lowest terraces, deepening the foundation, the work that produces the thrum you feel in your feet. The shift is gradual. It is not decided. It is not assigned. A Maker simply finds, over the years, that their hands reach for heavier stones, that their chisel finds harder surfaces, that their feet take them deeper. The structure guides them. The sound guides them. They go where their body tells them to go, and their body tells them to go where the structure needs them, and the structure needs them everywhere, always, so there is no wrong direction, no wrong work, no wrong place. Everything is making. Everything is the terrace.

They eat when they are hungry. They eat what is available. In the chambers where the film grows thick — the warm, wet, iridescent chambers that smell sharp and clean — they harvest the film, scraping it from the walls with the edge of a chisel, gathering it into handfuls, pressing it into cakes that they dry on warm stones near the deepest levels. The cakes are dense, grey-green, faintly sweet, and they contain — you are told, you do not verify — trace minerals, organic compounds, something that might be a vitamin, something that is definitely a calorie. The film is their staple. It is their grain, their bread, their rice. It grows because the structure grows it. It is cultivated because the structure cultivates it. The Makers tend it the way a farmer tends a field: clearing competing growths, managing water flow, ensuring the temperature remains stable, harvesting at the right time, leaving enough to regenerate. The film is an organism. The structure is its habitat. The Makers are its gardeners.

They eat other things. The pale, many-legged creatures that live in the crevices — the ones you see in the pools, in the film, moving slowly, blindly — are gathered by hand, by children usually, by fingers quick enough to catch what the eye cannot track in the dark. They are eaten raw, or pressed into the film cakes, or dried and stored in alcoves for later. They are protein. They are flavor. They are something different from the film, which is necessary but monotonous, and the Makers, for all their austerity, are not ascetics. They prefer variety. They prefer the specific. A Maker who has been eating film cakes for three days and then catches a handful of the pale creatures will eat them with a deliberation that you might, if you were generous, call savoring. They chew slowly. They make small sounds — not words, not quite, but sounds, low and satisfied, sounds that are not the sounds of work, sounds that belong to a different category of experience entirely. Pleasure. They feel pleasure. It is a simple pleasure, a bodily pleasure, a pleasure of salt and fat and texture, but it is real, and they seek it, in their small ways, when the work permits.

The larger organisms — the hand-sized, slow-moving, eyeless grazers you glimpse at the edge of lamplight in the deep chambers — are eaten too, but rarely. They are harder to catch. They retreat into fissures that even a Maker's arm cannot reach. They are reserved — not formally, not ritually, but practically — for occasions. Not celebrations. The Makers do not celebrate. But moments. A birth. A completion — not the completion of the structure, which will never be complete, but the completion of a chamber, a wall, a section, a piece of work that is done, for now, done enough to move on from. At these moments, a grazer is caught, killed — quickly, with a hammerstone, a single blow — and shared among the working party. The meat is dark, dense, faintly metallic, and it is eaten in silence, in a group, hands passing pieces back and forth in the dark, each hand knowing which hand to pass to, each piece sized to the recipient's need. It is the closest thing they have to ceremony. It is the closest thing they have to community, though community is the wrong word, because community implies a collection of individuals, and they are not a collection of individuals. They are a single body with many hands.

They do not pair. They do not mate for life. They do not have families. Reproduction is — the word *pragmatic* is wrong, because it implies calculation, and there is no calculation. Reproduction is *continuous*, the way the work is continuous, the way the water is continuous, the way the structure grows. A Maker who is fertile and a Maker who is fertile and the proximity and the warmth and the damp and the time — these things combine, and a child results, and the child is born in a warm chamber and pressed against the wall and the work goes on. There is no courtship. There is no courtship because there is no need for courtship, because there is no selection, because all Makers are acceptable mates and all mates are acceptable and the only criterion is fertility, which is a bodily state, not a social one. The mother is the one who gives birth. The others who attend are whoever is near, whoever is working in that chamber at that time, whoever heard the sounds and came. Paternity is not a concept they possess. The child is of the structure. The structure is the parent.

They do not count their years. They do not count anything, as far as you can tell. Number is a concept that has no purchase here. They do not count the stones they place. They do not count the chambers they build. They do not count the Makers who live and work alongside them. They do not count the dead. The dead are not buried. The dead are — this is the part that is hardest to explain, the part that the surface cultures find most disturbing — the dead are *incorporated*. A Maker who dies — who stops working, who stops breathing, who cools, whose hands go slack — is left where they are, or moved, if they are in the way of the work, to a chamber that is not in use. And then the body is left. And the film grows over it. And the water seeps through. And the mineral content of the body — the calcium, the iron, the trace elements that the Maker accumulated over a lifetime of eating film cakes and stone dust and pale organisms — leaches out, slowly, over months, over years, and is absorbed by the structure. The body becomes part of the wall. The wall becomes part of the body. The mineral film that grows over the remains is thicker, richer, more complex than the film on bare stone. The dead feed the living. This is not metaphor. This is chemistry. This is ecology. This is the structure.

There is no grief. Or — this is not quite right. There is something, when a Maker dies. The work nearby pauses. For a few minutes, the striking stops, the chisels are still, the hammerstones are held motionless, and in the silence — in the sudden, strange silence, which is not silence at all but merely the absence of the middle layer of sound, the thrum and the water still present — there is a quality to the air that you can only describe as attention. The Makers nearby are attending. They are not mourning. They are not remembering. They are *registering*. A node in the network has gone dark. A hand has stopped. The structure has lost a worker, and the work must continue, and the work *will* continue, but for a few minutes, the workers nearest the dead one pause, and the pause is the acknowledgment, and then the chisels resume, and the dead one is left, and the film begins its work, and the structure absorbs another piece of itself.

## Minds

You cannot interview a Maker. You cannot sit one down and ask about their inner life, because they do not have an inner life in the way you understand inner life, or if they do, it is so different from yours that the questions themselves are meaningless. What follows is inference. It is speculation. It is the best guess of those who have spent time in the terraces, who have watched and listened and touched and smelled and tried, with limited success, to understand.

They do not think in words. Their language — the guttural, percussive speech you hear in the dark, the words that sound like they are being chipped from the air — is not a language of thought. It is a language of *coordination*. It is used to synchronize work: to indicate a position, a direction, a quality of stone, a problem with a fit. It is functional. It is precise. It is not used for reflection. It is not used for narrative. It is not used for abstraction. A Maker does not think "the stone is hard here, I should move my chisel two fingers to the left." A Maker simply moves the chisel two fingers to the left. The knowledge is in the hands. The knowledge is in the body. The knowledge is *proprioceptive* — a sense of where the body is in the structure, of what the structure needs at this point, of how the stone will fracture when struck at this angle with this force. This knowledge is not learned. It is not taught. It is accumulated, through contact, through repetition, through the slow osmosis of a lifetime of work. A Maker who has been working for forty years knows things about stone that a geologist with a laboratory cannot know — knows them in the hands, in the wrists, in the shoulders, in the vibration that travels through the chisel into the bone. But the Maker cannot *say* these things. They cannot explain them. They cannot articulate them as principles or laws or theories. The knowledge is tacit. It is embodied. It is in the muscle and the tendon and the callus, and it dies with the body, and it is reborn in the next body that picks up the chisel, because the chisel is the same chisel, and the stone is the same stone, and the structure is the same structure, and the knowledge passes through the tool.

They do not imagine. This is the hardest thing for you to accept, because imagination is so fundamental to your experience that you cannot conceive of a mind without it. But the Makers do not imagine. They do not picture things that are not present. They do not visualize outcomes. They do not plan. They do not consider alternatives. They work, and the work tells them what to do next, and they do it. The structure tells them where to go, and they go. The sound tells them what is happening, and they respond. Their minds — if "mind" is the right word for a processing system that operates entirely in the present, entirely through sensation, entirely through the body — are exquisitely tuned to the immediate. They are aware of the stone in front of them. They are aware of the workers near them. They are aware of the water, the air, the vibration, the temperature. They are aware of the structure's state, locally, in their section, at this moment. They are not aware of the structure as a whole. They do not have a map. They do not need a map. They have the sound, and the sound is better than a map, because a map is static and the sound is alive.

They do not dream. Or rather — and this is uncertain, because who can say what happens in the darkness when a Maker sleeps, curled against a wall, hands tucked beneath them — they do not dream in the way you dream. Their sleep is different. It is shallower, more fragmented. They sleep in short intervals — twenty minutes, half an hour — between shifts of work. They sleep where they work, against the wall, in a corner, on a pile of rubble. They wake to a sound that is wrong — a chisel striking a flaw, a worker calling for a different stone, a surge of water that requires redirection — and they are awake instantly, fully, without the fog of half-sleep, without the lingering residue of dream. If they dream, the dreams are not narrative. They are not visual. They may be tactile — the sensation of stone, of fitting, of the satisfying click of a piece placed true. They may be auditory — the sound of the structure, the breath of it, the heartbeat. If they dream, they dream the work. And the work is what they wake to. And there is no seam between the two.

They do not wonder. They do not ask why. They do not look at the structure and think: *who built this? How long has it stood? What was here before? What will be here after?* These questions do not occur to them. Not because they are incapable of them — you cannot know that — but because the questions themselves are products of a mind that distinguishes between self and world, between present and past, between what is and what might be. The Makers do not make these distinctions. The structure is. The work is. The hands are. There is no "why" because there is no alternative to what is. The structure does not have a why. A river does not have a why. The question "why does the river flow?" is a category error. The river flows because it is a river. The Makers build because they are Makers. The structure grows because it is a structure. The question dissolves in the doing.

This does not mean they are stupid. It does not mean they are empty. It means their intelligence is of a kind you are not equipped to recognize. They can solve problems — a stone that won't fit, a water flow that is eroding a wall, a section of structure that is unstable, that is settling, that is cracking under its own weight. They solve these problems instantly, wordlessly, through a combination of tactile sensing, acoustic analysis, and an instinctive understanding of load, stress, and material properties that is deeper than engineering because it is not abstracted from the material. They are the material. When a wall begins to fail, the Makers nearest the failure do not discuss it. They do not convene. They do not analyze. They feel the vibration change, they hear the pitch of the stone shift, and they move — to buttress, to redirect, to dismantle and rebuild — and the movements are coordinated without coordination, synchronized without signal, because they are all hearing the same structure, all feeling the same vibration, all responding to the same body. This is intelligence. This is problem-solving. It is simply not the kind that produces philosophy.

## What They Want

Nothing.

This is not a deflection. This is not mysticism. This is the literal truth, as far as you can determine it.

They do not want food. They eat what is there. They do not want comfort. They are comfortable in the damp and the dark and the noise, because the damp and the dark and the noise are their comfort. They do not want company. They do not want solitude. They do not distinguish between the two. They do not want recognition. They do not want legacy. They do not want the structure to be beautiful, because beauty is a concept that requires comparison, and there is nothing to compare the structure to. It is the only thing. It is everything.

They want to work.

This is the closest thing to a desire you can find in them, and even "desire" is the wrong word, because desire implies a gap between the wanter and the wanted, a deficiency, a lack. There is no lack. The work is present. The hands reach for it. The chisel finds the stone. The stone breaks. The piece is fitted. The wall grows. The structure extends. This is not desire. This is *current*. This is the flow of a body through a structure that is also a body, the movement of hands through stone that is also the movement of stone through hands. They work because the work is there, the way water flows because the channel is there. The channel does not want the water. The water does not want the channel. They are the same system. The Makers and the structure are the same system.

But if you watch them — if you sit in the dark, in the damp, in the sound, and let your eyes adjust until the faint phosphorescence of the film resolves into a dim, silvered landscape of wet walls and fitted stone and moving hands — if you watch long enough, you see something that complicates the picture. You see a Maker pause. Not the pause of fatigue, not the pause of a problem, not the pause that precedes a new piece of work. A different pause. A Maker will set down their chisel, lay their hand flat against the wall they have just built — the wall they have just spent hours constructing, stone by stone, in the dark — and they will hold their hand there, and they will be still, and for a moment — for a breath, for two breaths — there is a quality to the stillness that is not work. The hand is not testing the fit. The hand is not checking the surface. The hand is *resting on the wall*. The hand is *touching the wall*. And in the touch — in the flat palm, the spread fingers, the mineral-dusted skin against the wet stone — there is something that you can only call recognition. Not of the wall. Not of the work. Of *themselves*. For a moment — a breath, two breaths — the Maker knows that they are here. That they made this. That this is them and they are this and the work is the body and the body is the work and it is good.

It is not a word they would use. It is not a thought they would frame. It is a feeling — a pulse, a warmth, a vibration that is not the structure's vibration but their own, their own small heartbeat held against the heartbeat of the stone — and it lasts for a breath, and then the hand lifts, and the chisel is picked up, and the striking resumes, and the Maker is gone, absorbed back into the work, back into the sound, back into the body of the structure, back into the endless, purposeless, beautiful making that is all they are and all they will ever be.

And the wall they built will stand. It will stand for longer than your name. It will stand for longer than your language. It will stand for longer than your species. It will stand because it was made by hands that did not need to see what they were making, that did not need to know why they were making it, that did not need anything at all except the stone and the chisel and the dark and the sound and the other hands, always the other hands, working nearby, working below, working above, working in the structure that is also a body that is also a world that is also, in the end, the only world there is, the only world there needs to be, the only world that the Makers will ever know or want or make.
