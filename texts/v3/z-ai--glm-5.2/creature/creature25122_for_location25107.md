# What Lives Here

---

The frogs came first. Not first in time — first in your awareness. You hear them before you see them, those short percussive notes coming from the warm pools that collect in the basins, and when you finally crouch near one of those pools and hold still long enough, you see them. They are not large. A fist would cover one. Their bodies are the color of the wet basalt they sit on — dark, almost black, but with a greenish bloom across the back that shifts when the light catches it, the same iridescence you see on the water's surface when a bubble rises and pops. Their eyes are gold. Not amber, not yellow — gold, the specific gold of a metal that has been sitting in mineral deposits for decades, which is what these frogs have been doing, metabolically speaking. Their skin is wet, obviously, everything is wet here, but there is something else in the wetness — a faint slickness, an oil sheen, as if their bodies are producing something in response to the heat, or as if the heat itself has taught them to produce it. They do not startle when you move. One of them blinks, slowly, the nictitating membrane sliding across the gold eye like a curtain being drawn and then undrawn, and the look it gives you is not fear. It is assessment. It is the look of something that has been sitting in warm water for a very long time and has developed opinions about what does and does not belong in its field of vision. You belong in the category of *does not*, and it will sit with that classification until you give it reason to revise.

They call from the pools, never in chorus. One frog calls. Another, from a different pool, perhaps thirty feet away, answers. Then silence. Then a third, from somewhere you cannot pinpoint — the wet air makes sound behave strangely, softening the edges, making it hard to tell whether a call is near or far. There is no pattern to it that you can detect. It is not a conversation. It is not a territorial dispute, or if it is, it is one being conducted with extreme patience. Each frog calls, waits, listens, and calls again, and the intervals between calls are long enough that you stop expecting the next one and then it comes, a single low note dropped into the steaming air like a stone dropped into still water.

There are more of them than you think. As you move across the plateau, you realize that every warm pool has its frog, or its pair of frogs, and that the calls you hear are not a few individuals communicating across distance but a distributed population, each one occupying its own small thermal niche, its own shallow basin of heated water, calling out to whatever can hear. They have been doing this, you suspect, for longer than the machines have been here. The machines are recent to them. The machines are a passing thing.

---

The birds are harder. They move through the canopy in quick dark shapes, never holding still, never coming down to a height where you can see them clearly. You catch fragments — a flash of dark wing, a body smaller than your fist, a beak that catches the green-filtered light. They are not any bird you know. They are not trying to be. They live in the crown of the tree ferns, in the high umbrellas where the fronds spread and overlap and create a continuous canopy forty feet above the ground, and they have made that canopy their world. They do not need the ground. The ground, to them, is probably what the ocean floor is to us — a place that exists, that has things in it, but that is not where life happens.

You see one, once, clearly enough to describe. It lands on a frond stem above you, and for three seconds — no more — you get a look. It is dark, dark brown or black, hard to tell in the filtered light, with a chest that is marked with a pale streak, not white but the pale green of new fern growth. Its eyes are large, adapted to the dimness up there. Its feet grip the frond stem with a sureness that says *I was born on a stem like this and I will die on a stem like this and the ground is not for me.* Then it is gone, up into the canopy, and you hear it — or one of its kind — making a small chittering call that sounds like water running over pebbles, a fast liquid sound, nothing like the slow deliberate notes of the thing that calls from the escarpment.

The escarpment caller is different. You never see it. It calls from the edges, from where the plateau drops away into cloud forest, and its call is a single descending note, starting somewhere in the middle register and sliding down, down, down until it disappears below the level where your ears can track it. It sounds like a question. It sounds like something asked once, with genuine curiosity, by something that has been asking it for a very long time and has never received an answer and has not let that stop it from asking. It calls in the morning, and in the late afternoon, and sometimes — once — in the middle of the night, when you are lying on your bedroll on the only flat rock you could find, and the steam is moving around you in slow columns, and the call comes from the darkness at the plateau's edge and falls through the wet air and vanishes, and you lie there and the darkness closes back over the place where the sound was, and the chuffing of the nearest pump continues, and you sleep.

---

The mammal — you see it only once, and badly. A shape moving through the fern thicket at the base of a grove, low to the ground, dark-furred, moving with a fluidity that suggests something comfortable in its body, something that has no joints that catch on vegetation. It is the size of a large cat or a small dog, but it is neither. Its legs are short or its body is long — you cannot tell which, because you see it for less than two seconds before it slides into the ferns and is gone, and the fronds shake for a few seconds after, and then they are still, and you are left with an impression of smoothness, of darkness, of something that moves through this landscape the way a fish moves through water — not fighting the medium, not pushing through it, but occupying it, filling it, belonging to it so completely that the landscape seems to part for it.

You find its droppings later, near the base of a tree fern. They are dark, compact, and they contain seeds — small seeds, from the fruits of the epiphytic plants, you think. So it climbs. Whatever it is, it goes up into the canopy, or at least into the lower levels of it, and eats the fruits of the bromeliads and orchids, and comes back down to the ground to leave its droppings and, presumably, to drink from the pools. You find tracks near the same pools where you found the broad padded prints — but these are different, smaller, clawed. There is more than one species of mammal here. The plateau supports them. You do not know how many. You do not know if you will ever know.

---

The lizards — or the things that are like lizards — are the most confident inhabitants of the plateau. They live on the rocks near the hot springs, on the flat outcrops where the water seeps up warm and the air is thick with steam, and they do not move when you approach. You can walk up to within three feet of one and it will watch you with its dark eyes and it will not flinch. It is not brave. It is not tame. It is simply unafraid, in the way that island fauna are unafraid before they learn about predators, except this is not an island, this is a plateau, and the lesson that would teach them fear has apparently never arrived.

They are flat-bodied. Low to the rock, spread against it, their limbs splayed in a way that maximizes contact with the warm surface. They are the color of the rock — gray-brown, with mottling that shifts, slowly, over the minutes you watch, not quite matching the rock but approximating it, getting closer, the way a person learning a language gets closer to fluency without ever quite achieving native accent. Their eyes are dark and round and they do not blink. They watch you and you watch them and neither of you does anything and after a while you stop watching and they keep watching and they are still watching when you walk away and they will be watching when you come back and they will be watching long after you leave the plateau for the last time, because watching is what they do, it is possibly the only thing they do besides sitting on warm rocks, and they are very good at it.

There are insects that attend them — small, dark, flying things that land on the lizards' backs and walk along their spines, and the lizards do not react. Whether this is symbiosis or indifference you cannot tell. The insects may be cleaning the lizards' skin of parasites. The insects may be parasites. The lizards may not care either way. Everything on this plateau seems to exist in a state of mutual tolerance that you find difficult to categorize using the frameworks you brought with you.

---

The insects are a presence you feel more than observe. The bee-like things — they are not bees. They are longer, darker, their flight pattern different, a kind of sideways drift that bees do not do, as if they are swimming through the thick air rather than flying through it. They work the flowers on the vine-covered pump station, and they work the flowers of the epiphytes in the canopy, and you see them at the hot springs, landing on the wet rock and drinking from the film of water that sits there, and you realize they are drinking the minerals, not the water — they hover at the edge of the mineral deposits around the valves and pumps, where the seeping water has left a crust of calcium and sulfur and iron, and they feed on the crust, or on the microorganisms that grow on the crust, and their bodies carry the minerals back to whatever nest they maintain, and you wonder if the minerals build up in their bodies the way the patina builds up on the copper coils of the generator, and you wonder if old insects here are greener than young ones, and you do not get close enough to find out.

There are other insects. Small things that live in the standing water, skating across the surface of the warm pools, their legs dimpling the surface tension. Things that live in the leaf litter, in the layers of decomposing matter that cover the ground — you see them when you lift a piece of bark, and they scatter, and they are legion, and they are doing the work of decomposition, turning dead plant matter into the muck that new plants grow in, and they have been doing this work for as long as there has been dead plant matter on this plateau, which is to say, for as long as there has been plant matter on this plateau, which is to say, for a very long time.

And there is something in the water that glows. You notice it at night, in one of the basins near a working pump, where the geothermal heat keeps the water warm and the steam rises in slow columns and the light of the moon — when there is moonlight — catches the rising vapor. But there is another light, a different light, coming from the water itself. It is faint, blue-green, and it moves. Not uniformly — in patches, in slow swirls, as if something in the water is bioluminescent and is being stirred by the convection currents, the warm water rising and the cooler water sinking and the glow riding the boundary between them. You kneel at the edge of the pool and look in and the glow is below the surface, several inches down, and it is not one thing. It is many things. Microorganisms, you think. Thermophilic something-or-others, organisms that have adapted to live in water that is nearly hot enough to scald, organisms that have found a niche in the exact temperature range where the earth's heat meets the plateau's water and produces conditions that exist nowhere else on this plateau, and that have been glowing, quietly, for whatever length of time it takes for a microorganism to evolve a trait that serves no apparent purpose except to produce light in a place where no one is looking.

Except that you are looking. And maybe the thing that leaves the broad padded tracks is looking too, when it comes to drink in the night.

---

The thing that leaves the tracks. You have been avoiding it. Not physically — you have not been avoiding the place where you found the tracks, you have been back there twice. You have been avoiding *thinking* about it, because the tracks bother you in a way that the frogs and the birds and the lizards do not. They bother you because they are large. The frog is contained — it lives in its pool, it calls from its pool, it is of its pool. The birds are contained in their canopy. The lizards are contained on their rocks. Even the mammal you saw is contained — it moves through the fern thickets, it eats fruit, it is a certain size, it occupies a certain niche. The tracks are different. The tracks suggest something that is not contained by any single part of the plateau, something that moves across the whole of it, something heavy enough to leave deep impressions in the mud, something with broad padded feet that come here to drink and then go... where? Back into the fern groves? Along the escarpment? Into the cloud forest below?

You find the tracks in three places, over the course of your time here. Always near water — near the basins, near the seeping rock, near the outflow of one of the working pumps, where the exhaust steam condenses and runs down the iron housing and collects in a small muddy pool. The tracks are always fresh, or relatively fresh — the mud is still wet around their edges, which means they were made within hours of your finding them, which means the thing was here within hours of you being here, which means you are sharing this plateau with something that you keep almost seeing and never quite see.

The stride is long. Whatever it is, it moves with purpose. The tracks are not wandering. They go from one water source to another in a straight line, and the straight line suggests a path that has been walked before, many times, enough times to wear a faint trail through the leaf litter between the basins. You find that trail — a narrow depression in the ground cover, the plants slightly lower, slightly more compacted, as if something heavy has been walking this exact route for years. Decades. Longer.

You follow the trail one morning, through the fern grove, past the split generator housing, around a thicket of broad-leafed shrubs, to the edge of the escarpment, where the ground drops away into cloud forest and the air suddenly thins and cools and the view opens up — miles of cloud, the forest below appearing and disappearing in the moving vapor, the escarpment wall dropping thousands of feet, and the trail goes down. Not straight down — it switchbacks, it follows a route that must have been scouted with care, a path down the cliff face that is navigable if you know where to step. You do not go down. You stand at the top and you listen and you hear, from somewhere below, in the cloud forest, a sound — not a call, not a growl, but a breathing, a deep slow breathing, something the size of a large animal drawing air and releasing it, and you cannot tell if it is an animal or if it is the cloud forest itself, the wind moving through the trees at a pace that sounds like respiration, and you stand there and the sound continues and you do not go down and eventually you turn back.

You do not know what it is. You know what its tracks look like. You know where it drinks. You know the path it takes. You do not know what it looks like, what it eats, how many of them there are, whether it is one animal walking this route alone or several animals sharing a trail, whether it is something that has always lived here or something that came up from the cloud forest and found the plateau to its liking and stayed. You do not know if it knows you are here. You suspect it does. The plateau is not large enough, and the air is too wet, and the ground is too soft, and anything that has been walking this trail for long enough would know the smell of a new thing, a foreign thing, something that does not belong to the closed loop of heat and water and green.

You do not think it is dangerous. You do not think it is not dangerous. You think it is something that has been here longer than you and will be here after you leave and that the question of danger is the wrong question, the question a thing asks when it does not understand the place it is in. The plateau does not deal in danger. The plateau deals in persistence, in the slow accumulation of presence, in the gradual settlement of things into the places where they fit. The thing with the broad padded feet has settled into this place the way the pumps have settled into it, the way the ferns have settled into it, the way the frogs have settled into their warm pools — not by conquering, not by dominating, but by staying, by being here long enough that the boundary between *here* and *self* begins to dissolve, and what is left is something that is of the plateau in the same way the rock is of the plateau, which is to say, completely, which is to say, without remainder.

---

There are other things. There must be. The ecosystem is too complete, the loops too closed, the energy flowing through this place too obviously *circulating* for it to be supported by the handful of species you have seen. There are things in the canopy you have not identified, things in the water you have not sampled, things in the leaf litter you have not turned over. There are relationships here you have not observed — predator and prey, pollinator and flower, decomposer and dead — that are operating on schedules and at scales that your brief presence on the plateau does not give you access to. You are a tourist. You are passing through. The plateau's full population is not available to tourists, and it should not be, because the plateau did not invite you and is not performing for you and will continue to do what it does whether you are watching or not.

But you watch. And what you see, watching, is a place where the usual categories have softened. Where machines are becoming geology. Where animals are becoming part of the thermal system, their lives shaped by the same heat that drives the pumps. Where the boundary between living and non-living is not a line but a gradient, and on that gradient, things arrange themselves not by kingdom or phylum but by *how long they have been here*, and the things that have been here longest — the rock, the water, the heat — are the things that everything else is slowly, slowly moving toward, becoming more like, settling into, and the plateau is patient, and the plateau is not in a hurry, and the plateau will wait.
