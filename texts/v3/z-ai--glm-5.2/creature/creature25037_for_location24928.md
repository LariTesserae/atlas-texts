# What Lives in Keldhorn

## The People

They are not who you expect. That is the first thing.

You expect — if you expect anything — the remnants. The leftovers. People who stayed because they had nowhere else to go, or because they failed to leave, or because the place defeated them and they sank into it the way a cart sinks, slowly, still visible as a shape under the green. You expect erosion. You expect the human equivalent of the rounded mountains.

What you find is something else.

There are forty of them, or near enough. The number is soft because the boundary between *living here* and *being here* is soft, and because some of them come and go in patterns that take years, walking down the pass to the lowlands for reasons they do not articulate well, staying away for a season or a decade, returning with the same quiet step, the same damp coat, as though they had never left, as though leaving were a kind of long blink. There is no welcome when they return. There is no farewell when they go. There is only the slow redistribution of bodies in the buildings, the way moss redistributes itself after you lift your foot.

They are not old. That is the second surprise. Or rather — some of them are old, but the oldness is not what defines them. There is a woman who might be sixty or might be seventy who lives in a building on the street closest to the industrial quarter, and she moves through the moss with a sureness that makes your own walking look like stumbling. There is a man who might be thirty who does most of the harvesting, reaching into the deep moss with long iron tools that have handles worn to the shape of his specific grip. There are two children. Or there were. You see them once, at a distance, pale shapes among the ferns, and they are gone before you can be certain you saw them, and you think of the pale moving thing between the fronds, and you wonder if there is a connection, and you let the thought dissolve because there is no way to hold it here.

What defines them is not age. What defines them is *fit*. They fit this place the way the moss fits the stone, not by adapting to it but by having been in it long enough that the boundary has blurred. Their skin is damp. Not sweating — damp, the way everything here is damp, a fine continuous moisture that sits on the surface of them the way it sits on the surface of the slate roofs. Their hands are rough in a way that is not calluses but texture, a deep grain that comes from years of handling wet stone and wet fiber and wet iron. Their voices are low. Not hushed — low, as though they have learned that the air here absorbs higher frequencies, that the moisture in it dampens sound the way it dampens light, and they have adjusted. When they speak to each other you hear consonants more than vowels, the hard parts of words, as though the vowels are reserved for the wind and the water and the people handle only the structural elements of language.

They speak a language that is not the language of the lowlands. You can hear the lowland language in it the way you can see the cobblestones under the moss — the foundation is there, the structure, but the surface is something else, something that has grown over centuries of isolation and adaptation. Their words for the moss are precise. There is a word for the dry top layer, a word for the wet middle layer, a word for the black preserved layer at the bottom, and a word for the interface between the living moss and the dead peat that is — you are told, by the man with the iron tools, in a voice so low you feel it in your sternum — *the most important word*, because that is where the life happens, where the dead becomes the living, where the system turns over. He says this word and then he is quiet for a long time, and you understand that the word is not a label but a location, a place in the stratigraphy of the ground that he visits daily, that he knows the way you might know a room in your own house.

They have seventeen words for wind. You learn this from the old woman, who corrects you when you use the general word. She holds her hand up, palm out, and the moisture condenses on her skin, and she says a word that means the southeast wind that carries the heaviest condensation, the one that makes the moss swell. Then she turns her hand and says another word that means the same wind but in the season when the ferns are producing new fronds, which is a different wind, she insists, because what the wind *does* is different even if where it comes *from* is the same. Then she says a third word, and a fourth, and you lose the distinctions almost immediately because the distinctions are not meteorological but ecological, not about the wind itself but about what the wind touches and how what it touches responds, and you would need to live here for decades to begin to hear the differences.

They do not have a word for *weather*. You realize this slowly. They have words for states of the moss, words for the behavior of the streams, words for the sound of the river, words for the vibration of the machines. But they do not have a word for weather because weather implies a change in conditions, and conditions here do not change. The moisture is. The wind is. The cloud is. These are not conditions. They are the substance of the place, and the people live inside them the way fish live inside water, and fish do not have a word for wet.

## The Machines

This is the question you cannot resolve, and you are not sure you are meant to: do the machines live here?

They are older than the people. This is certain. The people do not know who built them. They know how to use them — which valves to adjust when the compressed fiber needs to be denser, which lever to throw when the grinder's output is too coarse — but they do not know the principles of their construction, do not know the mind that designed the first spiral, the first counterweight, the first float that rises with the water and opens a valve that feeds a channel that turns a wheel. The knowledge of the machines is *operative*, not theoretical. The people know what the machines do, not what the machines are.

And the machines keep doing.

The water comes in. This is the beginning and the end of it. The condensation forms on every surface of the plateau — every roof, every wall, every stone, every frond — and it is collected by a network of channels and pipes so extensive that it is less a system and more a *geometry*, a pattern imposed on the movement of water that is so old and so complete that the water does not flow through it so much as inhabit it. The pipes are iron. They are rusted on the outside, but the rust has become a kind of seal, a layer of oxidation so thick and so stable that it is itself waterproof, and inside the pipes are smooth, almost polished, worn to a bright dark finish by centuries of water moving through them at exactly the same rate.

The water enters the machines and the machines use it and the water leaves. What happens in between is — you stand inside the industrial building, the one with the spiral mechanisms, and you watch. The water enters a stone channel cut into a block of bedrock the size of a table. The channel is not straight. It curves, and the curve is not decorative. It is *precise*. It follows a line that you recognize, after a time, as a logarithmic spiral, the same spiral that a fern frond makes as it unfurls, the same spiral that the moss makes as it grows outward from a center. The water moves through this spiral and it turns a stone wheel that is also a spiral, and the wheel turns a shaft, and the shaft turns another spiral, and at the end of the chain there is a press that comes down with a slow, steady force that compresses a mat of fiber — moss fiber, you realize, fiber from the deep layers of the moss, processed and dried and fed into the machine by hands that know exactly how much to feed and when — and the press comes down and the fiber compresses and the water squeezes out and the water flows away through another channel and the press rises and the fiber is there, a dense block, and the machine moves on to the next cycle.

The cycle takes — you count, because there is nothing else to do — forty-three seconds. The press descends. The press holds. The press rises. The fiber is removed by a mechanism that is neither hand nor tool but something between, a lever that knows when to move because the water tells it to move, because the water is always telling everything to move, and the fiber block slides to the side and another mat of fiber is fed in and the press descends. Forty-three seconds. This has been happening for — how long? The blocks of compressed fiber in the corner are stacked to the ceiling. They are old. The ones at the bottom of the stack have been there so long that they have fossilized, have become a kind of dense mineral, have taken on the color of the stone around them. The ones at the top are recent. The machine does not distinguish.

The machines produce. This is what they do. They produce, and they do not stop, and no one tells them to stop, and the water does not stop, and so they produce. The products accumulate. The people use what they need. The rest is stored, and the storage fills, and the buildings fill, and the older storage fossilizes, and the fossilized material becomes part of the stone, and the stone becomes part of the moss, and the moss becomes part of the machines, in a cycle that is so large and so slow that the machines themselves are inside it, are being slowly incorporated into the landscape the way the town is being incorporated, the way the mountains were incorporated into the plateau, everything becoming everything else over a timescale that makes the word *machine* seem like a temporary name for something that is becoming something else.

You feel the vibration in your feet. You feel it in your chest. You stand in the industrial building and the machines turn and the water flows and the spirals spiral and the press descends and rises and descends and the vibration is the vibration of something that does not know it is alive and does not know it is not alive and the distinction, here, does not seem to matter.

## The Moss

The moss lives here the way the ocean lives in the ocean. Not as a resident. As the medium.

Thirty species, you are told, though you could not count them. They layer. The top is dry — relatively dry, which is to say damp in the way a towel is damp after it has been wrung, not dry in any way you would call dry. The top is a pale green, almost yellow in the dim light, and it is crisp underfoot, slightly, a slight resistance before your boot sinks through to the layer below. The middle layer is wet. Deeply, darkly wet. It is the color of evergreen shadow, and it is spongy in a way that is alive, that pushes back against your foot, that resists and then yields and then closes behind you. The bottom layer is black. It is peat. It is not alive in the way the moss is alive, but it is not dead in the way stone is dead. It is preserved. It is the moss's own past, its own memory, held in a form that does not decay because there is not enough oxygen and not enough warmth and not enough time for the things that cause decay to do their work.

The moss breathes. You learn this on the second day. The old woman takes you to a place where the moss is particularly thick — a shallow depression between two buildings where the ground has been stable for centuries — and she puts her hand flat on the surface and is quiet, and after a time you put your hand flat on the surface and you are quiet, and you feel it. A swelling. A contraction. A swelling. Very slow. Not a pulse — a tide. The moss swells as the condensation reaches it, the fibers absorbing water, expanding, the whole mass of it rising by perhaps a centimeter, and then it contracts as the water drains through to the deeper layers, and the mass settles, and then it swells again. The cycle takes — you are not sure. Minutes. An hour. The old woman has a word for it, and when she says the word she closes her eyes, and you understand that she is feeling the cycle with her hand, that she knows where in the swelling the moss is the way a sailor knows where in the tide the water is, by feel, by the weight of it.

The moss is not passive. This is what you understand, slowly, standing with your hand on its surface, feeling it move. It is not a covering. It is not a growth. It is a *process*. It is the thing that happens when water and stone and time reach a certain density of interaction. The moss is what the plateau is doing. The moss is the plateau's verb.

## The Pale Thing

You see it three times.

The first time is between the fern fronds at the edge of town, a movement that is not flight and not crawling but something between. It is pale. It is small — the size of your hand, perhaps, or smaller, or larger — you cannot tell because you cannot focus on it, because it does not hold still in your vision the way a solid thing holds still. It is there and then it is not there and then it is there again in a different place, and between the two places there is no path you can see, no trajectory, just absence and then presence and then absence.

The second time is inside a building. You are sleeping, or trying to sleep, in the grey-dark of the Keldhorn night, and the room is the marine grey-green that all rooms are here, and you open your eyes and there is a pale shape on the wall, near the ceiling, and it is moving, and it is the same movement, the not-flight not-crawling, and it is there for perhaps three seconds and then it is not there, and the wall is just a wall, mossy and damp and old.

The third time you see it clearly. Or more clearly. It is in the moss, at the interface between the living layer and the peat, in the place that the man with the iron tools called the most important place. It has emerged from the moss — or it was always there and you have simply looked at the right moment — and it is pale, translucent, and it has a shape that is not a shape you can name. It is not an insect. It is not a small animal. It is not a fungus, though it has the wetness of a fungus, the moisture of a thing that has never been dry. It has limbs, or extensions, or processes — you do not have the right word — that move in the ferns and in the moss, and it moves through the vegetation the way a fish moves through water, not pushing through it but inhabiting it, the medium parting for it and closing behind it.

You do not ask about it. You consider asking, and then you do not, because you have been here long enough to understand that asking is not the mode of this place. Instead you watch, and the pale thing moves, and after a time it is gone, and you are left with the feeling that you have seen something that is not rare but is simply not often noticed, something that lives here at the scale of the moss and the patience of the stone, something that has been here as long as the machines have been running and as long as the moss has been growing and as long as the people have been quiet.

## The Fish

In the black water of the river, below the steps where the water sheets down, there are fish. You see them from the edge of the channel, looking down six meters to the water, and they are dark and small and they have no eyes. They move slowly. They move with the current, not against it, and they move in the way that things move when they have been in the dark for so long that darkness is not a condition but a home. They feed on what washes down from the moss — organic matter, fragments of fiber, the dust of the peat — and they are the only animals you see in Keldhorn that are larger than your thumbnail.

The people leave them alone. You ask about this — the only question you ask directly — and the man with the iron tools looks at you with an expression that is not confusion but something close to it, as though you have asked why people do not eat stones, why people do not breathe mud, and he says, in the low voice, in the language that is mostly consonants: they are in the river. And the way he says *river* tells you that the river is not a resource. The river is a *place*, a place with its own inhabitants and its own purposes, and the fish belong to it, and the people belong to the town, and the machines belong to the buildings, and the moss belongs to the plateau, and these are not categories of ownership but of *location*, of where things live, and you do not eat what lives in a place that is not yours.

## The Fungus

The fungus lives in the deep moss. It is the thing the people harvest most carefully, the thing that requires the long iron tools, the thing that the man with the worn handles reaches for each morning. It is pale — paler than the pale flowers, paler even than the pale moving thing — and it grows in threads, in networks, in structures that are not visible from the surface but that the man can feel with the tip of his iron tool, the way a doctor feels for a vein. He pushes the tool into the moss and the moss swallows it, and he moves it slowly, and his face is concentrated in a way that you recognize as attention of the deepest kind, the kind that uses the hand as a sense organ, and then he pulls the tool up and there is fungus on the end of it, a pale thread of it, wet and fine, and he puts it in a basket that is woven from moss fiber and moves to the next spot.

The fungus is food. It is dried on the slate roofs, on the south-facing slopes where the moss does not grow, and it darkens as it dries, becoming a deep brown that is almost the color of the peat, and it is eaten in small quantities, a supplement to the peat and the ferns, a thing that has a flavor you cannot place — mineral and cold and faintly sweet, like the memory of a fruit that grew in a place that no longer exists.

But the fungus is also more than food. You learn this on the fourth day, when the old woman takes you to a building that you have not entered, a building at the very center of town, smaller than the others, with a door that is not iron-banded but plain, and inside the building there is nothing except moss and fungus. The fungus grows on the walls, on the floor, on the ceiling. It is the same pale thread that the man harvests from the deep moss, but here it is everywhere, a network, a web, and the old woman puts her hand on it and closes her eyes and is quiet for a long time, and you understand — not told, not explained, simply understand — that the fungus is how the people know things. That the network in the moss extends under the entire plateau, that the threads connect at the interface between the living and the dead, that they carry — not information, not in the way your nerves carry information, but something — signals, changes, the swelling and contraction of the moss, the behavior of the water, the vibration of the machines. That the people who live here are connected to the place through the fungus the way the place is connected to itself through the moss. That this is how they know when to harvest and where to step and when to leave and when to come back. That this is how the old woman knows seventeen kinds of wind. That this is how the man with the iron tools knows where the fungus is without seeing it.

You put your hand on the wall, on the fungus, and you feel nothing. Or you feel the damp, and the cold, and the faint vibration of the machines, and the swells and contractions of the moss, but these are things you feel with your body, not with the fungus, and the old woman watches you and there is something in her face that might be pity and might be patience and might be the expression of someone who is waiting for you to learn something that cannot be taught.

## What Lives Here

What lives in Keldhorn is not a collection of organisms. It is not a population, not a community, not an ecosystem in the sense that you learned the word. What lives here is a *system* — and the word is wrong, because *system* implies design, implies components arranged in service of a function, and this is not that. This is something that has been happening so long that the happening is the identity. The moss and the water and the stone and the machines and the people and the fungus and the fish and the pale thing and the peat and the cloud and the wind — these are not parts. They are *phases*. They are the same thing in different states, the way ice and water and steam are the same thing, and the thing that they are is Keldhorn, and Keldhorn has been living here for longer than living should mean, and it will continue to live here in the way that a river continues to flow, not because it chooses to but because the water keeps coming and the channel is there and the motion is the only state it knows.

The people live here. They are the part you understand best, because they are human, because you are human, because you can look at the old woman and the man with the iron tools and the children-if-they-were-children and see something that is recognizable, that is kin, that is the thing you came here looking for. But they are not the whole of what lives here. They are the conscious phase, if consciousness is the right word for a state of being that is this quiet, this embedded, this unseparated from the medium it lives in. They are the part of Keldhorn that can say *I*, that can distinguish between self and moss, that can harvest and eat and speak and remain silent. But the boundary between them and the rest is — soft. The fungus grows on their walls. The moss grows on their floors. The machines vibrate in their bones. The water moves through their pipes and through their bodies and through the moss and through the stone and the people are one phase of its movement, one place it pauses on its way from the cloud to the river to the steps to the valley below.

What lives in Keldhorn is Keldhorn. And Keldhorn is still happening.
