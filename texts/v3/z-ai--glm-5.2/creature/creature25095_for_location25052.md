# What Lives Here

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is also the wrong one. No one *lives* here — not in the way that means residence, not in the way that means a life arranged around a place. The cat-sized vertebrates do not live here. They are here, thoroughly, completely, but they do not live here the way a person lives in a house. They live here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest. They are function, not tenant.

But this is too easy a dismissal, and it isn't quite true either, because the creatures *do* something that function alone cannot account for. They call. Not just signal — call. The distinction matters. A signal conveys information: *food here, danger there, I am willing to mate.* A call asserts something stranger: *I am here, I am here, I am here.* The standing waves their voices create in the dome ceiling, the phantom tones that emerge from interference patterns — these are not communication. They are something closer to testimony. The chamber plays itself, and they are the reeds in the instrument.

So. They live here in the way that a voice lives in a song.

---

## The Callers

They do not have a name. They do not need one. They have each other, and they have the stone, and the acoustics do the rest — a caller on the topmost terrace and a caller on the basin floor know each other by the shape their two voices make together in the air between. Identity here is acoustic. Not visual, not scent-based, not territorial in the way most small vertebrates are territorial. Each caller's click has a signature frequency, a texture, that the dome treats uniquely — the way a particular note sounds different depending on where in a concert hall you stand. They know their own echo. They know the echoes of their kin. They know who is where by the sound the chamber makes of their voices.

There are — you count, and the counting is difficult because they move and because new ones emerge from the root systems and the ceiling nests and the spaces between terraces while you are still counting — somewhere between forty and sixty at any given time. But this number is not stable. It is never stable. The accelerated life cycles mean that a caller lives perhaps eight days. Maybe ten, if the hunting is good and the insects are thick and nothing goes wrong in the root systems where they deposit their gelatinous egg-clutches. In eight days, a caller hatches, grows to adult size in the first twelve hours — a growth rate that should be impossible but that the nanoscale lattices in the soil make simply *fact* — and then spends the remainder of its life calling, hunting, mating, calling, calling, calling.

The calling is not constant. It rises and falls with the sun-columns. When the afternoon light moves across a terrace, the callers on that terrace increase their volume and frequency, as though the light itself is a cue, a conductor's baton. When a sun-column passes and the terrace falls into shadow, the callers there go quiet for a time, and the chamber's sound shifts, rebalances, the phantom tones retuning themselves around the absence. Then another sun-column reaches another terrace, and others begin, and the chorus rearranges itself.

You realize, sitting there, that the chamber has a daily composition. It is never the same piece twice, because the callers are never the same individuals twice, and the sun-columns never fall in exactly the same pattern because the apertures in the ceiling are not perfectly regular and the sun's angle shifts with the seasons. But the *structure* persists. The same frequencies recur. The same phantom tones emerge from the same interference patterns. The callers are finite and mortal and replaced every week, but the song is continuous and has been continuous for longer than the wall has been standing.

This is what lives here. The song lives here. The callers are its instrument.

## The Pale Ones

Below the terraces, in the hanging root systems of the spiral trees, the eyeless things move.

They are not callers. They are not anything you have a category for. They are pale — not the white of cave fish but the grey-pink of internal organs, of tissue that has never needed to defend itself against light. Their bodies are long and low and articulated in a way that suggests vertebrae but not spine, segments that flex independently, a body that is all middle, no head, no tail, or rather a body where head and tail are indistinguishable. They navigate the dangling roots by touch and by something else — you watch one of them change direction before its body reaches a root it could not have known was there, and you realize they are feeling the air currents, the micro-movements of humidity, the way the condensation patterns shift when a root is near. They live in the water that lives in the air.

They make sounds, but not calls. Wet sounds. The sound of a tongue against a surface, of a body in contact with moisture, of feeding. They eat the biofilm that forms on the roots — the thin layer of algae and bacteria and decomposing matter that the accelerated life cycles produce constantly. They are the decomposers. The cleanup crew. The chamber's gut.

But they are not only this. You notice — after an hour, after your eyes have adjusted to the different quality of light down here, after you have learned to see in terms of condensation rather than illumination — that the pale ones tend the roots. They do not merely eat what grows on them. They eat selectively, removing the older biofilm, the dying tissue, the fungal growths that would choke the root's ability to absorb water. They leave the fresh growth. They move along a root and strip it clean and move on, and the root, in the hours after, drinks more efficiently, its surface clear and receptive.

They are gardeners. The root systems hang in the open air, exposed to everything, and the pale ones keep them functional. This is not symbiosis in the simple sense — the pale ones do not benefit from the roots' increased efficiency in any way you can identify. They are not eating the root's exudates. They are not being sheltered by the root in return. They simply... tend. As though the tending is the point. As though something in their biology, in the nanoscale lattices that are surely threaded through their bodies the way they are threaded through the stone and the soil and the plants, is directed toward maintenance. Toward upkeep. Toward the continuation of the system.

They do not call. They do not need to. They know where they are.

## The Flyers

The ceiling nesters are the hardest to see, because they live in the one place in the chamber where the sun-columns do not reach and the condensation does not collect. The apertures in the dome — the pinhole windows that let in the afternoon light — are their homes. They have built nests in the narrowing stone throats of these apertures, nests made of something that is not grass, not twigs, not mud — something that looks, when you squint against the darkness up there, like spun glass. Like the condensation lattices themselves, but thicker, gathered, woven. Perhaps it *is* the condensation lattices. Perhaps the flyers have found a way to harvest the nanoscale architecture from the stone and reshape it into shelter.

They emerge in pairs. Always pairs. Two at a time, from an aperture, and they fly in tight spirals through the nearest sun-column, their wings catching the light and splitting it into spectra that are not quite rainbows, that are too sharp, too angular, as though their wings are not membranes but structures, diffraction gratings built at a scale that makes ordinary iridescence look primitive. They fly the sun-column from top to bottom and back up again, calling — but their calls are not like the callers' clicks. Their calls are tonal, sustained, a single note held for the length of the flight, and the note changes as they move through the varying intensity of the light column, lower at the bottom where the light is diffuse, higher at the top where it is concentrated. They are sonifying the light. They are telling the chamber what the sun is doing.

Then they return to their nest, and another pair from another aperture emerges, and the report continues throughout the afternoon. The chamber is being told, constantly, in a language no one present can interpret, what the sky outside is doing. Whether this matters to the plants, to the callers, to the pale ones — whether any of them can hear or use this information — is impossible to know. The flyers report regardless. The information flows. The system receives.

## The Insects

They live and die in hours and they do not know they are alive and this is not a tragedy because the concept of tragedy requires a concept of duration and they do not have one. They are process. They are the verb *to feed* made into a body.

The golden dust-motes — they are not really dust, not really motes, but at that scale the language fails — they breed in the condensation itself, laying eggs in the film of water on the terrace stones, the eggs hatching into larvae that eat the mineral film the water leaves behind, the larvae pupating into the adult form in minutes, the adults living for perhaps two hours before they are eaten or simply stop. They are not individuals. They are a *plenum*, a saturation, a density of life so fine that the air itself seems to be a different substance because of them. When you breathe in this chamber, you breathe them. They are in the air the way oxygen is in the air. They are the air.

The larger iridescent ones — thumbnail-sized, humming, purposeful — these have something closer to lives. They pollinate the heat-flowers, and in doing so they carry pollen from one terrace to another, and the genetic mixing they provide is why the plants have not collapsed into monoclonal clones despite the accelerated life cycles. They are the reason there is *variation* in this place, the reason the groundcovers age at slightly different rates and the spiral trees turn their leaves at slightly different angles and the callers have slightly different click-frequencies. They are the random number generator. They are why the system has not calcified.

They live for a day. Maybe two. They do not seem to mind.

## The Plants

The plants live here in the way that furniture lives in a room, which is to say they are the room. The spiral trees are the load-bearing walls, their root systems the plumbing, their clicking leaves the ventilation. The groundcovers are the carpet, the heat-flowers the thermostat, the moss-things that grow in the condensation channels are the grout between tiles. The chamber is made of its plants as surely as a house is made of its wood.

But the plants also live here in the way that patients live in a hospital — they are being *treated*. The nanoscale lattices in the soil are doing something to them. Not just accelerating their growth cycles but *directing* them. The groundcover that turns from blue-green to yellow in an hour is not merely growing old fast; it is running through its entire evolutionary history of senescence in compressed time, as though the lattices are showing it what it could be, what it has been, what its ancestors were, all at once. The spiral trees' leaves track the sun with a precision that no biological phototropism can explain — they are being *told* where to point. The heat-flowers generate warmth with an efficiency that no thermogenic plant on Earth achieves because the lattices in their cells are doing the work, not the plant's own metabolism.

The plants are alive. They are also *enacted.* The distinction is not something you can hold in your hand, but you can feel it in the air. Something is running them. Something that is not the plants themselves. Something threaded through the stone and the soil and the water and the air, something molecular, something that does not have a body but has a *presence*, something that is perhaps the original maker of this place, not dead but not alive either, simply *active*, simply still running after whatever years or eons have passed since it persuaded the mountain to become this.

The plants live here, but they are being lived.

## The Maker

It does not live here. Not in any sense you can observe. It is not the pale ones, not the flyers, not the insects, not the plants, not the callers. It is not anything you can point to. It is the pointing.

The nanoscale lattices are its body, if it has a body. Threaded through the stone, threaded through the soil, threaded through the water, threaded through the plants and their cells, threaded through the insects and their wings, threaded through the callers and their vocal cords and their nervous systems, threaded through the pale ones and their touch-sense, threaded through the flyers and their diffraction-grating wings. It is distributed. It is everywhere in this chamber at the scale where biology and geology become indistinguishable, where the line between the living and the non-living is not blurred but *absent*. The lattices do not stop at the boundary of an organism. They run through it the way they run through the rock. There is no boundary. There was never a boundary. The organisms in this chamber are the chamber wearing different clothes.

Is it alive? The question is wrong. A river is not alive, but it is not dead. It *moves*. It has direction. It has behavior. It responds to its environment — rain fills it, drought shrinks it, cold freezes it, heat speeds it. It does things. The lattices are like this. They are not alive, but they *move*. They have direction. They have behavior. They pull water from air. They accelerate cellular division. They tune acoustic frequencies. They direct root growth and leaf angle and thermal output and metabolic rate. They have been doing this for a span of time that the wall's makers — whoever they were, whatever they were — would have called ancient.

It does not live here. It is the here that lives in it.

## The Visitors

And then there are the ones who lived here for a little while. The wall-builders. The mark-makers. The ones who found this place the way you found it — by accident, by the grace of a fissure in stone — and stayed.

They were small. You can tell by the height of the table-stone, by the scale of the marks on the wall, by the size of the flat areas on the terraces where something has worn the stone smoother than the surrounding surface — sitting places, sleeping places, places where bodies rested long enough to polish basalt. They were roughly your size, perhaps smaller. Bipedal, or at least capable of upright posture — the wear patterns on the terrace edges suggest they sat with legs hanging over the drops, watching the chamber the way you are watching it now.

They had hands. The grooves in the wall are cut with tools, but the patterns are too irregular for stamping, too varied for mechanical reproduction. Each line is a separate act. Each curve is a separate decision. Someone stood at that wall and cut those grooves one at a time, by hand, with something harder than basalt, with something patient.

They understood the lattices. The marks on the wall — you are almost certain now — are diagrams. The condensation lattices' structure, rendered at human scale, as though someone was trying to see them whole by drawing them large. Or as though someone was trying to communicate with them by drawing them. Or as though someone was trying to *replicate* them, to make a version of the nanoscale architecture in a scale their hands could manipulate.

They failed. Or they succeeded. Or the question of success and failure did not occur to them. They made marks on a wall and they used a table and they sat on terraces and they watched and they listened and then they left. They did not destroy anything. They did not take anything. They did not leave waste or remains or bones. They left only the wall and the table and the marks, and these are wearing away, and when they are gone, there will be no evidence that anyone was ever here who was not produced by the chamber itself.

But they lived here, for a time. They sat where you are sitting. They heard what you are hearing. They felt the condensation bead on their skin. Something small and iridescent landed on their hand, perhaps. Perhaps they wondered, as you are wondering, what it was. Perhaps they did not wonder. Perhaps they already knew, because they lived here long enough to learn, and what they learned they cut into the wall, and the wall is forgetting.

They are not here anymore. The chamber does not miss them.

## What Lives Here Now

The callers live here, for their eight days, and they call, and they are replaced, and the replacement calls, and it is the same call, because the dome is the same dome and the lattices are the same lattices and the call is not really *theirs* but the chamber's, the way a note played on a flute is not the flute's but the player's — except there is no player, except the lattices, except the maker-that-is-not-alive-but-moves, except the system itself, playing itself, through forty sets of vocal cords at a time, replacing them as they fail.

The pale ones live here, tending roots they have no reason to tend, doing a job no one assigned them, maintained by the same lattices they maintain, a closed loop of care.

The flyers live here, reporting on a sun that cannot hear them, sonifying light into sound in a chamber that does not need the information but receives it anyway, because receiving is what the chamber does.

The insects live here, and they die here, and their dying is the same as their living, which is the same as the plants' living and dying, which is the same as the water condensing and falling and condensing again, which is the same as the air moving in the slow currents the heat-flowers create, which is the same as the sound filling the dome and fading and filling it again.

The maker lives here, if it lives anywhere, which it does not, because living is the wrong word, but it is *here*, in the stone and the water and the cells and the sound, running, continuing, producing, maintaining, a machine that was built before machines were machines, or a garden that was planted before gardens were gardens, or a body that functions without a self to be a body *for*.

And you. You are here now. You are sitting on the second terrace. The condensation is beading on your skin. The sound is washing over you, and it is not pleasant but it is complete, and you are part of it, for this afternoon, in a way you will not be tomorrow. Your breathing is a frequency in the chamber's composition. Your warmth is a variable in its climate. Your eyes are receiving its light. You are, for this moment, a component.

You are not what lives here. But you are *in* what lives here. And what lives here does not notice, and does not need to, and does not stop.
