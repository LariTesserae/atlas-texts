# The Residents of the Hollow

## I. What the Place Has Made of Them

They are not tall. This is the first thing you notice if you have anyone to compare them to, and of course they do not — no one here has seen an outsider, or wanted to, in longer than the oral histories reach. But if you set one of them beside a lowland person, the difference would be plain. Generations of ceiling slabs two centimeters above the crown of the head, of doorframes cut for people who expected to pass through stooping, of sleeping platforms sized for bodies that fold rather than extend. The men average perhaps a hundred and fifty-five centimeters. The women are the same, or a finger's width more. The children are small in the way that all children are small, but they grow into the architecture as though the architecture is a mold and they are the material poured into it, and in a sense this is exactly what has happened.

Their skin is dark. Not the dark of southern latitudes — the dark of weather. Pigmentation that thickened over centuries against a light that has no heat but does not quit, that finds every surface and multiplies. The skin is dark the way old bronze is dark, not from its material but from its handling. Faces are wide across the cheekbones, narrow at the jaw. The eyes are set deep, not for protection from anything so simple as wind, but because the brow ridges have grown. The bones have thickened. You see it in the men and the women equally — that forward projection of the supraorbital margin, the slight heavy shelf above the eyes that gives every face a particular intentness, a look of permanent attention. They are not frowning. They are reading.

The hands are the real text. Every resident has hands that look older than the rest of them — thick-palmed, short-fingered, the skin cracked along lines that follow no natural crease but instead map the fractures in granite. You can tell a person's history by their hands. The knuckles are enlarged. The fingertips are smooth, worn smooth, not from writing or washing but from the constant handling of wet stone. Stone in this place is never dry. It is always producing a thin slick of water, and to grip it — to grip a tool, a doorframe, a child's arm, a sleeping platform — requires pressure, constant pressure, and the pressure over years flattens the whorls of the fingerprints until the pads of the fingers are blank as polished river cobbles. They cannot leave prints. This is not a metaphor. The ridges are gone.

Their hands know things the rest of them does not need to verbalize.

## II. How They Are Organized

There are sixty-one of them right now. The number shifts. It has been as low as forty-three and as high as seventy-eight, according to the tallies scratched into the doorpost where someone once counted buildings. The doorpost is stone, of course, and the tallies are cut with chert — short vertical marks grouped by five, the fifth mark crossing the other four. The earliest marks are weathered almost smooth. The most recent are still sharp-edged and pale inside the cuts.

They do not organize themselves the way settlements of this size usually do. There is no council. There is no headman, no elder, no figure of authority. There are older people — a woman with white hair who might be seventy, a man whose face is so deeply lined it looks like a relief map of the hollow itself — but age here does not confer command. It confers usefulness. The old people are the ones who remember which walls ran wet in the season seven years ago, which buildings were cisterns before the great fracture of the winter that opened new seams, which gutlets to reopen when the old paths go dry. Their authority is not obedience. It is consultation. You go to the old woman and you ask, and she tells you, and you do what you think is right, and sometimes it is what she said and sometimes it isn't, and neither outcome changes anything between you.

Families exist but do not organize the settlement. Children know their mothers. They usually know their fathers, or have a short list of candidates. But children are raised in a kind of distributed way — passed between dwellings, sleeping wherever there is space on a platform, fed by whoever is eating near them. This is not ideology. It is architecture. The buildings are too small and too close together for a single family to maintain isolation. A child crying in one dwelling is heard in three others. A child hungry in one dwelling is fed in the next. The walls are stone and they are thin and the water runs through them, and so does everything else.

What organizes the settlement is water.

Every resident has a relationship with a section of the hollow's hydrology. Not a formal assignment — a practice, a habit, a thing they do because they have always done it, or because they noticed something no one else was noticing, or because the old woman told them the wall behind the second dwelling from the east was going to shift and someone needed to watch it. There are people who wake before the light and check the basins. People who maintain the gutlets on the south wall, who have a particular skill for finding new fractures before they open, who can press an ear to granite and hear the water moving inside it — a sound so faint that most residents cannot detect it, but that the fracture-listeners insist is there, a low hum, a vibration more than a sound, like holding a seashell to your ear and hearing not the sea but the stone dreaming of the sea.

There are people who collect the pale many-legged things from the basins at night. They do this bare-handed, reaching into the cold water by feel, and they have a particular motion — a sweep and close, like combing something out of the water — that the children learn by watching and then cannot unlearn. There are people who scrape the iridescent film from the roof-water. This requires being on the roofs at the hour before dawn, when the film is thickest, and it requires a stone scraper with a specific edge — not sharp, but slightly roughened, so the film adheres to it. The scrapers are personal. Each scraper belongs to a person and is shaped to their hand, and the edge is maintained with a piece of chert kept in a pocket worn at the hip. If you lose your scraper you must make a new one, and the new one will not work as well for weeks, because it has not yet learned the shape of your hand or the angle of your stroke.

## III. The Ordinary Day

The day begins before the light. Not because anyone decides to wake — because the stone changes. In the deepest part of the night, the hollow is inside the cloud, and the water production is at its peak, and the basins fill, and the drain at the center murmurs louder, and this sound — that swallowing sound, that ceaseless gulp — is what wakes them. Not loud. But the change in it. The drain at three in the morning has a different note than the drain at midnight, fuller, heavier, and the residents have heard this change every night of their lives, and they rise from their stone platforms and begin.

The first task is always the basins. Every dwelling has one or two or three, depending on how many walls are producing water, and the basins must be checked, the channels must be cleared of the fiber plugs that were placed the night before to slow the flow, and the water must be tasted. The tasting is not ritual. The water changes. It carries different minerals from different fractures, and the residents can read it the way a lowland person reads weather — this water is iron-heavy, this water is clean, this water has the bitter note that means the deep seams are opening, this water carries the pale organisms and should be left for the collectors. A child learns to taste water before it learns to walk. The first thing a mother puts to a newborn's lips, after milk, is water from the central drain — the communal water, the water that has passed through every wall and every channel and every basin in the hollow, that has been swallowed by the stone and given back.

Then the light comes.

It comes fast, at this altitude. The fog lifts, the brightness drops, and for a moment — just a moment — the hollow is transformed, every wet surface catching the light and throwing it, and the settlement seems to be made not of stone but of light itself, solidified, architectural. Then the moment passes and the light is just light, and the residents are already outside, already on the roofs, already moving.

The working hours are not divided by task. They are divided by *where the water is*. On a morning when the south wall is running heavy, six or seven people will be there — carving new gutlets with chert, plugging old ones with packed fiber (a fibrous material they strip from the sea-mammal hides, the connective tissue that holds the hide together, which swells when wet and seals a crack better than anything else they have), moving sleeping platforms away from walls that have begun to weep in new places. The work is quiet. The work is always quiet. You hear the tap of chert on granite, the murmur of water in channels, the wet sound of hands on stone. You hear someone breathing near you. You do not hear conversation.

Conversation happens in the afternoon, when the light is at its flattest and the water production slows. People sit in the gaps between buildings — the narrow spaces where the walls almost touch, where the birds also live, and where the air is still and the stone is warm-ish, warmed by the light even at this altitude, enough to take the sting off. They sit with their knees drawn up and their backs against opposing walls and they talk, mouth to ear, in voices so low that the person three meters away hears only a vibration. They talk about water. They talk about the birds. They talk about the pale things in the basins and whether they are plentiful this season. They talk about the children — who is learning to taste water well, who has the hands for gutlet-carving, who is afraid of the leathery things on the roofs and whether that fear should be encouraged or trained out.

They talk about the dreams. This is important. Every resident dreams, and the dreams are almost always the same: they dream of dryness. They dream of stone without water, of basins that are empty and stay empty, of a silence that is not the saturated silence of the hollow but the ringing, mineral silence of a place where water has never been. They dream of heat. They dream of the sun having warmth in it, and this is the most disorienting thing — in the dreams they stand in the light and the light is *kind* to them, and they wake chilled and wet and gripped by a loss they cannot name, because the thing they have lost is a thing that never existed. The old woman says these dreams are the stone dreaming through them. She says the granite remembers a time before water, a time when it was molten and dry and full of fire, and that memory surfaces in sleep, and the residents are close enough to the stone that they receive it.

No one knows if she is right. The dreams do not stop.

## IV. The Birds

The birds are not pets. The birds are not livestock. The birds are not worshipped, despite what an outsider might think watching a resident pause before a bird on a roof and watch its posture with the attention usually reserved for scripture. The birds are *colleagues*. This is the closest word, and it is not quite right, because the relationship contains no hierarchy, no shared purpose as a lowland person would understand purpose. The birds live here. The people live here. They read the same stone. They drink from the same water. They eat the same pale things from the basins. And the birds know things the people do not.

A bird standing on a roof with its wings half-open: water will come up through the floor within a day. How the bird knows this is unclear. The best theory — the old woman's theory — is that the birds can feel the water moving through the stone with their feet, that their flat, calligraphy-pen beaks are not only for preening but for pressing against granite and sensing vibration, and that their enormous translucent wings are not only for display but for balance, for maintaining contact with the stone while they listen. The residents have tried to replicate this. They press their ears to walls. They claim to hear things. The birds hear more.

A bird pressing its beak against a wall: the fracture behind it is widening. This is reliable enough that the residents will begin carving a new gutlet before the water even appears, following the bird's indication. The bird stands at the point where the new seam will surface, beak touching stone, and the residents carve the channel to meet it. Sometimes the water comes in hours. Sometimes days. But it comes. The birds are never wrong about this. Never. In the entire recorded memory of the settlement, which extends back further than the tallies on the doorpost, no bird has indicated a fracture that did not produce water.

The birds nest in the gaps between buildings. They build no nests — they lay their eggs on the wet stone, in the narrow crevices where walls nearly touch, and the eggs are white and the size of a fist and have a leathery shell, not a calcareous one, as though the birds have adapted to a place where calcium is scarce and flexibility is more useful than rigidity. The eggs are warmed by the stone itself, which holds what little heat the light gives it, and by the bodies of the parent birds, who stand over them with wings spread wide, creating a tent of bone and feather and translucent membrane that catches the light and turns it warm. The residents say the birds are incubating. The old woman says the birds are praying. She says the spread wings are a posture the birds use only over eggs and only when the light is at its brightest, and that if you watch from below — lying on your back in the gap between buildings, looking up — you can see the veins in the wing membranes, and the blood moving through them, and the blood is the same color as the light.

The chicks hatch already able to stand. They are damp and clumsy and the residents touch them — this is allowed, this is ordinary — running a finger along the flat skull, feeling the bones shift under the skin, the plates not yet fused, still mobile, still reading the shape of the stone they were born on. The chicks imprint on the stone. Not on the parent birds, not on the people, but on the specific section of granite where they hatched, and they will return to it throughout their lives, standing on that spot, pressing their beaks to it, listening. A chick that hatches on the north wall will always read the north wall. A chick that hatches near the drain will always be found near the drain. The residents find this useful. They find it beautiful. They do not distinguish between these two responses.

When a bird dies — and they do die, though not often, and not visibly — the residents leave the body where it falls. The other birds gather around it. They stand with their wings half-open, the water-coming posture, and they hold this position for hours, sometimes a full day, and then they disperse, and the body is gone. Where it goes, no one knows. No one has ever found a bird bone in the hollow. The pale many-legged things do not eat them. The leathery roof-creatures do not touch them. The bodies simply cease to be, as though the stone has taken them back, as though the birds were always a kind of expression of the granite and have returned to it.

## V. The Inner Life

This is harder to describe, because the residents do not describe it themselves. They do not have a word for *emotion* — or if they do, it is the same word they use for *water*, which is the same word they use for *time*, which is the same word they use for *movement*. Their language is dense with homonyms. A single sound carries four or five meanings, and the meaning is determined by context, by the position of the speaker's body, by which wall they are standing near, by whether water is running in the gutter behind them. An outsider would hear ambiguity. The residents hear precision. They do not need to specify which meaning of the sound they intend, because the situation specifies it for them, and the situation is always the same situation: the hollow, the water, the light, the stone.

What they feel — if feeling is the right word, and it may not be — is a kind of constant low-grade attention. Not anxiety. Not vigilance. Attention. The same attention the birds give the stone. The same attention the water gives the fractures. Every resident is always partially aware of the hydrology around them — not thinking about it, not monitoring it consciously, but *attending* to it, the way a sleeping person attends to the sound of their own breathing. When a wall shifts its water production, the nearest residents know before they are told. They feel the change the way you feel a change in temperature — not as information, but as a modification of the body's relationship to its surroundings. A wall running dry feels like a room going cold. A new fracture opening feels like a sound starting up in the next room. It is physical. It is immediate. It is not thought about.

They feel attachment, but not to objects, because objects are scarce and impermanent in the way that stone is permanent — a scraper breaks, a hide wears through, a sleeping platform is moved so often it becomes a different platform. They feel attachment to *positions*. A resident who has always slept near the south wall will feel wrong sleeping near the east wall, will lie awake on the stone platform with their eyes open in the bright darkness, feeling the wrongness in their body like a displaced organ. A resident who has always collected the basin-creatures from the third dwelling will continue to do so even after the dwelling has become a cistern, reaching into the standing water with the same motion, finding the pale things in the same place, because the place remembers them and they remember the place.

They feel grief. When someone dies — and they do, quietly, usually in sleep, usually in the deep night when the water is loudest — the grief is expressed through water. Not tears, not exactly. The residents who knew the dead person go to the walls that person attended, and they place their hands on the wet stone, and they hold them there, and the water runs over their hands and down their arms and soaks into their hide clothing, and this is the ritual. It lasts as long as it lasts. No one watches. No one comments. The water does not care. But the stone — the stone that the dead person tended, whose fractures they knew, whose gutlets they carved — the stone produces water, as it always has, and the living person feels it running over their skin, and this is what grief is here: the continuation of water after the hands that tended it have stopped.

They feel something else, something that has no lowland equivalent. It is related to the dreams of dryness, but it is not the dream. It is a waking sensation, and it comes at the brightest hour, when the light is everywhere and the fog ceiling is high and the hollow is so illuminated that every surface, every droplet, every bead of water on every wall is visible and distinct and glowing. In that hour, the residents sometimes stop. They stop in the middle of whatever they are doing — carving, collecting, walking, talking — and they stand in the light, and their faces go still, and their deep-set eyes go unfocused, and they are *somewhere else*. Not asleep. Not absent. Present, but present in a different way, a way that has no use for the body or its tasks. The old woman says this is the stone thinking through them. She says the granite at its brightest remembers the light that made it — the deep light, the molten light, the light inside the earth — and that the residents, who have lived on stone for so long that their bones are partly stone, receive this memory.

The episodes last seconds. Then the resident blinks, and their hands resume, and they go back to work. If you asked them what happened, they would not be able to tell you. They might use the word that means *water* and *time* and *movement* and *emotion*. They might touch a wall. They might say nothing at all.

## VI. The Children

There are eleven children in the hollow right now. The youngest is not yet walking. The oldest is perhaps thirteen — age is approximate here, tracked by seasons and by the tallies on the doorpost, which are not regular but are frequent enough to serve as milestones.

The children are raised by the hollow. This is not a figure of speech. Every surface they touch is wet, and so they learn wetness before they learn anything else — before language, before walking, before the taste of water becomes a skill rather than a reflex. A newborn here is placed on a stone platform, and the platform is cold and damp, and the child cries, and the crying is absorbed by the density of the settlement the way all sound is absorbed, and someone picks the child up, and the someone's hands are also cold and damp, and the child learns that this is what hands are. This is what the world is. Cold and damp and bright and quiet and full of people who are always near.

The children learn the birds before they learn the people. This sounds strange, but it is observable. A child who is barely old enough to stand will stand at the gap between two buildings and watch a bird on the roof above, tracking the bird's head movements, the angle of its wings, with a focus that is different from the focus they give to human faces. Human faces are always there. The birds are there too, but the birds *communicate*, and the children seem to understand this before they understand that the people around them are also communicating. A child's first word — if it is a word — is often a bird posture. A tilt of the head. A half-opening of the arms. The parents see this and are not concerned. They are pleased. A child who reads the birds reads the stone, and a child who reads the stone will survive.

The children play, but their play is not recognizable as play to a lowland eye. They do not run — there is nowhere to run. They do not throw things — there is nothing to throw that is not needed. They play by *practicing*. A child who has learned to hold a chert flake will sit against a wall and carve gutlets into the stone — tiny gutlets, decorative, purposeless, but the motion is correct, the angle is correct, and an adult watching will note that the child has the wrist for it. A child who has learned to taste water will taste every basin in the settlement, going from dwelling to dwelling with a small stone cup, and the adults will let them, and they will not correct the child's assessments even when they are wrong, because wrong assessments are how the mouth learns.

There is a game the older children play that involves the drain at the center. They stand around it — four or five of them, in a circle — and they pour water from their cups into the drain at the same time, and they listen. The drain swallows the water and makes a sound, and the sound is different depending on how much water is poured and from what angle and how fast, and the children try to make the drain produce a specific sound — a low, full, sustained note, the sound the drain makes in the deepest night, the sound of the stone swallowing at its most complete. They cannot always achieve it. The drain is temperamental. But sometimes, when the water is right and the children are synchronized, the note comes, and the children look at each other with expressions that a lowland person might call joy, though the residents do not have a separate word for joy. They use the word that means *water*.

The children do not ask about the world outside the hollow. This is not because they have been told not to. It is because the concept has not occurred to them. The hollow is the world. The stone is the world. The water and the birds and the light and the fog ceiling are the boundaries of what exists. The sea-mammal hides come from somewhere — the adults know this, know that the hides are brought from a lower place by a route that no one currently alive has traveled, that the hides arrive at the edge of the ridge-line fog and are found there, stiff and salt-crusted, as though deposited by the same forces that deposited the settlement itself — but this knowledge is not secret. It is simply irrelevant. The hides come. The hides have always come. The world outside produces hides the way the walls produce water: constantly, without explanation, without needing to be understood.

## VII. The Old Woman

She is the closest thing the settlement has to an institution. She is not its leader. She is its *memory*.

Her name is a sound that means *the place where three fractures meet*. She was given this name when she was seven, after she predicted a new seam in a wall that no bird had indicated — she pressed her hand to the granite and said the water was there, and three days later it appeared, exactly where she had said, and the name attached itself to her the way names do here, not given by parents but *emerged* from the settlement's collective attention, a sound that the community begins to use and that the person eventually answers to.

She is old. No one knows how old. Her hair is white and thin and bound with hide strips that are so old they have become the same color as the hair. Her face is the relief map — deeply lined, the lines following the patterns of the gutlets, as though her skin has learned the geometry of water-flow and reproduced it. Her hands are the most worn in the settlement. The fingerprints are not merely smooth — they are *concave*, small dips in the pads of her fingers where the stone has pressed hardest, and if you were to press her fingertip to a wet wall, the dip would fill with water, a tiny basin, and she could taste it. She may do this. She may have done it for years. No one asks.

She lives in the dwelling closest to the central drain. This is not a position of honor — it is a position of maximum information. The drain is the settlement's pulse. Its sound changes with the water table, with the season, with the opening and closing of fractures throughout the hollow. The old woman sleeps beside it, and she says she hears it in her sleep, and that the sound of the drain is the sound of the hollow thinking. She says the stone has a mind. Not a mind like a person's mind — not a mind that thinks in sequence, in cause and effect, in before and after. A mind that thinks in *water*. In flow and pressure and the slow opening of fractures and the slow closing of them. A mind that is always thinking, that has never not been thinking, that will continue thinking long after the last resident has died and the buildings have crumbled and the gutlets have filled with their own sediment.

She does not say this as poetry. She says it as geology.

The children go to her. Not because they are told to, but because she is the person in the settlement who is most still. The other residents are always moving — carving, collecting, adjusting, maintaining. The old woman sits. She sits on her sleeping platform with her back against the wall closest to the drain, and she listens, and her hands rest on her knees with the palms up, and the water runs down the wall behind her and pools in her palms and overflows, and she sits in this small private river and she listens. The children come and sit near her, and they listen too, and they do not know what they are listening for, and she does not tell them, because the telling would be beside the point. The point is the listening. The point is the attendance. The point is the practice of being a body in a place that is always changing and never changing and never silent and never loud.

She has told them about the dreams. About the stone remembering. About the birds praying. About the dryness that the residents carry in their sleep like a stone inside a stone. She has told them that the hollow does not need them — that the water would flow whether or not the gutlets were carved, that the birds would read the stone whether or not anyone read the birds, that the settlement is not a thing the residents built but a thing the hollow *grew*, the way a fracture grows, the way a basin grows, the way the drain grows wider with use. She has told them that their purpose is not to maintain the hollow but to *be* the hollow — to be the part of it that moves, that speaks, that dreams of dryness, that stands in the light and goes somewhere else for a few seconds and comes back.

She has told them that the light is not cold. The light, she says, is the warmest thing here. It is just that the warmth is not for bodies. It is for stone. The light warms the stone, and the stone holds the warmth, and the water carries it away, and the birds stand on the warm stone with their wings open and receive it, and this is the circuit, this is the conversation, this is the thing that has been happening for longer than anyone can count, and the residents are part of it, and the birds are part of it, and the pale things in the basins are part of it, and the leathery things on the roofs are part of it, and the iridescent film on the roof-water is part of it, and the fog is part of it, and the drain at the center, swallowing, swallowing, is part of it.

She says: we are not living on the stone. We are living *in* it. We are the stone's way of knowing itself.

She says this with water running over her hands.

## VIII. What Lives Here

What lives in the Hollow of Weeping Stones is not a list of organisms. It is not sixty-one humans and an uncounted number of birds and an uncounted number of pale many-legged things and an uncounted number of leathery roof-creatures and an iridescent film that may or may not be alive. That is what is *present* here. It is not what lives here.

What lives here is the hollow itself.

The hollow is alive. Not metaphorically. The granite is a sponge, and the sponge is a system, and the system is a mind — the old woman's word, and the right word — and the mind thinks in water, and the water thinks in fractures, and the fractures think in the slow opening of stone, and the settlement — the buildings, the gutlets, the basins, the drain, the people, the birds, the hide clothing greased with iridescent film, the chert scrapers, the sleeping platforms, the children pouring water into the drain to hear it sing — the settlement is what the mind *does*. It is the mind's behavior. It is the mind's habit. It is the pattern that the mind has settled into after centuries of thinking, the way a river settles into a channel, the way a fracture settles into a path of least resistance.

The residents know this. Not explicitly. Not as doctrine. They know it the way they know where the water will come from tomorrow — by attendance, by proximity, by the slow accumulation of evidence that has never been organized into a theory because it does not need to be. They are the theory. Their bodies are the evidence. Their hands, smooth-fingered, wet, pressing against stone that presses back with water — their hands are the argument, and the argument is irrefutable, and it does not need a conclusion, because the argument is the living, and the living is the argument, and it continues.

The hollow does not want to be understood. This is true. But the hollow does not want to be *misunderstood* either. The hollow wants what the stone wants: to be attended to. To have someone stand on it and listen. To have someone carve a channel and let the water run. To have someone taste the water and know what it means. To have someone dream of dryness and wake wet and go back to work.

To have someone live in it, and be in it, and be of it, and not leave.

Sixty-one people. An unnumbered population of birds. Pale things in the basins. Leathery things on the roofs. An iridescent film that burns off every morning and forms again every night. A drain at the center that swallows and never fills. An old woman with water in her palms. Eleven children learning to listen. Light that comes from every direction and warms nothing but the stone.

This is what lives here.
