# The People of the Crying Cliff

They are few. You would not need to count them twice.

Perhaps three hundred, if you include the children, and you should always include the children, because there are not many of them, and the people know this, and they do not speak of it the way they do not speak of the lowland forests visible from the rim — as a fact that has become geography, a feature of the world rather than a problem to solve. Three hundred is not a number that sustains itself easily across generations. The birth rate is low. The infant mortality is not discussed but is marked: small cairns at the eastern edge of the settlement, where the sparse sedge gives way to bare stone, each one a fist-sized basalt knob placed atop another, no more than that, no inscription, no name, because names are spoken and the dead are kept in the speaking, not in the stone. There are perhaps forty cairns. They span a long time.

---

They are dark-skinned, but not in the way of lowland peoples near the equator. The darkness has a quality to it — not the deep mahogany of tropical sun but something denser, ash-toned, as though the basalt itself has lent its color to the people who live on it. The sun here is direct and unfiltered, the air thin, and their skin has responded across generations with a pigment that is less warm than mineral. They look, in certain light, like they were carved from the same stone as their walls.

They are not tall. Generations of adequate but limited nutrition — the algae, the sedge, the occasional fig — have produced a people whose bodies are compact, dense, efficient. The women are broad-hipped, which matters in a place where childbirth carries the weight it does here. The men are wiry, not muscular in the way of laborers who work with heavy loads but lean in the way of people who move carefully and constantly, who climb and balance and carry water and chip stone for hours at a stretch. Everyone walks with a particular gait — toes gripped, weight forward, the walk of someone who has grown up on wet stone and knows that a misstep on the cliff rim is not a misstep you correct.

Their hands are remarkable. The constant work of stone — shaping, dressing, carving channels, hollowing basins — has given them hands that look older than they are. The fingertips are broad, flattened, callused in patterns that correspond to the tools they use: a ridge across the index finger where the copper chisel rests, a pad of horn on the thumb where it presses against basalt to brace a strike. The skin between the fingers is often cracked, despite the moisture, or perhaps because of it — the constant wet softens the skin, and the constant work abrades it, and the result is a hand that is simultaneously too soft and too hard, the cracks deep but clean, never infected. The water is too pure for that.

Their hair is straight and black and worn short, by both men and women, though for different reasons that have converged on the same practical solution. For the men, long hair catches moisture and drips into the eyes during stone work. For the women, long hair is a liability near the hearths, where the small fires are fed with sedge that sparks. Short hair is not a style. It is a decision the landscape has made for them.

They dress in sedge cloth — a rough, open weave that is less garment than wrap, draped and tucked rather than cut and fitted. The cloth is pale, almost white, the natural color of the sedge fiber, and it darkens with wear, turning the color of wet stone within days of weaving. Everyone owns two or three wraps. One for work, one for rest, one for sleeping. The work wrap is stiff with dried algae, stained green at the edges where it has been used to carry the harvest from the reservoirs. The sleep wrap is softer, broken down by use and by the constant damp, the fibers separating until the cloth is almost a felt. They do not wash their clothes. The water washes everything, always, and the cloth is never truly dry, so the distinction between clean and dirty is academic. Things are either new or worn out. That is enough.

---

Their faces are still.

This is the thing you notice after the silence. A people without animals has not learned the small expressiveness that comes from mirroring creatures — the tilted head, the widened eyes, the quick flash of teeth that signals amusement or aggression. The people of Verre Alta have no animals to read, no animals to perform for, no animals whose emotional vocabularies they have absorbed into their own. Their faces move when they speak, but the movement is spare, economical, a tightening around the eyes or a shift of the jaw that carries meaning the way a shifted stone changes the fit of a wall. They smile, but the smile is subtle — a loosening, an opening, not a display. They do not grin. Grinning is for social mammals who need to signal harmlessness across a distance, and these people are always close to each other, always within arm's reach, and harmlessness is assumed until it is not.

Their eyes are the exception. Their eyes are active, restless, constantly tracking — the mist, the stone, the water channels, each other's hands. They watch the way a predator watches, or rather the way a person watches who has no predators to watch for and so turns all that attention inward, onto the landscape, onto the people around them. They notice things. A crack in a reservoir wall. A fern frond that has begun to curl. A shift in the taste of the water — they can taste it, the mineral signature, and they know when the condensation pattern has changed, even slightly, even before the mist visibly thickens or thins. They are meteorologists by immersion, not by training. They have lived inside the weather for so long that they have become part of its instrumentation.

---

They speak a language that belongs to no family an outsider would recognize, because no outsider has ever been present to recognize it. It is a language shaped by the mist. The high frequencies are absorbed by the wet air, so the language lives in its consonants and its low vowels — a ground-down, rounded speech, words that feel like stones in the mouth, smoothed by use. There are no sharp sibilants. There is no whistling, no clicking. The language is soft and heavy, and it carries through fog the way a bass note carries through a wall.

They have words for water that an outsider would find excessive. Words for the condensation as it forms, as it gathers, as it runs, as it pools, as it warms, as it cools, as it tastes of iron, as it tastes of moss, as it tastes of nothing. Words for the mist at dawn, which is different from the mist at midday, which is different from the mist at dusk. Words for the specific sound the cliff makes when it is crying hard versus when it is crying softly, and these words are not onomatopoeic but descriptive, technical, precise — the way a sailor has words for wind that a land dweller does not need.

They have a word for the lowland forests visible from the rim. It means *the underneath*. They have a word for the plateau itself, which means *the above*, but also, in a different register, *the only*. They do not have a word for *elsewhere* in the sense that other cultures might use it — a place you could go. They have a word that means *where we are not*, but it is an abstract term, used in storytelling, not in planning.

They tell stories. The stories are about stone and water, mostly. About the plateau rising, which they understand as an act of will — not a god's will, but the stone's own will, a decision the basalt made to come up and see the sky. In their stories, the stone is not inert. It is patient, and it remembers, and it has opinions, and the people must take these opinions into account when they build, when they carve, when they walk. The cliff cries, in their telling, not because it is sad but because it is talkative — it has been underground for a long time, and now it has things to say, and the water is its voice. The people listen. They have arranged their entire lives around listening.

They also tell stories about each other, but the stories are small. A woman who found a new way to weave sedge that held up longer in the damp. A man who carved a basin so perfectly placed that it filled twice as fast as the others. A child who was born during the thinnest mist anyone could remember and who grew up to be able to taste the approach of condensation before it formed. These are their heroes. Not warriors. Not leaders. People who paid attention.

---

They do not have leaders, or they do not have leaders in the way that the word usually implies. No one commands. No one is elevated above. Decisions are made by the simple mechanism of people talking until they agree, and the talking happens in the cleared central space, and it happens slowly, and it is not a debate but a convergence — the way water finds its path, not by being directed but by following the contours that already exist. The oldest people speak first, usually, but not always, and age here is not just years but attention — a person who has noticed more, who has seen more cycles of the mist and the sedge and the algae, is heard more readily than a person who has not, regardless of their years.

There is a person, though. Not a leader. Something else. The person who tends the largest reservoir, the one where the algae grows. This person — it is not a hereditary role, not an elected role, but a role that settles on someone the way moss settles on stone, gradually and without announcement — is responsible for the harvest, for the timing, for the scraping of the algae from the submerged rock surfaces. It is the most important task in the community, because the algae is their staple, their survival, and the person who manages it holds the town's continuity in their hands. They do not wield this as power. They wield it as attention. They know the reservoir's temperature, its depth, the exact rate at which the algae regrows after harvest, the way the growth changes with the season — for there are seasons here, subtle ones, shifts in the mist's density and the sun's angle that the reservoir-tender reads like text.

The current reservoir-tender is a woman in her middle years, quiet even by the standards of her people, who touches the water's surface with her fingertips a dozen times a day and can tell you, from the feel of it, whether the algae will be ready to harvest tomorrow or next week. She does not explain this. She does not need to. The people trust her the way they trust the cliff to cry — not because she has proven herself but because she is part of the system, and the system works.

---

They do not have religion, or they would not call it that. They have the cliff, and the water, and the stone, and they have a relationship with these things that is closer to etiquette than worship. You do not take more than the reservoir offers. You do not carve where the stone does not want to be carved — and the stone has preferences, they believe, grain lines and fracture patterns that suggest where a basin should go and where it should not. You do not speak loudly near the crying cliff, not because it is sacred but because it is rude, the way it is rude to talk over someone who is speaking. The cliff was here first. The cliff is talking. You wait.

When someone dies — and they do die, of the small infections that find their way into cracked hands, of the accidents that happen on wet stone near a cliff edge, of the simple wearing-out that comes to people who live hard lives in a thin-air environment — they are placed in the amphitheater of the cliff, in a crevice at the western rim where the condensation is thickest, and the water covers them, and the moss grows over them, and they become part of the crying. This is not burial. It is return. The stone came up from underground, and the people go back into the stone, or into the water that runs over the stone, and the distinction between person and place dissolves the way the distinction between air and water dissolves here, slowly, continuously, without a clear line where one becomes the other.

The cairns at the eastern edge are for the children who did not live long enough to be returned. They are too small for the crevice, the people feel, too new, not yet part of the stone. So they are marked with stone instead, small stones, and the marking is its own kind of mourning — the only instance in their culture where the people impose a human shape onto the landscape rather than accepting the landscape's shape. It is, perhaps, the only act of defiance in their history.

---

And they are lonely, though they would not name it that, because they have no word for the opposite condition to compare it to. They have each other, and they are close, physically and emotionally, in the way that three hundred people in two hundred stone rooms must be — every conversation overheard, every argument felt through the shared walls, every birth and every death witnessed by the whole community because there is nowhere else to be. But there is a loneliness that comes from being the only kind of thing in a place. The moss does not keep them company. The ferns do not answer. The cliff cries, but it cries the same thing, always, and the people have learned to hear it not as conversation but as atmosphere — the sound of the world they live in, constant and unresponsive, beautiful and indifferent.

Sometimes a child will stand at the cliff edge and look down at the lowland forests — *the underneath* — and the adults will let the child look, because there is no danger in looking, and because they remember looking themselves, and because they know that the looking is not desire, exactly, but a kind of reaching, a question that the cliff cannot answer and the child will eventually stop asking. They all stop asking. The plateau is not a prison. It is simply the world, and the world is the size of the world, and if the world ends at a three-hundred-meter drop into a forest you cannot reach, then that is where the world ends, and there is nothing beyond it except story.

They live in the story. They live in the mist. They live in the patient, mineral, weeping patience of a place that was underground and came up and found the sky and found it empty of everything except water and stone and the few stubborn green things and the few quiet dark people who have learned, over generations, to be exactly as patient as the rock they live on.

The mist condenses. The cliff cries. The people listen. This is what they do. This is all they have ever done. This is enough.
