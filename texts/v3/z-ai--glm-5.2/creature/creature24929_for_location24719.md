# What Lives in the Nave

---

## The Groove Beetles

Begin with what you can almost overlook.

They are the color of the floor — not similar to it, not approximate, but the precise color, the precise sheen, as if they were cut from the same material and merely shaped differently. You do not see them until one moves, and even then what you see is not the beetle but the interruption in the surface, a dark fleck traveling a dark channel with the unhurried conviction of something that has never had a reason to hurry.

They are the width of the grooves. Exactly. Not approximately, not within a tolerance — exactly, the way a key fits a lock that was made for it, except neither was made for the other. They became each other. The grooves were worn by water over millennia, and the beetles were shaped by the grooves over millennia, and now the fit is so precise that a beetle cannot turn around in its channel. It can move forward. It can move backward. It cannot turn. It does not need to turn. The channel is a circuit. It loops — not obviously, not within any visible section, but in its full topology, somewhere deep in the root-wood's labyrinth, the groove connects back to itself. The beetles travel a path that has no terminus. They have always been traveling it.

Their shells are smooth, domed, segmented in a pattern that mirrors the grain of the wood they traverse. Under the faint amber light they gleam like wet stone. They are wet — everything here is wet — and the moisture on their shells is the same moisture that films the floor, the walls, the air itself. You could not distinguish the water on a beetle's back from the water on the wood beside it. They belong to the same system.

There are thousands. You see only a few at any moment because the channels are narrow and widely spaced and the floor is vast, but if you knelt and pressed your face to the wood and looked down the length of a single groove, you would see them — a procession, spaced at intervals so regular they seem measured, each beetle separated from the next by a distance that is, again, exact. They do not cluster. They do not spread out. The spacing is maintained by something — territorial chemistry, perhaps, or simply the geometry of a feeding pattern established so long ago it has become instinct, and the instinct has become reflex, and the reflex has become structure, and the structure is indistinguishable from the beetle itself.

What they eat is the biofilm. The thin, living layer of microorganisms that coats every wet surface in the Nave — bacteria, fungi, slime molds, things that have no common name because they have never been named — adheres to the channel walls in a film so fine it is invisible. The beetles graze it. Their mandibles are broad, flat, angled to scrape rather than bite, and as they move they clean the channel walls with a thoroughness that is, in its own small way, industrial. They are a maintenance system. The channels must remain clear for water to flow. The water must flow for the organism to function. The beetles keep the channels clear. They have been keeping them clear since before the channels looked like channels. They are the reason the channels are channels.

A single beetle lives, as near as anyone could determine, for a very long time. Not by insect standards — by any standards. Their metabolism is keyed to the temperature of the Nave, which is constant, and to the chemistry of the biofilm, which is constant, and to the rhythm of the water's flow, which is constant. Nothing in their environment places oxidative stress on them. Nothing varies. Their cells divide at a rate synchronized to the ambient hum of the organism's processes, and that hum has not changed, so the rate has not changed, and the beetles do not age in any way that is measurable. They do not die of age. They die, occasionally, when a foot presses down on a channel — but there are no feet. They die when the wood shifts and a groove pinches closed — but the wood has not shifted in millennia. In practice, they do not die. They continue. They are the oldest individual things in the Nave, and they do not know what they are, and it does not matter.

## The Tenders

These are what you notice second, after the sound, after the beetles. They move on the walls.

They are not insects. They are not arachnids. They are not isopods, though they resemble isopods in the way that a whale resembles a fish — convergent form driven by convergent need, not shared ancestry. They are pale, almost translucent, the color of the wood beneath them diluted to half-strength. Their bodies are segmented, flattened, the size of a human thumb, and they move with a slowness that makes the beetles look frantic. A tender will traverse the width of your palm in a minute. It will traverse the wall from floor to ceiling in a day. It has no reason to move faster.

Each segment carries a pair of legs — fine, hair-thin, tipped with structures that are not claws but tools. Some are spatulate, for scraping. Some are bristled, for brushing. Some are needle-fine, for probing. A single tender carries a toolkit as varied as a surgeon's, and it uses them all. It moves along the wall and it works. It tests the bark with its anterior appendages, pressing, reading the surface the way a blind person reads a page — by touch, by texture, by the subtle give-and-resistance of living tissue. Where it finds dead cells, it stops. It positions itself. Its mandibles — and they are mandibles, precise and angled like surgical scissors — open, close, excise. A sliver of bark the width of a hair falls away. The tender catches it, holds it, carries it.

The waste is not dropped. The tenders carry what they remove to designated sites — small concavities in the root-wood floor where detritus accumulates and is, slowly, broken down by other organisms, by the water, by the organism's own digestive enzymes. The tenders are a waste-management system. They are also, and simultaneously, a diagnostic system. The probing of their anterior appendages is not random. They are testing for infection, for necrosis, for structural weakness, for any deviation from the norm. They have been doing this for so long that they know the norm the way the wood knows the norm: cellularly, intimately, in the grain.

They do not react to you.

This is worth emphasizing, because it is strange. A tender, moving along the wall at eye-level, will pass within inches of your face and not alter its course by a millimeter. It will not flinch when you breathe. It will not pause when you move. You could extend your hand and let it walk onto your palm and it would continue probing and testing and clipping, treating your skin as simply another surface — finding nothing wrong with it, finding nothing right with it, finding it irrelevant. You are not a predator. You are not prey. You are not a surface it is programmed to evaluate. You are a thing that has no category, and so it has no response.

Its eyes — if they are eyes — are two dark spots on its head, reduced to pigment patches with no lenses, no corneas, no structures that would suggest image formation. It does not see. It has never needed to see. The Nave is lit, but the light is not for the fauna. The light is for the organism's own processes, a byproduct of the photoluminescent sap, and the fauna evolved in darkness and have never, in their entire evolutionary history, had cause to develop sight. The light is an accident of their environment. They ignore it the way you ignore the cosmic microwave background.

There are more tenders than you would guess. The walls, seen up close, are not smooth. They are textured with life — the tenders, the biofilm, the fine mesh of secondary growths that colonize every surface. The tenders are the visible apex of a maintenance ecology that extends down through scales of size: mites that clean the tenders' own segments, microorganisms that break down what the tenders discard, smaller organisms still that live on the breakdown products. A whole pyramid of service, invisible at its base, visible at its tip, and all of it running, all of it maintained, all of it unchanging.

## The Sap Dwellers

These are the ones you cannot see clearly, and they are the ones that stay with you.

They live in the vascular bundles. Inside them. In the sap.

The vascular bundles overhead are thick as a human torso, and they are not opaque. The outer membrane — the cambium, or whatever this organism's equivalent is — has a translucency that varies with the sap's flow rate. When the cellular gates open and the pressure peaks, the vein-wall becomes almost glassy, and you can see into the interior. What you see is amber fluid, faintly luminous, moving with the force of a river. And in the fluid, shapes.

They are long. This is the first thing you can determine — longer than your arm, perhaps longer than you, though the magnification and refraction of the sap make size difficult to judge. They are sinuous, moving in patterns that follow the flow but are not dictated by it. They choose their direction. They are not debris. They are not air bubbles. They are alive.

Their bodies appear dark against the amber sap — dark blue, or dark green, or a color that is both and neither, something that shifts as they move. They are not fish. They are not eels. They are not larvae, though there is something larval about them, a softness, an unfinished quality, as if they never completed their development because development was never the point. They do not have fins. They move by undulation, by a wave that passes from anterior to posterior, and the wave is timed — precisely, inevitably — to the rhythm of the sap's pulse. When the cellular gates open and the flow accelerates, the dwellers move. When the gates close and the flow slows, they rest. They have synchronized themselves to the heartbeat of the organism. They are, in a sense, riding the heartbeat. They are passengers in a circulatory system, and they have been passengers for so long that they have become cargo.

What they eat, you cannot determine. Perhaps the sap itself — its sugars, its minerals, its dissolved compounds. Perhaps microorganisms that live in the sap, a plankton-equivalent that has never been described because no one has ever looked inside a vascular bundle of something this old. Perhaps they eat each other. The sap is an ocean, and oceans have food chains, and the dwellers are simply the largest trophic level in an ecosystem no one knows exists.

They do not leave the veins. This seems certain. Their bodies are adapted to the pressure, the chemistry, the specific viscosity of the sap. They could not survive in air, any more than a deep-sea fish could survive on a dock. The vascular bundles are their world — kilometers of internal plumbing, a network of veins and arteries and capillaries that extends through the entire organism, and they move through it the way whales move through ocean basins: freely, widely, but always within the medium. They have never touched the air. They have never touched the wood. They have never touched anything that is not sap.

Sometimes a dweller will press close to the inner wall of a vein, and the membrane will stretch, and for a moment you can see detail — a mouth, or something that functions as a mouth, featureless and soft, without teeth or mandibles. A smooth, round opening, like the aperture of a sea squirt, filtering. And behind it, the body, dark and flowing, extending into the amber glow until the membrane springs back and the shape is lost.

They are the closest thing the Nave has to mystery. The beetles are understood. The tenders are understood. The birds are understood. But the sap dwellers are interior, hidden, accessible only through the translucent wall of a living vein, and they move in a medium that has never been sampled, and their lives are conducted entirely within the body of something so old it has forgotten its own species. They are the fauna of an inside world, and the inside world is all they know.

## The Birds

They are birds. They are also not birds. They are what birds become when birds have lived in one place for long enough that the place has rewritten them.

They are small — sparrow-sized, finch-sized — and they are the color of the wood, because everything here is the color of the wood, because the wood is the only palette and everything that lives here has been painted from it. Gray-brown. Dark walnut. The amber of old sap. Their feathers are not feathers in the way you think of feathers. They are flat, dense, slightly oily — adapted to the constant moisture, shedding water the way a duck's feathers do, but with none of a duck's buoyancy. These birds do not swim. They have never seen a body of water larger than the trickle in a root-channel.

Their beaks curve downward at the tip. This is the adaptation that defines them. The curve is precise — it matches the radius of the root-channel grooves. A bird can insert its beak into a groove, hook the tip under a beetle, and extract it with a motion so practiced it looks involuntary. The bird and the beetle and the groove are three parts of a single mechanism, and the mechanism is feeding, and it has been operating unchanged for so long that the bird's skull has shaped itself around it. The beak is not a generalist tool. It is a key. It fits one lock.

They nest in the upper reaches, in the crotches where vascular bundles diverge, where the wood is warmest and the amber glow is strongest. The nests are not built from twigs or grass — there are no twigs here, no grass, nothing that is not the organism itself. The nests are built from bark flakes, from desiccated fronds, from the shed exoskeletons of tenders, from anything that falls from the walls and ceiling and can be woven into a cup. They are untidy. They are perfectly functional. They have been built in the same locations for so long that the wood beneath them has developed calluses — thickened patches where the nests sit, the organism's response to the constant presence of a foreign body, a kind of scar that has become a foundation.

The birds call. Brief, sharp, not melodic — a single note, or two notes in quick succession, like the tap of a chisel on stone. The call echoes in the vault, amplified by the space, and then it is absorbed by the roar. The birds call to each other, or to themselves, or to the space. The space does not answer. The birds call anyway. They have always called. The calling is part of the sound of the Nave, as integral as the throb of the sap and the hiss of the capillaries, though quieter, and intermittent, and easy to miss.

They descend to the floor to feed. This is the only time you see them clearly. A bird will drop from its nest — not fly, *drop*, wings folded, falling with the casual trust of something that has never known a predator — and land on the root-wood floor with a sound that is barely audible. It will stand for a moment, head cocked, listening. Then it will move to a channel, insert its beak, and feed. It does not look at you. It does not look at anything. Its eyes are small, dark, functional — it can see, but it does not use sight to hunt. It listens for the click of a beetle's shell against the groove walls. It homes on the sound. The beak descends. The beetle is caught.

The birds eat and return to the heights. The round trip takes less than a minute. It has taken less than a minute for centuries. The birds do not linger on the floor. The floor is not their domain. The floor is the beetles' domain, and the tenders' domain, and the domain of smaller things still. The birds are visitors to the floor. Their domain is the air, the upper reaches, the warm amber spaces between the vascular bundles where the nest-calluses wait.

They do not flock. Each bird occupies a territory — a section of the ceiling, a set of nest-sites, a stretch of channel on the floor below. The territories are ancient. Their boundaries are invisible but respected, maintained by calls and occasional displays — a wing-flutter, a posture, a brief interaction that resolves without contact. The birds have not fought, not truly, in longer than the nests have existed. The territories are so old they are geological. They are borders drawn in a medium that is not maps or scent-marks but simply *habit*, the accumulated weight of ten thousand generations doing the same thing in the same place.

There are perhaps fifty of them. Perhaps a hundred. The space is large enough that they vanish into it, each in its territory, each on its circuit of nest and floor and nest, and the Nave absorbs them the way it absorbs everything: completely, without acknowledgment, as if they were always part of the sound.

## The Ferals

There is something else.

You notice it the way you notice a shift in a pattern you didn't know you were tracking. A change in the quality of the silence between bird-calls. A texture in the air that is different from the ambient moisture. A feeling — not a sight, not a sound, but the animal awareness of being in the presence of something that is aware of you.

They are on the floor, in the far reaches of the Nave, where the light is no different but the space feels larger because the walls are farther away. They are low. They are still. You see them as shapes that do not belong — outlines that break the pattern of root-wood and channel and vine, that are too regular, too intentional, too *composed* to be part of the architecture.

They are mammals.

This is wrong, somehow. Everything else here is arthropod, avian, annelid — things with exoskeletons or feathers, things that belong to the ancient clades of the invertebrate world. Mammals should not be here. Mammals are latecomers, recent, warm-blooded in a place that is cool and ancient and indifferent. But here they are.

They are low-slung, long-bodied, the size of ferrets or large rats, and they are covered in a fine, dense fur that is — of course, inevitably — the color of the wood. Dark. Brown-black. The fur is not dry. It is filmed with moisture, like everything else, and it gleams under the amber light with the same dull sheen as the beetles' shells. Their eyes are not reduced. Their eyes are large, dark, fully functional, and they are open, and they are looking at you.

This is what distinguishes them from everything else in the Nave. They look at you. The beetles do not look at you. The tenders do not look at you. The birds do not look at you. The sap dwellers cannot look at you. But the ferals — they turn their heads, and their eyes find yours, and they hold, and in that holding there is something that the rest of the Nave's fauna does not possess: *assessment*. They are evaluating you. They are deciding what you are.

They move differently from everything else, too. The beetles move with mechanical precision. The tenders move with geological slowness. The birds move in quick, purposeful arcs. The ferals move like animals that are thinking about moving — pausing, considering, choosing a path. They flow over the root-wood floor, through the vines, across the channels, with a fluidity that is not instinct but intelligence. They are solving the problem of the floor in real time, navigating obstacles that the other fauna navigate by rote.

They are omnivores, or they are opportunists, or both. You see one stop at a channel and dip its face to the water — not drinking, not feeding, but *testing*, the way a person might dip a finger in a stream to gauge temperature. It moves on. Another investigates a fallen frond, nosing under it, finding something — a grub, a tender, a fragment of biofilm — and eating it with quick, neat bites. A third sits upright on its haunches, watching the ceiling, tracking the movement of a bird across the vascular bundles. Not hunting. Watching. The way a creature watches that has learned, over very long time, that watching is how you survive.

They are not part of the system. This is the thing you understand about them slowly, the way you understand the stillness of the Nave slowly — by accumulation, by the weight of evidence. The beetles are part of the system. The tenders are part of the system. The birds are part of the system. The ferals are *in* the system but not *of* it. They are interlopers who arrived at some point in the organism's history and adapted, and their adaptation is not complete. It is ongoing. They are still changing. In a place where nothing changes, they are the thing that is changing.

Their fur grows in patterns that suggest molting — seasonal, perhaps, though there are no seasons here, or seasonal in some other rhythm, some internal clock that is not synchronized to the Nave's constancy. They bear young — you see one with a smaller version of itself clinging to the fur of its back, the young one's eyes still half-shut, its body still curled in the fetal posture of something recently born. Reproduction. New individuals. In the Nave, where the organism itself has outlasted its reproductive era, where the birds nest in the same sites for centuries but you never see a fledgling, where the beetles do not die and therefore do not need to be replaced — the ferals are having young. They are the only things here that are *new*.

They do not approach you. They do not flee. They maintain a distance — fifteen meters, twenty — and they watch, and the watching is not fear and it is not curiosity and it is not aggression. It is *attention*. They are paying attention to you because you are something that has changed, and they are the only things in the Nave that recognize change, because they are the only things in the Nave that have experienced it. They were born. They have grown. They have learned. They will, eventually, die. They are temporal creatures in a place that has forgotten time, and they know this about themselves, or they know something like this, something that is not language but is the pre-linguistic awareness of existing in a flow rather than a stasis.

They live in the walls. Not on the walls, like the tenders. In them. In the gaps between the outer bark and the inner heartwood, in the fissures and crevices that the organism's growth has created and never sealed. They have made tunnels — not by burrowing, not by destroying, but by occupying and *inhabiting*, by wearing smooth the surfaces they pass over until the passages are theirs. The organism does not resist them. The organism does not notice them. They are too small, too few, too recent to register against the scale of the Nave's processes. They are mice in a cathedral, and the cathedral does not know they exist.

But they know the cathedral exists. They live in it. They are shaped by it — their color, their size, their moisture-resistant fur, their large eyes adapted to the amber glow. And they are not shaped by it — their intelligence, their adaptability, their temporal lives, their capacity to notice you. They are the Nave's contradiction. They are the thing that proves the Nave is not perfect, not complete, not finished, because in a perfect and complete and finished system there is no niche for a creature that is still becoming. And yet here they are. Still becoming. In the Nave of the Great Sapwood, where nothing changes, something is changing.

## What Does Not Live Here But Is Here

There is the organism itself.

It is not fauna. It is not flora in any way that word usefully captures. It is the place. It is the walls and the floor and the ceiling and the sound and the light and the water and the air. It is the factory and the factory's product and the factory's fuel. It does not live *in* the Nave. It *is* the Nave, and the Nave is it, and the distinction between habitat and inhabitant dissolved so long ago that the distinction itself is the only thing that has truly died here.

It does not think. This seems important to say, because the space feels aware — the sound like a voice, the breathing walls like respiration, the light like attention. But it does not think. It processes. It runs. It works. The throb in your sternum is not a heartbeat in any meaningful sense; it is a pressure differential in a vascular system. The hiss is not breath; it is capillary action. The groan is not suffering; it is stress. The organism has no more consciousness than a liver has consciousness, and a liver is what it most resembles — a vast, functioning, self-maintaining organ, doing its work in the dark, unware of the body it belongs to, if it belongs to any body at all.

But it is here. It is the largest presence. It is the presence that contains all other presences. And when you stand in the Nave and feel the sound in your bones and the moisture on your skin and the faint warmth of metabolic processes happening in the walls around you, you are inside something, and that something is alive, and it does not know you are there, and it will continue not knowing you are there long after you have left, and it will continue, and continue, and the engine will run, and the sap will flow, and the beetles will travel their circuits, and the tenders will tend, and the birds will call their single notes into the roar, and the ferals will watch from the shadows with their large dark eyes, being new, being temporary, being the only things in this place that know what temporary means.

And that is who lives in the Nave of the Great Sapwood.
