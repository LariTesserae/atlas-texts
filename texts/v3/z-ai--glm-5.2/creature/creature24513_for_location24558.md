# What Lives in the Narrows of Ghé

---

Let me start with what you would never notice, because nothing notices them, because they are the medium in which everything else exists.

The mites. Or the things that occupy the place of mites. Eight-legged or twelve-legged — the taxonomists who have been here disagree, and the specimens they collected decomposed before they reached a laboratory — they live on every surface. Every leaf, every stone, every strip of bark, every petal that falls. They are so small that a single one would fit on the head of a pin with room to spare, and there are so many of them that if you could somehow strip them from a square meter of the Narrows and hold them in your hand, the mass would be considerable. Ounces. Perhaps more. They are the reason decomposition happens at the speed it does here. They are the first to arrive on anything dead and the first to arrive on anything living that has weakened enough to be considered *almost* dead. They do not make sound. They do not make light. They are the closest thing the Narrows has to silence, and even they are not silent — if you press your ear to a fallen log and listen past the roar, you can hear them. A faint dry scratching, like sand settling. Like something eating. Which is what it is.

They are always eating.

---

Above the mites, the fungi. And I do not mean above them taxonomically. I mean physically. The fungi grow *on* the mites, on the mites' waste, on the byproducts of mite digestion. The threadlike white molds that coat fallen wood within hours — those are mite-fed. The mites break the outer structure of the wood, the fungal hyphae enter the cracks, and together they reduce a trunk to pulp in days. The relationship is not symbiotic. It is something more intimate than symbiosis, something that doesn't have a clean name. The mites do not benefit from the fungi. The fungi do not benefit from the mites. They simply *are* together, in the same place, doing the same work, and neither could accomplish it alone.

The bioluminescent patches on the basalt are a different matter. Those are not feeding on mites or on wood. They are feeding on the rock itself. The stone is young, still mineral-rich, and the fungi — if they are fungi, and there is some argument that they are not, that they are something without a kingdom yet — extract trace elements from the basalt through a process that no one has fully described. The cold blue-green light is a byproduct. A waste emission. The light that lets you stumble through the Narrows is the excretion of something eating stone.

The gelatinous black fungus in the crotches of living trees is something else again. It does not glow. It pulses. The pulse is slow — perhaps once every four seconds, a visible swelling and contraction, like a heartbeat seen through skin. It is warm to the touch. Warmer than the air, warmer than the tree it sits on. Someone once took a sample and described, in notes that survived longer than the sample did, a structure that resembled muscle fiber more than mycelium. The pulsing does not correspond to any external rhythm. Not the cicada drone, not the frog calls, not the settling of the basalt. It has its own time. Whatever that time is, it is not shared with anything else in the Narrows. It is the only thing here that seems to exist at its own pace.

No one has asked what it is doing. The people who have been here have been too overwhelmed to ask questions that specific. But it is doing something. It is doing something with that pulse.

---

The cicada-analogs. Let me give them a name, because they deserve one and because calling them an analog is a refusal to see them. They are *ghé-clickers*, in the local nomenclature of the few who use local nomenclature. Broad-bodied, dark-shelled, membrane wings. They sit on the trunks of the trees, on the underside of leaves, on the root networks that run across the basalt, and they call.

The calling mechanism is not entirely understood. True cicadas use tymbal organs — ribbed membranes that buckle under muscle tension. The ghé-clickers have something similar but located differently, on the ventral surface of the abdomen rather than the dorsolateral position, and the frequency they produce is lower than any known cicada. The sustained drone — the one you feel in your teeth, in your sternum, in the fluid of your eyes — runs at roughly 43 hertz. It is continuous. It has been continuous, as far as anyone can tell, for as long as the Narrows has existed. No one has ever recorded silence from the ghé-clickers. There is always a population calling. When one tires, another begins, and the overlap is seamless, so that the drone is not a series of individual calls but a single sound produced by a collective that does not know it is a collective.

They do not respond to disturbance. You can walk through a cluster of them, brush them from their perches, and they will land and resume calling without interruption. They do not respond to light. They do not respond to vibration. They do not respond to each other. As far as anyone has been able to determine, the call is not communicative. It is not a mating signal, not a territorial claim, not a warning. It is simply what they do. It is their mode of existence. They are calling the way a heart is beating — not *to* anything, not *for* anything. Just because the cessation of the call would be the cessation of the organism.

There are other insects. Hundreds, perhaps thousands of species. The clickers that run in irregular bursts occupy a different acoustic frequency, a higher register that sits *on top of* the drone like foam on a wave. The wet-stone-strikers — a species of beetle-analog that produces sound by snapping a specialized thoracic plate against its elytra — fill in the gaps, the micro-silences that exist not in the drone itself but in your perception of it. And beneath all of it, in the leaf litter and the thin soil, things that are almost certainly insects but that have not been seen — only felt, as movement underfoot, as a subliminal tickle of leg against skin when you kneel and put your hand on the ground.

The soil is more animal than mineral. If you scoop a handful and hold it, it writhes.

---

The frogs.

I need to describe the frogs carefully because they are, in a sense, the voice of the Narrows. Not its body — the vegetation is its body — but its voice, the part of it that speaks.

The largest species is the one with the bioluminescent throat sac. It is called, by the very few who have named it, the *drum-frog*, and the name is apt. It is fist-sized, dark green, with skin that has a waxy sheen, as if it has been rubbed with the same oil that coats every surface in the Narrows. Its throat sac, when inflated, is translucent, and the bioluminescence is not steady but pulsing — a slow, rhythmic glow that syncs with the call, so that each thump of sound is accompanied by a pulse of cold light. When a chorus of drum-frogs calls together, the light is almost enough to read by. Almost. It strobes across the understory in waves, illuminating fern fronds and root networks and the slick surface of the basalt in frozen frames, like lightning in a dark room.

The drum-frog call is the concussion you feel in your lungs. It is a single note — low, percussive, more felt than heard — and it is produced by the sac expanding and contracting with enough force that you can see the skin vibrate. A single drum-frog calling is a thump. A hundred calling in unison is a *detonation*. The waves move across the terrain like weather, as if the frogs are passing the signal from one to another, each pool triggering the next, so that the sound rolls through the Narrows in pulses that you can track if you stand still and focus, and if the sound has not already disoriented you past the point of focus.

There are smaller species. The piping frogs — thumbnail-sized, pale, almost translucent — that produce a high, clear note like a glass harp, and that live in the film of water that coats the surfaces of leaves during the humid intervals. They are not aquatic in the sense of living in pools. They live in the *dampness itself*, in the layer of moisture that exists on every surface, and they are so small and so fragile that a dry period — a few hours without condensation — can kill them. They breed on leaves. Their eggs are laid in the film of water on a single broad leaf, and the tadpoles — if that is what they are — develop in that film, swimming in a centimeter of water, and they mature in days, because the leaf will not stay wet for longer than that. They are the most ephemeral things in the Narrows. They live and die in the time it takes a leaf to dry.

And the burrowing frogs. The ones that survive the draining of the pools. They are squat, muscular, the color of the mud they live in, and when the water sinks through a fissure in the basalt, they dig. They dig down into the mud with powerful hind legs, and they secrete a mucus that hardens into a cocoon, and inside that cocoon they wait. Their metabolism drops to something that is barely distinguishable from death. Their heart beats once an hour. Once a day. Once a week, perhaps. They wait for the water to return, and when it does — when a new pool forms over them, when the mud softens — they emerge. They emerge *ravenous*. They eat everything. Insects, smaller frogs, each other. They emerge from the mud and they eat with a ferocity that is not hunger but something closer to resurrection, as if they are trying to consume enough of the world to justify returning to it.

Some of them have been waiting a very long time. There is no way to know how long. The cocoon does not degrade in the mud. It is possible — and this is speculation, but speculation grounded in what is known of similar species in other places — that some of the burrowing frogs in the Narrows have been waiting since before the Narrows existed. That they were here when the basalt was still flat coastal ground, when the forest was something else, when the world was different. That they have been sleeping through the entire emergence of this place. That they are older than the trees.

When they emerge, they call. A deep, grating sound, like a stone dragged across another stone. It is the ugliest sound in the Narrows, and it is the oldest.

---

The birds — or the things that occupy the niche of birds — are called *echoes* by the few people who have spent enough time in the Narrows to name them. Not because they echo, but because that is what they use to see.

They are dark-feathered. Nearly black, though in the faint fungal light the feathers have a sheen that is not quite blue and not quite green, a color that exists only in the Narrows, that has never been seen in daylight by a human eye. Their eyes are large — absurdly large, dominating the skull, adapted to the near-total darkness of the understory — but they do not see with them. The eyes register the bioluminescence, perhaps, the cold strobes of the drum-frogs, the faint glow of the fungi on stone, but they do not see *shape*. What the echoes see is sound.

Their clicks are sharp, directional, produced by a structure in the throat that is more sophisticated than any known echolocation organ. Each click is a pulse of sound that radiates forward in a narrow cone, and the return — the echo — is processed by the large eyes, which contain not photoreceptors but mechanoreceptors, cells that respond to vibration rather than light. The echoes see the world in sound. They see the trees as columns of density, the vines as threads of texture, the frogs as warm, moving shapes, the insects as clouds of tiny reflections. Their world is not dark. It is not light. It is *sonic*. It is a world built of return.

They feed in the canopy, on the insects, on the frogs that climb to escape the ground-level predators. They feed on each other, sometimes. The larger species of echo — there are at least four, possibly more — will take the smaller ones if the opportunity presents itself, and the smaller ones will take the chicks of the larger ones from unguarded nests. The canopy is a war. It is a continuous, invisible, aerial war conducted entirely by sound, in a medium of darkness, by creatures who have never seen the sky they fly beneath.

Their calls — the ones that are not echolocation clicks, the ones that are *communication* — are the most variable sounds in the Narrows. The scream is a territorial announcement, a sound that carries through the understory like a blade, cutting through the drone and the frog chorus and everything else. It is the sound that gives the Narrows its choral quality. When six or seven echoes scream from different heights, in different registers, the overtones interact — they beat against each other, they create difference tones, they produce phantom frequencies that exist in the air but not in any single call. The Narrows *harmonizes*. Not by design. By density. By the sheer number of sounds pressing against each other until they create sounds that no individual is making.

The warbling call — the descending, almost musical one, the one that sounds like grief — is something else. It is produced by a single species, a medium-sized echo that nests in the crotches of trees, in the same spaces where the pulsing black fungus grows. The two are often found together. The relationship is not understood. The warbling call is not territorial. It is not a mating call. It is produced at irregular intervals, by solitary individuals, usually at night — though in the Narrows the concept of night is arbitrary — and it descends through the canopy like something falling, like something being lowered into a grave. It is the only sound in the Narrows that sounds *intentional*. That sounds as if it means something. That sounds as if something here is not merely existing but *expressing*.

No one knows what it is expressing. The people who have heard it do not agree on what it makes them feel. Some say grief. Some say longing. Some say nothing — just sound, just physiology, just a vocal apparatus doing what it does. But the people who say that are usually the ones who stayed the shortest time.

---

The lizards — or the things that occupy the niche of lizards — are the silence.

Everything else in the Narrows calls, drones, clicks, screams, warbles, thumps. The lizards do not. They are the only large animals in the Narrows that are entirely mute. They have no vocal apparatus. They produce no sound voluntarily. They are dark-scaled, low-slung, with bodies that hug the basalt and the root networks, and they move with a fluidity that is almost disturbing — as if they are not walking but flowing, as if they are liquid animals poured across the stone.

They are fast. Faster than anything else in the Narrows. They hunt by ambush, by speed, by the sudden absence of distance between themselves and their prey. They eat frogs. They eat insects. They eat the smaller echoes when they can catch them on the ground. They eat each other, in the larger species, and the smaller species eat the eggs of the larger. The Narrows does not have a food chain. It has a food *circle*, and the lizards are the animal that closes it.

Their eyes reflect the fungal light. Not by design — the tapetum lucidum behind the retina is a simple reflector, and it catches whatever light is available — but the effect is startling. In the darkness, in the faint blue-green glow, you see pairs of lights at ground level, candle-bright, moving. They move in patterns that seem purposeful but that you cannot parse. They approach and retreat. They cluster and scatter. They vanish when the drum-frogs strobe and reappear in the darkness between pulses. If you stand still long enough, they approach you. They come close — close enough to touch — and you feel the warmth of them, which is the warmth of the air, which is the warmth of your own skin, and you cannot tell where the lizard ends and you begin.

They are studying you. They are studying you the way they study everything — by vibration, by the sound you make when you breathe, by the heat you radiate, by the disturbance you create in the sonic landscape of the Narrows. You are, to them, a moving absence of sound, the way they are to you. You are both the same kind of thing: a hole in the noise, a silence that walks.

---

The large things. The things that make the tracks.

Three-toed. Broad. Deeply pressed into the thin soil. The tracks are the size of a human hand spread wide, and the depth of the impression suggests an animal that weighs considerably more than a human — two, three times as much, perhaps more. The stride is short for the size of the animal, which means the legs are short, which means the animal is low to the ground. Wide-bodied. Heavy. Moving through the understory by pushing through the vines rather than climbing over them.

You hear them before you see them — or rather, you hear the *damage* they do. The cracking of vines. The scrape of a broad body against bark. The dislodging of stones from the terrace edges. The sound is distinctive against the background roar because it is *mechanical* — it is the sound of something physical displacing other physical things, and it cuts through the biological noise the way a rock cuts through water. When you hear it, your body responds before your mind does. Something in the mammalian hindbrain recognizes the sound of a large animal moving nearby, and you go still.

You do not see them. The people who have been to the Narrows — and there are not many, and they do not return often — have not seen them. The tracks are there. The sound of their passage is there. The vines reform behind them so quickly that you cannot even find the path they took — within an hour, sometimes less, the understory has closed the wound, and the only evidence that anything passed is the tracks in the soil, and even those are being colonized by mites and fungi and will be gone by morning.

What are they? The honest answer is that no one knows. The tracks are three-toed, which suggests something reptilian, or avian, or something between. The size and the gait suggest something that has no close analog in any known ecosystem. The behavior — the nocturnal (in a place where nocturnal is meaningless), the unseen, the heard-but-not-seen — suggests an animal that has adapted so thoroughly to darkness that it has no need of visibility, no need of the light that even the echoes and the lizards use in their own ways. It lives below the layer of perception. It exists in the Narrows the way the basalt exists — as something you know is there by inference, by the effect it has on the things around it, but that you cannot directly encounter.

It is possible that they are the reason the Narrows feels the way it does. It is possible that the feeling of being watched — and everyone in the Narrows feels watched, sooner or later — is not paranoia. It is possible that the large things have senses that detect you not by sound or smell or vibration but by something else, something that has no name because no one has survived long enough in the Narrows to name it. It is possible that they are aware of you from the moment you enter, and that they track you throughout your time there, and that they choose not to reveal themselves.

Or it is possible that they are simply animals. Heavy, slow, blind animals pushing through the undergrowth, eating whatever they find, leaving tracks in the mud, and dying and being decomposed in a week like everything else in the Narrows.

I do not know. The Narrows does not tell.

---

The things in the water.

The pools form in the depressions between terraces, and they last a day, sometimes two, and in that time they are full of life. Not life that has colonized them from outside — life that was already *there*, in the mud, in the dry depression, waiting. When the water fills the space, the life emerges. Eggs that were laid in the previous cycle hatch. Dormant adults revive. The population of a single pool can go from zero to dense in hours.

What lives in the pools is harder to describe than anything else in the Narrows because it is the most ephemeral and because the water is too dark with tannins and ash to see through. You see ripples. You see the displacement of fungal light on the surface. You see, sometimes, a shape — a silhouette, a suggestion of movement below — but you do not see the thing itself.

There are serpentine shapes. Long, sinuous, moving through the water with an undulation that suggests eels or something like eels. But there are also walking shapes — things that move across the bottom of the pool on legs, disturbing the mud, sending up clouds of sediment that catch the bioluminescent light and glow briefly before settling. And there are things that stay still. Things that sit on the bottom and do not move and that you would mistake for stones if you did not see them blink, or shift, or slowly extend a limb and retract it.

They are all temporary. When the pool drains — and it will drain, suddenly, through a fissure that opens in the basalt — they go with it. Into the mud. Into dormancy. Into the dry husk that waits for water. Some of them may be the same species as the burrowing frogs. Some of them may be something else entirely. Some of them may be larval forms of things that, in their adult stage, live in the canopy or on the ground or in the spaces between. The Narrows does not have clean categories. Its life does not sort. The pools are a stage in a life cycle that is so complex and so fast that no one has traced it from beginning to end, because by the time you have identified the beginning, the end has already happened, and the beginning has begun again.

---

And then there are the things I have not described.

The things in the canopy that are not echoes and not insects. The shapes that move in the upper tiers when a shaft of light penetrates a canopy gap — shapes that scatter, that are gone before the understory explodes upward to close the gap. The things that live in the flowers, that pollinate them, that have never been seen because the flowers bloom only in the light and the light is rare.

The things in the basalt itself. In the fissures. In the spaces between the stones. The Narrows is still settling, still shifting, and the cracks that open and close may open into spaces that are not empty. No one has looked. No one has been able to look. The cracks are too narrow, too transient, too deep.

The thing that the pulsing black fungus is connected to. Because it is connected to something. The pulse is too regular, too independent, too *purposeful* to be a byproduct. The fungus is not eating the tree. It is not eating the stone. It is doing something else. Something that involves rhythm, that involves heat, that involves a timescale that is not the timescale of the Narrows. Something patient. Something that does not grow fast, does not decompose fast, does not compete for light or space or nutrients. Something that *waits*.

And the thing that makes the sound that is not any of the sounds I have described. The sound that you hear when you have been in the Narrows long enough for your ears to stop trying to process the roar and start trying to find *patterns* in it. The sound that is below the drone, below the clicks, below the frog chorus and the echo screams and the warbling and the wet-stone-striking. A sound that is almost below hearing. A sound that you feel in your bones, in the marrow, in the place where blood is made. A sound that might be the basalt settling. Might be the fungus pulsing. Might be the large things, communicating in a register that your ears cannot resolve as language. Might be nothing. Might be the sound of the Narrows itself — not any individual in it, but the *whole*, the total, the compressed sum of every living thing in this place calling and clicking and screaming and growing and dying and being born all at once, a sound that is not sound but *existence*, the noise of matter insisting on being alive.

---

What lives in the Narrows of Ghé?

Everything. Everything that can live in darkness and heat and damp and noise and speed and impermanence. Everything that does not need to be seen. Everything that does not need to last. Everything that has found a way to exist not despite the chaos but *as* the chaos, not despite the noise but *as* the noise.

What lives here is life in a condition that most life would call unlivable. Not hostile — the Narrows is not hostile, it is too busy to be hostile — but *unrelenting*. It is life without pause, without rest, without the courtesy of silence or the mercy of space. It is life compressed into a narrow place between sea and stone, between darkness and warmth, between the speed of growth and the speed of decay, and it is life that has responded to that compression not by diminishing but by *compounding*, by filling every frequency, every surface, every cubic centimeter of air and water and soil with more of itself.

What lives in the Narrows is everything that lives in the Narrows. And that is, as nearly as anyone can tell, more species per square meter than any other place on the coast, perhaps any other place that has been measured. Not because it is rich. Because it is *fast*. Because the turnover is so rapid that evolution does not have time to settle on a winner. Because every gap is filled immediately by something different, and every pool is colonized by something new, and every death is an opportunity for something that was not there before.

The Narrows does not have inhabitants. It has *inhabitance*. It is a place where the concept of "who lives here" has no stable answer, because the answer changes from hour to hour, from pool to pool, from stone to stone. The question is not who lives here. The question is what is living here *right now*, and by the time you formulate the question, the answer has changed.

But the sound does not change. The sound is the one constant. The specific individuals change, the species shift, the populations rise and fall and rise again, but the sound — the vast, layered, compressed, screaming, droning, clicking, weeping, thumping, pulsing sound — is always. It was here before you arrived. It will be here after you leave. It will be here after the basalt has finished settling and the canopy has finished growing and the soil has deepened and the Narrows has become something else, something older, something slower.

The sound is what lives here. Everything else is just making it.
