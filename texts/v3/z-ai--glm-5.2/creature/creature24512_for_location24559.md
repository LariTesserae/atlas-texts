# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that almost nothing here is *from* here.

This is a place without a deep past. Every living thing in the Installed Sea is a migrant, a squatter, an opportunist that arrived after the water did. There are no endemic species. There is no evolutionary history written into these floor plates. Every organism here is running old software in new hardware — ancient instincts bumping against grooved steel and finding it, if not familiar, then at least usable. A cod does not know that the overhang beneath pumping station 44-N is not a rock ledge. It knows only that the overhang provides shade, and shade means ambush cover, and ambush cover means food. The cod is not wrong. The overhang does provide these things. The cod lives here now, and its logic is sound, and the fact that the overhang is a manufactured structure bolted to a concrete pour in a sea without a seabed does not trouble it in any way that matters.

This is the primary quality of life in the Installed Sea: **it has settled in without deliberation**. There was no colonization event. No moment of discovery. The fish simply found the edges of the installation — the places where the manufactured floor plates meet the remaining natural substrate, that jagged borderland where poured concrete gives way to old silt — and they crossed over. One at a time, or in small groups, or in the case of the pollock, in vast milling columns that poured through the boundary zone like smoke through a doorframe. They came because the current carried them, or because prey had moved in and the predators followed, or because the warm vents were drawing them in with the promise of thermal regulation in seven-degree water. They came for the same reasons animals go anywhere: because there was shelter, and warmth, and food, and space.

They are still coming.

## The Fish

The dominant vertebrate life is unremarkable. This cannot be overstated. These are not strange creatures. They are the animals you would expect to find in a cold northern sea — because they *are* the animals of a cold northern sea, transplanted without their knowledge or consent into a built environment that happens to meet their needs.

**Cod** are everywhere. *Gadus morhua*, the Atlantic cod, heavy-headed, mottled in browns and greens that match nothing in this grey landscape but which they wear anyway, like visitors who have not dressed for the occasion. They move with the current when it suits them and against it when it does not, and they position themselves behind the structural baffles — the upright concrete panels installed to redirect water flow — where the current eddies and slows. There they wait. The cod is a patient predator. It does not need a reef. It needs a place where small things move slowly, and the Installed Sea is full of such places: the quiet zones behind every pump housing, the still pools that form in the rectangular depressions where floor plates have been set slightly lower than their neighbors, the shadowed gaps beneath the walkway grates that run between processing towers. The cod have found all of these. They have claimed them with the territorial pragmatism of fish that have survived ice ages and bottom trawls and the collapse of their populations and do not intend to be displaced by something as abstract as a manufactured environment.

They hunt the **pollock**, which are here in staggering numbers. *Pollachius virens*, greenish-silver, fast, moving in schools so dense they cast shadows on the floor plates — shadows that move and shift and merge with the shadows of the fish themselves until you cannot tell, looking down from a processing platform, what is fish and what is dark and what is the grid of light from above filtered through ten thousand bodies. The pollock do not live here so much as *flow through* here. They are a current of animals within the managed current of water, circling the installation in wide loops that take them past the same structures again and again. They do not sleep, exactly. They slow. They gather in the thermal columns at certain hours — if hours mean anything here, where the light does not change — and ride the warm upwellings in loose spirals, and then they regroup and move on. They are the closest thing the Installed Sea has to a circulatory system of its own.

There are **herring**, too, though fewer. They came in through the boundary zones and moved immediately for the lit areas, drawn to the lamp arrays overhead with the same phototactic certainty that draws them to the surface on summer evenings. They cluster directly beneath the brightest fixtures, their bodies catching the white-amber light and scattering it into silver fragments. They are beautiful in a way that nothing else here is beautiful, and they do not know it, and they will die here in the beaks of the squid that come down from the ceiling at intervals to feed, and their beauty will not have mattered to anything except, perhaps, whatever it is in the squid's optic lobe that registers brightness and movement and triggers the strike.

The **flatfish** are the most unsettling. They have found the floor plates and they have settled onto them as though the plates were natural substrate, and they have done what flatfish do: they have buried themselves. Or tried to. There is no sand here. There is no silt. The floor is grooved steel and curing concrete, and the flatfish — plaice, mainly, *Pleuronectes platessa*, with their twisted faces and their mottled backs — lie flat against the manufactured surface and press their bodies into the grooves between plates and wait. They are camouflaged for a seabed that is not here. Their mottling matches sand and gravel and fine sediment, and against the grey industrial floor they are perfectly visible, perfectly obvious, and they do not know this, and they lie there with both eyes on the same side of their head staring up at the manufactured sky, and they are waiting for prey that will never come to them because prey can see them, and they are waiting for predators that will come to them because predators can see them, and they are the one species here that the installation has genuinely disadvantaged. They are animals adapted for a world that has been removed and replaced, and they have not noticed. They will probably persist in small numbers. They will probably always look out of place.

There are no sharks here yet. This is worth noting. The boundary zones are open. The installation does not exclude large predators. But the sharks have not come, and the reason, if there is one, is not clear. Perhaps the noise. Perhaps the light. Perhaps they simply have not found it yet. Their absence is conspicuous because everything else that swims has arrived. The sharks are the one obvious missing category, and the fish that live here behave as though they know it — with a boldness, a lack of flighty caution, that suggests the absence of the apex predator has already begun to change the community. The cod are bolder than cod should be. The pollock school more loosely than they would in open water. The ecosystem is settling into a shape that includes no top-down control except, possibly, the squid.

## The Squid

The squid are different.

Everything else here stumbled in and made do. The squid arrived and **began to learn**.

They are not a single species. The bulk of them appear to be *Todarodes sagittatus*, the European flying squid, a fast-growing, short-lived, open-water animal that should not, by its nature, be interested in structure. But there are others — smaller, darker, with shorter mantles and longer arms — that may be *Alloteuthis*, a genus of inshore squid that would naturally associate with substrate. The two types do not school together. They occupy the same space in different ways: the *Alloteuthis* stay low, near the floor plates, moving in and out of the conduit housings and the gaps between structural elements with the quick, darting movements of animals that are used to having things to hide behind. The *Todarodes* stay high, in the thermal columns, in the warm layer that collects near the ceiling lamps.

It is the *Todarodes* that have done something noteworthy.

They have discovered the ceiling.

This sounds simple. It is not simple. A squid in open water moves through a featureless medium. There is no up except the direction of light, and there is no down except the direction of pressure. The installation has given the squid something they have never had: a ceiling. A surface above them that is not water but solid, and warm, and studded with bright objects that generate heat. The squid have responded to this with a behavior that is, if not unprecedented, then at least unusual: they have colonized it.

You can see them there, clustered in groups of twenty or thirty, hovering in the warm water just below the lamp fixtures, their mantles tilted slightly upward, their arms trailing below them like the tassels of something suspended. They are not resting. Squid do not rest in the way that fish rest. They are *holding position* — maintaining their place in the thermal layer with minute adjustments of their fins, the broad muscular flaps along their mantles beating in slow, steady rhythms that look, from below, like breathing. They are warm. They are lit. They are in a place no squid has ever been: a manufactured sky, fifty meters above a manufactured floor, in water that moves because machines move it.

And they are communicating.

The color changes are constant. Every squid in every cluster is shifting through patterns — pale to dark, dark to mottled, mottled to barred, barred to a deep reddish-brown that pulses once and fades. The patterns are not random. They are synchronized within groups and differentiated between groups. One cluster will go pale while another goes dark. A single animal will flash a stripe pattern that ripples outward through its neighbors like a wave. These are signals. Chromatophore signals, the same kind of signals squid use in open water to coordinate hunting or mating or alarm, but here they are being used in a new context, in a place with fixed reference points and stable light and consistent temperature, and the squid are — possibly, probably, almost certainly — *adapting their communication to the space*.

The ceiling gives them something to signal about. In open water, a squid's signals float off into the dark, received by whoever is near, meaningful only in the instant. Here, the signals have a backdrop: the lit grid, the lamp fixtures, the flat manufactured surface. The squid can see each other against this backdrop. They can see each other from further away. They can see patterns that would be invisible in open water. And they are, perhaps, producing patterns that would be meaningless in open water but that here, against the steady light of the installed sky, carry information.

No one has studied this. There is no one here to study it. The squid are doing something in the dark water beneath the bright ceiling of a sea that was built last year, and no one is watching except the fish, who do not care, and the flatfish, who cannot see that far up.

The squid also hunt. They come down from the ceiling in coordinated descents — not rapid, not the explosive strikes of open-water predation, but controlled sinks, arms pulled in, fins beating in unison, the whole group dropping through a thermal column like a curtain being lowered. They hit the herring schools at the base of the column and the water erupts in silver and ink and the brief, violent confusion of a feeding event, and then they rise again, slowly, back to the warm layer near the lamps, and they resume their signaling, and their mantles shift through colors that no one is interpreting.

They will die here. *Todarodes* live one or two years. The individuals in the ceiling clusters now are not the ones that arrived first. They are the second or third generation since the installation was flooded. They were born here, in the sense that they hatched from eggs laid in the warm water near the vents, in the quiet pools behind the baffles, in the hollow interiors of conduit housings where the current slows to nothing and the temperature holds steady. They have never known another sea. They have never seen the sun. They have never been in water that was not managed, not channeled, not lit by turbines.

They do not know that the ceiling is not a sky. They do not know that the floor is not a seabed. They know only that there is warmth above and structure below and prey in between, and they have organized themselves accordingly, and they are, in their way, flourishing.

## The Crustaceans

The crabs arrived on foot.

This is the simplest and most comprehensible fact about the Installed Sea's fauna. The crabs walked in. They came across the boundary zone, over the junction where natural substrate meets manufactured floor, and they kept walking, and they are still walking. They cover the floor plates in a slow, deliberate diaspora, their legs clicking on the grooved steel with a sound like fingernails tapped on a table, a sound that is swallowed almost immediately by the turbine noise but that is there, faintly, if you listen for it.

They are **edible crabs**, *Cancer pagurus*, heavy-shelled, dark-backed, with the thick black-tipped claws of animals that crush and tear. They are not fast. They are not graceful. They move across the installed floor with the unhurried confidence of animals that have armor and know it, and they stop at every seam, every joint, every gap between plates, and they probe with their claws. They are looking for food. They are looking for shelter. They are looking, with the mechanical persistence of animals whose behavioral repertoire was fixed millions of years before this floor was poured, for the things that crabs look for: crevices, cracks, burrows, hiding places.

The floor has seams. The seams are narrow — a centimeter, perhaps two — but they are there, and the crabs find them, and the smaller crabs, the juveniles with shells the width of a fist, wedge themselves into these seams and sit there, half in and half out, their claws extended, their eyestalks tracking the passage of fish above them. They have found the equivalent of rocky crevices. They have found the crab habitat that exists everywhere in every ocean where there is hard substrate: the gaps. The installation is nothing if not gaps. Every plate meets its neighbor at a seam. Every structure is bolted down with fasteners that leave recesses. Every conduit housing has entry points. Every manifold junction has access panels. The Installed Sea is, from a crab's perspective, a vast and continuous landscape of hiding places, and the crabs are spreading through it with the slow inevitability of water finding cracks.

They eat what the current brings. Organic matter drifts through the installation — carried by the managed currents, caught in the eddies behind structures, settling into the low points and the still pools. It is the detritus of the surrounding ocean: dead plankton, fish feces, fragments of tissue, the slow rain of biological material that falls everywhere in the sea and that here, instead of settling into silt, settles onto steel and concrete. The crabs find it. They pick at it with their claws. They are the cleanup crew. They are doing the job they do on every seabed in the northern hemisphere, and they are doing it here, on a floor that was poured six months ago, and they do not care.

There are **shrimp**, too, smaller and faster, translucent things that dart in and out of the conduit housings in tight schools. They are less visible than the crabs but probably more numerous. They occupy the mid-water near the structures, clinging to the surfaces with their pleopods, picking at biofilm — the thin organic layer that is beginning, just beginning, to form on the steel and concrete. The biofilm is the first stage of colonization. Bacteria first, then diatoms, then the protozoans that feed on them, then the shrimp that feed on those. It is the start of a food web that did not exist here eighteen months ago. The shrimp are the proof that it is working.

And there are **amphipods** — small, laterally compressed crustaceans that crawl and swim in equal measure, that occupy the filament-plants near the warm vents and the seams between floor plates and the undersides of every structure where the light does not reach. They are the smallest fauna visible to the naked eye. They are also, probably, the most important. Everything eats them. The cod eat them. The pollock eat them. The squid eat them. The shrimp eat them. They are the base of the food web, the organisms that convert detritus and biofilm and filament-plant matter into animal tissue, and they are here in numbers that are already, after such a short time, difficult to overstate. You can see them in the warm pools behind the baffles, hundreds of them, moving in the still water like a living sediment, like the floor itself coming alive.

## The Filament-Plants

It is not clear that they are plants.

They look like plants. They are green-brown, filamentous, flexible, and they wave in the current with the gentle oscillation of seaweed. They are anchored — somehow — to the warmest surfaces: the vents, the pipe joints, the places where heated water meets steel and the steel stays warm. They grow from these anchor points in thin, branching strands that reach into the current and filter the water for whatever it is they filter.

But their origin is ambiguous. The installation is a built environment. Things were installed here. It is possible that the filaments were planted — seeded into the warm zones by whoever built this place, as part of some biological initialization protocol. It is possible that they are engineered organisms, designed for this environment, optimized for steel surfaces and managed currents. They do not look like any local seaweed. They are too thin. Too regular. Their branching pattern is too consistent. Natural seaweeds are messy — they grow in response to current and light and grazing pressure, and their shapes reflect the chaos of those forces. These filaments are orderly. They branch at consistent intervals. They reach consistent lengths. They look designed.

Or they look like the very early stage of something that will, in time, become messy. It is hard to tell. They have been here for only a few months. They have not been tested by storm or grazer or competition. They have not had time to be shaped by the forces that make natural things look natural. They are still in their initial form, the form they had when they arrived — whether through seeding or settlement — and that form is simple, and regular, and strange.

What lives among them: the amphipods, certainly. Small copepods, probably. Nematode worms, almost certainly, though no one has looked. The filaments are beginning to accumulate a community. The warm water around them is beginning to carry a faint cloud of organic matter — exudates, fragments, the chemical signature of living things growing. The fish do not come here yet. The filaments are too thin to provide shelter, too sparse to concentrate food. But the microfauna are here, and where microfauna gather, larger things eventually follow. In a year, perhaps, the filament beds will be dense enough to attract grazing. In five years, they may cover the warm zones in a continuous mat. In twenty years, if they persist, they will be a habitat — a manufactured kelp forest growing on installed substrate in a sea without a seabed.

For now they are what they are: thin green-brown threads in the warm water, waving in the current, testing the air. Probes. Tests. The first word of a sentence whose grammar has not been determined.

## The Things That Are Not Here

There are no mammals. No seals, no dolphins, no whales. The installation's boundary zones are open, but the mammals have not entered. This may be the noise. Marine mammals are acoustic animals, and the Installed Sea is deafeningly loud — the turbine hum, the pump thud, the clatter of machinery. For an animal that navigates and communicates by sound, this place would be painful, disorienting, possibly dangerous. The mammals stay outside. They hunt the fish that gather at the boundary, where the manufactured floor meets the natural substrate, and they do not cross over. The installation is, for them, a wall of noise.

There are no birds. Obviously. There is no surface. There is no sky. The ceiling is sixty meters up and it is solid. The birds are above it, in the real air, and they do not know that below them is a sea that has been replaced.

There are no jellies. This is strange. Jellies are among the most opportunistic animals in the ocean. They colonize new spaces faster than almost anything. But they have not appeared here, or if they have, they have not survived. Perhaps the currents are wrong. Perhaps the pumps pull them in and crush them. Perhaps there is something about the water chemistry — the alkaline cloud of curing concrete, the trace compounds leaching from the steel — that jellies cannot tolerate. Their absence is notable because their presence would be expected. The jellies are the one group that should be here and is not.

There are no deep-sea organisms. The installation is in what was a shallow sea, and the fauna reflect that. There are no anglerfish, no viperfish, no ghostly gelatinous things from the lightless deep. The water is lit, and the animals that came in are animals that can tolerate light, that evolved in light, that expect light. The darkness that exists here — in the conduit housings, in the hollow interiors of structures, in the shadowed undersides of platforms — is shallow darkness, temporary darkness, the darkness of shade rather than depth. Nothing here is adapted for the permanent dark. Everything here can see.

There are no humans. This is the most significant absence. The installation is built. It is functioning. The pumps pump. The turbines turn. The lights are on. But there are no humans. No maintenance crews. No scientists. No one monitoring the calibration of the machinery or the growth of the filaments or the behavior of the squid. Either the humans have left and will return, or the humans have left and will not return, or the humans were never here in the way that would imply occupation — they were here as builders, as installers, as the hands that poured the concrete and welded the plates and turned on the lights, and then they withdrew, and the installation runs on its own, and the animals have moved in, and the humans are gone.

The fish do not notice this absence. The squid do not notice it. The crabs do not notice it. The absence of humans is meaningful only to humans, and there are none here to register it.

## What Is Being Made

Something is being made here. Not by the animals — they are only living, only doing what their bodies tell them to do — but by the place itself. The Installed Sea is a young landscape, and young landscapes become things. They develop. They accrete. They are changed by what lives in them and they change what lives in them, and the process is slow and irreversible and it has already begun.

The biofilm is forming. The amphipods are feeding. The crabs are probing the seams. The squid are learning the ceiling. The filaments are growing. The fish are settling into territories and feeding patterns and daily rhythms that are not daily because there is no day, only the steady flat light of the lamp grid and the managed currents that shift on a schedule that no animal can read. The ecosystem is booting up. It is in its first iteration. It is running the simplest possible version of itself — few species, simple interactions, no established equilibria, no complex food webs, no niche specialization. It is an ecosystem at version 0.1.

But it is an ecosystem.

The concrete will cure and stop leaching alkaline. The steel will begin to corrode — slowly, in cold water, but it will corrode, and the corrosion will change the water chemistry, and the changed chemistry will favor some organisms and disfavor others. The seams between floor plates will widen as the structure settles and the joints loosen. Silt will find its way in — carried by the currents, filtered from the inflow at the boundary zones — and it will settle into the low points and the still pools and it will begin, grain by grain, to cover the manufactured floor with something that looks, eventually, like seabed. The filaments will spread. The biofilm will thicken. Barnacles will come — they always come — and they will roughen the smooth surfaces and create texture where there was none, and the texture will create habitat, and the habitat will create opportunity, and the opportunity will be taken.

In fifty years, the Installed Sea will be harder to recognize. The sharp lines will have softened. The clean surfaces will have fouled. The empty infrastructure — the unused platforms, the unconnected cable runs, the valve stations with nothing to valve — will have been colonized so thoroughly that it will be difficult to distinguish the structure from the life growing on it. The squid will have been here for fifty generations. They will have adapted — to the light, to the ceiling, to the managed currents, to the constant noise. They will be different. Not a new species, probably, but a new behavior, a new way of being squid, shaped by a place that no squid has ever been shaped by before.

The cod will still be there. The cod will always be there. The cod are indestructible in the way that successful animals are indestructible: not by being strong, but by being flexible, by being willing, by being ready to treat any structure as a reef and any current as a meal delivery system and any warm patch as a reason to stay. The cod will be here when the installation is old. They will be here if the pumps fail and the lights go out and the water goes dark and cold. They will be here because they were here before, in the sea that existed before this sea, and they will be here after, in whatever comes next.

What lives in the Installed Sea is a community in its first moment. A community that does not know it is a community. A group of animals and organisms that have been brought together by the accident of a built environment and that are, without intention or awareness, beginning to constitute a thing: a living system, a made sea's first ecology, the opening notes of a composition that will play for as long as the turbines turn and the pumps pump and the lights stay on.

They are the first tenants of a new building. They do not know the landlord. They do not know the lease terms. They have moved in because the door was open and the heat was on and there were places to hide and things to eat. They are making it theirs. They are making it live.
