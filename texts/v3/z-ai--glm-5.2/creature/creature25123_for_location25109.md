# What Lives in the Weeping Works

---

The first thing to understand is that nobody lives here.

That is almost true, and the almost is where everything important happens.

---

The **pale ones** in the channels — call them that, because they have no name and would not answer to one — are the most visible inhabitants, and they are not inhabitants at all. They are *aspects of the water.* This is not metaphor. Watch one for long enough and the distinction between organism and medium begins to dissolve. Its body is translucent, yes, but so is the water it occupies. Its internal structures branch like the fissure systems that feed the channels, like the root systems of the shrubs above, like the mineral deposits branching across the grate. It is shaped like what it lives in. It moves like what it moves through. When the water stops — and it never truly stops, but when it slows to its lowest ebb in whatever cycle governs such things — the pale ones stop too, and they hang in the water like suspended glass, like thoughts waiting for a mind, and they are nearly invisible. When the water moves, they move, and whether they are carried or choosing is a question that implies a distinction they do not recognize.

There are perhaps forty of them. Perhaps more. They overlap in the dimness, and their edges blur, and counting them is like counting the words in a sentence you half-remember. They school, or flock, or chain — the description changes depending on how long you watch. For the first few minutes they seem coordinated, moving in the same direction with a unity that suggests intention. Then one will turn, or the current will shift, and the group will reorganize around a new axis, and you realize that what looked like coordination was just *agreement* — agreement produced by the fact that they all want the same thing, and the thing they want is determined by the water, and the water wants very little.

What do they want? They want the dark-not-dark. They cluster where the luminescence is strongest, which is also where the mineral content is highest, which is also where the water is newest. They are drinking something from the water — not nutrients in any sense a surface ecologist would recognize, but something. The stone here is limestone and ironstone and something older, and the water that percolates through it carries dissolved substances that have names in geology and no names in biology, and the pale ones metabolize these substances and produce the faint light, or hold the faint light, or *are* the faint light. The chain is: stone, water, pale one, dark-not-dark. Remove any link and the system collapses. Nothing here is redundant.

They do not have eyes. They do not have mouths. They have the branching internal structures, which seem to serve every function simultaneously — absorption, circulation, luminescence, perhaps reproduction, perhaps thought, if thought is what you call it when a being processes its environment and responds. They respond. A hand held near the water's surface — not touching, just near — will cause the nearest pale ones to drift away. Not in alarm. In *acknowledgment.* They know you are there. They do not care. You are a temperature fluctuation, a shadow, a momentary displacement of the air that touches their water. You are weather, to them. You will pass.

Their life cycle is invisible because it is identical to their life. They do not seem to die. They seem to *divide* — a pale one will, over the course of what must be weeks or months, grow longer, and then at some point the internal branching will bifurcate in a way that is no longer structural but *intentional*, and the two halves will drift apart, and there will be two where there was one. Whether this is reproduction or fission or something else entirely — whether the two are the same individual or different individuals or whether the concept of individual does not apply — is a question for beings who have individuals, and the pale ones do not.

They are old. Not as individuals — individuals are a fiction here — but as a *form.* They have been in this water since the water found the channels, which is to say since the engineers left and the pumping stopped and the fissure opened or widened or became what it is. They were here before the engineers left, probably. They may have been here before the engineers arrived. The water was here before the engineers. The fissure was here. The stone was here. The pale ones may be as old as the water's passage through the stone, which is to say: they may be immeasurably old, and they may also be brand new, and both of these may be true because time in the channels does not work the way time works on the surface. Time in the channels is measured in water. A gallon of water has passed. Another gallon. The same gallon. The water is always new and always the same, and the pale ones are always new and always the same, and the distinction between being born and continuing to exist is one they have no need to make.

---

The **wall creatures** are different. They are definite. They have boundaries.

You can see them if you bring your face close to the weeping brick — close enough that your breath fogs the stone and your eyes are two inches from the film. The film itself is alive, or nearly alive, or alive in a way that does not require the word. It is dark green where light reaches it, black where it doesn't, and it covers the walls in a velvet nap that is slightly warmer than the surrounding stone — you can feel this with your fingertips, though touching it feels intrusive, like touching someone's skin without asking.

Within the film, the creatures move. They are small — the largest is perhaps a centimeter, the smallest is barely visible — and they are many. They are pale like the channel dwellers, but their pallor is different: chalky, opaque, like unfinished porcelain. They have legs. Too many legs to count, and the legs are so fine they are more like cilia than limbs, and they move the creatures through the film the way fingers move through hair — not on top of it, not under it, but *within* it, parting it, consuming it, excreting it. The film is their habitat and their food and their product. They eat what they live in and they produce what they eat, and this loop has been turning for as long as the walls have wept.

They are not social. They do not coordinate. Each one is a solitary processor, moving through the film on its own trajectory, consuming, excreting, moving on. When two encounter each other — and they do, constantly, in the dense patches — they pause, touch antennae or whatever the fine threads on their heads are, and then continue. They are not communicating. They are *confirming.* Yes, you are here. Yes, I am here. Yes, the film is here. Yes, the wall is weeping. And then they move on, because there is film to process and the wall is always weeping and the work is never done.

The work. What is the work? They are maintaining the film. The film is maintaining the wall. The wall is maintaining the water's passage. The water is maintaining everything. Each layer supports the next, and the wall creatures are the layer between stone and moisture, the negotiators, the interface. They are translators. They translate water into film and film into something the stone can hold, and in doing so they slow the water's passage just enough that the wall weeps rather than streams, that the moisture clings rather than cascades, that the chamber stays damp rather than flooded. They are engineers. They are the new engineers. They do not know this.

They have no nests. They have no rest. They have the film, and the film is continuous, and they are continuous within it. When one dies — and they do die, they must die, though the deaths are invisible because a dead wall creature is indistinguishable from a speck of substrate in the film — the film absorbs it, and the film feeds the next creature, and the loop does not notice the interruption. They are replaceable to themselves. They are the only thing here that does not seem to mind being temporary.

---

The **ceiling nesters** are the most mysterious, because they are the most absent.

The nests are there. Mud and fiber, packed into the mortar gaps, built with a precision that implies hands or mandibles or beaks — some *instrument* of construction, some appendage designed for placement and compression. The fiber is organic: dark, fibrous, like plant matter that has been chewed or decomposed or digested and then regathered. The mud is the chamber's own — the fine clay that washes from the brick over decades, that accumulates in the gaps, that the nesters have repurposed from waste into architecture.

The nests are old. The oldest layers have mineralized, have become part of the brick, have rejoined the stone from which they were derived. The newer layers are softer, darker, still yielding to the touch. There are perhaps thirty nests visible, and some of them are stacked — new construction atop old, the way a city builds on its own ruins. The nesters have been here for generations. They have been here longer than the pale ones, possibly. They have been here since the works were works, since the engineers were engineers, since this was a place with a human purpose. They may have been here before. They may have been nesting in the gaps of the geological formation before the brick was laid, and then the brick was laid, and they continued nesting, because the brick had gaps, and gaps are what they need.

What are they? The droppings suggest something avian or mammalian, but the droppings are strange — they are small, uniform, dark, and they do not decompose the way surface droppings do. They are collected in piles beneath the nests, and the piles host their own miniature ecology of fungi and decomposers, but the decomposition is slow, slower than it should be, as if the droppings contain something that resists breakdown. They do not smell. Nothing in this place smells the way surface biology smells. The droppings have a mineral quality, a stony quality, as if the nesters' digestion extracts everything organic and leaves only the stone.

You do not see them. You will not see them. They are not here now, or they are here and they are so still and so dark and so integrated into the ceiling's architecture that they are invisible. If they are here, they are in the deepest nests, the ones packed furthest into the gaps, the ones where the mortar has receded most completely and the space behind the brick opens into — what? Cavities? Voids? The interior of the wall? They have found spaces that the engineers did not intend and did not know about, spaces that exist in the negative architecture of brick and mortar, spaces that are not rooms but are not solid, and they live in these spaces, and they emerge — to build, to deposit droppings, to do whatever else they do — on a schedule that the chamber keeps and that no surface calendar can predict.

They are the only things here that *leave.* The pale ones stay in the water. The wall creatures stay in the film. The nesters come and go, and where they go is into the wall, into the stone, into the structure of the earth itself, and what they do there is not knowable from this chamber. They may have other chambers. They may have networks of nests extending through the geology for hundreds of feet in every direction, a whole civilization of mud-and-fiber construction hidden in the voids of the limestone. They may connect this chamber to other chambers, other waters, other dark places that the surface world does not know about and does not need to know about. The Weeping Works may be a single room in a vast and unvisited city, and the nesters may be its only citizens.

Or they may be small, simple creatures who found a good wall and made their homes in it and have no further ambitions. This is also possible. The chamber does not distinguish between these possibilities. The chamber is not curious about its inhabitants. It holds them the way water holds things: without opinion, without preference, without any investment in what they are or what they might become.

---

And the **pale growth** in the northeast corner.

This is the hardest to describe, because it is the only thing here that is *becoming* rather than *being.* Everything else in this chamber has arrived at itself. The pale ones are what they are. The wall creatures are what they are. The nests are what they are. The rust is what it is. The water is what it is. But the pale growth — the stack of cups, the white stemless thing pushing from the pooled water — is in the process. It is mid-sentence. It has not finished.

It is not a mushroom. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus, though it may be related to fungi in the way that humans are related to sea squirts — distantly, fundamentally, in a way that matters more to taxonomists than to the organism. It has no chlorophyll. It has no gills. It has no root system, no cap, no stem in any structural sense. It is a *succession of vessels*, each one open at the top, each one holding water, each one slightly smaller than the one beneath, so that the whole structure forms a kind of inverted spiral, a funnel that narrows as it rises, and at the top — at the top is the newest cup, the one still forming, its edges still soft, still growing upward, still reaching for — what? The ceiling? The darkness? The moisture that condenses on the vaulted brick and falls in single drops that land, with some regularity, in the uppermost cup?

Yes. The drops. The ceiling weeps, and the weeping collects on the bricks directly above the growth, and the drops fall — not steadily, but with a rhythm that has something to do with temperature and humidity and the slow breathing of the chamber — and they fall into the cup, and the cup holds the water, and the water feeds the growth, and the growth grows. The growth is a machine for collecting water. It is a pump. It is the fourth pumping engine, the one the engineers did not build, the one that has evolved to fill the function that the other three have abandoned. It does not pump water through channels or up through pipes. It pumps water upward, into itself, into its own structure, and the structure grows, and as it grows it reaches closer to the ceiling, and as it reaches closer to the ceiling the drops fall a shorter distance, and the collection becomes more efficient, and the growth becomes faster, and —

Where does it stop? When the uppermost cup reaches the ceiling? When the growth fills the corner, cups stacked on cups, a tower of vessels climbing toward the brick? Or does it stop before then, or does it never stop, or has it been growing for decades already and is already far larger than it appears, its lower layers buried in the pool, its oldest cups fossilized like the oldest nests, the visible growth just the tip of something that extends downward into the water and into the stone beneath the water and into whatever aquifer feeds the fissure that feeds the chamber?

It is the only thing here that wants. The pale ones do not want. The wall creatures do not want. The nesters, if they want, want elsewhere. But the growth wants. It wants upward. It wants the ceiling. It wants the water that falls from the ceiling. It wants, and its wanting is visible in its posture, in the thrust of its uppermost cup, in the way it leans — and it does lean, very slightly, toward the point on the ceiling where the drops collect most reliably. It leans toward its source. It is the only thing in this chamber that has a direction.

It is also the only thing that produces something for others. The water in its cups is not the same as the water in the channels. It is processed. It is changed. It carries something — the dark-not-dark is faintly present in the cup water, faintly luminescent, and when a cup overflows, which it does occasionally, the overflow runs into the pool, and the pool feeds the channels, and the channels carry the luminescence to the pale ones, and the pale ones metabolize it, and the cycle — the *cycle* — is complete. The growth is the producer. The pale ones are the consumers. The wall film is the decomposer. The nesters are the — what? The observers? The archivists? They build with mud and fiber and they leave droppings that do not decompose, and in their own way they are recording this place, encoding it in mineralized waste and architectural accumulation, and the record they keep is the record of the chamber itself, and the chamber is the record of the water, and the water is the record of the stone, and the stone is the record of time, and time is the record of — this. This place. This slow, patient, weeping persistence of things that have been left alone long enough to find each other and to find the shape of their being together.

---

Nobody lives here.

Everything lives here. The water lives. The stone lives in the way stone lives, which is slowly, which is by being, which is by holding and releasing and holding again. The rust lives, in its way, as a process, as a transformation, as the iron becoming something else, something that will eventually become something else again. The film lives. The pale ones live. The wall creatures live. The nesters live, somewhere, in the gaps. The growth lives, and wants, and reaches.

And the machine lives. The easternmost engine, the one that turns, the one that exhales its wet breath every few minutes — it lives too. Not in the way the organisms live. In the way a heartbeat lives, in the way a tide lives, in the way any rhythm that persists long enough becomes indistinguishable from life. It turns because the water moves it. It breathes because the turning forces air through chambers that were designed for steam and now conduct only moisture and the slow chemistry of corrosion. It has been breathing for decades. It will breathe for decades more, or it will stop tomorrow, and if it stops the chamber will absorb the silence the way it absorbs everything — by being already full of silence, by having so much silence that one more silence makes no difference.

The wheel turns. The water falls. The pale ones drift. The wall creatures process. The nests hold their vacancies. The growth reaches upward, cup by cup, fraction by fraction, toward the ceiling that weeps for it.

This is who lives in the Weeping Works. Not a person. Not an animal. Not a god or a spirit or a ghost. A *community* — though that word implies a closeness, a knowing, a relationship, and the relationships here are not known by their participants. The pale ones do not know the growth. The growth does not know the wall creatures. The wall creatures do not know the nesters. The nesters do not know the machine. The machine does not know anything. And yet they are all here, all in this room, all connected by water and by the slow patience of a place that has outlasted its purpose and found a new one, and the new one is simply this: to be. To weep. To hold what comes and release what goes and to keep, keep, keep, the way stone keeps, the way water keeps, the way darkness keeps the things that live in it.

You will leave. They will not notice. They do not need to notice. They are complete without your noticing, and they were complete before you came, and they will be complete after you leave, and their completeness is not a loneliness, because loneliness requires a self that misses another self, and the selves here are not the kind that miss. They are the kind that *are*. They are the kind that drift, and process, and nest, and grow, and turn, and weep, and the weeping is not grief. The weeping is just what water does. The weeping is just what this place does. The weeping is the sound of something that has become what it is, and is in no rush to become anything else, and does not need you to hear it, and does not need you to understand, and does not need you at all.
