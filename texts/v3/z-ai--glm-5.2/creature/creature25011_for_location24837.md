# What Lives Here

---

They are not what you expect.

Nothing about the Sevvedi Tableland produces what you expect, so this should not surprise, and yet it does, because the mind prepares itself for places the way it prepares itself for conversations — with assumptions it cannot help but make, and that the world, if the world is generous, will quietly dismantle.

The people of Old Karravat are slow.

Not dull. Not simple. Not sluggish in the way that heat makes a body sluggish. They are slow the way a geological process is slow — deliberately, constitutionally, with a patience that is not a choice but a condition, the same way the stone's warmth is a condition. You watch one of them cross a street — a carved groove three meters deep, worn smooth by centuries of exactly this kind of crossing — and you see that there is no wasted motion. Not because they are efficient. Because they have never learned to waste motion. The concept does not exist. Every movement is complete. Every step is a full step. They do not shuffle. They do not hurry. They do not lag. They move at the speed of their place, which is the speed of thermal gradients and mineral erosion and the slow accumulation of dust in channels that will be dust for a thousand years.

---

## Bodies

They are lean. Not thin — lean, in the way that something is lean when it has been refined, when everything unnecessary has been burned away by a long heat. Their bodies are dense with the same economy their movement possesses. Compact joints. Long, dry hands with nails that have a faint metallic sheen — not paint, not decoration, but the same oxidized warmth as the iron strapping on their buildings, as though they have absorbed the tableland into themselves at a level deeper than diet. Their skin is dark, darker than the climate would suggest — not sun-darkened but mineral-darkened, pigmented by the same ferrembric substrate that their livestock graze on, because they are, in the end, eating the same ground the skree eat, processed differently, through different chambers, but from the same source. Their coloring runs to deep reddish-brown, rust-dark, with variations toward umber and a nearly black sepia in the oldest among them. Their hair is dark and fine, worn in ways that suggest function over statement — cut short, bound back, braided flat against the skull. Nothing loose. Nothing that catches wind or snags on stone.

Their eyes are the thing you notice last and remember longest.

They are not what you would call bright. They are not vivid. They are the color of the tableland's sky — pale, washed, almost white at the edges, shading to a thin blue-grey at the center. They do not catch light the way other eyes do. They seem to hold it, the way the stone holds heat — taking it in, releasing it slowly, so that in low light, at dusk, when the memory-light begins to surface in the ground, a person's eyes will carry a faint amber afterglow, as though they too are remembering light that passed through them earlier and are only now letting it go.

They do not startle. You learn this quickly. A flier — one of the rigid-winged hunters — dropped from a thermal column directly overhead on your second day, passing close enough that you felt the displacement of air, and the three people standing in the nearest street-groove did not flinch. They tracked it. Their eyes followed its descent. One of them tilted her head, a motion so slight it might have been involuntary, and then they returned to what they were doing, which was nothing you could identify. Standing. Holding tools, or parts of tools. Being present in a place.

They do not startle because nothing here is sudden. The tableland has trained this out of them, or trained it into them — the understanding that the world operates on a timescale in which suddenness is not a useful category. Things arrive. Things depart. The skree come and go. The memory-light surfaces and recedes. The thermal engines tick through their cycles. None of this is sudden. None of this requires a flinch.

---

## Breath

They breathe less than you do.

This is not immediately obvious. You spend the first days attributing it to altitude, to the thin air, to your own body adjusting to a place where the atmosphere carries less. But then you watch — carefully, the way you have learned to watch here, which is slowly, over long stretches, without expectation — and you realize that their breathing is genuinely slower. Shallower. A breath every several seconds, drawn in through the nose, released through barely-parted lips, the exhalation carrying a faint warmth that you can see in the early morning when the air is at its coolest.

Their lungs, you think, must be different. Not larger — different in structure, in the way they process what they take in. The air here is thin but it is not empty. It carries mineral dust, sulfur compounds, the faint metallic taste of ferrembric sand. They are breathing the tableland, literally, and their bodies are doing something with it beyond oxygen exchange. You do not know what. You do not ask.

Their breath does not fog. Even in the coldest hour before dawn, when the stone has released the last of its stored heat and the air sits at a temperature that should produce visible breath, it does not. They exhale and nothing appears. As though the warmth is kept inside, held, reabsorbed. As though nothing is wasted, not even breath.

## What They Do

They make things.

This sounds simple. It is not simple. The making is the central fact of Old Karravat, the organizing principle around which everything else — the sparse architecture, the carved streets, the thermal engines, the long pipes carrying gases between buildings — is structured. They are makers. They have always been makers. The city exists to make, and the people in it exist to serve that making, and the making itself is so old and so continuous that no one remembers what the first thing made was, or why.

The factories run on heat differential. This much you can observe. The tableland's surface stone, warmed by the sun through the day, holds a temperature significantly higher than the air above it, and the thermal engines capture this gradient through a system of sealed chambers, expansion plates, and slow-turning mechanisms that have been polished to mirrors by their own operation. The engines do not roar. They tick. They creak. They turn with a slowness that is almost geological, and the work they do — the grinding, the pressing, the refining of ferrembric substrate into something else — proceeds at the same pace.

You stand in a factory doorway — the doorway is narrow, high, placed for ventilation — and watch.

A woman works at a press. She is feeding it. In her hands: raw ferrembric, ground to a coarse sand, dark red, warm even in powder form. She feeds it into a hopper. The press descends — slowly, with a weight that you feel in your feet through the floor — and compresses the powder into a dense, dark block. She removes the block. She sets it on a rack. She feeds more powder into the hopper. The press descends. She removes the block. She sets it on the rack.

She has been doing this, you learn, for some time. Not hours. Not days. The rack holds hundreds of blocks. Some of them are old — you can see the color shift, the dark red fading to a deeper brown, the surface oxidizing. The oldest blocks on the lowest racks are nearly black, and they ring when you — when she — touches them, a dull, warm tone, like a bell struck underwater.

She does not explain what the blocks are for. Not because she is secretive. Because the question does not seem to arise. They are blocks. They are made. They go on the rack. Other blocks, older ones, are taken from the lower racks by other people and carried to other buildings, where other processes occur — melting, reshaping, combining with other materials, producing the ingots and powders and pressed shapes that are the tableland's only visible export. Where the exports go, you have not yet determined. They are carried away, somewhere, by routes that do not announce themselves.

In another building — longer, lower, with a roof-line that slopes to match the thermal vents along its spine — a man operates a furnace. Not a combustion furnace. A solar furnace, its aperture angled to catch the tableland's intense light, focusing it through a series of mineral lenses onto a crucible that glows a pale, almost white amber. He tends the crucible. He adds material — ground nightstone, the dark glassy mineral, and something else, a powder the color of the salt-fields — and stirs it with a long iron rod. The mixture does not boil. It does not bubble. It thickens. It develops a viscosity that you would associate with glassmaking, but the color is wrong — too dark, too warm, with a reddish undertone that shifts as it cools.

He pours the mixture into molds. The molds are ceramic, old, their surfaces crazed with a network of fine cracks that have been filled with something darker. He pours with a precision that seems automatic — the right amount, the right speed, the right angle — and the mixture settles into the mold and begins immediately to harden. He does not watch it harden. He moves to the next mold.

These people are not artisans. That word implies a relationship to the object, a personal investment in its form, a satisfaction in the making. They are not artisans. They are something else — something that does not have a word in your language, or in theirs, because they do not need a word for what they are. They make things because that is what is done here. They make things the way the skree graze on stone — because the substrate is available, and the body is built for it, and the process is its own justification.

## How They Live Together

They do not gather.

This is the second surprise. Old Karravat is a city — forty kilometers of structured settlement, factories, carved streets, pipework, infrastructure — and its people do not gather. They live alone, or in pairs, or in small groups that seem more operational than familial. You see a woman and a man working in the same factory, and you assume they are together, and then you watch, and you realize that they are working in the same factory because the factory requires two people, and when the work is done, they leave in opposite directions, and there is no farewell, no acknowledgment, no visible marker that they will return to the same place tomorrow and do the same thing. They simply do.

There are children. You see them — small, quick, darker-eyed than the adults, their movements less controlled, still learning the tableland's pace. They do not play. Or rather — they play, but the play is not recognizable as play. They climb on buildings. They handle materials. They sit in the street-grooves and watch the dust-lice process the stone dust, and their faces hold a concentration that you would associate with study, not play. They are learning. They are always learning. The tableland is the curriculum, and the lesson is: this is how things are. This is what the stone does. This is where the warm air rises. This is how the engines tick. This is how the skree move, and this is how you move among them, and this is how you stand when the memory-light surfaces nearby, and this is how you wait.

They are taught by being present. You do not see a school. You do not see instruction. You see children standing near adults, watching, and the adults not sending them away. The children touch things. The adults do not stop them. The children learn the weight of a ferrembric ingot, the texture of ground nightstone, the temperature at which the salt-fields begin to shift and crack. They learn by handling. They learn the tableland through their hands, the same way the skree learn it through their mandibles.

There is affection. You see it — brief, physical, expressed through proximity rather than speech. A hand on a shoulder. Two people sitting against a wall in the late afternoon, their backs to the warm stone, their shoulders touching. A child leaning against an adult's leg while watching a thermal engine turn. The affection is not performed. It is not declared. It is present the way warmth is present in the stone — quietly, thoroughly, without needing to announce itself.

## What They Say

They speak. You have heard them.

Their language is sparse. Not in vocabulary — in usage. They speak when speech is necessary, and they do not seem to find it necessary often. The words are low-pitched, consonant-heavy, spoken without emphasis, as though volume is a form of waste that the tableland does not permit. You hear exchanges that last three or four syllables and accomplish what appears to be a complete transfer of information. You hear longer conversations — but these are rare, and they seem to occur only at certain times, in certain places: on rooftops at dusk, or in the deep street-grooves where the stone is oldest, or near the edges of the city where the tableland opens out and the sky goes white and the skree are visible on the horizon.

You do not understand the language. You may never understand it. But you begin, after days of listening, to detect patterns. There is a word — or a sound, or a breath — that they use when the memory-light surfaces. It is not a warning. It is not a name. It is something closer to an acknowledgment, the way you might nod to a person you pass on a street: I see you. You are here. The word does not name the light. It seems, instead, to address it.

This should be stranger than it is. But the tableland has already taught you, by the time you notice this, that strangeness is a category that applies poorly here. The stone remembers light. The people acknowledge the stone's remembering. The skree avoid it. The fliers circle it. Everything in this place is in relationship with everything else, and the relationships are old, and no one — not the people, not the animals, not the stone — feels the need to explain them.

## What They Know

They know the tableland.

Not the way a scholar knows a subject — not as information, not as data, not as a collection of facts that can be organized and transmitted. They know it the way a body knows temperature. The way a hand knows the weight of a familiar object. They know where the ferrembric is richest by the color of the surface. They know where the salt-fields will form before they form, because the stone in those areas develops a quality — a texture, a slight change in how it holds heat — that signals the chemistry beneath. They know the skree's routes, not because the routes are fixed but because they have watched the herds long enough to understand the logic of their movement, which is not random but follows a pattern determined by mineral concentration and thermal variation and something else, something the herders — for there are herders, of a sort, people who walk with the skree for days at a time — call by a word that means something like *the stone's appetite*.

They know the memory-light. They know it the way you know weather — not as prediction but as recognition. They feel it coming. Not in the stone, which is where it manifests, but in the air, in the quality of silence, in the way the dust-lice move and the vault-things shift in their deep chambers. The memory-light has a precursor, and the people of Old Karravat can read it, and they move around it the way water moves around stone — not because they are afraid but because the path of least resistance, in this place, is also the path of greatest respect.

They do not know what the memory-light is. This is important. They have lived with it for as long as they have lived here — centuries, millennia, the timescale is uncertain because they do not keep records in the way you would recognize — and they do not know what it is. They have theories. Some of them, the older ones, speak about it in terms that sound almost geological — the stone's way of processing something, a mineral reaction on a timescale too slow to observe directly. Others speak about it differently. They use words that you do not understand, words that seem to refer to something that is not mineral, not chemical, not any category that maps onto your understanding of what stone is and what stone does.

You asked, once, with gestures, with the few words you had picked up, pointing to a patch of memory-light that had surfaced in the street-groove where you were standing. The man you asked — old, dark, his eyes carrying the faint amber afterglow that comes with age and long exposure — looked at the light. Looked at you. Looked at the light again.

He said one word. It was a word you did not know.

Then he put his hand on the stone, flat, palm down, the way you might put a hand on someone's chest to feel their heartbeat. He held it there. The light shifted beneath his palm, moved, surfaced a meter away, and faded.

He removed his hand. He looked at you. He said the word again.

Then he walked away, down the groove, toward a factory where the thermal engines were beginning their evening cycle, and you were left standing in the warm stone channel with the afterimage of amber light in your eyes and the distinct, unshakeable feeling that he had told you everything he could, and that it was enough, and that you would spend a long time understanding what it meant.

## What They Are

You have been here long enough, now, to risk a statement.

They are not human. Or they were human, once, and the tableland has changed them, or they were always something else that happens to look enough like human that the difference did not register until you had been here for weeks and your own body had begun to slow and your own breathing had begun to shallow and you stood on a rooftop at dusk and realized that your eyes were holding light they should have released.

They eat ferrembric. Not directly — not the way the skree do, mandibles on stone — but processed, refined, through the factories, through the ovens, through the slow chemistry of grain and heat. Their food is mineral. Their bodies are mineral. They are built from the same substrate as the ground they walk on, and when they die — and they do die, slowly, without event, sitting against a warm wall or lying in a carved room with the thermal engine ticking its patient rhythm — their bodies are returned to the stone, and the dust-lice process them, and the cycle takes them back.

They are the tableland.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. They are the tableland in the way that a wave is the ocean — a temporary shape, a specific expression, a form taken by the underlying substance and held for a time and then released. They know this. They do not speak of it, because it does not require speech. It is the ground fact of their existence, the way mortality is the ground fact of yours, and they carry it with the same mixture of acceptance and inattention that you carry yours.

They are old. Not the individuals — the individuals live and die on a timescale you can recognize. But the people, the continuity, the thing that moves through them the way the memory-light moves through the stone — that is old. Older than the city. Older than the carved streets. Older than the thermal engines, which have been ticking for so long that their moving parts have polished themselves to mirrors that reflect a version of this place that no longer exists.

They are listening. This is what you understand, finally, after weeks of silence and warm stone and the slow rhythm of a world that does not need you. They are listening to the tableland. The stone is saying something — through its heat, through its mineral, through the memory-light that surfaces and fades and surfaces again — and the people of Old Karravat are listening, and they have been listening for so long that the listening has become a way of being, a way of inhabiting time, a way of standing in a carved street with your hand on warm stone and your breath held shallow and your eyes releasing light they absorbed hours ago.

You do not know what the stone is saying. You may never know.

But you have been here long enough, now, that you can stand in the silence with them. You can feel the warmth through your feet. You can hear the tick of the engines, the scritch of distant skree, the low pressure of wind that is not quite a sound. You can watch the memory-light surface in the stone around you, pale amber, shifting, and you can feel that it is not for you — it is not addressed to you — but you are here, and it is here, and the people who have lived with it for longer than you can imagine are here, and no one is asking you to leave.

The tableland is paying attention to something you cannot hear.

They are listening.

You are beginning to listen too.
