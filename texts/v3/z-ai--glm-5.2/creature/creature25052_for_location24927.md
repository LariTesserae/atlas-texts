The people of Kallet Vel do not have a word for what they are. This is not because they lack language — they have one, low-toned, consonant-heavy, spoken mostly in the spaces between drips where the acoustic world briefly thins — but because what they are is not a category they have found reason to name. They are the ones who are here. They were here before the ones who came through the gorge, and they will be here after. Or they won't. It isn't a question that organizes them.

Start with the hands, because the hands are what you see first, and the hands are what they are.

The hands are grey. Not stained — *informed*. The limestone dust works into the skin the way the water works into the stone, slowly, from the outside in, until there is no clear line where the hand ends and the stone begins. The older ones — and there are old ones here, older than you'd expect in a place this damp, this pressed — have hands that are almost the same color as the walls they maintain. The knuckles are wide. The fingertips are broad and slightly roughened, not calloused in the way of someone who grips a single tool in a single way all their life, but worn in the way of someone who grips *everything* — stone, hide, bone, wet reed, the smooth flank of a fish — and who has been gripping everything for a long time. The palms are dry. This is the one dry thing about them. The palms are dry because the hands are always working, always closed around something, and the work presses the moisture out. Open hands would be wet hands. They do not open their hands often.

They are not tall. This is not deprivation — there is food, fish from the pool and channels, and some of it is traded through the gorge for things that cannot be found or made in the basin, and the trading happens with a regularity that suggests someone outside Kallet Vel depends on it as much as Kallet Vel depends on whatever comes back. But the people are compact. Short-limbed, deep-chested, built close to the ground the way buildings here are built close to the dry shelves. The children move low and quick. The adults move low and deliberate. No one stands fully upright against the sky because there is no sky to stand against, only the flat pale underside of cloud, and the ceiling of the world is not high enough to make height useful. You learn to take up the space that is left, and the space that is left is always lower than you would choose.

They layer. This is the first thing they learn and the last thing they forget. Hide against the skin, felt over hide, hide over felt, a final layer of something that might once have been woven wool but has felted itself through long wear and longer damp into something denser, something that sheds water the way a stone sheds water — not perfectly, but enough. The layers are not uniform. Each person assembles their own stratigraphy from what is available, and what is available shifts. A hide wears through. A felted layer rots at the seams. You patch it with what you have, or you discard it and find another, or you go without, and going without is a choice that makes itself visible quickly in the grey cold. The layers smell of wet stone and tallow and something faintly mineral, something that is not unpleasant but that is not a smell you would identify as human if you encountered it without context. It is the smell of a body that has negotiated with its environment long enough that the negotiation has produced a third thing, neither body nor stone but the result of their long contact.

There are not as many of them as the buildings suggest. The buildings are dense, but the occupancy is not. Some structures hold families — three, four, five people in a space that would be tight for two anywhere else, but here the tightness is warmth, and the warmth is life, and the closeness is not experienced as intrusion. You enter a dwelling and the heat of bodies hits you before your eyes adjust. They sleep in piles. Not by preference but by physics. A single body in a stone room in Kallet Vel will be cold by morning. Three bodies will not. The arithmetic is simple and nobody discusses it.

Other structures are not dwellings. They are storage — stone stacked on stone, reed mats rolled, bone sorted by type and size in a way that suggests a taxonomy not immediately legible to an outsider but clearly meaningful to the sorter. A structure that is a workshop, where the knapping is done, the floor littered with flakes that are swept toward the walls and left there, building up a sediment of chert and obsidian and whatever else will fracture the right way. A structure that seems to hold nothing at all, and you would call it empty except that the lichen on its inner walls is thicker than anywhere else, and someone has clearly been in recently enough to leave finger-marks in the pale growth where they brushed past.

The empty structure is where the old woman lives. Or the old man. Or the old *something* — you cannot tell, and it does not seem to matter, and the people of Kallet Vel do not clarify this for you because the question is not a question they carry. This person is old in a way that makes the other old people seem provisional. They are somewhere beyond age, in a territory where the body has completed its negotiation with stone and water and has arrived at a state that is no longer deterioration but transformation. The skin is the color of the lichen. The eyes are not closed but not quite open, and what they see is not what you see, or not only what you see. This person does not speak in the low consonant language of the others. This person speaks in sounds that are closer to the drip, closer to the *glottal* sound the pool makes, and the others listen when these sounds come, and sometimes they act on them — moving a wall, clearing a channel, placing a stone — and sometimes they do not, and the distinction between when they act and when they don't is not one you can parse. It is not random. It is *heard*. They hear something in those sounds that tells them whether to move or to wait, and they have been hearing it long enough that the hearing is not a decision but a response, the way a hand pulls back from heat.

The old one is the one who carves the face.

Not always. The face is not the only one. Now that you have noticed the first, you see others — older ones, nearly erased by the lichen's patient return, the chisel marks softened and finally swallowed. Faces in the rock at the edge of the basin, all facing the pool, or facing the channels that feed the pool, or facing the place where the pool drains back down. They are not carved in sequence. They are carved when they are needed, and what constitutes *need* is determined by something the carver knows and the others trust and no one has explained to anyone, because it is not the kind of knowledge that survives explanation. It is the kind of knowledge that lives in the hands and the stone and the sound of the water, and it does not transfer through language. It transfers through the act of carving and through the long, slow education of watching someone carve.

The carver is not always the oldest. The old one is the carver now, but there was one before, and there will be one after. The choosing is not formal. It is not a ceremony. It is a drift, a slow gravitation, the way water finds a path — the person who begins to hear what the carver hears begins to stand closer, to watch longer, to hold the tools, and eventually the tools are in their hands and the old hands are resting and no one has said anything. This is how Kallet Vel does things. Nothing is decided. Everything is *become*.

---

The children are not like the adults, and this is the most important thing about them.

The children move differently. They are quick where the adults are deliberate. They are curious where the adults are patient. They touch things the adults do not touch — the water in the channels, the undersides of stones, the lichen, the fish in the pool. An adult does not put a hand in the pool. This is not prohibition. It is not fear. It is simply not done, the way you do not put your hand in someone's mouth. The pool has its own business, and the business is between the pool and whatever it returns to, and the hands of adults belong to the dry surfaces and the work that happens there. But the children have not yet learned this boundary, or they have learned it and are still testing it, or — and this is the possibility that the adults seem to carry without naming — the children belong to a different category of being, one that is still partly in negotiation with the water, still partly *of* it, in a way that the adults have aged out of or been dried out of.

The girl with the chert — the one turning it in her hands like a text — is reading it. That is the only word for what she is doing. She holds it to the grey light and tilts it and her eyes move across the fractures and the grain the way a scholar's eyes move across a manuscript, and her lips move slightly, and she is not performing. She is *working*. The chert tells her something. What it tells her is not what it will become — knife, pendant, scraper — but where it came from, what layer of what stone, which direction the fracture runs, what it wants to be cut into. The stone has a preference. She is listening to it. She is perhaps nine years old.

There is a boy — maybe seven, maybe younger, it is difficult to tell with the layers and the compact build — who sits at the edge of the pool with his feet in the water. Nobody stops him. Nobody comments. The eyeless fish drift around his ankles. He is still in a way that the other children are not still, and his stillness is not the stillness of boredom or cold but the stillness of something that is *waiting to hear*. His face has the same quality as the old carver's face — not closed, not open, just *attuned*, tuned to a frequency that the rest of the settlement cannot pick up but knows is there because the pool makes its glottal sound and the boy's head tilts and the old carver's head tilts at the same moment and neither of them looks at the other.

He will be the next carver. Or he will not. It is too early to know. But something in him is already moving toward the stone the way water moves toward the crack that will receive it — not by choice, by *inclination*, by the fundamental physics of what he is in relation to what the place is.

---

And then the things that are not human.

The lichen first, because the lichen was here first, and the lichen will be here last. It is not a resident of Kallet Vel. Kallet Vel is a resident of it. The pale, almost luminous growth that covers the north-facing walls is a single organism — or if it is not a single organism, it is a single *presence*, a continuous skin of life that has been spreading across this limestone since before the gorge opened, since before the water found its paths, since before there was anything here but stone and the slow chemical patience of something that eats stone and grows and eats and grows and does not hurry and does not stop. The people scrape it from the places where they need grip — doorways, steps, the surfaces where mortar must adhere — and the lichen does not resist. It does not recoil. It simply begins again, from the edge, from the spore, from the invisible thread of itself that remains in the grain of the rock. It will return to every surface that is abandoned. It is returning now, even to the surfaces that are not abandoned, at a pace that the human eye cannot measure in a day or a week but that a year will reveal. The face the carver is working on — the new one, the fresh one — already has a faint bloom at its edges. The lichen is coming back. It is always coming back. It is the thing that Kallet Vel is built *inside of*, not the walls, not the gorge, but this living skin that holds the stone the way the stone holds the people.

The fish are not what you would call a community. They are a fact. They exist in the pool and in the deeper channels, pale and translucent, their organs visible through the skin of their flanks like shadows through wet paper. They do not school. They drift. They seem to have no direction, no purpose beyond the continuation of their own soft presence in the water, and yet they are not aimless, because when the pool makes its glottal sound — when it drains, when it exhales whatever it has been holding — the fish move. All of them. At once. Toward the center, toward the sound, and then back, and then they drift again. It is a response so uniform and so immediate that it cannot be instinct alone. It is *listening*. The fish listen to the pool the way the carver listens to the stone, the way the boy listens to the water, and what they hear is not for us.

The crustaceans are quieter. They hold to the undersides of stones in the channels, flat and grey and barely distinguishable from the stone they grip. They are the food that does not require the pool. You flip a stone, you find one, you take it, you eat it or you carry it. The children do this. The adults do this. It is the most casual act in Kallet Vel, the thing that happens without thought, the way breathing happens. But the crustaceans are not infinite. There are fewer than there were — someone says this, or you infer it from the way a child flips three stones and finds nothing and moves on without surprise. The crustaceans are the thing that is diminishing. The lichen is not. The fish are not. The stone is not. The crustaceans are the margin, and the margin is thinning, and nobody has decided what this means yet, but the old carver has been at the wall more often in recent days, and the face is closer to finished than it was.

The small mammals in the crevices — you see one, briefly, a flash of grey-brown fur, a body low and quick, gone between stones before you can frame it — are not named. They are not eaten. They are not pets. They exist in the same spaces the people exist in, occupying the smaller gaps, the tighter crevices, the places where a hand cannot reach and a stone cannot be placed. They are tolerated. They are, perhaps, *expected*, in the way that the drip is expected, in the way that the wet is expected. They are part of the density, part of the saturation, one more presence pressed against the others. If they have a role, it is the role of *being there*, of being another thing that lives in this place and makes it fuller and does not explain itself.

---

And then the pool itself, because the pool is not a feature of Kallet Vel. The pool *lives* in Kallet Vel.

You can call it a geological phenomenon — a point where the water table presses closest to the surface, where the limestone's porosity creates a natural sump, where the cycle of upwelling and drainage finds its axis. This is accurate. This is also insufficient. The pool breathes. Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. The water level rises and falls in a rhythm that is not tidal, not seasonal, not tied to any rainfall you can observe through the permanent cloud cover. It is a rhythm that comes from below, from whatever the water returns to when it drains, and the rhythm has the quality of respiration. The pool inhales the water that the settlement channels toward it. The pool exhales the water that presses up through the fissures. The glottal sound is the moment between — the pause, the catch, the shift from one direction to the other. It is the sound of a throat. It is the sound of something that swallows and is swallowed.

The people do not worship the pool. There is no altar. There are no offerings, unless you count the water itself, which flows to the pool because the ground was built to direct it there, which is the same as saying the settlement is the offering. There is no prayer. There is no ritual. But there is *attention*, constant and diffuse, the way you attend to the breathing of someone sleeping beside you. You do not watch them breathe. You do not count the breaths. But you know, at every moment, that the breathing is happening, and if it stopped, you would know that too, instantly, in your body before your mind. The people of Kallet Vel attend to the pool this way. It is the center not because it is sacred but because it is *necessary*, and the necessity is not a matter of water supply or drainage or engineering. It is a matter of the place being alive, and the pool being where the life is most legible.

The face in the rock watches the pool because the face is a *marker*. It marks something the carver has heard. It marks a moment in the pool's rhythm, or a change in the pool's rhythm, or a thing the pool has said that required a face to be carved — a face, not a word, because the carver does not deal in words, deals in the older language of stone and shape and the negative space where a mouth should be. The face is the carver's transcription of what the pool has said. It is not a portrait. It is a *record*. And when the lichen covers it, the record will be complete, and whatever the pool said will have been absorbed into the stone and the lichen and the slow patience of the place, and the carver will listen for the next thing, and the next face will be begun.

---

So who lives here?

People who are becoming stone. Not dying — *becoming*. The limestone dust in their skin, the compact bodies, the grey clothes against grey walls, the hands that grip and grip and grip until the gripping has reshaped the hand itself. They are being absorbed, slowly, the way the lichen absorbs the rock it grows on, the way the water absorbs the stone it moves through. They are not fading. They are *joining*. The old carver is further along this path than anyone else — the skin colored like lichen, the eyes half-closed, the sounds that come from the mouth closer to the sound of water than to the sound of speech. The old carver is almost done becoming. And what they are becoming is not a corpse, not a monument, not a memory. They are becoming *part of it* — part of the stone, part of the water's path, part of the face that watches the pool. The carving is not art. The carving is *rehearsal*. The carver is practicing the shape they will take when the becoming is complete.

The boy at the pool's edge is beginning. He does not know this. He will know it the way you know a cold has entered your chest — not at the moment of entry but at the moment it becomes undeniable, and by then it will have been true for a long time. He will carve. He will listen. He will grey. He will become. And the settlement will continue around him, the buildings leaning on each other, the water finding its paths, the lichen returning, the faces multiplying and erasing and multiplying, and the people will continue to be the part that changes, the temporary voice in a conversation that does not need them but has, for now, *included* them.

Kallet Vel lives. Not the settlement — the settlement is a symptom. Kallet Vel *the place* lives, and the people are its current inhabitants the way the lichen was its previous inhabitant and the water is its permanent one. They live in it the way a thought lives in a mind — present, shaping, shaped, and eventually replaced by the next thought, while the mind continues.

The pool exhales. The drip resumes. The girl turns the chert in her hands and listens. The old carver lifts the chisel. The boy does not move.

Something below the water moves, and does not show itself, and is not asked to.
