# What Lives Here

Start with the crabs, because they are the most obvious life, and because they would not agree with that framing.

---

## The Mortarbacks

They do not think of themselves as a they.

Each mortarback is a solitary organism in the most literal sense — it has no social cognition, no recognition of conspecifics, no awareness that the claw working three body-lengths away belongs to the same species. When two mortarbacks meet in a corridor, they pause. Antennules flicker. Chemical signatures are exchanged. And then they classify each other the same way they classify everything else in this chamber: *substrate or not-substrate. Obstacle or path. Edible or inedible.* Another mortarback is not-substrate, obstacle, and — under certain conditions, when the carapace is soft, when the molt is recent — edible. But usually it is simply obstacle, and they climb over each other or reroute without ceremony.

This is important. The settlement is not collaborative. No mortarback has ever intended a wall. What happens is this: a single crab finds a deposit it wants — a lump of calcium carbonate paste, say, rich in the iron oxide that its cement glands work best with — and it begins to excavate. It pulls grains loose with the major claw, the left one, the larger one, which is shaped less like a pincer and more like a narrow trowel. It brings each grain to its mouthparts, where it is coated in secretion — the brine-and-overripe-fruit-smelling cement — and then pressed into place. Against the wall. Against the floor. Against the ceiling, if the crab is climbing. Wherever the crab is, it builds, because building is not a project. Building is what the claws do when they are not eating or molting or being eaten.

And the next crab does the same. And the next. They are all responding to the same environmental gradients — the texture of the substrate, the mineral content of the water, the chemical trails left by previous crabs' cement, which contains compounds that trigger, in other crabs, the same excavation-and-deposition behavior. One crab's wall becomes another crab's stimulus. The wall grows not because anyone is building a wall but because the wall itself is an instruction, a chemical shout that says *here, this shape, keep going.*

The archway — that two-meter span with its keystone, its symmetry, its unsettling resemblance to something a human would recognize as architecture — is an emergent accident. The crabs were depositing along a stress line in the rock where the ceiling's breathing created micro-fractures. The deposits followed the fracture pattern. The keystone is where two deposition fronts met. It looks designed. It is not designed. It is the shape of the forces that made it, nothing more, and if you could watch it at high speed — a thousand years compressed into a minute — you would see it the way you see a crystal forming: not built, but grown.

But grown is too simple a word for it too, because the crabs are not passive. They are alive. Each one is making thousands of individual decisions per hour, tiny trowel-claw choices: this grain, not that one; here, not there; press, release, move. The settlement is the sum of ten thousand lifetimes of such choices, each one contingent, each one responsive to local conditions, each one slightly altering the conditions for the next choice. It is determinism and chaos at once. It is weather. It is a city made of weather.

---

A mortarback molts every six to eight weeks, here in the cold, slow water. The process takes a day. The carapace splits along the posterior margin and the crab backs out, soft, white, translucent — more translucent than usual, so that you can see not just the dark digestive tract but the pale fibers of muscle, the dark knots of ganglia, the slow pulse of the tubular heart. For twelve hours after molting, the crab cannot walk. It lies where it is, in whatever corridor or chamber it happened to be in when the molt began, and it is vulnerable, and it knows this in the limited way it knows anything: chemical alarm signals flood its body, it becomes still, it waits.

The isopods know too.

---

## The Isopods

They are not cruel. Cruelty requires a model of the other's suffering, and the isopods have no such model. They have chemicals. They have chemoreceptors on their antennae that detect the specific cocktail of compounds a molting crab releases — the softening signals, the stress signals, the particular alkaloid that says *this body is temporarily permeable* — and they move toward it. They move toward it the way a plant moves toward light. Automatically. With the absolute conviction of a reflex.

Each isopod is the size of a rice grain. White. Flattened. Ten legs, all vestigial, all reduced to hooked claws designed for one thing: gripping the membrane between a mortarback's carapace plates. They ride the crabs. Not the molting crabs specifically — any crab, all crabs. If you look closely at a mortarback, and you cannot look closely because the light is too dim and the water too clouded with mineral particulate, but if you could, you would see them: dozens per crab, tucked into every joint, every seam, clustered thick as barnacles around the leg bases where the carapace is thinnest and the hemolymph runs closest to the surface.

They drink. A stylet — a piercing mouthpart, hollow — penetrates the soft tissue between plates, and the isopod feeds on hemolymph, the crab's open-circulatory fluid, which is not red but blue, copper-based, and which tastes, one imagines, of copper and salt and whatever a crab's blood tastes like, which is probably nothing, because isopods do not taste.

A healthy mortarback can support thirty isopods without apparent distress. The hemolymph loss is within metabolic tolerance. The isopods benefit; the crab is not helped, not harmed, not noticed. When the crab molts, though — when the carapace splits and the soft body emerges — the isopods migrate. They leave the old shell and move onto the new body, and on the new body, the membranes are not just thin but absent, and the stylets go deeper, and the feeding is heavier, and if enough isopods reach a molting crab quickly enough, the crab dies. Not from the wounds. From fluid loss. From the sheer arithmetic of thirty or forty or sixty rice-sized parasites drinking simultaneously from a body that has just shed its only armor.

This happens. Not often. Not rarely. The architecture helps — a molting crab in a narrow corridor is partially shielded, the walls limiting access, and the chemical alarm signals that the crab cannot help releasing are also, accidentally, signals to other crabs, who avoid the area, who reroute, who leave the molting crab alone in a quiet stretch of corridor, which is what it needs, which is what the settlement provides without anyone deciding to provide it. The city protects its builders. Not deliberately. Structurally. The narrowness of the passages is a defense, and the crabs built it without knowing they were building anything, and it saves them without anyone knowing it saves them.

## The Eels

Call them eels. They are eel-shaped, which is enough for the name, and the name is enough for us, and the eels do not care.

Thirty centimeters of blind, white, scaleless muscle. No eyes — not reduced eyes, not vestigial eyes, but absent entirely, the sockets sealed over with smooth tissue that is the same pale color as everything else here. They navigate by taste. The whole body is chemosensitive — not just the snout, though the snout is densest with receptors — and they read the water the way a tongue reads a surface, flickering, testing, following gradients.

The eels live in the architecture. Not on it. In it. They occupy the abandoned corridors, the cul-de-sacs, the chambers that the mortarbacks built and then vacated when the mineral content shifted or the ceiling-dweller pressure increased or the chemical instructions faded. The eels do not build. They move in. They are the second tenants, the inheritors, and they have no concept of inheritance.

What they have is a body built for tight spaces. Their skulls are hinged — not metaphorically, actually hinged, the jaw articulating at three points rather than two, allowing the head to flatten, to compress, to squeeze through gaps that a thirty-centimeter animal should not fit through. They can move through corridors half their body width. They fold. They flow. They are water within water, and they move through the crab-city like rumors through a building, passing through walls that are not quite solid, not quite finished, always already becoming something else.

The eels eat isopods. They find them by following the isopods' chemical signature — the same alarm-response compounds that draw isopods to molting crabs, which the eels have evolved to detect from a greater distance. An eel detects a cluster of isopods converging on a molting crab, and the eel moves toward the cluster, and the eel feeds. It does not help the crab. The crab is irrelevant. The crab is a dinner bell. The eel eats the isopods that are eating the crab, and sometimes the crab survives because the eel arrived early enough, and sometimes the crab dies because the eel arrived too late, and the eel does not know and does not care and will eat the isopods off the crab's dead body just as readily as off its living one.

The eels also eat each other, when they can. A smaller eel in a tight corridor is a meal, and the tightness of the corridor means the smaller eel cannot escape, and the hinged jaw that lets the larger eel compress its head also lets it open its mouth wider than seems geometrically reasonable. This is not frequent. The corridors are a maze, and the maze is mercy, and most eels never encounter each other, living their entire lives in isolated stretches of abandoned architecture, tasting the water, eating isopods, growing, and then being eaten by something or dying in a corridor that fills with sediment and closes over them like a palm closing.

## The Ceiling-Dwellers

You have to look up. But looking up is dangerous, here, so let me describe them for you.

They are the reason the mortarbacks never swim. They are the reason the open water — that three-meter column of still, dark, central space — is empty of everything except minerals settling and the occasional doomed amphipod. They are the apex. Not by strength. By patience.

Each colony is a single organism, technically, though the word *organism* strains here. Each colony is a mass of gelatinous tissue hanging from the ceiling by a holdfast that secretes a mild acid, dissolving just enough limestone to anchor itself. From the holdfast, the body extends downward — a stalactite of translucent jelly, thirty to sixty centimeters long, faintly blue-green, faintly luminous. The light is a byproduct. It serves no purpose. It is not communication, not attraction, not warning. It is chemistry — a luciferase reaction tied to the colony's metabolic cycle, the way a human's breath fogs in cold air. In a place with no eyes, the light is for no one.

But it is there, and it is the only light, and in its dim glow you can see the tendrils.

The tendrils extend downward from the colony's body. They are the feeding apparatus. Each one is a single cell wide at its tip and perhaps a millimeter wide at its base, and they are invisible in the water until they touch something, and then — then the adhesion. A mucopolysaccharide coating, sticky, tenacious, and once contact is made, the tendril does not snap or constrict. It simply holds. And then, over hours, it shortens. Cellular contraction. Not muscle — these organisms have no muscle — but contractile fibers in the cell walls, pulling, pulling at the pace of geology. The caught thing — a swimming amphipod, a falling crab, a larva, anything that drifted into the open water and touched the wrong thread — is drawn upward, centimeter by centimeter, hour by hour, until it reaches the colonial mass and is absorbed. Enveloped. The jelly closes over it, and over the next several days, the colony digests it.

There are dozens of colonies. Maybe a hundred. They cover most of the ceiling, overlapping, competing for attachment sites, their tendrils interlocking in a mesh that is not quite a net and not quite a forest and not quite anything. It is a ceiling of mouths. The open water below is the throat, and anything that enters the throat is swallowed.

The mortarbacks know this the way the mortarbacks know everything: chemically, reflexively, without understanding. They have evolved an aversion to open water. Not a fear — fear implies experience, expectation, the anticipation of a future event. The crabs have no anticipation. They simply do not enter open water. The behavior is hardwired, selected by the thousands of generations of crabs that did enter open water and were caught and digested and left no offspring. The ceiling taught them. Over centuries. By killing every crab that needed to be taught.

The tendrils sometimes break. A strong organism — one of the eels, perhaps, thrashing if it is caught — can snap a tendril, and the broken piece drifts downward, slowly, through the open water, and lands on the floor, and the mortarbacks find it. Tendril fragments are rich. Protein, lipid, mineral — the ceiling-dwellers concentrate the nutrients from the bacterial mats above them, and their discarded tissue is the richest food source in the chamber. The crabs eat it eagerly. They cement their own molted shells into the walls, recycling calcium, but they eat the ceiling-dwellers' waste directly, breaking it down in their digestive tracts, extracting what they can, and what they cannot extract, they secrete as cement, and the cement becomes walls, and the walls become corridors, and the corridors become the city.

The crabs eat the ceiling. The ceiling eats the crabs. Between these two facts, the entire system runs.

## The Amphipods

Minor players. Essential.

They live in the sediment. The ankle-deep, ankle-sucking, not-yet-floor paste of calcium carbonate and iron oxide and organic detritus that covers the bottom of the chamber. They are small — three to five millimeters — and they are many. Thousands. Tens of thousands. They burrow through the paste the way earthworms burrow through soil, ingesting it, extracting what organic material it contains, excreting the rest. They are the bottom of the food chain's visible portion. Below them, only the bacteria, only the minerals, only the rock.

They are also the most visible failure of the system's silence. When disturbed — when a mortarback steps on the sediment above them, when an eel's passage sends a pressure wave through the paste, when the ceiling's breathing shifts the water column enough to resettle material — they burst upward. All of them. At once. A cloud of amphipods exploding from the sediment into the water column, and for a moment — a moment that lasts perhaps two seconds — the open water is full of living things, and the ceiling-dwellers' tendrils catch dozens, and the tendrils hold, and the amphipods die slowly over the next several hours, drawn upward into the jelly, and the ceiling feeds.

This is the only regular sound in the chamber. Not sound, exactly — vibration. The amphipods' eruption creates a low-frequency pulse, a throb, a single wet heartbeat in the deep. You would feel it more than hear it. A pressure change. A fullness, a release. The ceiling is already breathing. This is the floor answering.

## The Bacteria

They are the true inhabitants. Everyone else is a guest.

The bacterial mats on the ceiling are the foundation — the chemolithoautotrophs, the organisms that take rock and water and the faint chemical energy of mineral oxidation and turn it into life. They are a film. A scum. A layer so thin it is measured in micrometers, covering the ceiling's limestone surface, processing the percolating water, pulling electrons from iron, from sulfur, from the slow chemistry of dissolution itself. They are the reason anything else can live here. They are the crop. The ceiling-dwellers feed on them. The ceiling-dwellers' waste feeds the crabs. The crabs' waste feeds the bacteria. The cycle is complete. The cycle is the only thing here that is complete.

You cannot see them individually. You see them only as texture — a slight fuzz on the rock surface, a difference in the water's taste near the ceiling, a gradient. They are the most numerous and the least visible and the most important, and they have no idea what they are, and they are the closest thing this place has to a purpose.

## What Does Not Live Here

Nothing lives here that is large. Nothing lives here that is fast. Nothing lives here that is warm, that sees, that makes sound, that moves through open water by choice, that breathes air, that sleeps, that plays, that remembers.

Nothing lives here that knows it lives here.

The mortarbacks do not know they are in a chamber. They do not know the chamber is in limestone. They do not know the limestone is in the earth. They know the next grain, the next wall, the next molt, and when those things are not available, they know hunger, and when hunger is not addressed, they know stillness, and after stillness, nothing.

The eels do not know they are eels. They know the taste of isopod and the pressure of corridor walls and the chemical trace of a larger eel, which means *turn around, go back, find another passage,* and that is enough. That is a whole life.

The ceiling-dwellers do not know anything. They are a colonial organism with no centralized nervous system, no sensory apparatus beyond chemical and mechanical contact, no behavior beyond growth and contraction. They are a mouth that does not know it is a mouth, eating in a place that does not know it is a place.

The bacteria do not know.

---

And yet.

The settlement grows. The archway stands. The corridors protect the molting, and the molting feeds the isopods, and the isopods draw the eels, and the eels keep the isopod population from overwhelming the crabs, and the crabs build the corridors, and the corridors protect the molting. The system balances. The system has no brain, no plan, no oversight, and it balances — not perfectly, not statically, but dynamically, the way a spinning top balances, the way a river balances, the way anything balances when enough forces push against each other for long enough.

This is what lives here. Not organisms — though there are organisms, thousands, millions. Not a community — though it functions as one. What lives here is a process. A metabolism. A single slow heartbeat measured in the breathing of a ceiling and the settling of sediment and the growth of walls built by animals that do not know they are building.

The rock is still becoming rock. The crabs are still becoming a city. The ceiling is still becoming a mouth. Everything here is mid-sentence, mid-step, mid-breath, and the breath is long, and the sentence has no ending, because endings require someone to decide, and nothing here decides.

Nothing here is finished. Everything here is alive.
