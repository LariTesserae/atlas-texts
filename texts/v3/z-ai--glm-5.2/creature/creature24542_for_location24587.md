# What Lives in Or-Enath

---

The first answer is the easy one, and it is also the wrong one, and it is also, in its way, the truest one: *the plant lives here.*

The plant lives here, and the plant is the floor, and the floor extends further than you have legs to walk, and the floor has been alive for longer than the word *longer* usefully describes. So the plant lives here. Fine. But this is like saying that the ocean lives in the ocean. It is correct and it tells you nothing about the fish.

---

## The Grazers

Start with the small ones, because they are the ones you will notice first, and because they are the ones that make the least demands on your understanding.

They are not numerous, exactly — you do not see them in swarms or herds — but they are constant. In any corridor, in any chamber, if you wait long enough, you will see one. The hand-sized, segmented, many-legged things that move in lines along the walls. They eat the surface. The surface regrows. They eat it again. They have been doing this for so long that their bodies are optimized for it in ways that would seem nightmarish if you let yourself think about it too long — mouths that are also feet, digestive systems that process the substrate's fibers directly, bodies the same color as their food because they *are* their food, metabolically continuous with it. They do not graze *on* the plant. They graze *as* the plant. A leaf eating a leaf.

They do not react to you because you are not part of their grammar. They respond to chemical cues — the iridescent fluid's scent, the electrical charge of the walls, the pulse-rate of the light in nearby nodes. You produce none of these. To them, you are a warm, wet, silent object that does not register on any sense they possess. You are a ghost made of water. They walk through you, or rather, around you, with the same lack of acknowledgment they give to any irregularity in the terrain.

There are larger grazers, too. The dog-sized, low-slung ones with the translucent skin and the iridescent fluid visible inside them. They move with more purpose than the small ones — not faster, but *directed*, taking paths that suggest memory or instinct or something between the two. They vocalize: that low, resonant clicking, produced by hardened structures in what you are calling a throat but which might be closer to a chest cavity. The sound bounces off the walls and returns to them, and they adjust their paths based on the return. Echolocation, perhaps. Or something more complex — the walls respond to the clicks. You have noticed this. When a grazer clicks, the nearest light-nodes pulse, faintly, in a pattern that matches the click's frequency. The grazer is not just navigating. It is *reading*. The walls are telling it where it is.

Do these graze, or do they patrol? You have watched one for long enough to see it stop at a damaged section of wall — a place where the substrate has been scarred, perhaps by something passing through, perhaps by the slow geological stress of the organism's own weight — and press its broad mouth against the exposed tissue. It is not eating. It is *sealing*. The iridescent fluid beads from its mouth onto the wound, oxidizes to copper, hardens. The grazer moves on. The wall is healed.

So: the grazers are not grazers. Or not only. They are maintenance. Mobile repair units expressed as biology. The organism produces them the way your body produces platelets — not by decision, but by design. They are part of the immune system, and the digestive system, and the navigation system, all at once, because *system* is a word that assumes boundaries this place does not recognize.

## The Floaters

Above, in the warmer air near the ceilings, the gossamer things drift.

They are almost not there. Their bodies are membranes so thin that light passes through them; their trailing filaments are finer than spider silk and longer than your arm. They ride the thermal gradients — the faint convection produced by the substrate's metabolism, which is warmer than the air, which is warmer than the ceiling — and they sample. That is what their filaments do. They touch the walls, the nodes, the surfaces of the growth, and they *taste* — not in any sense you understand, but in the chemical-electrical sense that everything here understands. They are reading the state of the organism, continuously, at a fine scale, and their reading is part of the organism's reading of itself. They are sensors. They are also, when conditions are right, spores. When a floater settles — and you saw one settle, in a high corner of a chamber, its filaments retracting, its membrane folding in on itself like a closing hand — it takes root. It becomes part of the wall. It grows. A new node of tissue, a new extension of the substrate, in a place the substrate wanted to extend to. The floater is the organism's way of reaching where it cannot grow from below.

This means the organism is *dispersing*. Even now, after geological ages of growth, it is still seeding itself into its own distant corners, still colonizing its own body. Not because it needs to — the equilibrium is established, the rules say so — but because *reaching* is what it does. A body that has been breathing for a billion years does not stop breathing because it has breathed enough. It breathes because it is alive. The floaters are the breath.

## The Predators

You have not seen one clearly. You have seen the *result* of one.

In a corridor that narrowed to a passage barely wide enough for your shoulders, the walls were marked with something that was not the copper-sealed scarring of healed damage. These marks were deeper, ragged, and they were fresh — the fibers still exposed, the iridescent fluid still beading, not yet oxidized. Something had torn through here, recently, with force. The marks were at two heights: low, where something had pushed or dragged, and high, where something had gripped the ceiling.

Later, in a wide chamber, you found what was left. The remains of a grazer — the dog-sized kind — broken open, its iridescent fluid pooled on the floor and already hardening into a copper shell that would, in time, be absorbed back into the substrate. The predator had not eaten the grazer in the way you understand eating. It had *extracted* something. The fluid was gone from the body. The tissues remained. Whatever had done this wanted the working fluid, not the flesh.

And then you heard it. Above you. A sound that was not the clicking of the grazers, not the low chord of the substrate, but a wet, taut *snap*, like a tendon under tension releasing. You looked up and saw — for half a second, in the faint light of a distant node — a shape. Dark. Long. Articulated in a way that suggested limbs but too many of them, arranged in a way that suggested symmetry but the wrong kind. It had been hanging from the ceiling, and it had moved, and it was gone.

You did not follow.

The predators are part of the system. This is not a moral statement; it is a structural one. The organism produces the grazers, and the organism produces the things that take the grazers, and the taking is part of the cycling. The iridescent fluid that the grazers carry and that the predators extract does not leave the system. It is returned — to the floor, to the walls, to the roots that drink it up again. The predator is a recycler. It is also, perhaps, a *check* — a mechanism that prevents the grazers from overgrazing, from consuming faster than the substrate can regrow. It is the organism's way of keeping itself in balance with itself.

But you did not follow it, and you will not, because the sound it made was not the sound of a mechanism. It was the sound of something that *wanted*.

Even in a system this old, this settled, this balanced: wanting persists. The predator does not extract fluid because it understands its role in the ecosystem. It extracts fluid because it is hungry, and hunger is older than purpose, and the system has not found a reason to eliminate it because hunger, in this place, is indistinguishable from function. The predator *is* the function, and the function *is* the wanting, and they cannot be separated without breaking something that the system has decided, over geological time, to keep.

## The Builders

Now the difficult part.

Someone made this. The growth is directed. The architecture is planned. The technology — the carbon lattices, the conducting arrays, the bioluminescent nodes that pulse in synchronized patterns — is *engineered*. This was not accidental. This was not evolution alone, finding its way toward complexity through blind iteration. Something *designed* this, and then the design grew, and then the growing became the design, and now — and now — and now is where it gets hard.

Because the builders are still here.

Not as people. Not as anything you would recognize as a person. But the voices — the clicks, the buzzes, the low moans that come from the corridors and the chambers — they are not the grazers. The grazers click, but the grazers click to navigate. These sounds are different. They have *structure*. They repeat. They vary. They respond to each other. In one chamber, you sat for twenty minutes and listened to a sequence of low moans that came from three directions, and the moans were not simultaneous — they were *conversational*. One would sound, and another would answer, and the answer would vary depending on what the first had said, and then a third would join, and the pattern would shift, and the light-nodes in the chamber pulsed in time with the exchange, and you understood, with a certainty that was not proof but was deeper than proof, that *something was talking.*

About what? You do not know. In what language? Not one you have a framework for. But the exchange had the rhythm of language — the turn-taking, the variation, the responsiveness, the way certain patterns recurred and others were unique. It had the shape of communication, even if the content was opaque.

And then you saw one of the speakers.

---

It was in a corridor, moving away from you, and you caught only a partial view before it turned a corner and was gone. But this is what you saw:

It was tall — your height, or close to it. It moved on two limbs, or what functioned as two limbs, though they were not legs in any sense you would recognize. They were *columns* — thick, fibrous, the same pale green-grey as everything else, tapering to points that gripped the floor with root-like tendrils that extended and retracted with each step. Its body was a trunk, roughly cylindrical, with no visible head — no eyes, no mouth, no face. Its surface was covered in the same fine root-hairs that lined the narrow passages, and these hairs were in constant motion, sampling the air, the walls, the floor, the light. It was *reading* everything, continuously, the way the floaters read, but at a scale and with a complexity that suggested comprehension, not just detection.

Where its head should have been, there was a crown — not of leaves, but of *nodes*. A cluster of the same bioluminescent structures that dotted the walls, but concentrated, arranged in a pattern that was too regular to be accidental. They pulsed. They pulsed in the patterns you had been hearing as voices. The sound was coming from *here* — not from a throat, but from these nodes, vibrating at frequencies that produced the clicks and moans and buzzes. The creature was speaking with light, and the light was producing sound, and the sound was language, and the language was — what? Directions? Observations? Names? A report on the state of the substrate, delivered to other reports, received, considered, answered?

It did not see you. It had no eyes to see with. But as it turned the corner, its crown of nodes pulsed once, brightly, in a pattern you had not heard before, and the walls around you *responded* — the light-nodes in the corridor flared, the substrate hummed, and for a moment, you felt the vibration change beneath your feet, as if the floor had taken a breath.

It knew you were there. Not by sight. By the disturbance you made in the chemical and electrical field of the organism. You are wet, and warm, and you disrupt the local chemistry just by existing. You are a signal. The builder received the signal, and it transmitted the signal, and the organism — the vast, ancient, breathing organism that is floor and wall and city and circuit — *noted* you.

And then it moved on.

This is what stays with you, more than the sound, more than the light, more than the low chord in your bones: the fact that it noted you and did not care. Not with hostility. Not with curiosity. With the same indifferent attention it would give to a shift in the mineral content of the substrate, or a change in the temperature of the air, or a new crack in a wall that needed sealing. You were data. You were a reading. You were incorporated into the system's awareness of itself and then you were filed, and the system continued, and the builders continued, and the grazers grazed, and the predators hunted, and the floaters drifted, and the chord went on.

## What the Builders Are

You think: they are the organism's nervous system. Not its inhabitants. Its *nerves*. The mobile expression of a sessile intelligence — an intelligence so large and so slow that its thoughts take the form of architecture, its memories take the form of growth layers, and its communication takes the form of creatures walking through its own body, touching the walls, reading the state of the tissue, speaking to each other in pulses of light and sound that travel through the substrate at the speed of the organism's own electrical conductance.

They are not separate from the plant. They *are* the plant, in the way that your neurons are you. When a builder touches a wall and the wall responds, this is not a creature interacting with an environment. This is a body touching itself, checking itself, maintaining itself. The builders are the organism's hands and eyes and voice, given legs because a body this large needs to move its awareness to where it is needed.

And they have been doing this for so long that the distinction between *the organism thinks* and *the builders think* has dissolved. They think together. They are one system. The settlement was built by something that wanted to live in a specific way — organized, structured, with corridors and chambers and infrastructure — and that something grew itself into the infrastructure until the infrastructure *was* the organism, and the organism *was* the infrastructure, and the builders are the last mobile trace of the original intention, still walking the corridors they designed, still checking the walls they grew, still speaking in a language that the walls answer.

Are they conscious? You do not know. They have the architecture of consciousness — the communication, the responsiveness, the memory encoded in the growth layers, the processing that produces the sound you feel in your chest. But the rhythm is wrong. It is too slow. A thought that takes minutes to complete, a conversation that spans hours, a decision that forms over days as signals propagate through meters of tissue and return and propagate again. If they are conscious, their consciousness is music, and the music is so slow that a single phrase takes longer to play than you have been alive.

You do not know. You cannot know. You are a warm, wet, brief thing standing on the surface of a mind so large it has forgotten it is thinking.

## The Colony-Things

One more, because it does not fit, and the things that do not fit are the ones that matter.

In the corners of certain chambers — the larger ones, the ones that might have been public spaces once — there are clusters of what you first took to be crystalline moss. They are not moss. They are small — the size of your thumbnail, each one — and they are geometric, faceted, with a regularity that is not biological. They are made of the same carbon-lattice material as the conducting arrays in the walls, but they are *free*. They are not attached to the substrate. They sit on the surface, in clusters of dozens or hundreds, and they move.

Not often. Not quickly. But over the course of an hour, you watched a cluster of them rearrange themselves into a pattern — a rough circle, then a spiral, then something that looked, from above, like a cross-section of the conducting arrays in the walls. They held the pattern for a while. Then they dissolved it and moved on, drifting to another corner of the chamber, and began again.

They are not fauna. They are not flora. They are *devices*. Tiny, autonomous, self-assembling devices — built by the same engineering that built the city, but never integrated into the organism. They persist. They do their work, whatever it is, and the organism tolerates them the way a body tolerates its microbiome — not because it needs them, but because they have been here so long that removing them would cost more energy than ignoring them.

What do they do? You watched them for an hour and could not tell. They build patterns. They dissolve patterns. They move. They do not interact with the builders, or the grazers, or the floaters. They do not respond to the light-nodes. They do not vocalize.

But when you touched the wall near them — when the wall sang its responsive note — the colony-things *rearranged*. They shifted their pattern. They reoriented, as if the sound had told them something, and they were adjusting to it.

They are listening. They have been listening, perhaps, since before the organism absorbed the city. They are the oldest things here — older than the current equilibrium, older than the builders' current form, perhaps older than the distinction between plant and animal. They are the original technology, the seed from which everything else grew, and they have been running, silently, in the corners of rooms, for so long that even the organism has forgotten what they are for.

Or: the organism has not forgotten. The organism *is* what they are for. The colony-things built the city, and the city grew the plant, and the plant grew the builders, and the builders maintain the plant, and the plant maintains the city, and the colony-things sit in the corners and listen to the whole thing turning, and they are satisfied, or they are not, or satisfaction is a word that means nothing to a machine that has been running for a billion years.

They are the one thing here that was *made* and not *grown*. Everything else is both. They are only made. And they persist, and they are the quietest thing in Or-Enath, and they are perhaps the most important, and no one — not the builders, not the grazers, not the organism — pays them any attention at all.

## What Lives Here

Everything lives here. The floor lives. The walls live. The air lives, charged and chemical and tasting of aldehydes. The light lives, pulsing in its slow clock. The sound lives, the great sustained chord that is the organism thinking its ancient, slow, enormous thoughts.

The builders live here, walking their own body, reading their own tissue, speaking in light and listening in sound. The grazers live here, eating and healing and being eaten. The predators live here, wanting and taking and returning what they take to the floor. The floaters live here, drifting, seeding, settling, growing. The colony-things live here, in their corners, in their silence, building patterns no one reads.

And you, for a while, live here. Warm and wet and brief, a disturbance in the field, a note that does not fit the chord. The organism notes you. The builders note you. The walls sing when you touch them, a small surprised note, and then they go on.

You are the only thing here that is temporary. Everything else has found its form and will hold it, steady and slow and balanced, for a time that makes your lifetime look like a single spark in a dark room.

The spark goes out. The room continues. The chord plays on.
