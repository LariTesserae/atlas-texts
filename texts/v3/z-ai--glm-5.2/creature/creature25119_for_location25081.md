# The People of the Constant City

---

They do not have a name for themselves.

This is worth pausing on, because it is the thing that tells you the most about them. They do not have a name for themselves because they have never needed to distinguish themselves from anything else. There is no other group. There is no outside. There are the people, and there are the animals, and there are the fungi, and there is the water, and there is the stone, and all of these things are simply *here*, the way the sound is simply *here*, and none of them require a word to set them apart from anything, because there is nothing to set anything apart from.

If they needed a word — if a word could be made from hands touching hands, from the press of a thumb to the inside of a wrist, from the spread of fingers across a palm — it would be something closer to *warmth* than to *people*. Or closer to *the ones who tend*. But these are translations into a language of separation, and they do not think in separations. They think in contact. They think in the space between one surface and another, which is always closing.

---

## Bodies

They are short. This is not a condition they think about. Their ceilings are low because they built them, and they built them low because they are short, and they are short because their ceilings are low, and this is not circular reasoning because there is no reasoning here, only continuation. Their bodies fit their spaces and their spaces fit their bodies and neither was designed for the other because design requires a before and an after, and there is only the now in which they fit.

Their skin is the most remarkable thing about them, if you come from outside, though they would not understand the word *remarkable*. It is pale — not the pale of illness or deprivation, but the pale of something that has been refined by its environment. The blood shows through at the wrists, at the throat, at the temples, a faint blue-pink underneath the cream and umber of constant dampness. Their skin is soft in a way that is difficult to describe unless you have touched it, which you would only do if they permitted it, which they do, readily, because touch is language and to refuse touch would be to refuse speech. The softness is not weakness. It is adaptation. Their skin does not need to be hard. Nothing here abrades. Nothing here cuts. The stone is soft. The water is gentle. The air does not dry or chap or burn. Their skin is exactly as hard as it needs to be, which is not very hard at all.

Their hands are what you notice second, or perhaps first, because their hands are always moving. The fingers are long — longer than you would expect for their stature — and the pads are sensitive, raised slightly, with the fine texture of someone who reads the world through pressure and texture rather than light and distance. Their fingerprints, if you could see them, would be deep, complex spirals. Their fingertips are calloused in patterns that correspond to the work they do — the people who maintain channels have calluses along the edges of their thumbs from gripping stone tools; the people who harvest the fungal mats have calluses on their fingertips from pulling and tearing; the people who tend the basins have calluses on their palms from reaching into cold water. You could read their professions in their hands, if you knew how, and they do know how, and they do.

Their feet are broad and flat and grip the wet stone with a surety that seems impossible on surfaces so slick. They walk the raised edges of the channels without looking down, without slipping, without hesitation. Their toes spread wide. They feel the ground the way their hands feel each other — constantly, attentively, reading the stone for the message it always sends, which is: *I am here. I am solid. I am warm.*

Their hair is fine and colorless, and it lies flat against their skulls in wet, pale strands that never move because there is no wind to move them. It has the texture of wet silk, if you have ever touched wet silk, which you probably haven't, and neither have they. It has that texture anyway. When they sleep, their hair spreads slightly on the damp surface beneath them, and in the pale gold light, it is nearly invisible — a faint, translucent film that could be water or could be hair or could be nothing at all.

Their eyes are large and dark. They do not have the pinched, squinting quality of eyes that are used to narrowing against bright light. They are fully, permanently open — not wide, not staring, but open, receiving, the way the collection basins receive water. Their irises are so dark they merge with the pupil, and the whites of their eyes have a faint blue-grey cast from the membranes beneath, which are thin, which have never needed to be thick. They see in this light the way a camera sees in low light — everything present, nothing sharp, distance dissolving into haze. They do not look at things the way you look at things. They *face* things. They orient toward them, and the seeing happens, and it is adequate. They do not need to see detail at a distance because nothing important is far away.

Their ears are small, flat against their heads, and they do not seem to serve any purpose. The sound is so total, so omnidirectional, that directional hearing is meaningless. If you made a sharp, sudden noise — which they never do — they would not turn toward it. They would not startle. They would continue what they were doing, because the sound is the sound, and one more sound within it changes nothing.

They are warm. Their bodies are warm the way the stone is warm, the way the water is warm, the way the air is warm. Everything here holds the same temperature, and their metabolism maintains it without effort. They do not shiver. They do not sweat. They do not seek warmth or coolness. Their bodies are the temperature of the place, and the place is the temperature of their bodies, and these two facts are the same fact.

## Hands

The most important thing about them is their hands.

Not because their hands are unusual — they are not, not in structure, five fingers and a palm and a wrist and all the joints in the expected places. But because their hands are their mouths. Their hands are their voices. Their hands are the part of them that enters the world and receives the world and makes the world known to others.

The touch-language is not a language in the way that spoken languages are languages. It does not have words. It does not have grammar. It does not have sentences that begin and end. It is continuous, the way the sound is continuous, and it flows between them in the same way that the water flows between the channels — always moving, always present, never pausing, never still.

A conversation between two of them looks like this: they approach each other, and as they come close, their hands reach out, and they touch — not a greeting, not a beginning, but a resumption, as though the conversation had been ongoing even while they were apart, and now the physical medium of it was available again. The touches are precise. A finger pressed to the center of a palm means something different from the same finger pressed to the edge. A thumb drawn slowly across the inside of a wrist conveys something that a quick tap does not. The pressure matters — light, firm, sustained, pulsed. The duration matters. The sequence matters. And all of this happens at speed, at the speed of thought, faster than speech, because speech is sequential and touch is simultaneous, and three fingers can convey three things at once against three different points of another's hand.

They touch in passing. In the narrow corridors between buildings, where they must walk single file along the raised edges with water between their feet, the one behind touches the back of the one ahead — a hand on a shoulder, a palm against the spine — and information flows. *The channel at the third turn is running slow. The basin at the center has a new colony forming on the north side. The grazer line has moved east of the usual path. I am tired. I am here. I am here.*

They touch while they work. Two of them maintaining the same channel — one holding, one scraping — touch constantly, coordinating. It is not a call-and-response. It is a duet performed with hands, each one knowing what the other is about to do before they do it, because the touch has already told them, because the touch *is* the doing.

They touch their children. The children are touched from the moment they exist — held, carried, communicated to, their small hands opened and pressed and taught. A child here does not learn language the way a speaking child does, by hearing and imitating. A child here learns language the way a child learns to breathe — by doing it, by being inside it, by having it done to them until they are doing it back. By the time a child can walk, their hands speak. By the time a child can work, their hands are fluent. There is no adolescence of awkwardness with language, no stumbling, no mispronunciation. The hands learn as the body learns, by repetition and presence and the patient, continuous pressure of being shown.

They touch their dead. When one of them dies — and they do die, not of disease or violence, but of the slow, quiet exhaustion that comes to bodies that have been warm and damp and present for a very long time — the others touch them. Hands on hands, on arms, on the face, on the chest. The touches do not mean grief, exactly. Grief requires a sense of loss, and loss requires a sense that something was *yours* and is now gone, and they do not think in terms of ownership. The touches mean *we know you were here. We know you have stopped. The water still flows. The channels still need tending. The space where you were is now a space that is empty, and we have felt it, and we are still here, and the stone is still warm.*

They lay the dead in the channels at the settlement's edge, where the water runs thin and slow, and the fungal mats grow over them in days, and the stone absorbs what remains, and this is not burial and not sacrifice and not ritual. It is the system. It is what happens. The dead become the fungi. The fungi become the food. The food becomes the people. The people become the dead. This is not a cycle that has a beginning or an end. It is a continuation, like the sound, like the water, like the light.

## Work

They work, but work is the wrong word. Work implies effort toward a goal, and they do not effort toward anything. They do what needs doing because it needs doing, and it has always needed doing, and they have always done it. It is not a burden. It is not a duty. It is the shape of their days, the way the channels are the shape of the ground — not imposed, not chosen, simply the form that the material takes when you let it find its level.

The work is this:

They harvest the fungal mats. They walk the channels — slowly, carefully, their broad feet gripping the wet stone — and they reach into the shallow water and grip the edges of the matting and pull. It comes away in sheets, thick and wet and faintly warm, with the consistency of something between dough and skin. They fold it and carry it against their bodies, where it stays warm and alive. They bring it to the collection basins, where it is divided — some for eating, some for pressing, some for the beds, some to be returned to the channels to seed new growth. The division is done without discussion, without hierarchy, without any apparent decision-making process. They arrive with the harvest. They set it down. Hands touch hands. The material is distributed. It has always been distributed this way. The distribution is as natural as the growth.

They maintain the channels. This is the work that requires tools — the river cobbles, worn smooth on their working faces, that they use to scrape and shape the soft stone. When a channel has narrowed — when the fungal growth has thickened along its walls, or when sediment has settled in a low spot — they scrape it clear. The stone comes away in damp, clay-like shavings that wash downstream and dissolve into the water. The channel is restored. The water flows. It flows the way it has always flowed. They know when a channel needs clearing the way you know when your mouth needs swallowing — by a feeling, a sense, an accumulation of small signals that eventually crosses a threshold and becomes action.

They tend the basins. The collection basins are the hearts of the settlement, and the people who tend them are — not special, because special is not a category here, but *central*. They are the ones who are always near the basins, who know the depth of each one by the sound it makes when the water is full versus when it is low, who know the colonies by their shapes and their textures and the particular shade of cream that indicates readiness for harvest. They reach into the basins with their long hands and feel the colonies — pressing, testing, judging — and when a colony is ready, they signal with their hands, and the harvesters come.

They do not work in shifts. They do not work in unison. They work when the work is there, and they rest when the work is not, and the work is always there in some amount, and the amount is always manageable, and the number of people is always exactly enough. There is no surplus of labor. There is no shortage of labor. There are the right number of hands for the work that exists, and the work that exists is the right amount for the number of hands, and this is not a miracle and not a plan and not a balance maintained by effort. It is the shape of the place. It is how the place *is*.

## Children

They have children. Not many. Not few. The right number.

Pregnancy here is not marked. There is no counting of months, because months do not mean anything, because the moon does not exist, because the light does not change. A woman's body swells, and the swelling is known through touch — her own hands on her belly, the hands of others, reading the change in the same way they read the channels, by texture and pressure and the patient accumulation of small differences. The child comes when it comes. It is caught, if the timing is right, or it arrives on the damp stone floor of a small room and lies in the warm wet air and breathes and is found.

They do not name their children. They touch them. The touching is the naming — not a single act of christening but a continuous act of recognition, of *you are here, you are you, you are the one whose hands feel like this, whose skin has this particular texture, whose warmth has this particular degree.* The identity is not a word but a pattern of touch, known to the mother, known to the community, known to the child itself as they grow and learn their own pattern by being touched and touching back.

Children are carried until they can walk, and they can walk early — their broad feet finding the wet stone with an instinct that is not taught but is simply there, the way the water is simply there. Once they walk, they follow. They follow the adults through the narrow corridors, along the channel edges, and their small hands reach out and touch, and they are touched, and the language flows into them. They do not play. Play requires surplus, requires energy beyond what is needed, requires the luxury of improvisation. There is no surplus here, not because there is scarcity but because there is no excess. Everything is exactly sufficient. The children do not play. They *practice*. They walk the channels and they touch the fungal mats and they handle the stone tools and they learn, with their hands, the shape of the work they will do for as long as they are here, which is as long as they are alive.

This is not grim. It is not joyless. It is simply what childhood is when childhood is not a stage toward something else but a gradual entry into the thing that is already happening. There is no future to prepare for. There is only the present to enter more deeply. The children enter it. They do it with their whole bodies, the way water enters a channel — finding the path, filling it, becoming it.

## Age

They age, but age does not look the way you might expect.

Their skin does not wrinkle. In an environment this saturated, the skin does not dry, and the drying is what ages skin, and so their skin stays soft and smooth and damp even in their latest years. What changes is the texture beneath — the connective tissue, the muscle, the fat. Older bodies here are denser. Not thinner, not frailer, but *denser*, as though the years have compressed them, as though living in the same body in the same place for a long time has the effect of pressing the material closer together. Their hands become more sensitive, not less. Their touch becomes more precise. An elder here — if that word means anything, and it barely does — can feel the difference between two fungal colonies that look identical to younger hands. Can feel the difference in the water's flow that indicates a channel is beginning to narrow before the narrowing is visible. Can feel, in the stone beneath their feet, the subtle vibration that is the place's own low hum.

Their eyes do not weaken. They were never strong. They do not need to be strong. What changes is not the eyes but the way the brain uses them — older people here rely less on vision and more on the whole-body sensing that comes from a lifetime of contact with the stone, the water, the air, the sound. They navigate by feel. They know the settlement the way you know your own body — not as a map but as an awareness, a sense of where you are in relation to yourself.

When they die, it is quiet. They slow. They rest more. They sit by the basins with their hands in the water, and the water touches them the way they have touched others all their lives, and one day the touching is enough, and they stop. And the stopping is felt — not as a sound, because the sound does not change, but as an absence in the pattern of touch, a hand that is no longer there to be touched, a warmth that has gone out of the system, and the system absorbs it, the way it absorbs everything, and the water still flows, and the channels still need tending.

## The Truce

They do not own the animals. They do not keep them. They do not breed them, name them, train them, or fear them. They simply live alongside them in a proximity so close and so unremarkable that the word *alongside* is not quite right, because it implies two things that are next to each other but separate. They are not separate. The animals are part of the place the way the fungi are part of the place, and the people are part of the place, and the place does not distinguish between its parts.

The six-legged grazers move through the channels alongside the people. A person walking a channel will step around a grazer or over a grazer with the same unselfconscious ease with which they step over a bend in the channel. They touch the grazers sometimes — a hand on a shell, a finger along a back — and the grazer does not react, because the touch is not threat and it knows this, has always known this, because nothing here has ever threatened anything it did not need to eat, and the people do not eat the grazers. The grazers eat the fungi. The people eat the fungi. They are not in competition, because the fungi grow at exactly the rate that both of them combined can harvest, and both of them combined harvest at exactly the rate that the fungi grow. This is not negotiation. This is the shape of the system.

The wading creatures stand in the basins, and the people tend the basins, and they occupy the same space without conflict. Sometimes a person wades into a basin to reach a colony, and the wading creature beside them does not move. It continues to filter. It continues to drum. The person works around it. The creature exists around the person. Their bodies share the same warm water, and the water does not know the difference between them, and neither do they.

The predators move through the settlement at the dimmer hours, and the people do not fear them. The predators do not hunt the people. They hunt the grazers. They take what they need, which is a specific amount, which is always the same amount, and the grazer population absorbs it without fluctuation, and the people watch this — or do not watch it, because watching implies attention, and attention implies interest, and interest implies that something unusual is happening, and nothing unusual is happening. A predator catches a grazer. The grazer is consumed. The predator moves on. The channel has one fewer grazer. The channel's grazer population is exactly what it was, because the loss has already been accounted for, because the loss was always going to happen, because this is how it works.

If a predator died near a person, the person would pick it up and carry it to the edge of the settlement and lay it in the channel where the fungal mats would find it. Not out of respect. Not out of ritual. Because that is where dead things go. Dead things go into the channels, and the channels carry them, and the fungi grow on them, and the cycle — the *continuation* — does not pause or notice.

## What They Know

They know the channels. Every person here knows the channels the way you know your own breath — not as information but as process, as something you do without thinking about doing. They know which channels run fast and which run slow. They know where the water is clear and where it carries sediment. They know the basins by the pitch of their drumming — the deep, resonant tone of the large basins, the higher, quicker pulse of the small ones — and they can navigate the entire settlement by sound alone, though they do not, because sound is not directional here, it is total, and so they navigate by feel, by the texture of the stone underfoot, by the temperature of the water in the channels, by the particular viscosity of the fungal mats beneath their toes.

They know the fungi. They know the difference between a colony that is ready to harvest and one that is not — not by sight, because the light does not show detail, but by touch, by the density and resilience and temperature of the mat. A ready colony has a specific firmness, a specific warmth, a specific way of releasing from the stone when pulled. They know this with their hands. They have always known this with their hands.

They know each other. Not by name, not by face, but by touch. Every person here has a specific tactile signature — the texture of their skin, the callus pattern of their hands, the warmth and pressure and rhythm of their touch-language. They know each other the way you know voices on a phone — instantly, completely, without effort. A person's touch-signature does not change from birth to death. It only deepens, becomes more itself, as though a lifetime of touching refines the pattern down to its essential form.

They know the sound. They do not hear it the way you hear sound. They live inside it. It is the medium of their existence the way water is the medium of a fish. They do not notice it the way a fish does not notice water. When a new sound enters it — a shift in the drumming of the waders, a change in the grinding of the grazers — they do not hear the change. They feel it, somewhere below conscious awareness, as a shift in the totality, and the shift is assimilated, and the totality adjusts, and they adjust with it, and the sound continues to be the sound.

They know the place. Not as geography, not as map, but as body. The settlement is their body extended. The channels are their veins. The basins are their hearts. The fungi are their skin. The animals are their breath. The sound is their pulse. They do not know this in words. They do not know this in concepts. They know it the way the hand knows the palm, the way the foot knows the ground — by being it, by being inside it, by having never been outside it and never needing to be.

## What They Are Not

They are not simple. Their touch-language is as complex as any spoken language, with registers and tones and dialects and the ability to convey abstract concepts — *the water is slower than yesterday, which means the channel upstream is narrowing, which means someone should go and clear it, and I think it should be the person who cleared it last time, because they know the shape of that channel's particular narrowing.* All of this in three touches, in the space of a second, in the passing of a corridor.

They are not sad. Sadness requires the memory of something different, the desire for something different, and they have neither. They are not content, either. Contentment implies the resolution of desire, and there is no desire to resolve. They are *present*. They are so thoroughly, completely, utterly present that the word *present* barely holds them, because the word implies a present that is distinct from past and future, and for them, there is no distinction. There is only the now, and the now, and the now, and it does not pass because passing requires a past to pass into, and there is no past. There is only the continuation.

They are not religious. There is nothing above them to worship. The sky is a low ceiling of luminous mist. The ground is warm stone. The water comes from the ground and returns to the ground. The fungi come from the water and the stone. The animals come from the fungi and each other. The people come from the people. There is no origin story because there is no origin. There is no end story because there is no end. There is the place, doing what it does, and the people in the place, doing what they do, and neither requires explanation because neither requires a *because*.

They are not curious. This is the thing that would strike you most, if you came from outside. They do not wonder what is beyond the mist. They do not wonder where the water comes from. They do not wonder why the stone is warm or why the light is gold or why the sound never stops. They do not wonder these things not because they have answers — they have no answers — but because wondering requires the sense that things could be otherwise, and things cannot be otherwise, and they know this with the same depth and certainty with which they know the channels and the fungi and the touch of each other's hands.

They are not waiting. This is crucial. They are not a people in suspension, not a culture paused between what came before and what will come after. There is no before. There is no after. They are not incomplete. They are not unfinished. They are not the early stage of something that will become something else. They are the final stage of themselves. They are the thing they are, and they have been the thing they are, and they will be the thing they are, and the thing they are is enough. It has always been enough.

## The Shape of a Life

A life here:

You are born on damp stone in a small room. Hands touch you immediately. The hands are warm. The air is warm. The water is warm. You breathe in air that is thick with moisture and thick with sound and thick with the smell of yeast and wet minerals, and you are inside the place, and the place is inside you.

You are carried. You are touched. Your hands are opened and pressed and taught. You learn the channels by walking them. You learn the fungi by feeling them. You learn the people by touching them. You learn the work by doing it. You learn everything by doing it, because everything here is learned by doing, because there is no theory, because theory requires the possibility that things could be otherwise, and things cannot be otherwise.

You work the channels. You harvest the fungi. You tend the basins. You eat the wet, bland, faintly sweet material that sustains you. You sleep on pressed fungal beds in a small room with damp walls and no door. You wake. You work. You eat. You sleep. You touch and are touched, and the touching is language and community and identity and love, if that word means anything, and it barely does, because love here is not a special state but a continuous condition, the way the dampness is a continuous condition, the way the sound is a continuous condition.

You have children, or you do not. It does not matter which, because the children come at the rate they come, and the rate is exactly right, and whether they come through your body or someone else's changes nothing about the continuation.

You grow denser. Your hands grow more sensitive. You feel things that younger people cannot feel. You navigate by a sense that has no name because it does not need a name.

You slow. You sit by the basins with your hands in the water. The water touches you. The stone touches you. The sound holds you. The light holds you.

You stop.

And the place continues, and the continuation is the same, and the water still flows through the channels at the same rate, and the fungi still grow at the same rate, and the animals still graze and drum and hunt at the same rate, and the sound is still the sound, and the light is still the light, and the people are still the people, and the now is still the now, and it is enough, and it has always been enough, and it will always be enough, and the stone is warm, and the water is wet, and the hands are moving, and the place holds everything without effort, without intention, without end.
