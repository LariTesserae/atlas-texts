# What Lives Here

## The Workers

They come up the cable-lines in batches of six or eight, strapped into the freight platforms with their kit bags between their feet, rising through the cloud layer and emerging into the indigo light with the expressions of people who have not yet decided whether they have made a mistake. Most of them are young. Not children — the company won't take anyone under eighteen, though the paperwork is loose enough that this is more convention than policy — but young in the way of people who have not yet accumulated the specific kind of debt that drives a person to altitude. They come from the valleys, from the coastal towns, from places where the ground has been ground for so long that it is dependable. They have grown up with their feet on settled earth. They have never been somewhere that the world is still being decided.

The ones who stay longer than a month change. Not in any way you could photograph or name on a form, but they change. Their eyes adjust to the dimness and they stop squinting, and then after a while they stop seeing the dimness as dim at all — it becomes simply the way light is, and when they go back down to the valleys on their leave, they complain that the daylight is too bright, too diffuse, too soft. They develop a gait that is specific to the settlement: deliberate, weight-conscious, each step placed with a slight hesitation that comes from living somewhere that gravity occasionally second-guesses itself. They walk like people crossing a frozen lake, testing.

They live in the prefab dormitories stacked against the cliff face — four to a room, bunk beds bolted to the walls, the corrugated steel humming faintly when the foundries are running at full capacity. The rooms are warm enough; the company provides good heating. But the warmth is mechanical, blown in through ducts, and it has a dry quality that sits on the skin differently than valley warmth. The workers sleep in layers. They keep their boots close to the bed. This is not superstition, exactly — they would say it is habit, convenience, the practical matter of being ready for a shift change. But it is also true that something about sleeping up here makes people want to know where the ground is. They sleep with one foot touching the floor, or with a hand pressed to the wall where the rock is close behind the paneling, and if you asked them why they would not be able to tell you, but the gesture is universal.

There is a foreman whose name is Ardel Vasik, though whether this is his actual name or the name he uses up here is a distinction that does not seem to matter to anyone. He has been at the settlement longer than anyone — three years, which is longer than most by a factor of four. He does not work the fissure anymore. He stopped after the first year, and he will not say exactly why, but his hands shake in a way that is not consistent with cold or with age, and the shaking gets worse when the fissure's pulse is on its bright cycle. He manages logistics now. He coordinates the cable-lines, the processing schedules, the incoming shifts. He is good at this. He has the kind of mind that finds satisfaction in keeping things in order, and the settlement is a place where order requires constant maintenance, where the rail-lines need daily adjustment because the rock they are bolted to is still shifting, where the tram schedules have to be rewritten every week because the distance from the foundry to the sorting house is not the same distance as it was last week.

Ardel knows the settlement the way a shepherd knows a flock — not by studying it but by living inside its rhythms for so long that the rhythms have become part of his own. He knows which generator will fail first when the temperature drops. He knows that the third catwalk from the north end has developed a slight flex that means its bolts are loosening before anyone else notices. He knows, though he would not say this aloud, that the birds have changed their roosting patterns in the last six months, moving closer to the dormitory blocks, and that the *echoes* have been seen in the finished sections of the settlement rather than only in the unbuilt areas, and that this means something, though he has not decided what.

He drinks in the commissary in the evenings, the only consistently warm social space in the settlement — a long room with low ceilings and the same corrugated steel walls, fitted with benches and tables that were clearly designed for a larger population. At full capacity, the commissary should seat eighty. It usually seats twelve to fifteen. The emptiness of it has its own weight. Ardel sits at the table nearest the kitchen, where the heat from the stoves comes through the partition wall, and he drinks a spirit that is distilled down in the valleys and brought up on the cable-lines and that tastes, up here, of almost nothing — the thin air strips the flavor out of it so that what you get is the sensation of warmth without the taste of alcohol. He does not drink to forget. He drinks to warm. He would like to be warm all the way through, and he has not been since he arrived.

The other workers rotate through. They come, they stay for two or four or eight weeks, they leave. Some come back. Most don't. The ones who leave do not usually say why, except in the oblique language of people who are trying to describe something that does not fit into the vocabulary of employment dissatisfaction. They say the altitude got to them. They say they couldn't sleep. They say the place felt wrong. When pressed, they say things like: *the walls were breathing*. Or: *I kept hearing the machines after they shut them off*. Or: *I went for a walk and the walk was longer coming back*. They say these things and then they stop saying things, because they have realized that what they are describing sounds like madness, and they do not want to be the kind of person who says things that sound like madness.

But a few of them — a specific few — leave because of what they saw in the unbuilt sections. These workers do not talk about it at all. They leave a shift early or they finish their contract and they go down the cable-line and they do not look back, and if you meet them in a valley town months later they will recognize you and they will be friendly but they will not talk about the plateau. It is not that they are afraid. It is that they have seen something that has no place in the world below, and they have understood, with a clarity that does not require words, that talking about it would be a category error — like trying to describe a color to someone who has only ever lived in a cave. Not wrong, exactly. Just useless.

## The Birds

They were here first.

This is the thing that the workers do not think about, because the workers arrived with the company's charter and the construction materials and the cable-lines, and so they believe they are the first. But the birds were here. Before the scaffolding. Before the foundries. Before the first prefab panel was bolted to the raw rock, the birds were living in the crevices of the uplifted stone, roosting in the dark mineral's faint glow, eating the insects that had already colonized the impossible mosses that were already growing in the condensation channels.

The birds are not birds.

This needs to be said plainly, because the word *bird* is a cage that the thing does not fit inside, but there is no other word, so the word *bird* is what is used. They are sparrow-shaped and sparrow-sized and they have feathers and they eat and they excrete and they reproduce — or at least, there are more of them in the spring, though spring is an approximate concept at this altitude, and their numbers swell and contract in a rhythm that does not precisely match the seasons below. They are birds in every way that a bird can be identified, and if you caught one and held it and examined it, you would find a heart and a beak and hollow bones and all the apparatus of birdness. A biologist would classify them. A taxonomy would be assigned. There would be no objection.

But they are not birds.

The way they move is the first sign. Birds fly reactively — they react to wind, to predators, to the sight of food, to the movement of other birds. Their flight is a continuous improvisation, a real-time negotiation with air and instinct. These do not improvise. They execute. Each flight path appears to be determined before the bird leaves its perch, and the bird follows that path with a precision that is less like flying and more like traveling along a wire — a wire that is invisible and that exists, perhaps, only in whatever geometry these birds use to organize their world. When a flock moves, it moves as a single body, each bird aware of its position relative to every other bird, the whole formation rotating and shifting with a coordination that is too fast for reaction and too fluid for rehearsal. It is as though they are all reading from the same score.

And they are always watching. Not with the anxious, reactive attention of prey animals — not the nervous head-turning of a sparrow on a branch — but with a steady, comprehensive awareness. They know where you are. They know where everyone is. When you walk through the settlement, the birds on the rooftops and the catwalks adjust their positions to maintain sightlines, and the adjustment is so smooth that you do not notice it unless you stop and turn quickly and catch them mid-rearrangement. Then you see it: a dozen small heads turning in unison, a dozen pairs of eyes tracking you with an expression that is not birdlike at all. It is the expression of something that is very interested in what you are doing and that is patient enough to wait until you are finished.

Ardel has a theory. He does not share it. He watches the birds the way he watches the generator output — as a metric, as a data point, as something that tells him about the state of the settlement. When the birds cluster in groups of three, things are stable. When they cluster in groups of five, the weather is changing. When they cluster in groups of seven, someone will leave. He has noticed this pattern over three years and it has not been wrong, and he does not know what to do with this information except to keep watching, and to add the data to the growing weight of things he knows but does not say.

## The Echoes

They do not have a formal name because the workers have not agreed on what they are, and the company has not acknowledged that they exist, and the workers have not reported them because the reporting process would require them to describe something that resists description. The word *echoes* emerged organically — someone said it, and it spread, and it stuck, because it is the right word. Not because they are echoes in the acoustic sense. Because they are echoes in the way that the settlement's own sounds are echoes — delayed, flattened, not quite synchronized with their source. The *echoes* are like the settlement's reflection in a mirror that is not quite flat.

They are the size of large dogs or small deer, and they are roughly four-legged, and they move through the unbuilt sections of the settlement at the edges of the lamplight, and they do not like to be seen. This last point is important. They are not aggressive. They are not territorial. They do not damage the equipment or the structures. They simply are there, in the unfinished spaces, moving with a gait that is recognizably quadrupedal but that is wrong in a way that is difficult to articulate. It is as though they are not walking on the ground but are walking on a ground that is slightly offset from this one — a ground that occupies the same space but that is a few degrees out of phase, so that their feet touch down a moment before their legs seem to move, and their weight shifts before their bodies do, and the whole operation of locomotion looks like a translation of animal movement rather than animal movement itself. As though someone were describing walking to something that had never walked and it was doing its best to comply.

They are shy. This is the word the workers use, and it is accurate in the way that *birds* is accurate — it is the closest available word, and it points in the right direction, even if it does not arrive. The *echoes* retreat when approached. They move away from light. They do not enter the finished buildings. They stay in the scaffolding, in the foundation trenches, in the spaces where the framework is up but the walls are not. And they are building.

This is the part that the workers have not told the company. In the unbuilt sections, where the scaffolding extends over bare rock and empty shelf, something is being constructed that was not in the plans. It is difficult to see clearly — the *echoes* work in the dimness, in the spaces between the lamplight, and the workers who have glimpsed it say that what they saw was not so much a structure as a suggestion of a structure. As though the *echoes* were not building with materials but building with the idea of materials, or with something that was almost materials and that was becoming materials as it accumulated. Like the vines that grow into the walls. Like the moss that fills the cracks. Like the birds that roost in the patterns of the scaffolding. The *echoes* are filling the prepared spaces with something, and that something is becoming denser and more defined, and it is beginning to look, in certain lights and from certain angles, like architecture.

No one has entered the sections where the *echoes* have been building. The workers' informal rules — do not follow, do not make eye contact, do not go where they have been — are sufficient to keep people out, for now. But the unbuilt sections are growing smaller, and the *echoes'* construction is growing larger, and at some point the two will meet, and the settlement will be something other than what the company planned. It will be something that the company and the workers and the *echoes* and the birds and the vines and the fissure have all built together, without coordination, without agreement, without any single vision — a thing that is emerging from the overlap of all their separate intentions, like a sound that is made when many instruments play at once and the combination is not any of the instruments but the resonance between them.

## The Fissure Dwellers

There is one more category of inhabitant, though *inhabitant* may be the wrong word, and *category* may also be the wrong word, and the workers do not have a name for them because the workers do not see them. Or rather, the workers see them, but they see them as features of the landscape rather than as presences within it. The faint blue-green glow of the dark mineral in the fissure. The pulse on its forty-second cycle. The way the glow brightens and dims, brightens and dims, like breathing, like a heartbeat, like something vast and slow that is dreaming with its eyes open.

When the glow is at its brightest, the fissure is warm. Not warm the way a mineral deposit might hold geothermal heat — warm the way a body is warm, warm with the interior temperature of something that is metabolizing, something that is alive. The workers who extract the mineral blocks from the fissure walls wear heavy gloves not because the mineral is hot but because touching it with bare skin produces a sensation that is not quite heat and not quite pressure and not quite anything that has a name. A tingling that migrates. A sense of being noticed. Several workers have described the feeling of the mineral as *being read* — as though the stone were examining their hand the way their hand was examining the stone, and were learning something from the contact that the worker could not perceive.

The blocks are cut. They are sent down the cable-lines. The fissure fills in. This is the thing that the geological surveys noted and could not explain and that the company has classified as proprietary: the fissure's mineral grows back. You cut a block from the wall and within a week the wall has re-formed, the dark mineral seeping in from somewhere — not from below, not from the sides, but from the same nowhere that the birds came from and the moss grows toward and the *echoes* build with. The mineral is not a deposit. It is not a resource. It is a secretion. It is what the fissure produces, the way a tree produces bark or a body produces skin, and the company is harvesting it, and the fissure is replenishing it, and this arrangement has been going on for three years, and neither party has acknowledged the other, and the transaction continues.

There is something in the fissure. The workers know this the way you know a room is occupied before you turn the light on — by the quality of the silence, by the pressure of attention, by the way the air feels when it is being breathed by something other than you. It is not the *echoes*, which are small and shy and four-legged. It is not the birds, which are many and coordinated and watching. It is something else. Something singular. Something that is the source of the pulse, the origin of the glow, the reason the mineral is there and the vegetation grows toward it and the birds roost in its light and the *echoes* build their not-quite-structures in its direction.

The workers who have worked the fissure long enough — and there are not many, because the turnover is high — develop a sensitivity to the pulse. They can feel it in their teeth, in the bones of their hands, in the base of their skull. They say the pulse feels like thinking — not their thinking, but the thinking of something else, something that processes information on a forty-second cycle and that is very, very slow and very, very thorough and that is considering the settlement the way a gardener considers a garden, with patience and with interest and with a kind of care that is not tender but is attentive.

Ardel felt it for a year. That was when he stopped working the fissure. Not because anything happened. Nothing happened. That was the problem. Nothing happened, and yet he could feel it thinking, and he could feel it thinking about him, specifically about him, with the focused and unhurried attention of something that had all the time in the world and that was not in any hurry to reach a conclusion about what he was or what should be done with him. He could feel it deciding. Not deciding anything in particular. Just deciding. Weighing options. Considering possibilities. And he realized, over the course of that year, that whatever was in the fissure was not going to do anything to him. It was simply going to keep thinking about him, and about the settlement, and about the birds and the *echoes* and the vines and the unbuilt sections, and that this thinking was not passive but constructive — that the thinking was the process by which the plateau was becoming a place, and that everything that was happening here was happening inside the scope of that thought, and that the workers and the birds and the *echoes* and even the rock itself were all elements of a consideration that was still ongoing.

He stopped working the fissure because he did not want to be inside the thought. He does not know whether he succeeded. He is still here.

## What Does Not Yet Live But Will

The settlement has plans for a population of two thousand. The current population is forty-three. The infrastructure was built for the future — the streets that are too wide, the commissary that seats eighty, the dormitories with their empty rooms and stacked bunks. The scaffolding reaches out over the bare rock like arms extended in invitation. The plans are for people. People will come. But in the meantime, the spaces are filling with something else.

The *echoes* build in the unbuilt sections. The vines grow into the walls. The birds roost in patterns that mirror the scaffolding. The fissure thinks its slow thoughts, and the plateau settles and shifts and decides, and the dark mineral grows back as fast as it is cut, and the moss covers the stone, and the fog rolls through like breathing, and all of it — all of it — is alive in a way that does not yet have a word, because the thing that is becoming here has not yet decided what it is, and when it decides, the word will come with the deciding, and the word will be the deciding, and the people who live here will learn the word or they will leave, and the ones who learn it will be the ones who stay, and they will be different from what they were, and the settlement will be different from what was planned, and the plateau will be different from what it was, and all of it — the becoming and the staying and the leaving and the learning — will be one continuous event, one long slow exhalation of a place that is still being born.

Forty-three people live here. So do the birds. So do the *echoes*. So does the thing in the fissure. So do the vines. So does the stone itself, raw and new and mid-birth, still warm from its own rupture, still learning what it means to be high and cold and exposed to the indigo sky.

None of them know what they are becoming. That is the point. That is what it is to live in a place that is not yet finished. You participate in the becoming without knowing the outcome. You build, and something builds alongside you, and the thing that is built is neither yours nor its but the third thing, the unnamed thing, the thing that grows in the overlap.

And it is growing. It is growing now. In the dimness, in the pulse-light, in the spaces between the scaffolding, it is growing.
