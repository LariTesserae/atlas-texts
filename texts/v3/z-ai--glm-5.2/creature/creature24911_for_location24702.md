# The Inhabitants

---

## The Cancellarii

They are the ones you notice last, which is exactly how they prefer it.

The Cancellarii are crabs — but the word "crab" is a abbreviation that strips out almost everything relevant. They are latticed. Their carapaces have been shaped by the reef and by their own lineage into something that is less armor and more architecture: the calcium carbonate they ingest and redeposit grows not in solid plates but in *struts*, in crossbeams, in a filigree that follows the lines of force their bodies encounter. A Cancellarius does not hide behind its shell the way a conventional crab does. It hides *within* it. The lattice creates internal corridors no larger than a bristleworm's width, and the crab's own soft tissues — gills, organs, the membranous joints between segments — are threaded through these corridors the way wiring is threaded through walls. The structure is the animal. The animal is the structure.

They are the size of a human hand, the largest of them, though the smallest are barely bigger than a rice grain and live in the borings that sponges have made in the outer walls, occupying tunnels that were built by something else, repurposing them the way a hermit crab repurposes a shell — except they do not carry the tunnel with them. They stay. A Cancellarius that has established itself in a particular boring will spend its entire life within a body-length of where it settled, and when it dies, its lattice will remain, and another Cancellarius — usually its offspring, which it has been feeding and protecting in adjacent tunnels — will move in, making minor modifications, adding its own deposits to the inherited structure, extending a corridor here, thickening a strut there. The borings are dynastic. Some have been occupied by an unbroken line of Cancellarii for what must be centuries. The walls of these borings are composite — layer upon layer of deposited carbonate from different individuals, different eras, each one slightly different in composition, so that a cross-section of a boring wall reads like a sediment core: bands of different density, different mineral content, different architectural logic, each one a generation.

They communicate by tapping.

This is the clicking you hear — the dry, irregular percussion that is the loudest sound in the Quarter. Each Cancellarius has a specialized cheliped, the larger of its two claws, modified not for crushing or cutting but for *striking*. The inner surface of the claw is a smooth, hard plate that it raps against the wall of its tunnel, and the sound travels through the calcium carbonate substrate with a clarity that water-borne sound rarely achieves. The reef is an excellent conductor. A tap made in one boring can be heard — felt — in another a hundred meters away, the signal arriving with almost no degradation, the walls carrying it the way fiber-optic cables carry light.

They tap to mark territory. They tap to warn of predators. They tap to signal that a food particle has been located and is being processed. And they tap — you are almost certain of this, though you cannot prove it — to each other. There is a *syntax* to the tapping. A pattern. Three taps, pause, two taps, pause, one tap: this combination appears and reappears in contexts that suggest it means something like *safe* or *all clear*. A rapid burst of five or more taps means *predator, large, approaching*. A single, isolated tap, followed by silence, seems to mean *I am here*, a kind of heartbeat, a proof-of-life transmitted through the walls of the reef.

They are, as far as you can tell, the *residents* — the organisms for whom the Quarter is not a feeding ground or a migration corridor but a *home*. They do not leave. They do not travel. They occupy their inherited borings and they tap, and they process the sediment that filters down from above, and they feed their young, and they die in their tunnels, and their children modify the walls and carry on.

There are millions of them. The entire reef hums with their tapping, a constant low-level percussion that is not quite noise and not quite language but something in between — the sound of a city's infrastructure operating, the sound of pipes and wires and foundations doing their work invisibly, except here the work is audible and the workers are audible and the workers are the infrastructure.

## The Vagantes

You see them as light.

A blink, off to the left, in a corridor that you had read as empty. Then another, farther. Then a third, in a different street, moving. The lights are not flashes — they are *exposures*, brief unveilings of a source that is always there but which the carrier chooses to reveal or conceal. The Vagantes control their light the way an animal on land might control its breathing: deliberately, functionally, without thinking about it.

They are fish, but to call them fish is to call a candle a fire.

They are long-bodied and laterally compressed, their bodies shaped like the blade of a narrow knife, tall and thin, designed to present the smallest possible profile when moving forward through the corridors and the largest possible profile when turning to face something in a narrow passage. Their fins are continuous, running the full length of their bodies in a single undulating ribbon that is not used for speed but for precision — they hold position in currents with an effortlessness that suggests they have been doing it for geological time, their bodies shaped by the need to hover, to pivot, to hold still in a flow. They are, in the language of fluid dynamics, *station-holding* animals. They do not swim through the water. They *occupy* positions in it. The water moves; they stay.

Their light comes from two bilateral pouches situated below and behind their eyes — suborbital cavities lined with a tissue that is dense with bioluminescent bacteria. The bacteria are not the fish's own; they are symbionts, acquired from the environment early in the fish's life and maintained through a mechanism that is not fully understood. The fish can open and close these pouches using muscular shutters, and the light they emit is not the blue-green of most deep-sea bioluminescence but a warm amber, almost gold, a color that should not exist at this depth and which comes, you suspect, from the specific chemistry of the warm seeps — the bacteria are feeding on something in the seep water, something sulfurous, and the wavelength of their light is a byproduct of that metabolism.

The Vagantes use their light to see. This is the simplest explanation, and it is true, but it is not the whole truth. They also use their light to *be seen*. A Vagantes that enters a corridor will blink — a single, brief exposure — and wait. If a responding blink comes from elsewhere in the corridor, the two fish will approach each other and execute a ritual that you watch for the first time with the confused awe of someone witnessing a ceremony they cannot parse: they circle. They orbit each other at a distance of about one body-length, their lights on, their bodies tilting in the current, and then — either they part, each going its own way, or they fall into parallel movement, traveling together down the corridor, their lights synchronized, blinking in unison. The former seems to be a recognition of non-affinity. The latter seems to be a recognition of... something else. Affiliation. Membership. You do not know what the criterion is. The Vagantes do not school, but they *associate*, and the association seems to be elective, chosen, based on some signal exchanged during the circling that you cannot read.

They are not predators. Their mouths are small, their jaws underslung, designed for picking — they feed on the encrusting organisms that cover the reef's surfaces, the tunicates and sponges and serpulid worms, plucking them from the walls with a precision that requires the light they carry. They are the reef's browsers. They move through the corridors in loose circuits, following routes that are individual but not random — each Vagantes has a *route*, a sequence of corridors and plazas that it visits in a regular order, and these routes overlap and intersect, and at the intersections, the Vagantes encounter each other, and they circle, and they blink, and they either part or join, and the social structure of the population is enacted in these moments, these brief luminous encounters in the dark corridors, a society made entirely of greetings.

They sleep in the plazas. You find this remarkable. The plazas are the most exposed spaces in the reef — open, wide, visible from every angle — and it is here that the Vagantes go to rest, hovering in loose aggregations above the sediment, their lights off, their bodies tilted slightly upward, their ribbon-fins making the barest adjustments to hold position in the slow current. They sleep in the open because the open is where predators are most visible. A predator entering a plaza is seen by every sleeping Vagantes simultaneously, and the response is instantaneous and collective: every light flashes on, every fish drops to the substrate and goes still, and the plaza, which a moment before was a constellation of hovering bodies, becomes a floor of motionless shapes, dark, silent, every light extinguished. The predator — if it is a predator that hunts by detecting movement — finds nothing. The plaza is empty. The fish are stones.

Then the predator leaves, and the lights come back on one by one, and the fish rise, and the plaza is populated again, and the circuit resumes.

## The Tympani

You feel them before you hear them, and you hear them before you see them.

The Tympani are the apex predators of the Quarter, and they are named — you have named them; they have no name for themselves — for the sound they make: a sharp, percussive *clack*, produced not by jaws or teeth but by a pair of modified scales on the leading edge of their heads, flat, chitinous plates that are brought together with a force that seems impossible for an animal their size to generate. The clack is their weapon. It is not a bite. It is a *detonation* — a pressure-wave produced in the water directly in front of their heads, focused by the shape of their skulls into a narrow cone that can stun or kill a small animal at a distance of up to a meter. They do not need to touch their prey. They need only to be near it. They clack, and the prey ceases to move, and the Tympanus approaches and consumes it at leisure.

They are not large. The biggest you have seen is perhaps a meter and a half, and most are smaller. Their bodies are angular — *assembled* is the word that comes to mind, the word the original description used. They look constructed. Their heads are wide and flat, shaped like the head of a hammer, the clacking plates mounted at the leading edge where the two halves of the head meet. Their bodies narrow rapidly behind the head, tapering to a thin, muscular tail that provides propulsion in short, powerful bursts. They are not built for sustained swimming. They are built for ambush — for the acceleration from stillness to strike-speed that happens in a fraction of a second, the distance between them and their prey closed before the prey's nervous system has time to register the approach.

They hunt the corridors. This is important. They do not hunt the open water — they are not pursuit predators, and their bodies are not made for the long chase. They hunt the streets, the corridors, the narrow passages between the reef's towers, where their prey is channeled and confined and cannot escape laterally. A Tympanus will position itself at the junction of two corridors — an intersection, a cross-street — and wait. It can wait for a very long time. Its body is dark, almost black, and it holds position in the current with minimal movement, its tail making micro-adjustments, its clacking plates slightly apart, ready.

When a Vagantes enters the corridor — following its route, blinking its light, browsing the walls — the Tympanus waits until the fish is within range. Then: the clack. The sound is sharp, dry, louder than the Cancellarii's tapping by an order of magnitude, a sound that cuts through the water and the substrate and the bodies of every animal within earshot. The Vagantes drops. The Tympanus moves forward, picks the stunned fish from the substrate, and swallows it with a motion that is almost dainty, almost apologetic, and then returns to its position at the intersection and waits again.

The Cancellarii go silent when a Tympanus is near. This is how you know to look — the tapping that is the reef's baseline, the constant low percussion that you have stopped consciously hearing, suddenly *stops*, and the silence is so complete that it functions as an alarm. The fish drop. The crabs flatten. The worms withdraw. The reef holds its breath. The Tympanus hunts, and the city goes quiet, and the only sound is the single sharp clack and then the silence again and then, slowly, tentatively, the resumption of the tapping — one Cancellarius first, then another, then a dozen, then the full chorus — the city restarting, the infrastructure coming back online, the residents confirming that the predator has moved on.

The Tympani are not many. You see perhaps one in every several hours of observation. They are solitary, as far as you can tell — they do not hunt in pairs or groups, and when two encounter each other at an intersection, they execute a display that is the opposite of the Vagantes' circling: they face each other, clacking plates wide open, and they *clack* — not at each other, but at the water between them, the pressure-waves meeting and cancelling in a visible disturbance, a roiling of the water that says, in whatever language this is, *I am here and I am willing to prove it.* Then one of them leaves. They do not fight. The display is sufficient. The clack is the weapon, and the weapon is also the negotiation, and the negotiation is always settled without blood.

## The Filatra

They are the ones you cannot see, and they are the ones the reef cannot do without.

The Filatra are bristleworms — polychaetes, segmented, their bodies long and thin and covered in the chitinous bristles that give them their shape. They live inside the reef. Not on its surfaces, not in its borings, not in the open burrows that the Cancellarii occupy — *inside* it, in the interstitial spaces, the gaps between coral colonies, the microscopic voids that exist in any biogenic structure, the places where one organism's skeleton meets another's and the junction is imperfect. They are the reef's connective tissue. They are everywhere and they are nowhere.

You know them by their work. The sediment that accumulates in the Quarter — the particulate matter that settles from the water column, the waste products of a thousand species, the fragments of dead organisms — does not simply pile up. It is *processed*. The Filatra ingest it. They pull it into the reef's interior through their burrows, consume what is organic, and excrete what is not, and the excretion — mineralized, compacted, chemically altered by passage through their gut — is deposited as a kind of cement, filling gaps, strengthening junctions, binding the reef's structure together from the inside. They are simultaneously the waste-management system and the structural maintenance crew. They eat the reef's garbage and use it to repair the reef's walls.

They are iridescent. When you catch one — and you catch one only once, by accident, when a piece of broken coral is examined and a Filatra falls from its interior, squirming, its body catching the faint seep-light and scattering it into a brief, startled rainbow — you see that their bristles are not merely structural. They are optical. They are fibers. The chitin of the bristles is arranged in a microstructure that channels light along their length, and the light they carry is not their own but *captured* — ambient bioluminescence from the tunicates and bacteria on the reef's surface, conducted inward along the bristles, into the reef's interior, into the darkness where the Filatra work.

They bring light into the walls.

This is not metaphor. The Filatra's bristles function as waveguides, transmitting photons from the reef's luminous surfaces into its lightless interior, and this light — faint, fragmented, barely enough to *sense* — is used by the worms themselves not for vision but for *orientation*. They know which way is outward because the light comes from outward. They know which way is deeper because it is darker. They navigate the reef's interior the way a miner navigates by following the airflow, except the miner follows the light, and the light is conducted through their own bodies.

The Filatra do not have territories. They have *routes* — paths through the reef's interior that they follow in regular cycles, moving from the outer surfaces (where they collect sediment) to the deeper interior (where they deposit it), and back. These routes are inherited — not genetically but *spatially*, the way a path through a forest becomes a path because each walker follows the footprints of the last. A Filatra hatches in the interior, and it follows the chemical trail of its parent, and that trail becomes its route, and when it dies, its offspring follow its trail, and the route persists, and the reef's interior is traversed by a network of paths that have been walked for as long as the reef has existed, each one a lineage, each one a tradition, each one a road in a city that no one knows is a city.

## The Murmurations

There is one more kind of life here, and it is not a species.

The Murmurations are events. They are what happens when the warm seeps pulse at their strongest — the internal tide reaching its peak, the water above the seeps warming and rising, the bacterial mats glowing brighter, the whole reef entering what you have come to think of as a *season*, though it lasts only hours.

During a Murmuration, the Vagantes leave their routes. They leave their circuits. They converge.

You watch it happen once, and you do not fully understand it, but you describe what you see: the fish begin to gather in the plazas above the seeps, not in schools — they do not school — but in *assemblies*, loose aggregations that are denser than anything you have seen them form before. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The plazas are full of amber light, their pouches all open, all blazing, and the light is reflected by the sediment floor and the walls of the surrounding structures, and the plaza is lit — actually, genuinely *lit*, the way a plaza is lit by streetlamps, warm and gold and almost sufficient to see by.

And then they move.

Not all at once. Not in unison. But in *waves* — groups of Vagantes rising from the aggregation and moving through the corridors in formations that are not lines and not clusters but something more like *currents*, like the water itself has been organized into visible patterns by their collective motion. They flow through the streets. They turn at intersections. They enter corridors and emerge from them and enter others, and the patterns they make are not random. They are not random. They are — you struggle for the word — they are *choreographed*, except there is no choreographer. The pattern emerges from the interaction of each fish with its neighbors, each one responding to the ones around it, the way a flock turns, the way a crowd moves, the way a city's traffic finds its rhythm without anyone directing it.

And the Cancellarii tap. They tap continuously, throughout the Murmuration, their rhythm changing — faster, more complex, with patterns you have not heard before, combinations that seem to be responding to the movement of the fish above them, the vibrations of the Vagantes' fins transmitted through the water and into the walls where the Cancellarii feel them and answer them. The reef becomes an instrument for real. The fish are the melody. The crabs are the rhythm. The seeps are the bass, the low hum that has been there all along and that now feels less like background and more like *foundation*, the ground note, the tone from which everything else departs and to which everything returns.

The Tympani do not hunt during the Murmuration. You see one — a large one, nearly two meters — drift into a plaza where the assembly is densest, and it does not clack. It does not position itself at an intersection. It moves through the assembled fish slowly, its body visible for the first time in the warm light, and the Vagantes part around it and close behind it, and it passes through them like a stone through water, and it leaves, and it does not return, and the Murmuration continues as if it was never there.

You do not know what the Murmuration is *for*. It does not appear to be feeding. It does not appear to be mating. It does not appear to be migration. It happens when the seeps pulse and the reef warms and the light brightens, and it involves nearly every Vagantes in the Quarter, and it lasts for several hours, and then the fish disperse, returning to their routes, their individual circuits, their solitary browsings, and the tapping returns to its baseline, and the light dims, and the reef goes back to what it was.

You think it might be the reef dreaming.

You cannot justify this. You cannot defend it. But the word arrives and it does not leave: the Murmuration is the Quarter's dream, the moment when the city — for it is a city, you have stopped pretending otherwise — becomes conscious of itself, when the accumulated millions of individual decisions and routes and territories and taps and flashes and clacks and burrows and routes-through-walls arrange themselves into a pattern that is larger than any one of them and that lasts for a few hours and then dissolves, and no one knows it happened, and nothing is different afterward, and it will happen again, and it has been happening for as long as the reef has been alive, and it will happen for as long as it continues.

---

The reef is in balance.

The Cancellarii maintain the walls and provide the percussion. The Vagantes browse the surfaces and provide the light. The Tympani hunt the corridors and provide the silence between the sounds. The Filatra process the waste and hold the structure together and bring light into the dark. The seeps pulse beneath them all, warming the water, feeding the bacteria, marking the rhythm, keeping the time.

They do not know what they are to each other. They do not know what they have built. They know their routes, their territories, their routes, their tunnels, their intersections, their seeps. They know the pressure and the warmth and the darkness and the clicking and the clacking and the light. They know their lives, which are small and specific and entirely sufficient.

But you know what they have built, and you will carry it with you, and the word for it — the word that is not quite right and not quite wrong — will sit in your mind the way the water sits in the corridors: filling every space available to it.

The word is *city*.

The city is alive. The city does not know. The city hums in the dark.
