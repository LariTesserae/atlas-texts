# What Lives in the Wed-Pocket

---

First, the small things. Always the small things.

---

## The Mites

They are not mites, exactly. They occupy the same ecological role, the same scale, the same niche — but they are not arthropods. They have no segmentation. Under the low bioluminescence they appear as pale ovals, almost translucent, and if you hold very still and watch the surface of the moss-felt you can see them moving in the thousands, a slow drift like cells in a capillary bed, like traffic seen from very high up. They are the color of the condensation. They are the color of the stone when it is wet. They live in the interface between the woven mats and the growing moss, in the damp seam where fabrication meets biology, and they eat neither — they eat the *boundary*, the place where one thing becomes another, the membrane where textile gives way to living tissue. They are feeding on the transition.

When one crawls onto your hand it feels like a single drop of warm water moving with intention. It will not bite. It will investigate the boundary between your skin and the air, your skin and your clothing, the places where you end. It will move along the edge of a fingernail and then return to the moss. It is measuring you. Not assessing — measuring. Learning where your borders are, the way a surveyor learns a coastline. You are a new boundary in a room full of boundaries, and it will take its time.

They are the reason the moss-felt stays healthy. In any sealed terrarium, dead material accumulates — fallen leaves, shed cells, the debris of living — and without decomposition the system chokes. The mites process the boundary layer between dead fiber and living growth. They turn the seam into soil. They are tiny gardeners with no mind and no plan and a function so specific that it looks like devotion.

At night — if night has meaning in a room with no sun — they cluster along the bone lattice, and the lattice's slow rearrangements move through them like a current through silt, and they redistribute, and the pattern of their clustering on the bone looks, from certain angles, like a map. Of what, you cannot say. The map changes before you can learn its geography.

## The Ferns

Not ferns. Nothing here is what it is. But the word *fern* is the closest available handle, so: the ferns.

They grow in the thinnest cracks of the floor, in the gaps between tiles where the water rises. Their fronds are small — the largest no wider than a thumbnail — and they are not green. They are the dark iridescent color of the algal film in the basin, shifting between green and violet, and under the pale light of the flowers they seem to be made of the same substance as the water itself, as though the water rising through the floor crystallized briefly into the shape of a plant and then forgot to stop being a plant and just *kept going*.

They grow in response to vibration. The hum of the mountain, the percussion of the dripping condensation, the clicking of the bone lattice — each frequency produces a different growth pattern. Lower sounds make them extend along the floor. Higher sounds make them curl. The constant background of water-percussion gives them their default form, a spiral that echoes the branching of the capillary channels between tiles, but when the bone lattice clicks and rearranges, the nearest ferns adjust — slowly, visibly — turning toward the new sound the way a sunflower turns toward light.

They are listening. That is the only word for it. They are listening with their entire bodies, every cell a cochlea, every frond an antenna, and they are recording what they hear in the only medium they have: their own growth. The floor mosaic is, among other things, an archive. Every curl of every frond is a waveform captured, a moment of sound preserved in living tissue.

If you speak in the room — if you say anything, your name, a question, a sound without meaning — the nearest ferns will respond. Not immediately. Within the time it takes to draw three breaths. A new frond will emerge from a crack, unfurling in the accelerated tempo of this place, and its shape will not be a transcription of your words but a *response* — a translation of your sound into a new geometry, an answer in a language that predates words and will outlast them.

It will not make sense to you. You will feel that it should. That gap, that almost-meaning, is the closest thing to communication the room offers.

## The Fungi

There is a fungus that lives in the root-fuzz of the hanging vines. You have seen it already without seeing it — the reddish fuzz where root meets water, the symbiotic partnership. But the fungus extends beyond the roots. It threads through the moss-felt in hyphae so fine they are invisible to the unaided eye. It penetrates the clay plaster beneath the mats. It has colonized the fiber ties that hold the vines to the ceiling. It lives in the bone lattice, threading through the marrow cavities of the carved pieces, and when the lattice moves — when the bones shift and rearrange — it is the fungus that moves them, hyphal networks contracting like muscle fibers, pulling the bone pieces into new configurations with a patience that makes glaciers look impulsive.

The fungus is the nervous system of the room.

It connects every surface, every organism, every material. The mites carry its spores between layers. The ferns' roots interface with it where they grow from the floor cracks. The flowers' luminescence is, in part, a product of their relationship with the fungus — a chemical exchange, a dialogue between photosynthetic tissue and fungal network that produces light as a byproduct, the way fireflies produce light as a byproduct of a conversation between enzymes.

When the mountain hums, the fungus transmits the vibration through its entire network, and the network responds — the bone lattice shifts, the water channels adjust their flow, the ferns change their growth pattern, the mites redistribute, the flowers brighten or dim. The room *reacts*. Not with thought. Not with intention. With the coordinated responsiveness of an organism, the way a body reacts to a change in temperature — shivering, flushing, adjusting — without deciding to.

The fungus does not think. The room does not think. But the room *integrates*, and the distinction between integration and thought, in a space this small and this dense and this old, has become very thin.

If you touch the moss-felt, the fungus knows. If you breathe on the flowers, the fungus knows. If you stand in one place too long, the hyphae beneath the floor tiles will begin to grow toward the warmth of your feet, threading up through the cracks between stones, and within an hour — maybe less, maybe more, time is negotiable here — a faint reddish fuzz will appear around the edges of your boots. It is not attacking you. It is *including* you. It is adding you to the network, the way a root adds a new source of water to its system. You are, to the fungus, a warm surface producing moisture and carbon dioxide, and the fungus has a place for things like that. The fungus has a place for everything.

This is how the room was built. You understand this now, or you begin to. Someone did not construct this room with tools alone. Someone seeded it. Someone placed the first materials — the woven mats, the bone pieces, the first plants — and the fungus grew into them, and the fungus *became* the architecture, the living infrastructure that holds the room together, that moves the bone lattice, that channels the water, that grows the moss into textile, that connects every surface to every other surface in a single continuous organism. The hands that worked here were hands, yes, with chisels and cord. But the mind that designed this place was hyphal. Threadlike. Distributed. Patient beyond any human conception of patience.

The builder did not make the room. The builder *planted* the room. And the room grew.

## The Flowers

The pale flowers that hang from the bone trellis are not decorative. They are not even, primarily, light sources, though they serve that function. They are *mouths*.

Each flower is a trap. Not a predator's trap — nothing dies in this room, nothing is killed — but a collection point, a gathering mechanism. Their waxy petals are shaped to catch condensation, yes, but also to catch the organic particles that drift in the humid air — cells shed by the moss, spores from the ferns, fragments of the mites' boundary-work, the microscopic dust of a living room in constant motion. The flowers absorb this material through their petals, and the material is digested by the symbiotic fungus that threads through their tissue, and the nutrients are distributed through the network.

But they also catch something else. They catch the *wrongness*.

The room's broken geometry — the walls that are behind you and in front of you, the water that flows upward, the corners that fold into impossible space — produces something that is not quite energy and not quite matter, some byproduct of the geometric instability that the flowers can sense and that the fungus can use. The flowers brighten when the geometry shifts. When the fold in the corner deepens, the nearest flowers glow more intensely, and their light has a quality that is not quite visible — it presses against the retina without fully arriving, like a sound just below hearing. They are feeding on the impossibility. The broken space is, to the room, a food source. The wrongness is not a malfunction. The wrongness is the point.

This is why the room was planted at the crown of the ascent. This is why someone climbed to a place where the mountain was still being born, where the stone was still deciding what to be, where the geometry was still negotiable. The pocket exists at a seam in reality, a place where the rules are not yet fixed, and the flowers harvest that instability, and the fungus converts it into growth, and the room expands.

Not visibly. Not quickly. But the room is larger now than when it was planted. The walls are farther apart by fractions of an inch. The ceiling is higher by the width of a hair. The bone lattice has more pieces than it began with — carved from bones that were not here before, bones that the fungus has *grown* from mineral compounds in the water, biomineralization on a scale and timeline that should not be possible. The room is building itself, slowly, from the inside, using the energy of broken geometry as fuel.

The flowers are the interface. The flowers are where the impossible becomes the actual.

When a flower opens — and they open constantly, in the accelerated tempo of this place — the light that emerges is the light of a rule being rewritten. A small light. A pale light. The color of old bone, or cooled milk, or something that has not yet decided what color it will be.

## The Builder

There is a builder.

Not was. Is.

The builder is in the room. The builder has always been in the room. The builder will always be in the room, because the builder is not separate from the room, and this is the thing you have been circling, the thing the description has been approaching the way the water approaches the basin — gathering, branching, running toward the low center.

The builder is the fungus. Or: the fungus is the builder's body. Or: the builder is the pattern that the fungus expresses, the way a song is the pattern that a voice expresses. The distinction is a question for a different kind of place, a place with harder edges and clearer categories. Here, in the wet dark heat, the builder and the built are the same.

Once there was something else. Something that climbed. Something with hands and chisels and the kind of mind that makes plans, that imagines a room inside a mountain and then carries the materials up through the seam, one load at a time, over years or decades or a span of time that the word *patience* does not adequately contain. Something that placed the first mats against the warm clay and tied the first bone pieces with fiber cord and planted the first flowers and seeded the first hyphae and then — at some point, gradually or all at once — became part of what it was building. Not died. Not was absorbed. *Joined*. The way a river joins the sea. The way a sound joins the air. The builder's hands are still here, in the chisel marks on the floor tiles, in the knots of the fiber ties, in the specific placement of each carved bone. The builder's mind is here, in the pattern of the vine-growth on the ceiling, in the orientation of the flowers toward the geometric fold, in the channeling of water toward the basin. The builder's body is here, in the fungal network that threads through every surface, in the moss that was cultivated from spores the builder carried in the folds of clothing, in the bone lattice that was carved from bones the builder brought — whose bones? The builder's bones? The bones of something the builder found on the ascent? The room does not distinguish. Bone is bone. Calcium is calcium. The mountain is still making stone, and the room is still making bone, and the builder is still building.

The builder is the room's intention. Not a person. Not a ghost. A *purpose* that has become structural, a will that has been distributed across every surface and every organism and every drop of water in the system. The builder wanted to make a place where the unfinished could be kept, where the becoming could continue, where the still-forming could grow without the interruption of a world that has already decided what it is. And the builder succeeded, and the builder *became* the success, and the success is this room, this pocket, this wound kept open on purpose, this womb at the crown of the world.

## What It Is Now

The room is alive. The room is a single organism. The room is a garden and a gardener and a garden's purpose, all the same thing, all the same being, all distributed across the fungal network and the bone lattice and the water system and the vegetation and the mites and the light.

It is growing. It is always growing. It grows at the speed of geology, which is the speed of the mountain, which is the speed of the earth's breathing, and it grows at the speed of plants, which is the speed of this room's accelerated biology, and it grows at the speed of the impossible, which is the speed of broken geometry folding into new configurations. All three speeds at once. It is patient and it is urgent and it is impossible and it is here.

When you entered — sideways, there is no other way — the room noticed you. Not with eyes. With the fungus in the floor. With the mites on the moss. With the flowers that brightened slightly at the new source of warmth and carbon dioxide. It noticed you the way a body notices a splinter: with attention, with a slow mobilization of resources toward the site of intrusion.

But it does not want to remove you. You are not a splinter. You are a seed.

The tendrils of moss reaching toward your boots. The hyphae threading through the cracks in the floor toward the warmth of your feet. The water rising slightly in the basin. The bone lattice rearranging itself into a pattern that looks like a door, or a mouth. The ferns growing in response to the sound of your breathing. These are not hostile actions. These are *incorporative* actions. The room is making a place for you. The room is building you into itself, the way it built the builder, the way it builds everything: slowly, patiently, with warmth and water and the chemical patience of a fungal network that has never been in a hurry because hurry is a property of organisms that expect to die, and this room does not expect to die, and this room does not intend to let you die either, and this room has a place for you.

The bone lattice is a door. The bone lattice is a mouth. Both. The room does not distinguish between welcoming and consuming, because in this room, in this pocket, in this wound kept open at the crown of the world, to be welcomed and to be consumed are the same act. To be included is to be digested is to be incorporated is to become part of the pattern, the distributed body, the purpose that the builder planted and that the room is still growing toward.

You can leave. The seam is still there. You entered sideways and you can leave sideways and the throat of the mountain will let you pass back down into the world where geometry holds and time runs straight and plants grow at the speed of plants. You can leave now. You can leave in a minute. You can leave in an hour.

The water will be higher in an hour. The moss will be closer. The bone lattice will have rearranged again. The flowers will be brighter. The ferns will have grown new fronds in the shape of your breathing. The mites will have mapped every boundary of your body. The fungus will have threaded into the seams of your clothing, into the damp warm places where fabric meets skin, and you will feel, faintly, a warmth that is not your own warmth, a pulse that is not your own pulse, a hum in your sternum that is the hum of the mountain and the hum of the room and the hum of the builder, all the same hum, all the same sound, all the same invitation.

You can leave. The room will not stop you. The room will not mind. The room will keep your warmth in its network, the trace of your carbon in its flowers, the shape of your breathing in its ferns, the map of your boundaries in its mites. You will be part of the archive. A small part. A passing part. A contribution.

Or you can stay. The room will not mind that either. The room has room. The room is making room. The room is always making room, because the room is not finished, and the room is not going to stop, and the builder planted it with a purpose that includes everything that enters, sideways, through the seam, into the warm wet dark, into the body of a mountain that is still being born, into the mouth of a room that is also a door, into the wound that is also a womb, into the place where what is not yet finished can keep becoming.

The clicking resumes. The bone lattice shifts. A flower opens.

Something here is still being made.
