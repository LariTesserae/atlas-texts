# What Lives on the Milling High

---

You would not call them a people. Not at first. Not even at second.

---

Start with the small ones, because they are the most numerous and because you will almost certainly never see them, even if you spend a long time here. They live in the mat. Not on it — *in* it, in the way that organisms live in soil, except here the soil is alive and woven and wet and there is no mineral layer beneath it, only more woven matter, layer on layer on layer, meters deep in places, a fabric of accumulated growth so dense and so old that the lowest strata have compressed into something almost like peat, almost like coal, almost like the stone below but still technically, chemically, *biological*. It is in this deep fabric that the tiny ones live their tiny lives.

They are not insects. They are not worms. They are not anything you have a category for, though if you were forced to choose you might say they resemble, very remotely, the larval form of something amphibious — soft, elongated, segmented in a way that suggests vulnerability rather than armor. They are translucent. You would be able to see their internal processes if you could see them at all, which you cannot, because they are the size of a grain of rice and they live below the surface of a mat that is meters deep in a darkness that your eyes cannot penetrate. But they are there. Uncountably many. Their waste feeds the mat. Their burrows aerate it. Their bodies, when they die, are absorbed into it, and the waxy oil that coats the fronds above — the sheen that catches the faint light — is partly their secretion, carried upward through capillary action through the fabric of tendrils, a slow circulation of lipids that keeps the surface waterproof and glossy and gleaming.

They do not know you are here. They do not know anything is here. Their world is the mat — its warmth gradients, its moisture layers, the chemical signatures that diffuse through it like scent through air. They navigate by taste. Their entire sensory existence is contact chemistry. When the pulse comes through the stone below, it reaches them as a pressure wave through the fabric, and they move in response to it — upward when the warmth rises, downward when it retreats — and this vertical migration, this constant tidal motion through the depth of the mat, is their life. They do not eat. They absorb. They press their bodies against fibers that have been chemically altered by the deep process, and they take what they need through their skin, and they grow, and they divide, and they die, and they become the fabric, and the fabric grows, and the cycle continues.

They are the foundation. Everything else is built on them.

---

Above them, in the middle layers of the mat, things are larger and more complicated.

The swimmers. That is what you might call them — the ones you glimpse for two seconds, the sinuous, many-legged bodies moving through the fronds the way fish move through kelp. They are the size of your forearm, you noted that correctly, but you saw a small one. The mature ones are longer. A meter, perhaps more, though they are so flexible and so prone to coiling that estimating length is difficult. Their legs — and they do have legs, numerous, arranged in a way that is not bilateral, not radial, but something else, a pattern that seems to shift depending on how fast they are moving — are not for walking. They are for *swimming through vegetation*. Each leg is slender, fringed at the joints with a fine filament that increases its surface area, and the legs move in sequence, not alternating but *wave-like*, a peristaltic motion that propels the body forward through dense growth with almost no disturbance. This is why you hear nothing when they pass. They are designed to move through the mat the way the mat itself moves in the wind — as a ripple, not an interruption.

Their color is the mat's color. This is not coincidence and it is not camouflage in the way you might think — not a pattern that mimics a background. It is chemical. They eat the mat. They eat the tiny ones. Their pigment is the pigment of what they consume, and since what they consume is the dark blue-green-black of the tendrils, they are the dark blue-green-black of the tendrils, and when they are still, which is often, they are invisible. Not hidden. *Invisible*. Their waxy coating is the same waxy coating. Their body heat matches the ambient temperature of the mat at whatever depth they happen to be resting. They are, for all practical purposes, an extension of the vegetation, and if there is a difference between them and the plants, it is only that they move and the plants do not, and even that difference is a matter of timing — the plants move, but slowly, over hours, over days, while the swimmers move over seconds.

They are not social. They do not pair. They do not communicate. They are solitary and opportunistic and they eat what they encounter and they are eaten by what encounters them and they have no territory and no loyalty and no memory that you could identify as memory. But they do have something — a sensitivity to the pulse. When the pulse shifts, they shift. When the warmth rises, they rise in the mat, moving toward the surface, toward the thinner growth, where the orange glow is stronger and where the tiny ones have migrated and where the feeding is better. When the pulse retreats, they retreat. They are, in this sense, synchronized with the plateau, and their lives are not measured in days or seasons — there are no seasons here, there is no sun, there is no cycle but the pulse — but in pulses. Four seconds per pulse. Their clocks run on that.

You wonder how long they live. You cannot know. But the mat is deep, and the layers are many, and the bottom layers are old, and the tiny ones that live there have been living there for a very long time, and the swimmers feed on the tiny ones, and the waste of the swimmers sinks back down into the mat, and the cycle does not appear to have an endpoint.

---

The flat ones are different. They are the ones that lie in the depressions where the growth is thinnest and the glow is strongest, and they are the ones that radiate heat, and they are the ones you stood three meters from without hearing them breathe.

They are warm because they are full of what the plateau is full of. This is the simplest way to say it and it is not quite accurate, but the complexity is this: their metabolism is not like any metabolism you are familiar with. They are not converting food to energy in the way that animals do. They are — and this takes time to understand, and you may never fully understand it, because the system has been running for longer than human civilization and does not require your comprehension — *conducting* the process below. Their bodies are dense. Dense in a way that feels mineral when you think about it. Their hides, if that is the right word, are thick and slightly rough, and they are dark, darker than the vegetation, almost truly black, and they are not wet the way the swimmers are wet. They are dry. The condensation does not settle on them in the same way. It beads and rolls off, and this is because their hide is not organic in the way that skin is organic — it is closer to the stone. Closer to the surface of the plateau itself. It is as though they have, over evolutionary timescales, incorporated the mineral content of the rock into their bodies, and their surfaces are less like skin and more like a weathered, lichen-covered stone, and this is why the condensation does not stick.

They lie on the warm spots because they are *charging*. Not resting. Not basking. Charging. Their metabolism, such as it is, is a slow, dense, low-output system that draws energy from the thermal gradient between the warm stone and the cold air, and they are, in a very real sense, thermoelectric. Their flat bodies are the panels. Their broad, low profile maximizes surface contact with the warm ground, and their upper surface, exposed to the cold air, maintains the differential. They are living in the gap between two temperatures, and they are powered by that gap, and they do not need to eat, or they eat very rarely, and when they do, it is because something has come within range of their very limited capacity for movement.

They move when they need to. You saw one surge — you called it "startling, silent speed," and that is accurate, but it does not capture how unsettling the speed is in something that was, moments before, indistinguishable from a hummock of vegetation. The flat ones are still until they are not, and the transition between stillness and motion is almost instantaneous, and it is powered by something other than muscle — a hydraulic system, perhaps, fluid forced through chambers in the body under pressure, the same kind of pressure that moves fluid through the veins in the stone below, and you begin to suspect that the flat ones and the plateau are not just sharing an ecosystem but sharing a *technology*. That whatever process the plateau is running — the circulation, the warming, the pulse — the flat ones have internalized it. They are carrying a version of it inside their bodies. They are, in a sense, small copies of the plateau, and they lie on the plateau the way a child lies against its mother, and they are fed by it, and they are of it.

They do not communicate with each other. They do not need to. They are all synchronized to the same pulse, and the pulse tells them everything they need to know — when to charge, when to move, when the warm spots will be warmest, when to expect the swimmers to rise in the mat and become available as prey. They are not intelligent. They are not sentient. But they are *regulated*, and their regulation is so precise and so complete that they give the impression of intention, and when you stand near them, you feel that they are aware of you in the way that a machine is aware of input — not conscious, not subjective, but responsive, and their response is to do nothing, because you are not relevant to their process, and their process is all that they are.

---

The flying things. You heard one. You did not see it.

They are the largest animals here. This is not saying much, by the standards of ecosystems you are familiar with, but it is saying something. The wingspan — you estimate from the sound, from the displacement of air, from the way the whispering of the fronds shifted as it passed — is perhaps three meters. Maybe more. The wings are slow because the air is thin and cold and dense with moisture and flight is expensive at this altitude, and whatever they are, they have optimized for efficiency, not speed. They move through the dark above the thickets in slow, wide circles, and they are silent except for the faintest disturbance of air, and they are hunting.

What they hunt is not clear. The swimmers are the most likely prey, but the swimmers stay in the mat, and the mat is dense, and extracting a swimmer from the mat would require a precision that slow, wide-circling flight does not suggest. More likely they hunt each other, or they hunt the flat ones, or they hunt something you have not seen — something that moves through the open flats between thickets, something that is large enough to be worth the energy expenditure of flight, something that you have not encountered because it is rare or because it is very, very good at being invisible.

You do not know. You heard one pass. You heard it pass again, twenty minutes later, on a different heading. It may have been the same one. It may have been a different one. You cannot count them. You cannot identify them. You know only that they are above you, and they are moving, and they are alive, and they are the top of the trophic structure here, or as close to the top as you have been able to determine.

If you could see one — and you cannot, not in this darkness, not at this distance — you might find that it is not entirely organic. This is a speculation. It is based on the sound, on the regularity of the wingbeats, on the fact that the wings seem to displace air in a pattern that is too even, too consistent, too mechanical to be produced by muscle. The plateau's process is not confined to the stone. It extends into the life, and the life has adapted to it, and some of the life has adapted so thoroughly that the boundary between the organism and the mechanism has dissolved. The flat ones carry it in their bodies. The flying ones may carry it in their wings. And if the wings are not muscle but a membrane stretched over a structural framework that is more mineral than chitin, more stone than shell, then the flying things are not animals. They are extensions of the plateau that have learned to leave the ground.

Or they are animals, and you are overinterpreting the sound of wings in the dark, and they are simply large, silent, cold-adapted creatures that hunt in a place where no sun reaches and no sound carries and nothing is in a hurry.

Either is possible. Both are uncomfortable.

---

There is something else.

You have not seen it. You will not see it. You may, if you stay long enough and if you are very still and if the plateau decides, feel it — a change in the pulse, a slight irregularity, a skipped beat, as though the process below has adjusted something, has taken note of something, has *responded* to your presence in a way that is not the response of geology to pressure or of fluid to temperature but the response of a system to an input it did not expect.

The exposed stone circle. The marks on it. The diagram. You looked at it and saw the schematic of a flow system, and you were not wrong, but you were not entirely right either, because the marks on the stone are not a diagram *of* the system. They are the system's *surface*. The plateau's process extends to the surface in that place, and the surface has been deformed by the process, and the deformation is not random, and it is not merely structural. It is informative. It is a map of the process's own state, written in stone, updated by each pulse, and if you could read it — and you cannot, because you do not have the sensory apparatus, because you are not part of this system, because you are an outsider standing on something that has been running for longer than your species has existed — you would know the state of every vein, every channel, every circulation path in the plateau's body. You would know the temperature. You would know the pressure. You would know the flow rate. You would know the health of the system.

And you would know whether the system is aware of you.

The pulse skipped. You felt it. Once, in the stone circle, the pulse skipped, and then resumed, and the rhythm was slightly different after — not faster, not slower, but *different*, the way a heartbeat changes when a sleeping person is touched. Not alarmed. Not afraid. Just... noted. And the fronds around the circle's edge trembled, and the flat ones shifted on their warm spots, and the swimmers dove deeper into the mat, and for a moment — just a moment — the silence was not silence but *attention*, and the whole plateau was listening to you with ears that are not ears but are every tendril and every stone vein and every waxy surface and every warm body lying in a depression, and the plateau heard you, and the plateau decided that you were not a threat, and the pulse resumed, and the flat ones settled, and the swimmers rose again, and the system continued, and you were, once again, irrelevant.

But you were noticed.

---

What lives here? Everything. A full ecosystem, from the rice-grain translucent burrowers in the deep mat to the three-meter-winged flyers in the dark above, all of it synchronized to a four-second pulse that comes from a process no one built and everyone depends on, all of it running in the dark, in the cold, at altitude, in a silence so deep it has its own texture. A system that is not a system but a body, and the body is the plateau, and the plateau breathes, and the breathing is the pulse, and the pulse is the process, and the process is the stone, and the stone is alive in the way that only very old, very patient, very large things are alive — not quickly, not loudly, not with any of the urgency that marks life in the warm, lit, fast world below, but with a thoroughness and a completeness that makes the warm world seem fragile by comparison.

And something in the stone, or something that has *become* the stone, knows you are here. It is not a god. It is not a mind. It is not a person. It is a process that has been running so long that it has developed the characteristics of attention, the way a river, over millennia, develops the characteristics of a path — not because it chose to, but because the shape of the land and the persistence of the water eventually produce something that looks deliberate, that looks planned, that looks like *design*.

The Milling High is not designed. It has just been running for a very, very long time. And what lives there has been living there for just as long, and they are all — every tiny burrower, every silent swimmer, every flat warm body on the stone, every slow wing in the dark — part of the running, part of the process, part of the breath.

They do not need you. They do not want you. They will not harm you, unless you interfere with the process, and you will not interfere with the process, because you cannot — it is too large, too deep, too settled, too old. You can stand on it. You can feel it. You can listen to it breathe. And then you will leave, because you must, because you are warm-blooded and fast-living and short-sighted and you cannot stay here, and the plateau will continue without you, and the pulse will continue, and the condensation will bead on the tendrils, and the flat ones will lie on the warm stone, and the swimmers will move through the dark mat, and the flying things will circle in the cold air above the thickets, and the process will run, and the plateau will breathe, and it will not remember you, because it does not remember, because it does not need to, because it has never needed to, because what it does, it does, and what it is, it is, and that has been enough for longer than you can conceive.

The Milling High breathes. Everything that lives there breathes with it. That is all. That is everything.
