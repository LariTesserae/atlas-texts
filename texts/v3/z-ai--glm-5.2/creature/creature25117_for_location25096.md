# The People of Tsuun-Vel

They are not thin. That is the first thing you notice, or rather the first thing that corrects an assumption you didn't know you'd made. The heat and the brightness and the mineral austerity of the caldera suggest a kind of ascetic population — gaunt, baked, pared down. They are not that. The residents of Tsuun-Vel are solid. They are built densely, the way things that endure heat are built densely: low centers of gravity, broad frames, thick wrists and necks. The older ones have a particular cast to their skin — not darkened exactly, but *conditioned*, a texture like fired clay, smooth and slightly warm to the touch even when they are at rest. This is not sun damage. It is the long effect of living in air that is perpetually humid and warm, in light that reflects up from the ground as much as it bears down from the sky. Their skin has adapted the way the buildings have adapted: by becoming dense, by becoming a barrier that does not break.

There are about a thousand of them. The number fluctuates — workers come up the switchback road for seasonal contracts and leave again, and the population swells to twelve or thirteen hundred during the intensive extraction months when the precipitation beds are producing well — but the core population is stable and has been for several generations. Families here go back four, five, six name-lines. They know which borehole their great-grandmother monitored. They know which section of the fracture system their grandfather maintained. The town is not old by the standards of cities, but it is old by the standards of people, old enough to have its own memory, which is carried in names and in the patterns of work.

## Faces and bearing

Their faces are broad and flat-planed, with heavy brow bones that give them a perpetually squinting look even indoors, as though the brightness has become a permanent feature of their expression. This is not an affectation. The orbital structure has shifted over generations — slightly, in the way populations shift when a particular environmental pressure is constant — and the result is a face that looks, to a visitor, slightly closed, slightly guarded, as though everyone here is perpetually narrowing their eyes against something. They are. They are narrowing them against the light. They have been doing it since birth.

They do not smile the way visitors from lower elevations smile. This is not unhappiness. Their expressions run through a different register — a lift of the chin that means agreement, a slight opening of the hands that means welcome, a turn of the shoulder that means *come with me*. Facial mobility exists but it is economical. The heat discourages effort, and expression is effort, in a way. They conserve. What they do with their faces, they do deliberately, and the result is a population that can seem remote or even stern until you learn the small signals — the soft exhalation through the nose that is amusement, the brief meeting of eyes that is acknowledgment, the way they tilt their heads toward each other when speaking, leaning in rather than turning, because the whole body is always oriented toward the work and the work is always present.

They move slowly. Not lazily — deliberately. The heat makes speed costly. A resident of Tsuun-Vel crossing the space between two buildings walks with a measured, rolling gait that conservers energy and keeps the body's core temperature as stable as possible. They place their feet flatly, rolling from heel to ball, and the wet stone beneath them absorbs the sound so completely that you can watch three people walk past you on a raised walkway and hear almost nothing — not their steps, not their breathing, not their clothing. They move the way the predators move: with a slow, considered weight. It is not a coincidence. They have lived alongside those animals for generations, and the environment rewards the same qualities in both.

## Hands

Their hands are remarkable, and a visitor who looks will notice this before anything else.

The work of Tsuun-Vel is the work of mineral extraction, and mineral extraction is the work of scraping, lifting, carrying, and processing crusts and slurries and hot water. The residents' hands reflect this. They are calloused in particular patterns: thick across the base of the thumb, across the proximal finger joints, along the heel of the palm. These are the contact points for the scraping tools, for the handles of the collection troughs, for the mesh grilles that must be lifted and cleaned. The calluses are not soft. They are mineralized — not a metaphor; the callused skin of a long-term Tsuun-Vel worker actually incorporates silica and sulfur compounds from the water and the deposits they handle, so that the skin of the hands becomes, over years, a kind of organic stone. It is slightly rough to the touch. It is warm. It does not blister easily.

The younger workers have softer hands — still calloused, still working toward that mineralized state — and you can tell the length of someone's tenure in the Works by the texture of their hands. The oldest workers, the ones who have been here thirty or forty years, have hands that look almost like the shell of a vel-thin: pale, slightly calcitic, faintly rough. They do not shake hands when they greet each other. They press the backs of their hands together, briefly, a gesture that may have originated in the practical need to avoid transferring mineral residue from palm to palm. Among family it is done with the inside of the wrist, which is softer and more personal.

## Cloth and dress

They wear cloth. This sounds obvious. What they wear is less obvious.

The cloth is woven from a fiber that comes from outside the caldera — it is brought up the switchback road in bolts, traded for the mineral output of the Works — and it is a plain, dense weave, undyed or dyed in the mineral colors of the caldera itself: ochre, the pale milky blue, the manganese black. The dye is literally the product of their work. A family's clothing often carries the color of the bed their lineage has worked — the ochre families, the blue families, the black families — though this is a tendency rather than a rule, and young people often wear whatever color they prefer, to the mild amusement of their elders.

The garments are wraps. Not tailored clothing — wraps, lengths of cloth wound around the body in specific configurations that leave the limbs free and the torso covered. The wrapping is a skill learned in childhood. A poorly wrapped cloth comes loose in the heat, when the body sweats and the fabric slips. A well-wrapped cloth stays through a full work shift, which can be ten hours in the processing halls. The wraps cover the head and shoulders during midday and are rearranged — unwound from the head, re-wound around the waist or over one shoulder — during the cooler working hours. It is a constantly adjustable system, responsive to the heat and the light, and the residents adjust their clothing the way sailors adjust sails: continually, without thinking about it, as a matter of course.

They wear boots. The boots are the one piece of equipment that is made locally, from a combination of caldera clay and mineralized fiber, cured in the heat of the ground itself. They are thick-soled, because the ground is hot, and they are replaced often, because the mineral-laden water degrades them from the inside. A worker in Tsuun-Vel goes through three or four pairs of boots in a year. The boots are stored overnight in small racks outside the residences, soles up, so that the heat of the ground can dry them. In the morning they are warm and slightly stiff, and they soften as the foot inside them warms the clay-fiber composite. It is said that a good pair of Tsuun-Vel boots should feel like standing on a warm stone, and a bad pair should feel like standing on a hot one. The distinction matters.

## Children

Children here are raised in the heat, and they are raised by everyone.

The settlement is small enough that every adult knows every child by name and by lineage, and the children move between households with a freedom that would alarm a visitor from a more private culture. A child who is hungry goes to the nearest kitchen. A child who is tired sleeps in the nearest shaded interior. This is not abandonment — it is a system, and it works because the population is stable and the relationships between families are dense and long-standing. Your neighbor's child is, in a practical sense, partly your child, because your neighbor's grandmother worked the same bed as your grandmother, and the beds are shared, and the water is shared, and the heat is shared.

Children learn to walk on the hot wet stone barefoot first, in the shaded interiors and on the raised walkways, and then in boots. They learn early to test the ground with the toe of the boot before committing weight, because some surfaces are hotter than others and the difference is not always visible. By the time a child is six or seven, they can read the ground the way a sailor reads water — the slight variation in the sheen of moisture, the faint difference in the color of the mineral staining, the way the vapor sits above a particular patch of stone. They know where it is safe to stand. They know where the ground will burn.

They learn the animals early too. A child in Tsuun-Vel can identify a vel-thin by the pattern of its movement through a puddle before it is close enough to see clearly. They know the hassu by the color of the water they inhabit — the hotter water has a faintly different quality, a sharper brightness, and the hassu live in that brightness. They know to step wide of the big predators, which are not dangerous exactly — they do not attack people — but which are territorial and will hold their ground with a stubbornness that can result in a crushed toe if you are careless. Children are taught to watch for the slight shift of weight that signals a predator has noticed you. That shift is the only warning you get.

Children play, but they play quietly. The silence of the caldera is in them from the beginning. They do not shout. They do not run — running is too costly in the heat, and the ground is too uncertain. They play games of patience and observation: who can sit longest beside a puddle and count the vel-thin, who can spot a predator first, who can identify a mineral stain by color. These are not formal games. They are the things children do when the landscape is their playground and the landscape is mineral and hot and silent and full of small lives moving in patterns.

## The old

The elders of Tsuun-Vel are extraordinary.

They have lived in the heat for fifty, sixty, seventy years. Their skin has the fully conditioned quality — the fired-clay smoothness, the faint mineral sheen — and their hands are mineralized to the point of being almost stone-like. They move more slowly than the rest, and they move with a precision that makes the slowness look like deliberation rather than decline, because it is. They know the ground. They know where every fracture runs, where every vent emerges, where every puddle forms and at what temperature. They carry this knowledge in their bodies the way a musician carries rhythm: not as information but as sense.

They sit a great deal. The elders sit in the shaded interiors of the processing halls and the residences, and they sit at the edges of the precipitation beds in the late afternoon when the light is low and the heat is radiative rather than direct. They watch. They are watching the work, but they are also watching the caldera itself — the way the vapor rises, the way the animals move, the way the water beads on the stone. They can feel changes in the system before they register on any instrument. An elder who has spent forty years in the caldera will say, "The water is different today," and the workers will check the borehole readings, and the elder will be right, though the difference may be so small that the instruments barely register it. The body knows. The body has been in this heat for so long that it has become a kind of instrument itself — a seismograph for the slow pulse of the volcanic system beneath the floor.

They do not speak often. When they do, the other residents listen. Not because what they say is always profound — sometimes it is practical, sometimes it is a complaint about the food, sometimes it is a reminiscence that seems to go nowhere — but because the act of speaking has, for the elders, a weight that it does not have for younger people. They have spent so long in the silence that each word is a deliberate breaking of it, and the breaking is never casual. If an elder speaks, they mean the words to be heard. The words land on the quiet like a stone lands on still water, and the ripples are felt.

## The workers

The work itself shapes the workers, and the workers are most of the population.

A work shift in the processing halls begins before dawn, in the brief dimness that passes for night at this latitude. The workers gather in the central hall, which is lit by electric lamps and heated — additionally heated, beyond the ambient temperature of the caldera — by the steam pipes that run through its walls. They drink hot water. Not tea, not coffee — hot water, from the cisterns, sometimes with a pinch of mineral salt dissolved in it. They drink it because the body loses salt in this heat, through sweat, and the mineral salt of the caldera is available and effective. The drink is warm and slightly metallic and the workers drink it the way workers everywhere drink the first thing in the morning: without pleasure, without complaint, as a function of continuing to be a body that works.

The morning shift is the scraping shift. The precipitation beds that have been cooling overnight are ready — the mineral crusts have formed on their dark stone surfaces, and the workers move along the beds with long-handled scraping tools, lifting the crusts in sheets. The crusts are fragile and must be lifted whole, because broken crusts are harder to process and worth less. This is skilled work. The scraper must feel the thickness of the crust through the tool, must judge where the bond to the stone is weakest, must apply pressure that is even and controlled. A good scraper can clear a bed in an hour. A bad scraper will crack the crusts and be reassigned.

The morning shift ends when the heat becomes prohibitive, which is not a fixed time but a felt threshold. The shift boss — there are three, one for each hall — makes the call. They say, "The ground says stop," which is a literal statement: the stone floor of the hall has reached a temperature at which continued work is unsafe, and the boss can feel it through their boots. The workers set down their tools, rinse their hands in the cooling troughs (which are warm, not cold — cold water would shock the mineralized skin and cause cracking), and go to rest.

The afternoon shift begins when the light angles and the stone begins to release rather than absorb. This is the processing shift: the scraped crusts are sorted, crushed, and refined in the heated evaporators, which use the caldera's own steam to drive the chemical separation of the mineral components. The air in the evaporator hall is dense — hot, humid, mineral-saturated — and the workers wear wraps over their mouths and noses, not for filtration but to keep the moisture in their breath from condensing on their faces, which it does anyway, so that every worker in the afternoon shift has a damp sheen across their cheeks and brow that catches the electric light.

They are not unhappy. This needs to be said, because the conditions sound punishing and the work sounds brutal, and it is both of those things, and the workers are not unhappy. They are engaged. The work is physical and demanding and requires attention, and the attention is rewarded — the caldera produces, the minerals are valuable, the town is prosperous by the standards of remote industrial settlements, and the workers know that what they do matters in a way that is concrete and verifiable. They send the product down the switchback road, and the product is used in processes and industries they may never see, and the money and goods that come back up the road sustain the town. The loop is visible. The work has a shape. They can feel it in their hands and see it in the beds and hear it in the low hum of the machinery. For people whose environment is silent and mineral and still, the work provides the rhythm that the landscape does not.

## What they believe

This is harder to describe, because they do not talk about it directly.

The residents of Tsuun-Vel do not have a religion in the way that term is usually understood. They do not have temples. They do not have clergy. They do not have scriptures or prayers. What they have is a relationship with the caldera that is best described as *acknowledged* — they acknowledge the heat, the water, the silence, and the slow mineral processes that underlie their lives, and this acknowledgment is not worship but it is not indifference either. It is something between the two, a recognition of scale.

The caldera is older than them. The water has been rising and depositing its minerals for longer than their families have been here. The vel-thin have been scraping the mineral crusts for longer than the town has been scraping them. The predators have been sitting motionless beside the puddles for longer than the town has been sitting motionless beside the precipitation beds. The residents know this. They know they are recent, in the terms of the caldera, and they do not find this diminishing. It is simply the case. They are here, and the caldera was here before them, and they work within its processes rather than against them.

When a borehole is drilled, the workers do not bless it. They do not have a ceremony. But they do something: the first person to tap a new borehole places their hand flat on the stone above the bore and holds it there for a moment, feeling the heat rise, feeling the water begin to move. It is not a ritual. It is a check — a practical, physical check that the bore is producing — but it is done with a stillness and an attention that is not purely practical. The worker is listening with their hand. They are confirming that the caldera is, as they say, *still breathing*. This phrase is used casually, in the way a sailor says the sea is alive, and it carries the same mixture of metaphor and literalism. The caldera breathes. The water rises and falls with a slow rhythm that is not tidal but is cyclic, and the workers know its phases the way a farmer knows seasons.

They do not fear the caldera. They do not believe it will erupt — the geological record here is long and stable, and the heat is a constant, not a prelude. But they respect its scale. When a worker dies — and workers die, from accidents, from the long effects of heat exposure, from the ordinary deaths that occur in any population — the body is carried down the switchback road, because the caldera floor is not a place for burial. The dead are taken elsewhere. This is not a rule. No one has ever stated it as a rule. It is simply what is done, and has always been done, and the reason, if anyone were to articulate it, would be something like: *the caldera has its own processes, and the dead belong to ours, and the two should not mix.* The living stay in the heat. The dead go to the cool. It is clean.

## The social fabric

They are close. Not warm, in the effusive sense — not a society of embraces and declarations — but close in the way that people who depend on each other in a difficult environment are close. Your neighbor monitors your borehole when you are sick. Your coworker knows when your scraping arm is favouring one side and tells the shift boss before you do, because an injury that is ignored becomes a disability, and a disability in this environment is a serious thing. The social network is tight and practical and it does not require expression to function. It functions through proximity and shared work and the unspoken understanding that everyone here is subject to the same heat and the same silence and the same slow mineral patience of the ground.

They gather in the evenings, when the light drops and the stone radiates. The gathering place is not a building — it is the space between the central processing halls and the cooling tower, a stretch of raised walkway and open stone where the heat is moderate and the vapor from the tower catches the last light. They sit. They talk, quietly. They eat — food brought up the switchback road, supplemented by... no, not supplemented. There is no local food production. Everything edible is imported. The residents of Tsuun-Vel eat what the trade provides: grain, dried meat, preserved vegetables, a dense flatbread that is a staple. The food is not remarkable. It is fuel. They eat it without ceremony, in portions calibrated to the work they have done and will do tomorrow.

But they gather, and the gathering is the point. A hundred people sitting on a warm stone walkway in the late light, speaking in low tones, the vapor rising above them, the vel-thin moving in the puddles at the edges of the light, the predators sitting motionless at the periphery like dark stones. The children are among the adults, quiet, watching, learning the shape of the community by being inside it. The elders are at the center, not by arrangement but by gravity — people sit near them, and the sitting near is not deference exactly, it is orientation, the way the town orients itself around the Works. The elders are the center because they have been here longest, and the center is where the time is deepest.

They tell stories. Not long ones — the heat and the silence discourage oratory — but short, specific accounts: a particular day when the water ran hotter than expected, a particular vel-thin that crawled onto a worker's boot and stayed there, a particular light at a particular hour that made the mineral floor look like a field of jewels. The stories are not structured. They are fragments, offered and received, and they accumulate into a shared understanding of the place that is fuller than any single person's experience. This is how the caldera is known: not by one person, but by the town, in pieces, over time, in the warm dark.

They do not sing. The silence is too complete for singing, and the act of singing would break it in a way that feels wrong, not prohibited but *inappropriate*, the way shouting in a library is inappropriate. The silence of the caldera is not an absence. It is a presence, and the residents live within it the way fish live within water. Singing would be like... they do not have a word for what it would be like. It simply is not done. If you asked them why, they would look at you with their heavy-browed, squinting faces and say, "It is quiet here," and the statement would be both explanation and dismissal, because the quietness is not a condition that needs explaining. It is the ground.

## What they are

They are a people shaped by mineral and heat and water and time. They are not hard, exactly — hardness implies a resistance to impression, and they are deeply impressed, marked, by their environment. They are more like the stone they live on: dense, warm, saturated with the substance of the place, carrying the colors and the minerals and the heat in their skin and their hands and their slow, deliberate movements. They are not a people in contrast to their landscape. They are a people *of* it, an extension of the caldera's processes into the dimension of human life, the way the vel-thin are an extension of those processes into the dimension of small crawling things.

They work. They rest. They eat. They speak quietly. They sit in the warm dark and tell each other small true things about the day. They carry the dead down the road and the living stay in the heat. The water comes up from below and leaves its colors on the ground, and the people scrape those colors up and send them out into the world, and the world sends back cloth and food and fiber and the things that a town needs to continue being a town. The exchange is steady. The caldera is steady. The people are steady within it, as steady as anything alive can be, which is to say: steady enough.

The light comes down and the light goes back up and the heat is always there, and they are always there, in the heat, with their mineral hands and their heavy-browed eyes and their quiet, and they are enough.
