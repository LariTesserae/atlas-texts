# What Lives Here

---

## The Bacteria

Start with what you cannot see, because everything above them is scaffolding.

The bacterial mats are the floor's true skin. They spread across warm stone in films that look like discoloration — a blush of rust here, a milky veil there, a deep arterial red in the pools where iron concentrations peak. They are not one thing. They are dozens of things, layered, each species occupying a temperature band a few degrees wide, each one's waste becoming the next one's food. The stratification is vertical as well as horizontal: the top millimeter is photosynthetic, or whatever passes for photosynthesis when the light is this intense and the air this thin; the next layer down metabolizes sulfur; below that, something lives on hydrogen; below that, something else lives on the molecular remnants of the layers above it, recycling their dead into substrate. The mats are growing. They have been growing for longer than the industrial works existed. When the processing facility was built, the mats were already here, already old, already patient. The steel framework landed on them and they grew up around its base, up its rusting columns, incorporating the oxide into themselves, using the iron.

They respond to touch. If you press a finger to a bacterial film — and you shouldn't, but if you do — the color changes. The cells beneath your finger contract, flee, or die, and the patch goes pale for a moment before the surrounding cells migrate inward to fill the gap. The film heals. It takes minutes. The caldera's floor is, in this sense, a single continuous organism, broken into patches by geography but functionally connected through the warm water that seeps between terrace formations, carrying cells and chemical signals from one pool to the next. The mats communicate. Not in any way you'd recognize as communication — no signals, no intent — but chemically, through the water, through the mineral substrate, through the slow exchange of molecules that takes hours or days to travel a meter. If something disturbs one end of a terrace formation, the other end's bacterial community shifts its metabolic output. The mechanism is not understood. It may not be a mechanism. It may simply be chemistry, operating at a scale that mimics response.

At night, the mats glow. Not all of them — the sulfur-metabolizing layers do, producing a faint, steady red luminescence that is almost certainly a byproduct of their chemistry rather than any functional adaptation. But the effect is real, and it transforms the caldera floor into something that doesn't resemble any other landscape on earth. The warm pools become red lanterns. The terrace edges glow along their rims. The channels where warm water seeps between formations light up like veins, a circulatory system rendered visible by the organisms that line it. The light is not bright. It is present. And it is enough.

## The Lizards

The lizards are the most visible inhabitants and the least interesting to describe in isolation, which is to say they are perfectly adapted and adaptation, at its extreme, becomes a kind of anonymity. They are small — fifteen, twenty centimeters from nose to tail-tip — and slate-grey with a metallic sheen that is not pigmentation but structure: their scales are micro-ridged, arranged in overlapping rows that refract light the way a diffraction grating does, throwing back a cold, bright flash when the sun hits them. The effect is functional. The reflected light is heat that doesn't enter their bodies. They are thermal mirrors.

Their feet are the real adaptation. The basalt on the caldera floor reaches temperatures that would blister human skin on contact — sixty, seventy degrees in the direct sun, higher where geothermal heat vents through the rock. The lizards walk on it without apparent discomfort. Their feet are not calloused. They are not especially large. But the scales on their pads are different from the scales on their bodies — thicker, more loosely attached, separated from the underlying tissue by a layer of air that acts as insulation. They are, in effect, walking on the tips of their own dead cells. The arrangement is not unique to these lizards; desert species elsewhere do something similar. But the degree of it here is extreme. A lizard can stand on hot stone for several minutes before it needs to move. It chooses not to. It basks.

They watch. This is the thing that stays with you. The lizards watch you. Not in the diffident, reactive way that most lizards watch — the flick of attention, the readiness to flee — but with a sustained, focused tracking that lasts as long as you're in their line of sight. Their eyes are bright, fixed, round, and they follow your movement with a smoothness that suggests they are not reacting to you but predicting you, extrapolating your path and adjusting their gaze to meet you at the point where you'll be. It is probably nothing. It is probably standard visual tracking, optimized by an animal that lives in a landscape where everything is either predator or substrate. But the smoothness of it — the mechanical quality, the precision — is unsettling in a way you can't articulate. You feel watched. Not by one lizard. By the population. They are on every surface, at every elevation, their bodies flat against the warm stone, their eyes on you, and when you move, they move — not away from you but along with you, lateral, maintaining their positions relative to your body, a coordinated tracking pattern that looks intentional and isn't. Probably isn't.

They eat the bacterial mats. You don't see them do it — the description said you never see them eat — but the evidence is there. Their droppings, small and white and calcareous, accumulate on the rocks where they bask, and the droppings contain the mineral signatures of the bacterial substrate: sulfur, iron, silica. They are eating the floor. They are eating the floor and converting it into themselves and then depositing it back, slightly altered, slightly processed, and the bacteria colonize the droppings and the cycle continues. The lizards are a digestive system for the caldera. They take the slow chemistry of the bacterial mats and speed it up, pass it through their bodies, and return it to the stone in a form that the bacteria can use again. They are not parasites. They are partners. The ecosystem needs them the way a gut needs its flora.

They breed in the warm pools. The females lay eggs in the shallow water at the edges of the mineral terraces, tucking them into crevices where the temperature is stable — thirty-five degrees, roughly, the sweet spot for incubation. The eggs are small, leathery, and the water that surrounds them is chemically hostile to almost everything else. But the eggs are permeable — they absorb minerals from the pool water, and the hatchlings emerge already encrusted with a fine layer of calcium and silica that hardens within hours into their first set of scales. They are born armored. They are born from the caldera's chemistry. Their first act is to climb out of the pool and onto the warm stone and flatten themselves against it and begin to bask, and from that moment they are independent. There is no parental care. There is no childhood. There is emergence and then there is basking and then there is watching.

## The Birds

They are not eagles. They are not vultures. They are something else — heavy-bodied, broad-winged, dark, with a silhouette that suggests a bird designed for buoyancy rather than speed. Their wings are disproportionately large for their bodies, and they use them the way a glider uses its wings: rarely flapping, mostly adjusting, tipping, rotating, making constant micro-corrections to maintain their position in the thermal column that rises from the caldera floor. They can stay aloft for hours. You watch one bird circle for the better part of an afternoon, climbing slowly, its body tilting in increments so small they're almost imperceptible, and then at some invisible threshold it peels away from the thermal and descends over the rim and is gone.

They nest outside the caldera. This is almost certain, though no one has confirmed it — the rim is cluttered and broken and the outer slopes are unmapped, and the birds, whatever they are, do not advertise their nesting sites. But they must nest outside, because the caldera floor offers no surface that could support a nest of the size these birds would require. They come here to hunt. The thermal is their highway — a rising column of warm air that lifts them without effort, carries them above the floor, and gives them a vantage from which the entire caldera is visible. Their eyesight is extraordinary. From a height of several hundred meters, in air that shimmers with heat distortion, they can spot a lizard on a rock. You have watched them do it: a momentary tilt, a fractional adjustment of the wings, a descent that begins as a glide and ends as a stoop — fast, angled, the wings half-folded — and then a pull-up, empty-clawed, the lizard having moved at the last second. They miss more often than they catch. But they keep trying.

Their calls are the lowest-frequency sound the caldera produces. A croak, but subsonic — you feel it in your sternum, in your teeth, in the bones of your skull, before you hear it, and what you hear is less a sound than a pressure change, a momentary thickening of the air that resolves, if you're paying attention, into a vibration. The thin atmosphere at this altitude doesn't support low-frequency sound propagation; the wavelength is too long for the medium. But the birds call anyway. They call to each other across the caldera's twelve-kilometer width, and the call arrives not as sound but as a physical event — a tremor, a pulse, something that travels through the rock and the warm air and your body simultaneously. When two birds call at once, the interference pattern creates a standing wave that you can feel in your feet, through the soles of your boots, through the basalt. The ground hums. For a moment, the entire caldera is a resonance chamber, and the birds are playing it.

They are not numerous. You count four, five at most, visible at any given time, and the actual population may be no larger than a dozen individuals. They are long-lived — you suspect this without evidence, because their behavior suggests experience, learned patterns, a knowledge of the caldera's thermal dynamics that could only come from years of observation. They know where the thermals are strongest. They know where the lizards are densest. They know the shape of the wind over the rim and how it changes with the time of day. They are, in a sense, the caldera's cartographers. They have mapped it from above, in finer detail than any human survey, and the map is not stored anywhere but in the modified neurons of a handful of dark, broad-winged birds who will never land while you're watching.

They eat lizards. The lizards eat bacteria. The bacteria eat minerals and heat. The birds, when they die — if they die inside the caldera, which is rare but not impossible — fall to the floor and are consumed. Not by the lizards. By the segmented things.

## The Segmented Things

You did not imagine them.

They are real, and they are the caldera's most peculiar inhabitants, and they do not fit into any taxonomic category that you carry with you. This is not a claim of mystery for its own sake. It is an acknowledgment that the caldera has been isolated, thermally and chemically, for long enough that the things living here have diverged from any lineage you'd recognize, and the segmented things have diverged furthest of all.

They are flat. That is the first thing. Not flattened the way a flounder is flattened — a bilateral animal lying on its side — but flat in their body plan, dorsoventrally compressed, their bodies close to the ground, their profile low. They are segmented — visibly, externally, the segments visible as dark bands across a brownish carapace that has the texture of chitin but not the composition. You saw one in a trench between terrace formations and what you saw was a body like a centipede that had been pressed under glass, each segment distinct, the whole thing moving with a rippling motion that was not the coordinated leg-swing of a centipede but a peristaltic wave, a muscular contraction passing from front to back, the body flowing across the ground the way a flatworm flows, but larger, much larger, and armored.

Their size varies. The smallest you've seen was perhaps the length of your forearm. The largest — and you are not certain of this, because you saw it only as a shape in the condensation mist near a warm pool, a silhouette that could have been two animals close together — may have been a meter and a half. They move fast when they want to. Their normal pace is slow, deliberate, the peristaltic wave propelling them at a speed that suggests exploration, not flight. But when startled — and they are easily startled, by vibration more than by sight — they contract, all segments simultaneously, and the wave reverses, and they shoot backward, into a crack, under a rock, into a fissure, and they are gone. The reversal is instantaneous. There is no turn, no rotation. Forward motion becomes backward motion without any intermediate step. It is as if the animal has no front, or as if both ends are equally front, and the decision about which direction to go is made per segment, locally, the way a crowd decides to move.

They live in the warm, wet places. The trenches. The margins of the mineral pools. The flooded extraction shafts — and here is where it gets harder to write calmly, because you leaned over the edge of the open shaft, the one with the rotting timber collar, and you felt the vibration coming up from below, and what you felt was not water moving and not air moving but something moving, something large enough and rhythmic enough in its movement that the vibration traveled through thirty meters of air and up through the rock and into your hands on the timber frame. The segmented things live in the shafts. They use them the way the birds use the thermals — as infrastructure, as a medium, a thing that was built by humans for one purpose and has been repurposed by the caldera's inhabitants for another. The shafts are warm. They're wet. They're dark. They connect to flooded tunnels and collapsed lava tubes and the natural fissure system that underlies the entire caldera floor. The segmented things have colonized this network. They move through it. They may move through the entire twelve-kilometer extent of the caldera without ever coming to the surface.

What they eat is the question that you cannot answer and that bothers you more than the questions about their taxonomy or their size. The lizards eat bacteria. The birds eat lizards. The bacteria eat minerals. The segmented things eat — what? They are too large to subsist on bacterial mats. They are too numerous, or seem too numerous, to be sustained by the occasional fallen bird. You saw one near a warm pool, and it appeared to be drinking, or absorbing, its flat body pressed against the wet stone at the pool's margin, its segments pulsing slowly, and you thought: perhaps they are filter-feeding. Perhaps they are absorbing the bacterial film directly through their ventral surface, grazing on the mats the way a cow grazes on grass, but lying down to do it, pressing their entire body against the food source. It would explain the flatness. It would explain the peristalsis — a wave of contact, a wave of absorption, the body acting as a single digestive surface. But it would not explain the size of the larger ones. And it would not explain the vibration in the shaft, which felt less like feeding and more like passage — something traveling, something in transit, using the human-built tunnel as a corridor between one part of the caldera and another, moving with a rhythm and a mass that suggested purpose, direction, a destination.

You do not know what they are. You do not know what they do in the dark. You know that they are there, that they are numerous enough to encounter repeatedly in a day of walking, that they are unbothered by temperatures that would hospitalize a human, that they have been here longer than the industrial works, that they will be here after the works have finished becoming stone. They are the caldera's secret. Not hidden — you saw them, anyone who walked here would see them — but illegible. They exist on terms that the caldera sets, not terms that you understand.

## The Insects, or What Pass for Them

Small. Dark. Angular in their flight paths, each change of direction sharp, deliberate, as if they are visiting specific points in the air rather than flying through it. They congregate at the mineral pools, where the bacterial films are thickest and the warm water releases gases that you cannot smell but that they apparently can. They are not flies. Their wings are not membranous in the way that fly wings are; they have a textured, almost crystalline quality, catching the light and throwing it in directions that don't correspond to the wing's orientation, as if the wing surface is doing something to the photons other than reflecting them. They may be thermoregulating. They may be communicating. They may be doing something that has no name because no one has studied them and no one will study them, because the caldera is not a place that invites study. It is a place that invites departure.

There are not many of them. Dozens, at most, around any given pool. But they are constant. Where there is warm water, there are insects. Where there are insects, there are the webs — faint, geometric, strung across fissures in the basalt, anchored to outcroppings of mineral deposit, almost invisible until the light catches the silk at an angle that makes it flash. The web-builders are never visible. They hide in the cracks, in the dark, in the spaces between the rock's broken surfaces. You know they are there because the webs are maintained — repaired when damaged, rebuilt when destroyed by rain, expanded incrementally, day by day, as if the web-builder is surveying its territory and extending its infrastructure to cover new ground. The webs catch the insects. The insects feed on the bacteria. The bacteria feed on the minerals. The web-builders feed on the insects. Another loop. Another closed circuit.

The web-builders may be arachnids. They may be something that has arrived at an arachnid-like body plan through a different evolutionary path, convergent form driven by convergent need: the need to sit still and wait, the need to build, the need to let the food come to you rather than pursuing it. In a landscape where heat makes exertion expensive and where the prey is concentrated in specific micro-locations — the warm pools, the bacterial margins — the web is a logical solution. You do not see them because they do not need to be seen. They are the caldera's patience, embodied. They sit. They wait. The web does the work.

## The Human

There is a human here. Or there was. Recently.

The concrete-block building near the central terrace formation is the evidence, and the evidence is specific enough to be unsettling. The door works. The glass is unbroken. The interior contains a cot, a table, a lamp, and instruments in a wooden case. This is not the detritus of the industrial period — those remains are steel and rust and concrete, heavy and unambiguous. This is something else. This is someone who came here after the works were abandoned, after the road had begun to break apart, after the cable pylons had stopped functioning, and who set up a small, functional, careful habitation in the middle of a caldera floor that should not be habitable.

Whoever they were, they were not a tourist. The instruments in the wooden case suggest measurement — temperature, perhaps, or mineral content, or gas concentration, or something more specific. The lamp with the parabolic reflector suggests someone who worked at night, or who needed focused light, or who was signaling — to whom? from where? The cot suggests stays of some duration. This was not a day trip. Someone lived here, or came here repeatedly and stayed, and they were here recently enough that the dust on the glass hasn't had time to obscure the interior completely.

You do not find this person. You do not find their remains, their equipment beyond what's in the building, their notes, their explanation. You find the building and its contents and you find the absence that surrounds them — the absence of a second structure, of a path worn between the building and the shafts, of any sign that this person moved through the caldera and left traces. Either they were extraordinarily careful, leaving no mark beyond the building itself, or they didn't stay long, or they are still here, somewhere, in a part of the caldera you haven't walked, behind a terrace formation, inside a structure you took for ruin, down in the shafts where the vibration comes from and where the segmented things move in the dark.

This is not a comfortable thought. You stand at the window of the concrete-block building and look in at the cot, the table, the lamp, the instrument case, and you think about the person who arranged these objects, who set the lamp at that angle on the table, who closed the instrument case and latched it, who slid the steel door shut — or left it open — and walked away, or didn't walk away, and you are aware, suddenly, of the quiet. The quiet that is not silence. The quiet that is full of process. The quiet in which the lizards are watching you from every surface, and the birds are circling above, and the segmented things are moving in their trenches and their shafts, and the bacterial mats are glowing faintly in the shaded places, and somewhere, possibly, someone is also here, also in the quiet, also watching.

You don't open the door. You don't know why. The door works. You could open it. But the caldera has its own logic, and the logic says: some things that are here are here because they belong. And some things that are here are here because they haven't left yet. And you can't always tell the difference, and the difference may not matter, because the caldera is patient, and the caldera is warm, and the caldera will incorporate whatever you leave behind — your boot-prints, your sweat, your exhaled breath, your instruments, your body — into its chemistry, its terraces, its slow, luminous, watching skin.

## What Lives Here, In Sum

Heat lives here. That is the first thing and the last thing. Heat lives in the rock, in the water, in the air, in the bacteria that metabolize it, in the lizards that mirror it, in the birds that ride it, in the segmented things that move through its channels. Everything else is a guest of the heat. The minerals are guests. The water is a guest. The animals are guests, though they have been guests for so long that they have become hosts in their turn, shaping the landscape as much as it shapes them, the lizards' droppings feeding the bacteria, the birds' calls vibrating the rock, the segmented things widening the fissures they travel through, the web-builders anchoring their silk to mineral deposits that their prey help to build.

The ruins are guests. The steel and concrete and wood of the industrial works are guests, and they are being digested — slowly, warmly, without malice — by the caldera's chemistry. The rails will be terraces. The processing facility will be a cliff. The shafts will be burrows. The road will be a scar that the lizards bask on. Everything built here will be unbuilt, and then rebuilt, as stone.

The human — the recent human, the one with the instruments — is a guest. You are a guest. The caldera does not mind. The caldera does not notice. The heat rises, the water cycles, the animals move, the minerals accrete, and the quiet — the vast, packed, working quiet of a place that is loud with process and silent with sound — goes on, as it has gone on, as it will go on, with or without you, with or without anyone, its warmth rising into the thin air like a breath that never ends.
