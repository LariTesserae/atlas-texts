# What Lives There

---

There is no single word for them, because they are not singular.

You might not see them at first. The city does not announce its inhabitants the way a human city does — there are no crowds, no markets, no processions. The buildings stand empty in their winter-shed way, and the machines turn, and the water moves, and everything seems to operate on its own, a system complete without an operator. This is almost true. It is true the way a body is true: the heart beats without being told, the blood moves without being willed, and yet something is there. Something is the body.

They are in the wood.

Not hiding in it. *Of* it. The distinction matters, because when you finally do see one — and you will, if you stay long enough, if you stop looking for something that moves like an animal and start looking for something that moves like a plant moves, which is to say slowly, which is to say in a way that you only notice after it has already happened — you will understand that the question of where the organism ends and the environment begins has been the wrong question all along.

---

## The First One

The first one you see will probably be a maintenance-shape.

You will know it by its motion, or rather by the fact of its motion. Something on a platform edge — a mass of bark-colored fiber, roughly pyramidal, about the height of your knee — will shift. Not walk. Shift, the way a vine shifts its growth overnight, the way a root shifts its direction around a stone. But faster. It is not fast by your standards. It is fast by plant standards. It moves the way a chameleon moves, each step deliberate, each placement considered, and by the time you have registered that it has moved at all it has already re-settled in a new position three meters from where it was, and you are not entirely certain you didn't imagine the whole thing.

The maintenance-shapes are the most visible because they are the most active. They tend the machines. This is not metaphor. You will see one — once you know what to look for, you will see them everywhere — pressed against the housing of a pump-engine, its body conforming to the shape of the machinery the way a glove conforms to a hand. Its surface, which you had taken for bark, is actually a complex of sensors: lenticels that read vibration, patches of damp tissue that detect chemical changes in the sap-hydraulics, fine rootlets that probe the interfaces between moving parts. It is listening to the machine the way a doctor listens to a heart.

When it finds something — a gear that has worn unevenly, a fungal conduit that has become brittle, a joint where the cork insulation has rotted — it does not repair it in any way you would recognize. It grows into it. Its body extends, sends tendrils of living tissue into the gap, fills the worn surface with new woody material that hardens over hours into a functional replacement. It is healing the machine the way a body heals a wound: not by understanding the problem, but by growing over it.

They are not intelligent, the maintenance-shapes. Not in the way you mean when you use that word. They do not plan. They do not solve problems. They *respond*, with a complexity and specificity that looks like intelligence from the outside but is, from the inside, something more like chemistry — a vast, branching decision tree of stimulus and response, evolved over millennia, that produces outcomes indistinguishable from deliberate action. They are the immune system of the Verdant Machine, and they have been running as long as it has.

---

## The Walkers

The second kind you will notice are the ones that move.

They are taller than the maintenance-shapes — your height, or close to it, though their posture is different, a forward lean that suggests they evolved for movement along surfaces that were not always level or reliable. Their bodies are bipedal in the loose sense: two primary limbs for locomotion, two or four secondary limbs for manipulation, though the number is not fixed. You will see one with four arms and then, later, one with two, and you will initially think they are different species before you realize that the number of limbs is *developmental* — they grow what they need, based on what they do, based on the shape of the spaces they move through.

Their skin is not skin. It is bark, but not the dense dark bark of the old columns — a younger bark, thinner, more flexible, with a surface texture like the skin of a young birch. It is damp. It is always damp. They are not waterproof; they are permeable, and the moisture in the air enters them through their surface, and the water in the pipes enters them when they press against the pipes, and this is how they drink. They do not have mouths. They do not eat. They photosynthesize, poorly, in the dim filtered light — a vestigial process, a relic of an ancestor that lived in open air — and they absorb nutrients through contact with the substrate. They feed by touching things. A walker pressed against a column for an hour is eating.

They do not speak. The sounds they make are mechanical — clicking, the pop of sap-bubbles in their joints, a low creaking that is simply the sound of their bodies bending. They communicate, if that is the word, through the substrate. When one walks across a platform, its footsteps send vibrations through the wood, and those vibrations are received by the rootlets of other walkers, by the sensor patches of maintenance-shapes, by the machines themselves. The entire city feels them move. They know where each other are the way you know where your own limbs are: not by looking, but by the body's own internal sense of itself.

They do not build. The city is already built. They live in it the way a body lives in a body — the maintenance-shapes are the immune system, the machines are the organs, the pipes are the veins, and the walkers are... what? The hands? The eyes? The nervous system?

They are the part that moves. That is enough.

## The Deep Ones

You will probably not see these. Almost certainly not. But they are there, and if you spend enough time in the lower levels, in the root-layer where the water collects and the sound grows loud and the air is thick with steam and the geometry begins to soften, you will feel them.

They are large. That is the first thing. The root system goes deep, and they go with it, and down there — in the compressed dark, in the warmth and wet, in the places where the wood has grown so thick and so old that it has the density of stone — they move. You feel them as shifts in the vibration, subtle modulations in the thrumming that you have almost stopped hearing because it has become the sound of silence itself. The rhythm changes. A new frequency enters, holds, fades. Something down there has shifted position. Something down there has turned over in its sleep.

They are the oldest things in the city. Older than the machines, or as old as them, or older in a way that makes chronology meaningless — they were here when the first machine was grown, or the first machine was grown from them, or they and the machines emerged together from some process that has no beginning because it was a process of becoming rather than building. They are the root system, or they are *in* the root system, or they are what the root system has become after long enough, large enough, complex enough.

They do not come up. There is no need. Everything they need is below: water, nutrients, warmth, the mechanical rhythms that they may have created and may simply inhabit. They are the deep body of the Verdant Machine, the part that never sees light, the part that is so large it has become geography.

But they are not unconscious. You feel this, too, in the lower levels — a quality of attention, a presence that is not focused on you specifically but is *aware* in a diffuse, root-level way, the way a forest is aware of the deer that walks through it, not as an individual but as a pattern of pressure and weight. They know you are there. They do not care. You are too small, too brief, too dry. You are a weather event. You will pass.

## The In-Between Things

These are the ones that live in the breakdown.

Where space has softened, where geometry has loosened, where the rules have worn thin — there are things there. You see them the way you see the breakdown itself: not directly, but as a wrongness, a sense that something is in a place where nothing should be. A shadow that does not match any object. A sound that comes from a direction that does not exist. A movement at the edge of your vision that, when you turn to look, is still at the edge of your vision, having moved with you, not fleeing but *repositioning*, maintaining its distance from your attention.

They are not ghosts. They are not spirits. They are organisms — or parts of organisms, or extensions of organisms — that have adapted to live in the broken spaces, the folded angles, the pockets of reality that the machines' vibrations have worn into the substrate of the world. They navigate by the rules of the place they inhabit, which are not the rules you are used to. They do not experience distance the way you do. They do not experience sequence the way you do. They are in the gaps, and the gaps are real, and they have learned to be real in the gaps.

You will never get a clear look at one. This is not because they are hiding, though they may be, but because the spaces they occupy do not admit the kind of observation you are used to. Light does not behave consistently where they live. Distance does not scale. You see a flash of something dark and many-legged, and then you see it again from a different angle, and it is unclear whether you are seeing the same thing twice or two things or one thing that exists in two places, and then you see nothing, and the nothing is not empty but *full*, full of something that does not resolve into an image.

The cat-sized many-legged thing you saw on the pipe may have been one of these. Or it may have been an ordinary animal adapted to an extraordinary place. The distinction may not matter. In a city that has worn through the membrane between the possible and the actual, the distinction between an organism and a place, between a creature and a condition, between a thing that lives in the breakdown and the breakdown itself — these distinctions have also worn thin.

---

## The Question

So: what lives here?

Everything. Nothing that you would recognize. The Verdant Machine is not a city that has inhabitants. It is a city that is inhabitants. The buildings are grown by the organisms that live in them. The machines are tended by the organisms that are part of them. The root system is the body of the deep ones and the deep ones are the body of the root system. The walkers are the hands of the city and the maintenance-shapes are the immune system of the city and the in-between things are the dreams of the city, the thoughts it has in the broken places where thinking is possible.

They do not have a name for themselves. They do not need one. They know what they are the way a hand knows it is a hand: by what it does, by what it reaches for, by what it holds.

The city is a machine for being alive, and they are what it is like, from the inside, to be alive in that machine.

---

And if you stand at the edge, where it gets quiet enough to forget, and you turn back — not to return, just to look — you will see the city for what it is. Not a place. A body. A single vast body, rooted and growing and breathing and thinking in a way that takes centuries to complete a single thought, a body so old it has worn through the world and found something on the other side, a body that does not know you are leaving because a body does not know when a weather event ends, a body that will be here, humming, growing, breaking, repairing, long after the concept of observer has worn away like the geometry has worn away, like everything wears away, given enough time and enough vibration.

It will forget you.

It will not notice that it has forgotten you.

The machines will turn.
