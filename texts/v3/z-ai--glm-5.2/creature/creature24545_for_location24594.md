# What Lives Here

---

## The Centipede-things (working name: *Vekhi*)

They are the first thing you notice once you stop noticing the walls, and they are the last thing you stop hearing.

There are more of them than you initially think. The initial count — *hundreds* — is what you can see at any given moment. But they move through the channels in the floor, and the channels are deeper than they look, and the ones you see emerging are always balanced by others descending, so the total population is something you can only estimate by the sound. The sound is dense. Layered. If each individual is a single grain of sand drawn across glass, then the aggregate is a beach being slowly ground down by wind, and the wind is everywhere.

Up close — and they let you get close, they do not startle, they do not flee — you can see the structure more clearly. The body is not segmented in the way a centipede's is. It is *pleated*. Each fold of the translucent body wall overlaps the next, and the legs emerge from the seams between folds, two per seam, and the legs themselves are not jointed but *spiral* — tiny corkscrews of chitinous material that rotate as they move, screwing through the chemical film they lay down as they travel. This is why they move so fluidly on wet surfaces. They are not walking. They are boring through the water layer the way a drill bit enters wood.

Inside each pleated fold: the sphere of fluid. It is not a organ in the conventional sense. It does not pulse, does not flex. It simply sits there, dense and luminous, and the light it produces is the same amber as the air, and you begin to suspect after a while that it is not producing the light so much as *concentrating* it — taking the ambient glow that has no source and gathering it into something denser, something the body can use. They are, in a very literal sense, eating the light. Not photosynthesis. Something else. Something that feeds on order, on the thermodynamic novelty the structures produce, on the faint but constant stream of *novel information* that a growing, unfinished system pours into its environment like heat from a radiator.

When they converge on the lattice, the behavior changes. The aimless, fast, wall-climbing movement becomes something slower, more deliberate. They align themselves along the beams — not randomly, but in specific configurations, head to tail, forming lines that trace the lattice's geometry. And at the nodes, where the beams connect, they cluster. Three, five, seven of them, pressed close, their bodies flattening slightly against the dark mineral surface. The nodes pulse faster when they do this. The warmth increases. You can feel it from a meter away — a spike of heat that lasts ten, fifteen seconds, and then the cluster breaks apart, and the individuals drop or climb away, and the node returns to its slower rhythm.

They are *programming* it. Or *pruning* it. Or something for which neither word is quite right — something that is to programming what gardening is to engineering. They are shaping the lattice the way a vine shapes a trellis, by growing along it and stressing it and leaving residue that changes how it develops. The lattice is not a machine they are operating. It is a plant they are tending.

Three of them fall from the lattice when the hum changes key. They lie on the floor, legs curled, spheres dimming. This is not death — after several seconds the spheres brighten again, the legs uncurl, and they climb back up. But something has happened to them. Some exchange has occurred that temporarily exhausted them, drained the spheres of whatever they carry. They gave something to the lattice. Or the lattice took something from them. The distinction, again, is not clear.

They do not react to you. You can put your hand on the floor in front of one and it will walk over your palm, its spiral legs leaving faint wet tracks on your skin that tingle — not painfully, but noticeably, a low vibration, like touching a battery that is almost dead. The chemical they secrete is mildly basic. It smells like the air in the chamber — calcium and copper — and it evaporates from your skin in under a minute, leaving nothing but a faint warmth.

They communicate through the chittering, but the chittering is not language in any sense you'd recognize. It is not sequential. It does not have units. It is a continuous texture that changes in aggregate — the way a crowd's murmur changes when something happens, not because any individual said something specific but because the statistical distribution of sounds shifted. They are, you think, a *medium*. The chittering is how the room thinks. Not how the centipedes think — how the *room* thinks, using the centipedes the way a brain uses neurons: not as individual intelligences but as a substrate through which signals propagate and interact and, occasionally, produce something that neither the room nor the centipedes could produce alone.

There are, you think, probably thousands of them in the channels. And more all the time. In the far corner of the chamber, where a channel widens into something like a shallow basin, you see ones that are smaller — half the length, dimmer, their pleats fewer and less defined. Young. They are breeding, or budding, or the channels are producing them the way the walls produce water. Something is making more. The nursery is staffing itself.

## The Grazers (working name: *film-lice*)

You almost never see them clearly.

They live in the water film on the walls — the thin, constantly renewed layer of fluid that the weeping stone produces. They are pale, soft, the size of a rice grain, and they move by extending and contracting in a way that is less like crawling and more like the movement of a slug, if the slug were two-dimensional and lived entirely in a surface layer of moisture. They are flat. They are almost transparent. If the water film were not there, you suspect they would not survive — they seem to be *made of* the water film, their bodies barely distinguishable from the medium they inhabit.

They graze on something you cannot see. The walls are covered in a biofilm — not bacterial, not algal, something else entirely — that is invisible to the naked eye but present as a faint slickness, a texture difference between the wet stone and the *inhabited* wet stone. The grazers move through this film in wandering paths, and where they have passed, the wall is slightly cleaner, slightly brighter. They are eating the biofilm. The biofilm is eating the stone. The stone is producing the water that sustains both. It is a simple loop, and it is probably the oldest relationship in this chamber — the thing that was here first, before the structures, before the centipedes, before the ecosystem complexified into what it is now.

When a wall section stops weeping and begins to dry, the grazers in that section do not die. They do not migrate. They *contract*. They pull their bodies into tight, dense specks and wait — minutes, sometimes longer — until the water returns. You see this happen twice. Both times, the wall resumes weeping before the grazers have been dry for more than a few minutes. This is probably not a coincidence. The chamber may be calibrating its water cycle around the needs of its inhabitants, or the inhabitants have calibrated themselves to the cycle, or — and this is the option that feels most correct — there is no difference. The system and its inhabitants are the same thing, seen at different scales.

At night, if there is night here — and there is not, the light never changes — you imagine the grazers would be the most numerous things in the chamber. In the constant dim amber, they are simply the background texture of the walls, the reason the walls are not just wet but *inhabited*, the reason the water film has a faint granularity when you look at it closely. They are the bedrock of the ecosystem. Everything else is built on their patience.

## The Pool-dwellers (working name: *sinks*)

The pool at the southern edge is small. The size of a bathtub. But it is deep — deeper than it looks, the bottom lost in a darkness that the amber light does not fully penetrate. The water is dark, still, faintly luminescent. The color of old tea, or of something that was once clear and has been steeped in minerals and time.

Three shapes move in it. You are sure of at least three. There may be more.

They keep to the bottom. They surface only briefly — a ripple, a dark back breaking the water for less than a second, then gone. You catch one clear look at an individual as it rises near the edge: it is round, or close to round, perhaps the size of a fist, and it is dark — not translucent like the centipedes but opaque, a dense, matte brown-black that absorbs the amber light rather than reflecting it. It has no visible features. No eyes, no mouth, no appendages. It is a smooth, dark sphere, and it moves through the water with a motion that is less swimming than *sinking in a chosen direction* — controlling its buoyancy, rising and falling, drifting laterally with a precision that implies it is not at the mercy of the water but is *operating* in it.

The humming post stands in the center of the pool, waist-high, and the ripples around it never reach the edge. This is because the sinks are absorbing them. You watch: a ripple propagates outward from the post, travels a few centimeters, and then a dark shape intercepts it, and the ripple dies. The sinks are eating the vibrations. They are feeding on the post's hum the way the grazers feed on the biofilm and the centipedes feed on the lattice's fields — taking what the structures produce and converting it into themselves.

They are also, you suspect, cleaning the water. The pool is where the channels deliver everything the walls produce — minerals, chemical residue, the faint organic compounds the grazers and centipedes leave behind. It should be murky, should be building up sediment, should be getting thicker and darker over time. It is not. The water remains clear enough to see into, dark but not opaque. Something is processing it. The sinks sit at the bottom and take in water and, presumably, take out of it what they need and leave the rest clean enough to cycle back through the stone. They are filters. They are also animals. The distinction does not seem to bother them.

One of them surfaces near your foot as you crouch at the pool's edge. It touches the stone rim — a brief contact, the dark sphere pressing against the wet rock for a moment — and then withdraws. The spot where it touched feels different. Warmer. The stone there has a faint, slick residue, and when you look closely, the mineral veins in that section seem slightly more pronounced, slightly more defined. It left something behind. A mineral deposit. A trace of whatever it is the pool water carries that the stone needs to continue growing.

The sinks are the chamber's kidneys. And its mouth. And, possibly, its memory — the dense, dark bodies at the bottom of the pool, absorbing vibration and mineral and time, storing whatever the chamber needs to keep, breaking down whatever it doesn't.

## The Ribbon (working name: *the lung*)

It hangs from the ceiling near the lattice, and it is the only creature here that does not seem to be *doing* anything.

A meter long. Flat. Translucent, but not glowing — the amber light passes through it the way light passes through frosted glass, diffused and softened. It is anchored at one end to the stone above by a connection that looks less like adhesion and more like *continuity* — the ribbon and the stone are the same material at the junction, one becoming the other without a clear boundary. The rest of its body hangs free, undulating in a slow, constant, rhythmic motion that is, you realize after watching for several minutes, synchronized with the lattice's pulse. When the lattice hums faster, the ribbon moves faster. When the lattice slows, the ribbon slows. They are on the same circuit.

It breathes. That is the only word for what it is doing, though it has no visible mouth or gills or spiracles. The entire body expands and contracts — thinning as it lengthens, thickening as it shortens — and the air around it moves. You can feel it from below: a faint, slow current, warm air rising when the ribbon contracts, cooler air sinking when it extends. It is moving the atmosphere of the chamber. Circulating it. The warm, mineral-scented air that pushed you through the seam in the rock — this is where it comes from. Or this is one of the places it comes from. The ribbon is a pump. A diaphragm. A lung.

It is also, you think, an antenna. The undulation is too regular, too structured, to be simple respiration. The body is long and flat and translucent, and the amber light that passes through it does not pass through unchanged — it is refracted, slightly, shifted in ways that are hard to see but easy to *feel*. Standing beneath it, you have the distinct sensation of being observed. Not by the ribbon. By something *through* the ribbon. The way a telescope is not the eye but the instrument through which the eye sees.

If the centipedes are the chamber's hands, and the grazers are its skin, and the sinks are its kidneys, then the ribbon is its attention. Its awareness of itself, or its awareness of the world outside, or the interface between the two. It is the one part of the ecosystem that is not purely functional, not purely mechanical. It is the part that *listens*.

It does not react when you stand beneath it. But the air moves differently. The current shifts. For a moment, the rhythm breaks — a single beat that is longer than the others, a held breath — and then it resumes. It noticed you. It is not interested. Or it is interested but has no framework for what you are, no category, no response protocol. You are too new, too temporary, too unlike anything it has been grown to notice. You are a weather event. A gust that entered through a seam that was not meant to be open and will, presumably, not be open again.

The ribbon resumes its rhythm. The air moves. The chamber breathes.

## The Structures (and the question of what "lives" means)

The lattice is alive. This is not a metaphor.

The beams are not mineral. They are not metal. They are something that grows — you can see the growth lines if you look closely, concentric rings in cross-section, like tree rings but finer, more regular, laid down in layers that correspond to the pulsing of the nodes. The lattice has been growing for a long time, and it is not finished. The ends of the beams are rough, unfinished, still extending, still reaching for connections that have not yet been made. It is a partial skeleton waiting for its body.

The centipedes tend it. The ribbon breathes for it. The lattice is, in some sense, the central organism of this chamber — the thing around which everything else is organized. But it is also a *tool*, a structure being built *for* a purpose that is not yet complete. It is both the garden and the gardener. It is the thing being grown and the thing that is growing.

The half-sphere in the eastern wall is older. You can tell because the etchings on its surface are deeper, more numerous, layered over each other in a palimpsest of fine lines that suggests years of use. It is less unfinished than the lattice — it has a completeness to it, a density, as though it has already done whatever it was grown to do and is now in a maintenance phase. Water runs down it constantly. The etchings channel the water in specific paths — you can see this if you watch: the water doesn't run straight down but follows the whorls, tracing the fingerprint patterns, and where the paths converge at the base, the water that collects is different from the water elsewhere in the chamber. Darker. More viscous. The sinks do not approach the pool beneath the half-sphere. Whatever is in that water is not for them.

The half-sphere is a *gland*. It secretes something. The etchings are its ducts. The substance it produces — whatever it is — collects in the dark ring at its base and seeps back into the stone, and you suspect it is what directs the growth of the chamber itself. The substance that tells stone to be a wall. The substance that tells water to weep. The substance that says *here is a nursery, grow here, grow now, grow like this*.

The post in the pool is the youngest structure. Its hum is higher than the lattice's pulse, less integrated, more singular. It does one thing: it vibrates. The vibration enters the water, the sinks eat it, and the water stays clean. It is a simple machine. A pump. A filter-driver. But it is also growing — the base is slightly wider than the top, and the mineral lines suggest it is thickening, extending downward into the pool's depths, building a root system that will eventually, perhaps, connect to something deeper in the bedrock. It is the chamber's newest organ. It is still differentiating. It does not yet know what it will be when it grows up.

## The Intelligence (working name: none)

Something is growing this room.

Not the lattice — the lattice is one of the things being grown. Not the half-sphere — the half-sphere is a tool the grower uses. Not the centipedes — they are workers, hands, neurons in a larger network, but they are not the *will* behind the work.

The will is distributed. It is in the water that knows when to weep and when to stop. It is in the stone that grows in the right shapes. It is in the chemical gradients that tell the centipedes where to cluster and the grazers where to feed and the sinks where to surface. It is in the light that has no source and the air that the ribbon moves and the hum that the post produces and the pulse that the lattice beats. It is everywhere and it is nowhere and it is not a mind in any sense you can recognize because it is not separate from what it is doing. It does not *decide* to grow the chamber. It *is* the growing of the chamber, the way a river is not a thing that decides to flow but the flowing itself.

Someone planted a seed here. That is the only framework that makes sense, and it is probably wrong in every specific detail but right in its shape. Something was placed in this rock — a seed, a spore, a kernel of instruction, a fragment of a system that knows how to build nurseries — and it began. It grew stone. It grew water. It grew the structures that produce the energy and the order that life can eat. It grew the life. It is still growing all of these things, and it will keep growing them until the chamber is complete, and then — then you do not know what happens. Then the nursery opens, or the nursery *becomes* something else, or the thing that was being nurtured here emerges and the chamber is no longer needed and the water stops and the stone goes still and the centipedes die or disperse or transform.

Or none of that. Or something you cannot predict, because the technology here operates on principles you do not have words for. Cultivation, not construction. Growth, not assembly. Patience, not intention.

The nursery is growing something. The centipedes are part of the growing. The grazers are part of the growing. The sinks and the ribbon and the lattice and the walls and the water are all part of the growing. And the thing being grown — the *nursling*, the thing at the center of all of this — is not visible. It is not in the chamber. It may *be* the chamber. It may be the chamber's eventual output. It may be something that will exist when the chamber is done, the way a fruit exists when a flower is done, and until then, all you can see is the flower.

## What lives here, in sum

An ecosystem in its first generation. A biome that was seeded, not evolved — or that was evolved *to be seeded*, a portable ecosystem in a package, the way a spore carries everything it needs to become a fungus. The organisms here are not adapted to this environment. They are *expressions* of it. They grew from it the way a fever grows from a body, the way coral grows from a reef. They are the chamber's immune system, its maintenance crew, its sensory apparatus, its circulation, its waste processing, its breath. They are alive, and they are also *functions*, and the thing that distinguishes a function from a creature is a distinction this place does not observe.

The centipedes tend the lattice because the lattice needs tending. The grazers clean the walls because the walls need cleaning. The sinks filter the water because the water needs filtering. The ribbon moves the air because the air needs moving. None of them chose these roles. None of them were *assigned* these roles. They grew into them the way water grows into a channel — by being the shape that fits the space, by being the thing that is needed at the time it is needed, by being the answer to a question the chamber is asking with its whole body.

And the chamber asks constantly. The hum is the asking. The pulse is the asking. The cycling water and the growing stone and the shifting light are all the asking, and the living things are the answering, and the sound they make together — the weave of hum and crackle and chitter and drip — is the sound of a question being answered in real time, continuously, by everything, all at once.

This is what lives in the Nursery at Vekh-7: a question, and the things that are answering it, and the patience to let the answering take as long as it takes.

The stone is still deciding what it wants to be. So is everything else.
