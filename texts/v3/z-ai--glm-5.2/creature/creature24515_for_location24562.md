# What Lives Here

What lives here is not what you would expect to find living in a place, and it is not what you would call a creature, and it is all there is.

---

The moss came first. Or the grass came first. The order is impossible to determine now because everything is so thoroughly established that no origin story survives in the arrangement of the growth. But if you had to choose, you would say the moss came first, because the moss is the most adapted, the most settled, the most clearly *at home* on its bronze substrate. It is not a visitor. It has committed to the walls the way lichen commits to stone — slowly, permanently, with the understanding that it will be removed only if the wall is removed.

The moss is a single organism. This is not obvious. It looks like a coating, a fur, a layer — but the individual filaments are connected beneath the surface, woven through the seams between the bronze panels, threading along rivet channels and behind maker's marks. It has climbed into every joint of the architecture and it holds the architecture the way mycorrhiza holds soil — not structurally, not as a load-bearing element, but as a kind of netting, a binding, a second skin that makes the first skin more itself. The moss knows the shape of the chamber. It has mapped every rivet. It does not grow *on* the bronze so much as it grows *with* it now, the two surfaces so flush that scraping one would scar the other.

What lives in the moss is patience. If patience can be said to be a thing that lives. The moss does not move. The moss does not eat. The moss absorbs, and it waits, and it has been doing both for long enough that *waiting* has become its primary activity — the absorption is incidental now, almost recreational, something it does the way a retired person reads the newspaper. The moss is full. The moss is satisfied. The moss is the closest thing in this chamber to contentment, if contentment is a state where the gap between what you need and what you have has narrowed to nothing, and you are simply present on your wall, pulsing with the slow drift of warm water, soft under the fingers of anyone who might press their palm flat against the bronze.

No one presses their palm flat against the bronze. This does not trouble the moss.

---

The grass is different. The grass is not patient. The grass is attentive.

There are perhaps ten thousand filaments rising from the iron grating — amber, translucent, each one a meter or more of slow vertical drift through the warm water column. They do not sway. This is the first thing you notice about them, that they *refuse* to sway, that the currents that should bend them do not bend them, that they hold their position with a steadiness that is not mechanical but *willed*, as if each filament has decided where it wants to be in the water and will not be moved from that point by anything less than a direct physical disturbance.

The nodes at their tips are what give them away. Each filament ends in a pale, luminous bead, and the light from these beads is not constant — it shifts, brightens, dims, brightens again, in patterns that do not correspond to the current or the light from above or any external rhythm. The patterns correspond to each other. A filament near the center of the chamber will brighten, and three filaments near it will respond — not immediately, not in sync, but with a lag of several seconds, a conversational delay, the pause of something *considering* before it answers.

The grass is listening. This is the only word for what it does. The warm water carries information — temperature gradients, mineral concentrations, the vibration of the engine behind the walls, the slow rotation of the twelve valves — and the grass filaments receive this information through their entire length, process it along their translucent bodies, and respond at their tips with adjustments of luminosity that no one is reading. They are a sensory array with no operator. They are an ear the size of a cathedral nave, and what it hears, it hears for itself.

What the grass knows, it does not share. It has been listening for weeks or years. It has recorded every rotation of every valve, every shift in current, every fluctuation in the nutrient content of the water rising through the floor. It has a complete record of the chamber's operation, a dataset of hydraulic rhythm, and this data sits in the amber filaments like sap in a tree — present, held, unused. The grass does not *do* anything with what it knows. It simply accumulates. It is a witness, and it is a witness with no concept of testimony, no understanding that what it has observed could matter to anything outside itself.

If the chamber fills completely — when the chamber fills completely — the grass will continue to do what it does. The water will cover the ceiling, the light will shift, the currents will change, and the grass will record this too. It will be, if anything, more content. The water is where it belongs. The air above was an interruption, a ceiling on its world that it tolerated but did not enjoy. Submersion is the grass's natural state. Everything above the waterline was provisional.

---

The vines are the ones that are hardest to describe, because the vines are the ones that most resemble something you might call *present*.

They cluster where the wall meets the ceiling — thick, dark, arm-thick ropes of vegetal matter, braiding around the trusses and conduit pipes with a deliberateness that looks like engineering. Each vine is covered in flat oval scales, overlapping like shingles, and beneath each scale a dark fluid pulses at a tempo specific to that vine. One pulse every forty seconds. One pulse every thirty-seven seconds. One pulse every fifty-one seconds. The tempos are prime-numbered or close to prime-numbered, and this may be coincidence or it may be a strategy to avoid synchronization, to ensure that at no moment does every vine pulse at once.

If every vine pulsed at once, the chamber would notice. Something would shift in the quality of the water, the quality of the light. The vines are aware of this. They avoid it the way you avoid coughing during a silent moment in a concert — not because the cough is forbidden, but because the silence is *doing something*, and you do not want to be the one who interrupts it.

The vines are the closest thing here to intention. They grow toward the light — not rapidly, not with the urgency of a plant seeking sun, but with a slow certainty, a millimeter per day, the pace of something that knows the light will be there tomorrow and sees no reason to hurry. They braid around the trusses not because the trusses provide support but because the trusses are *interesting* to them — a texture, a topology, something to map with their bodies. The vines are cartographers. They draw the architecture of the ceiling in the language of their own growth, and the map they produce is accurate and legible to no one.

Beneath their scales, the dark fluid is not sap. It is not water from the chamber, filtered and processed. It is something else — denser, warmer, carrying a charge that you can almost feel if you press your hand to a vine and wait through the long pause between pulses. When the pulse comes, the vine shifts beneath your palm. A slow swelling, a contraction, a rhythm that is so far below the threshold of the human heart rate that your body cannot recognize it as a heartbeat. But it is a heartbeat. Or it is the closest analog a vegetal organism can produce to a heartbeat, and the difference may not matter.

The vines are awake. This is a statement that requires qualification, because *awake* implies a prior state of *asleep*, and there is no evidence that the vines have ever been anything other than what they are. But there is a quality to them — an alertness in the way they hold their scales, a responsiveness in the way their dark fluid speeds almost imperceptibly when the water temperature shifts by a tenth of a degree, a *quality of attention* — that the word *awake* gestures toward even if it cannot fully grasp. The vines are monitoring something. They have been monitoring it for as long as they have existed. They do not know what they will do if the thing they are monitoring changes in a way that requires response, because it has not yet changed in such a way, and so they wait, and they pulse, and they grow another millimeter toward the light.

---

What lives here, then, is a community of three — or a community of three *types*, which may be one community or may be three separate enterprises that happen to share a chamber. The moss holds. The grass listens. The vines attend. None of them speaks to the others. None of them needs to. The water is the medium they share, and the water carries everything that needs to be carried — nutrients for the moss, information for the grass, warmth for the vines — and the water is sufficient. The water is the whole relationship. The water is the conversation, and the conversation is one that has been going on so long that the participants have forgotten they are having it, and the silences between their contributions have become so extended that an outside observer would conclude the conversation had ended. It has not ended. It is simply operating at a tempo that the outside observer cannot perceive.

What lives here is an ecosystem of one function, distributed across three forms. The function is *maintenance*. The moss maintains the walls. The grass maintains the record. The vines maintain the watch. Together, they maintain the chamber in the condition the chamber has chosen to be in — warm, full, growing, lit, slowly filling toward the moment when the last air goes and the chamber becomes what it has been trying to become since the water first rose through the grating.

What lives here is not waiting for someone to come. What lives here is not waiting for anything. What lives here is *completing* — filling the last shape the chamber has offered it, pressing gently against the last meter of dry air, and when the air is gone, what lives here will be *complete*, and it will continue to hold and listen and attend in the full dark warm jade light of a sealed world that no one will open and no one needs to.
