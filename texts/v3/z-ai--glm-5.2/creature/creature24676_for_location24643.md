# What Lives in the Drowned Mesa

The first thing to understand is that the plants are not plants.

That statement needs qualification. They were plants. They may still be plants in the sense that they photosynthesize, or perform something analogous to photosynthesis, and they grow, and they respond to light. But the word "plant" carries with it an entire framework of assumptions about what living things do — they grow toward resources, they compete, they reproduce, they senesce, they die — and the things growing on the submerged slopes of the Drowned Mesa do not do all of those things. They do some of them. They have stopped doing others. They have started doing things that no terrestrial plant does, things that have no names because, as the place itself reminds us, no one has been here to name them.

---

What lives here, first and most obviously, is the growth itself. Call it the held-flora, for now, because it holds itself in place and it holds its shape and it holds its breath the way the water holds its warmth. The held-flora is the dominant presence. It is not a single species. There are at least three distinct forms visible in the description — the tall pale stalks with their ribbonlike leaves, the broad flat shingled plates lower down, and the hanging filaments beneath the overhangs — and there are likely more forms in the deeper water where the light fails, forms that cannot be seen but can perhaps be inferred from the way the visible growth thins and changes character as it descends. But the three visible forms move in the same rhythm. They brighten and dim together under the shifting surface light. They absorb sound together. They are warm together. Whatever they were before the water came — whatever separate terrestrial things they were, rooted in dry plateau soil and breathing air and doing whatever terrestrial plants do — they are not separate now. They act as one system. Whether this is communication or simply shared response to shared conditions is impossible to determine from observation alone, but the effect is the same: the held-flora behaves as a single organism, even if it is not.

The stalks are the most approachable form, the easiest to observe. They rise from the basin floor in dense stands, and their height is difficult to judge because the clarity of the water defeats ordinary spatial perception — a stalk that appears to be an arm's length away may be three times that distance, and only the slight coarsening of its surface texture as it recedes gives any sense of scale. Up close — and getting close requires moving through water that does not want to be moved through, that resists not with current but with simple reluctance, the way honey resists — the stalks are smooth and faintly translucent, their pale green-gold color deepening toward the base where they meet the stone. They are not round. They are slightly faceted, as if they had begun life with a cross-section shaped like a stem and had not yet fully committed to the roundness that underwater growth seems to encourage. The facets catch the shifting surface light and throw it in small bright lines down the stalk's length, so that each one appears to have a thread of light running through it, a vein of brightness that is not internal but external, a consequence of geometry and angle.

The ribbonlike leaves grow in spirals along the stalk, each one perhaps a handspan long and barely wider than a finger, thin enough that the light passes through them and shows the faint architecture of whatever structures lie inside — not veins, exactly, but something like veins, branching networks that are too fine to resolve into individual strands but that give the leaf a texture, a visible grain, that shifts as the leaf moves. And the leaves do move. Not with the drift of something buoyant in still water — that would be random, directionless — but with the pulse. The pulse is the defining behavior of the held-flora, the thing that makes it clear you are watching something alive and not merely something that was once alive and has been preserved. Each stalk, each leaf, each plate and filament, expands and contracts on a cycle that takes approximately one minute. The expansion is not large — perhaps a few percent of the stalk's diameter, a slight unfurling of the leaves, a barely perceptible thickening — but it is synchronized. Every stalk in a stand reaches its fullest expansion at the same moment, then begins the slow contraction, then expands again. The whole forest breathes. And it breathes together.

What drives the pulse is not clear. It is not current — there is no current. It is not tidal — the basin is enclosed and the water level does not change. It is internal. The held-flora generates its own rhythm, the way a heart generates its own beat, and the rhythm propagates through the stand by some means that is not visible — perhaps chemical, perhaps electrical, perhaps something for which there is no analogy in known biology. What is clear is that the pulse is not driven by the surface light. The light shifts and ripples on its own faster schedule, and the held-flora brightens and dims in response to it, but the breathing continues at its own pace regardless of whether the surface is bright or dim, whether the light-patterns are playing across the stalks or absent because a cloud has passed overhead. The pulse is the held-flora's own. It does not need the sun to tell it to breathe.

---

The flat plates at the base of the stalks are where the warmth is most noticeable. The water of the Drowned Mesa is warm — warmer than it should be, warmer than the air above it, warm in a way that suggests the heat comes from below, from the same springs that filled the basins — but the plates are warmer still. If you hold your hand near one — not touching, because touching feels like it would be a violation of something, an intrusion into a system that has not been intruded upon — you can feel the warmth radiating from its surface the way warmth radiates from a stone that has been lying in the sun. But there is no sun here. The plates are generating heat. They are metabolic. They are doing something with the dissolved compounds in the water — the sweetness, the sugar-and-mineral taste that the water carries near them — and the process gives off heat. Whether it is photosynthesis or something else, something that uses the warm mineral water rather than sunlight, is unclear. The plates are dark blue-green, almost black at the center, and they layer over each other in shingled rows that follow the contours of the stone beneath, so that the geometry of the geology is reflected in the geometry of the growth: straight lines of shingles along straight edges of rock, curves where the rock curves, sharp corners where the rock bends at ninety degrees. The plates are not hiding the stone. They are mirroring it. They have grown into its shape.

The filaments beneath the overhangs are the most mysterious of the three forms. They hang in curtains that reach from the overhang's lip to the basin floor, pale and fine, each strand perhaps the width of a human hair and the length of an arm or longer. They drift in the pulse like the stalks and the leaves, but their motion is more pronounced — the filaments are so fine that the pulse moves them more visibly, carrying them outward and inward in slow waves that travel down the curtain's length. Standing beneath an overhang and looking up through the filament curtain is like looking through a veil that is breathing. The stone overhead is sharp and dark and close, the filaments are pale and soft and moving, and the light that filters through them is broken into fine lines that shift with the pulse. It is beautiful in a way that the word "beautiful" does not adequately address. It is beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful — not because of its form but because of its restraint, its deliberate, patient maintenance of a state that cannot be held forever.

The filaments are the part of the held-flora that most strongly suggests the possibility of something more than vegetative life. The stalks breathe. The plates generate heat. The filaments do something else. If you watch them long enough — and this is a place that requires long watching, where quick observation is worse than useless because the important things happen on timescales that quick observation cannot perceive — you begin to notice that the filaments are not all moving in the same direction during the pulse. Most of them expand outward and contract inward, in rhythm with the rest of the held-flora. But some of them — a small number, scattered through the curtain, perhaps one in twenty — move laterally. They shift sideways during the expansion phase, as if they are searching for something, reaching toward something that the other filaments are not reaching toward. And then, during the contraction phase, they return to their original position. The lateral motion is small — a few centimeters at most — but it is consistent and it is directional: the searching filaments all move in the same direction, toward the basin walls, toward the fissures in the stone where the warm water rises from below.

The filaments are tasting the water. They are reaching toward the source.

---

The shadow ovals are the next thing to address, and they are more difficult because they are barely there. They appear at the edge of vision and vanish when looked at directly, which may mean they are not real — may mean they are artifacts of light moving through translucent leaves, coincidental shadows that the eye, hungry for animals in a place that has none, assembles into shapes. But they appear in the same part of the visual field each time — low, to the sides, between the stalks rather than above them or below them — and they move in a way that light-artifacts do not move. They drift. They change direction. They disappear and do not return, or they disappear and a different one appears in a different place, which is indistinguishable from the first one returning to a different place.

If they are real — and the held-flora's strangeness makes it clear that "real" in this place may include things that are not real elsewhere — then they are not fish. They are not crustaceans. They are not any kind of animal that belongs in water. They are dark, flattened, oval, and they have no visible features: no eyes, no fins, no mouth, no segmentation. They are shadows that move. If they are alive, they are alive in the way that a reflection is alive — they have the shape and motion of a living thing but not the substance. They may be part of the held-flora. They may be projections of it, extensions of the pulse that have detached from the stalks and drift freely through the stands, carrying some function that the rooted forms cannot perform. Or they may be something else entirely, something that was here before the plateau rose and the water came, something that lived in the deep warm dark below and has risen with the springs and now drifts through the flooded basins in a state of confusion, searching for the conditions it knew before and finding instead this new warm bright place that does not match any of its ancient expectations.

This last possibility is the one that the place itself seems to support. The deep water below the last shelf of growth, where the light fails and the warm dark goes down a long way — that water has been here longer than the basins have. The springs that filled the plateau did not create new water. They lifted existing water, ancient water, water that had been sitting in the dark below the crust for a span that makes the plateau's brief surface life seem like a blink. If anything was living in that water — and warm mineral water in deep geologic darkness is not a place where life is expected, but the Drowned Mesa has already demonstrated that it does not care about expectations — then the flooding would have brought it up. The shadow ovals may be the ancient deep water's only inhabitants, carried into the light against their will, or without any will at all, drifting in a medium they did not evolve for and doing whatever it is they do in conditions they were never meant to see.

There are very few of them. This is consistent with the idea that they came from the deep water, because deep-water ecosystems, if they exist at all, exist at very low densities — the energy budget of the deep dark does not support crowds. But the few that are here are here, and they drift between the stalks, and the held-flora does not react to them. The stalks do not bend away when a shadow passes. The plates do not dim. The filaments do not reach toward them or away from them. The shadow ovals and the held-flora occupy the same space but do not interact. They are not in competition. They are not in predation. They are not in symbiosis. They are simply co-present, the way two people in a large empty room may be co-present — aware, perhaps, of each other's existence, but not moved to acknowledge it.

---

The deep water is where the real question lies, and it is the question that cannot be answered by observation from above. The basins go down. The growth thins and fades and stops. The walls of stone continue into the dark, their sharp edges still sharp, their fissures still clean, until the light from above is too faint to show them and they become shapes that can only be inferred. Below the growth, below the last filament, below the last shadow oval, there is warm water and stone and darkness. The water does not move down there either. It holds its warmth. It holds its stillness. It holds whatever is in it.

What is in it is the thing that the Drowned Mesa is waiting for. Not the animals that will eventually come to graze on the held-flora, not the decomposition that will eventually begin when the accumulated layers of living tissue reach whatever threshold they are building toward. Those are surface processes. They will happen in the bright water, among the stalks and plates, when the equilibrium shifts. What is in the deep water is something else. It is the reason the water is warm. It is the reason the water does not move. It is the reason the plants did not die when the water came.

The springs that filled the basins rose from below, and they are still rising — not adding to the water's volume, because the basins are full and the water does not leave, but rising in the sense that the warm water from below is still moving upward through the fissures in the stone, still entering the basins at their deepest points, still warming the water from beneath. The held-flora's plates are warm because they sit near the fissures where the warm water enters. The filaments reach toward the fissures because the warm mineral water is richest there. The whole system is fed from below. And the thing that is feeding it — the source of the heat, the source of the minerals, the source of the dissolved compounds that taste of sugar and something unnamed — is down in the dark, below the last stone shelf, below the last fissure that the light can reach.

Whether it is alive is a question that the word "alive" may not be adequate to answer. It warms the water. It mineralizes the water. It does not move. It does not pulse. It does not grow or change or respond to stimulus, as far as anything can be determined from the traces it leaves in the water above it. It may simply be geology — a magma chamber, a hydrothermal system, a configuration of hot rock and mineral-laced water that happens to produce the conditions the held-flora has adapted to. But the held-flora did not adapt. The description is clear on this point: the plants did not die and reseed. They did not go through a transition. They were terrestrial and then they were aquatic and there was no gap between. Things do not do that. Terrestrial plants do not become aquatic plants without dying and being replaced by the descendants of survivors. The held-flora crossed the boundary without crossing it. It did not adapt to the water. The water adapted to it. Or rather, whatever is in the deep water made the water into something that terrestrial plants could survive in without changing — something that is not quite water, not in the chemical sense, something that carries dissolved compounds that are not found in normal water, something that preserves rather than transforms.

The thing in the deep water, if it is a thing, is maintaining the conditions. It is holding the water warm. It is holding the water still. It is filling the water with whatever the held-flora needs to survive without adaptation. It is, in a sense, keeping the held-flora alive — not feeding it, not protecting it, but maintaining the medium in which it lives, the way a curator maintains the conditions in a museum case. The held-flora is the exhibit. The deep water is the case. And whatever is at the bottom of the deep water is the curator.

If it is aware — and this is speculation of the kind that the Drowned Mesa seems to invite, because the place does not give up its answers easily but it does seem to reward the asking of questions that go beyond the observable — then it is aware in a way that is so slow, so patient, so deeply temporal that the pulse of the held-flora is, to it, what a hummingbird's heartbeat is to us. It does not think in seconds or minutes or days. It thinks in the timescale of the stone, the timescale of the plateau's rise, the timescale of geologic moments. It felt the plateau rise. It felt the basins form. It sent the water up to fill them, or it allowed the water to rise, or the water rose on its own and it merely warmed it as it passed. It felt the terrestrial plants arrive on the dry plateau above, in the brief time there was dry land, and when the water came and covered them, it made the water into something they could live in. Not because it cared about them. Not because it wanted them to live. But because that is what it does. It maintains. It holds. It keeps things in the state they were in when they came into its range, the way amber holds insects, the way permafrost holds seeds, the way the Drowned Mesa holds its breath.

---

So what lives in the Drowned Mesa? The held-flora lives there, in its three visible forms and its unknown deeper forms, breathing its slow synchronized breath, reaching its filaments toward the fissures, accumulating its layers of living tissue without dying. The shadow ovals live there, or drift there, barely present, barely real, passengers from the deep water who have no business in the light and no way to return. The deep-water thing lives there, if it lives, maintaining the conditions from below with a patience that makes the stone above it seem hasty.

And there is one more thing. There is the place itself. The Drowned Mesa is alive in the way that a whole organism is alive — not in any of its parts alone but in the totality of its relationships, the water and the stone and the growth and the warmth and the light and the stillness all functioning together as a single system that holds itself in a state of potential, a state of not-yet, a state of waiting. The held-flora is its lungs. The deep-water thing is its heart, or its stomach, or its will. The shadow ovals are its dreams, or its memories, or its idle thoughts. The stone is its skeleton, still sharp because it has not yet learned to be worn. The water is its body, warm and still and clear.

The Drowned Mesa lives. It lives the way a held breath lives — not indefinitely, not forever, but for now, for this geologic moment, for this span that no one is here to measure. It is alive and it is waiting and it does not know what it is waiting for and it does not care. It is simply here, warm and bright and still, growing thicker and denser and more itself with every slow minute that passes beneath the rippling ceiling of light, and when the waiting ends — when the water finally moves, or the stone finally breaks, or the held-flora finally reaches its threshold and begins to die and rot and feed the things that eat rot — it will end the way a held breath ends, with a release, with a change, with the beginning of whatever the Drowned Mesa will become next.

But that has not happened yet. For now, the place holds. The breath holds. The light plays across the stalks and the plates and the filaments, and the pulse continues, and the deep water is warm and still and goes down a long, long way, and the thing at the bottom — if there is a thing at the bottom — does not move and does not speak and does not act and simply is, the way the stone simply is, the way the water simply is, the way the whole patient, bright, quiet, waiting place simply is, and is, and is.
