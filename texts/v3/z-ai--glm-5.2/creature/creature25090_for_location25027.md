# What Lives in the Cortex Bogs

---

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is not wrong: the plants live here. The whole place lives. The root-ridges live, the pavement lives, the breathing-pores in the eastern marsh live, the spore-clouds live in the sense that anything reproductive and airborne is participating in life. The landscape is not inhabited. It *is* the inhabitant. You are walking through something's body.

But that is the first answer, and there are others, and the others take longer to find because they are harder to see, and because the place does not announce them. The place is too loud to announce anything. You have to be quiet inside the noise. You have to stop looking for movement the way you usually look for movement — the flick of a tail, the dart of an eye — and start looking for it the way the place looks for it, which is slowly, and in the wrong direction, and possibly not at all.

---

## The Drifters

You see them in the spore-fog before you see them clearly. In the eastern marsh, where the breathing-pores release their columns of golden-green mist and the fog drifts and recombines in patterns that almost seem deliberate — you notice, eventually, that some of the patterns do not recombine. They persist. They hold a coherence that the wind does not explain.

They are not animals. You understand this when one comes close enough to resolve. It is a body of spore-mist and fine fiber — root-hair, you think, or something like root-hair, woven into a matrix that holds its shape in the saturated air the way a jellyfish holds its shape in water. It is not dense. You can see through it. It has no front or back that you can identify, no features, no organs that map onto anything you recognize. It is roughly ovoid, perhaps a meter across, and it drifts in the warm currents that rise from the marsh, and when a gust funnels through one of the root-arches nearby it does not scatter. It contracts. The fibers tighten. The body compresses to something smaller and denser and lower to the surface, and it holds that compression until the gust passes, and then it relaxes again, slowly, resuming its full spread, and continues to drift.

There are dozens of them. In the marsh, in the lower ground where the air is wettest and the spore-density is highest, they move through the fog like slow thoughts. You watch one settle onto the surface of the warm standing water and it does not sink. It rests on the surface tension — no, not surface tension. It rests on the mat of fine algae and particulate that films the water, and it rests there for several minutes, and when it lifts off again it is slightly larger. It has accumulated mass. It has fed, or grown, or simply gathered, and the distinction between those three verbs is not clear here.

They do not react to you. You stand at the edge of the marsh and one drifts past close enough to touch and it does not change course, does not contract, does not acknowledge the displacement of air that your body creates. It is not ignoring you. It is operating in a register that does not include you. Its senses, if it has senses, are tuned to moisture gradients and spore-density and the subtle pressure-changes that precede structural collapse in the root-mat. It knows the landscape's moods. It does not know you.

Later, when you are back on higher ground, you see one caught in a growth-event. A section of pavement bulges upward beneath a drifter that has settled there, and the dome rises, and the bark-plates separate, and the drifter is lifted with it. You expect the drifter to flee. It does not. It flattens. It spreads itself across the surface of the new structure as it emerges, and the fine root-hairs of the new growth push up through it — *through* it — and the drifter's fiber-matrix integrates with the emerging structure, and for a moment you cannot tell where the drifter ends and the growth begins. Then the moment passes. The structure stops rising. The drifter peels away from the surface, slowly, leaving behind some portion of itself woven into the new bark, and it lifts off and drifts east again, diminished by a third, already gathering mass from the spore-fog as it goes.

You do not know if this is feeding or reproduction or something else. You do not know if the drifter was consumed or if it contributed or if those words mean anything here. You file it under the landscape's logic, which is the logic of growth finding the same solution again and again, and you move on.

## The Mosaic

On the western pavement, where the dark bark-plates interlock in their rhythmic regularity and the bright fractures between them vent thin fronds and warm air, there is something that is harder to see because it is not separate from the ground. You almost step on it before you notice that the section of pavement you are crossing is a different color.

The bark-plates here are not dark. They are pale — the same pale as new growth, as fresh root-tissue — but they are not new. They are established. They are load-bearing. They are old enough to have the same interlocking rhythm as the dark plates around them, but they are colored wrong, and when you kneel and look closely you see that they are not the same kind of tissue at all.

The pale plates are organized.

Not in the way a honeycomb is organized — not geometric, not regular in the mathematical sense. But organized in the way a garden is organized, or a brain. There is a pattern to the placement of the pale plates among the dark ones. The pattern is not obvious from ground level. You have to walk a long way — several hundred meters, tracing the pavement — before you can step back far enough in your mind to see the scale of it. The pale plates form something. A configuration. It is not a symbol and it is not a map and it is not a word. It is a structure. A tissue-structure. Like a cross-section of an organ laid flat and embedded in the skin of the world.

It is alive. When you press your hand to a pale plate, it is warmer than the dark plates around it — significantly warmer, almost hot — and it vibrates under your palm the way the root-ridge vibrated, but faster, and the rhythm of the vibration is not the slow pulse of sap moving through a trunk. It is quicker. It is irregular. It is, if you are honest about what it reminds you of, it is the rhythm of thinking.

The Mosaic — you start to think of it as the Mosaic, because it is not a single thing and it is not a collection of things, it is a pattern embedded in another pattern — the Mosaic does not move. It is not mobile. It is not separate from the substrate. It *is* the substrate, in the same way that your cortex is not separate from your brain. It is a specialization of the surface. A region of the root-mat that has differentiated into something denser, hotter, more active, more organized. Something that processes.

What does it process? You cannot tell. The spore-clouds, maybe. The moisture gradients. The growth-signals from the structures rising and falling across the plateau. The constant stream of chemical and atmospheric information that this place generates by its very operation. The Mosaic may be the place's awareness of itself. Not a mind — nothing so clean, so bounded, so human — but a region of intensified process, a thickening of the dialogue between plant and water and heat and light into something that is still plant and water and heat and light but is also, now, something more.

Or it may be a tumor. A growth that has found a self-sustaining configuration and is persisting in the middle of a landscape that builds and destroys everything around it. A lesion that the system has not yet dismantled because the lesion has made itself useful enough, or coherent enough, or simply dense enough, to resist the constant remodeling.

You cannot tell. You do not think the distinction matters. The Mosaic is there. It is thinking, or it is growing, or both. It is the closest thing to a face that the Cortex Bogs possess, and it does not look at you.

## The Settlers

There is a third kind of presence, and you find it last, in the places where structures have collapsed and are decomposing. Where the causeways have sagged into the water and the fibers are loosening and bleaching and the mass is converting from architecture to substrate. You find it because you smell something different — not the tannin-and-chlorophyll smell of the living mat, not the sweet fermented smell of the warm seeps, but something sharper. Something almost ammoniac. Something that belongs to breakdown, but not plant breakdown. Not the slow wet rot of fiber loosening. Something faster. Something metabolic.

In the ruins of a collapsed causeway, in the warm dark space between the softening layers of root-mat, there are shapes. Small. The size of a fist, or smaller. Pale. Not root-pale — the pale of something that has never needed pigment. They are clustered in the decomposing tissue the way mushrooms cluster in a fallen log, and for a moment you think they might be mushrooms, or something fungal, and then one of them moves and you see that it is not.

It has no legs. It has no eyes. It has a body that is soft and segmented and faintly translucent, and you can see the dark interior of it — gut-contents, or whatever the analog is — moving as it moves. It travels by peristalsis. The body compresses and extends, compresses and extends, and it pushes itself through the softening fiber of the dead root-structure, and as it moves it eats. Its anterior end — you cannot call it a head — secretes something that accelerates the decomposition of the plant tissue around it, and it takes in the liquefied matter, and you can see the dark strand of consumed material passing through its body and exiting at the posterior end as a fine trail of denser, darker residue that sinks into the substrate below.

It is an animal. It has to be. It moves. It consumes. It excretes. It has a body with an inside and an outside and a direction. It is the first animal you have seen here, and the description said there were none, and you understand now that the description was not wrong — there are no animals in the living parts of the Cortex Bogs. There are no animals in the architecture. The animals live only in the ruins. They live in the decomposition. They live in the narrow temporal window between collapse and reabsorption, the window in which dead plant-matter is soft enough to eat and not yet re-incorporated into new growth.

They are settlers. Opportunists. They exist only in the aftermath. When a structure falls, they are already there — emerging, you realize, from the structure itself, from cysts or nodes embedded in the living tissue that only activate when the tissue around them begins to die. They were planted. Not by a hand — by the tissue's own growth. The living root-structure produced them, embedded them in its own architecture, dormant, waiting for the event of its own collapse. And when the collapse came, they emerged, and they began to eat the ruin, and they accelerated the conversion of architecture to substrate, and by the time new growth emerged from the old site they would be gone — consumed, or reabsorbed, or simply compressed back into the mat by the weight of the new structure above them.

They are the only beings here that eat. The only beings that consume rather than grow. The only beings with an inside and an outside. And they exist only in the moments between. They are the landscape's way of cleaning up after itself. They are the only part of the Cortex Bogs that is not plant, and they were made by the plants.

You watch them for a long time. There is something compelling about them. They are the only thing here that you can understand on your own terms — they eat, they move, they have a body with a direction and a duration. They are familiar in a way that the drifters and the Mosaic are not. They are animals, and you are an animal, and in this place that is overwhelmingly, oppressively, relentlessly vegetal, that kinship means something. It means something even though they are tiny and pale and eyeless and their entire existence is a brief window of consumption in the middle of someone else's collapse.

You resist the urge to anthropomorphize. You resist it and you fail.

## The Landscape Itself

And then there is the fourth presence, which is not a thing but a behavior, which is the landscape's own relationship to time.

You begin to notice it after several hours. You notice it because you realize that the landscape has been curating your experience of it. Not deliberately — not with intent — but functionally. The growth-events you have witnessed have not been random. They have been proximate. Structures rise near you. Structures fall near you. The ground shifts under your feet and near your feet and at the edge of your vision. Not always — the landscape does not follow you — but more than chance would predict, if chance were the mechanism.

You think about the Mosaic. You think about its vibrations, its quick irregular rhythm. You think about the drifters in the marsh, gathering information from the air and depositing it in the root-mat. You think about the breathing-pores, releasing their spores in patterns that almost seem deliberate. You think about the fact that the landscape has no animals, no checks, no grazers, no winter, no pause — and yet it has not consumed itself. It grows and it grows and it grows, and the growth does not choke itself off, and the collapse does not outpace the emergence, and the system stays in a balance that is not static but dynamic, a balance maintained by adjustment, by response, by something that functions as feedback.

The landscape is aware. Not the way you are aware. Not with a self. But aware the way an immune system is aware, the way a growing embryo is aware — responsive, adaptive, processing information about itself and adjusting its own growth in response. The Mosaic is part of it. The drifters are part of it. The settlers are part of it. The spore-clouds are part of it. The whole place is part of it. The Cortex Bogs are not a place where something lives. The Cortex Bogs are the thing that lives, and the drifters and the Mosaic and the settlers and the root-ridges and the pavement and the marsh are its organs, its cells, its processes, its body.

And it noticed you. Not you specifically. But it noticed that something was here — something that moved differently from the wind, something that displaced air in patterns that were not atmospheric, something that left marks on the bark that did not match any growth-pattern in its memory. It noticed, and it responded, and the response was to grow near you. Not to threaten. Not to communicate. To *observe*. The way a body observes a splinter. The way a root observes a stone. By growing around it, by incorporating the disturbance into its own process, by learning the shape of the thing through the act of touching it.

When you gripped the root-ridge and it healed in minutes, that was not forgetting. That was digestion. The landscape took the impression of your hand and it absorbed it, and somewhere in the Mosaic or somewhere in the deep root-mat or somewhere in the spore-clouds that drift and recombine in patterns that almost seem deliberate, the shape of your grip is now part of the system. A small data-point. A minor perturbation. A fingerprint in the body's memory.

The Cortex Bogs live here. They are what lives here. They are the only thing that lives here, and they are everything that lives here, and the drifters and the Mosaic and the settlers are not inhabitants but aspects — the way sight and hunger and breathing are not inhabitants of a body but functions of it.

You are inside a body that is thinking. You are inside a body that is growing. You are inside a body that does not know you are a person and does not care, but that has registered your presence and will remember the shape of you, in some form, in some fiber, for as long as this system persists.

Which may be a very long time. Because nothing here has any reason to stop.
