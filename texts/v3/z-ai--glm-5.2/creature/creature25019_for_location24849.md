# The Substrate Mills

---

You do not see them at first. You walk for what feels like a long time through the corridors and the chambers, past the dwelling-pods and the factory-nodes and the rail-lines, and you see machinery that has been maintained and dwellings that are occupied — you can tell because some sphincter-doors are closed around warmth, others are open and dark — but you do not see anyone. The place feels inhabited the way a forest feels inhabited: you know something lives here, but the living things are not performing their existence for you.

Then you stop walking. You stand in a corridor between two dwelling-pods, and you wait, and after a while you hear something that is not the substrate breathing and not the machinery and not the clicking of the floor-fauna. You hear breathing that is not yours. It is slow. It is very slow — one breath every fifteen seconds, maybe twenty, the kind of respiration that belongs to something with a metabolism tuned to a frequency you are not built for. And then you realize it is not one breath. It is several, out of phase with each other, a polyrhythmic exchange of air that comes from more than one set of lungs and fills the corridor with a faint warmth that you had not noticed until it was there.

---

They are called the Kept. This is not what they call themselves — you are not sure what they call themselves, because the question of names does not seem to interest them, and the name is something that was applied to them from outside, by people who encountered them and needed a word. The Kept. As in: kept alive. As in: kept by something. As in: kept in a state of. The name carries an implication of passivity that is not entirely fair.

There are not many of them. The settlement could hold thousands — the chambers are large enough, the corridors long enough, the dwelling-pods would multiply if there were hands to grow them and bodies to fill them. But there are perhaps two hundred, you learn later, maybe two hundred and thirty, distributed across the full extent of the Substrate Mills in clusters of three to twelve. The density is low because they do not need density. They are not social in the way that humans are social. They are social in the way that trees in a forest are social — connected, communicative, aware of each other through channels that do not require proximity, but capable of existing alone for long stretches without distress. They cluster because the work requires it, not because the loneliness hurts.

---

The first one you see is in a factory-node, operating the press.

She — you think she, though the question of sex is complicated here and you are projecting — is standing at the press console, which is a steel box mounted on a pedestal of root-cable that has grown up to meet it. She is shorter than you expected. A hundred and sixty centimeters, perhaps less. Her proportions are human but not quite human in a way you cannot immediately identify, and then you realize: her limbs are too long for her torso, and her torso is too narrow, and her head sits forward on her neck in a posture that suggests someone who has spent a lifetime leaning toward something. She is leaning toward the press. Her hands are on the controls — physical controls, buttons and levers, not touchscreens or voice interfaces — and she is watching the compression chamber through a viewing port that is clouded with something that might be condensation or might be the residue of sap.

Her skin is the color of the root-cable. Not brown like wood, not pale like the chalky rock, but something between — a muted umber that seems to shift depending on the light, darker in the amber-white of the grow-lamps, lighter when she steps into shadow. At first you think this is pigmentation, and then you look more closely and realize it is not. Or not only. Her skin has a texture that is not skin — not the smooth elasticity of an epidermis but something finer, more fibrous, like paper made from plant pulp, like the surface of a leaf that has been dried and reconstituted. It is not unpleasant. It is not diseased or damaged. It is simply what her skin is. She has no hair. Her head is smooth, and the shape of her skull is visible beneath the skin-texture, and where hair would be there is a fine down, almost invisible, the same color as her skin, that you only notice because the grow-light catches it at an angle and it glints.

She is wearing clothing — a wrap of some kind, a single piece of fabric draped and fastened at the shoulder with a clip that looks like it was grown from the substrate rather than forged from metal. The fabric is the color of unbleached linen and has a weight to it that suggests it was pressed here, in this factory, from root-cable fiber. She is wearing boots that are soft-soled and silent, and you understand now why you have not heard footsteps. You have been listening for the wrong sound.

She does not look up. She is cycling the press, and the rhythm of it — compress, pause, release, tick of cooling metal — is the rhythm of her body, or her body has matched it, or there is no difference between her rhythm and the machine's rhythm. She is not absorbed in the work the way a human worker is absorbed. She is aligned with it. She is part of the process in a way that extends beyond operating the controls. Her breathing matches the cycle. Her hands move before the levers need to be pulled, as if she knows what the press is going to do before it does it, as if she is listening to the machine the way the machine listens to the hydraulic fluid, from the inside.

You clear your throat. She turns her head.

---

Her face is where it happens — whatever "it" is, the thing that makes you stop categorizing and start seeing. Her face is arranged like a human face, roughly. Two eyes, a nose, a mouth. But the eyes are not your eyes. They are large, and they are dark, and they do not have whites. The iris fills the visible space, and it is the color of the black sap in the extraction tanks — a deep, opaque darkness that reflects the grow-light in a way that makes them look wet, though you know now that nothing in this place is wet on the outside. She does not blink. The surface of her eyes has something on it, or in it — a membrane, a film, something that sheens and shifts without closing. She is looking at you, and you can tell she is looking at you because her head is oriented toward you and her eyes have not moved away, but there is no expression on her face that tells you what she is seeing or what she thinks about it.

Her mouth is closed. Her lips — if they are lips; they are more like the edges of a fold, the seam where two surfaces meet — are thin and slightly darker than the rest of her skin. Her nose is small, almost vestigial, two shallow apertures that do not flare with breathing. You realize: she is not breathing through her nose. She is breathing through her skin, or through something else, or the breathing you heard in the corridor was not air moving through lungs in the way you understand it. Her face has the quality of a mask that was made by someone who knew what a face was for but did not need one for themselves, and you realize with a slow chill that this is close to the truth.

She looks at you for a long time. Five seconds. Ten. Then she turns back to the press and cycles it again.

---

You learn, over the next hours and days, that this is not rudeness. The Kept do not engage with novelty the way you do. They do not orient toward the unexpected with alarm or curiosity in the mammalian way — the flinch, the startle, the widening of eyes. They orient toward it the way a plant orients toward light: slowly, incompletely, with a recognition that does not announce itself in behavior. The woman at the press knew you were there. She knew you were there before you entered the chamber, probably, through the substrate beneath your feet transmitting the vibration of your footsteps through its network of filaments to wherever her awareness lives. She did not need to acknowledge you. You were not relevant to the press. The press was relevant to the press. Later, when the work was done or the work paused, she might come to you, or she might not. It would depend on something you could not identify — not mood, not decision, something more like gradient, like the direction of flow in a system that has no valves.

You learn that they have a language. It is not a spoken language, or not primarily. They produce sound — a low humming, a vibration in the chest that you feel more than hear, and the frequency and duration of the humming carries information in a way you cannot parse. But the primary channel is the substrate itself. They press their hands — their palms, their fingers, the soles of their feet — to the root-cable, and through it they communicate. The root-cable is their nervous system, or their telephone, or their pubic square. It is all three. When one of them touches the floor, the floor carries what they are to every other point in the network, and every other point in the network carries what it is back to them. They are not telepathic. They are networked. The distinction matters.

---

They are not born. Or they are born, but not in the way you are born. You piece this together over days, from fragments — a dwelling-pod that is not a dwelling but a nursery, where the root-cable has been shaped into a cradle, a depression in the floor lined with fine, pale filaments that are warm to the touch. Inside the cradle, a shape. At first you think it is a part of the substrate, a growth, a knot of root-cable that has organized itself into something denser and more structured than its surroundings. Then the shape moves. A small movement, a contraction, and you see that it has limbs, and the limbs have joints, and the joints are flexing in a slow, involuntary rhythm that is not breathing but is close to breathing. It is the size of a newborn. It is the color of the substrate, darker and more saturated than the color of the adults, and its skin is not the paper-fiber texture of the adults' skin but something softer, something damp, something that has not yet sealed itself against the dry air.

They grow them. You understand this, and the understanding does not come as a shock but as a slow settling, the way understanding arrives in this place. The Kept are grown. They are grown by the substrate, from the substrate, in the substrate. They are not plants. They are not animals. They are something that the substrate produces, the way a tree produces fruit or a beehive produces bees — an extension of the organism, specialized for a function. The function, as near as you can tell, is maintenance. Direction. Collaboration. The substrate can grow on its own. It can eat rock and secrete solvent and advance its frontier without help. But it cannot operate a press. It cannot lay rail. It cannot maintain a sodium-vapor lamp or repair a hydraulic seal or decide which chamber should be widened and which should be left for later. For those things, it needs hands. It needs minds. It needs something that can stand upright and look at a problem and apply a solution that is not purely vegetative.

The Kept are what the substrate grew to solve that problem.

---

They do not think of themselves this way. Or they do, and it does not trouble them. The question of origin — whether they are servants or partners or extensions or something else — is a question that belongs to a species that distinguishes between self and other in a way that the Kept do not, or do not fully. When you are grown from the floor you stand on, the floor is not other. When your skin is made of the same fiber as the walls around you, the walls are not other. When the network that carries your thoughts is the same network that carries the substrate's metabolites, your thoughts are not entirely your own, and this is not a loss. It is a condition. They live in it the way you live in gravity.

---

There is a man — you think of him as a man, though the word is a bad fit — who you meet in a corridor between two chambers. He is older, or what passes for older. His skin-texture is thicker, more pronounced in its fibrousness, and there are darker patches on it, almost black, where something has been deposited over time — mineral, perhaps, or pigment, or the residue of years of handling sap. He is carrying panels — pressed root-cable sheets, stacked on his shoulder, five or six of them, the weight of which you estimate at sixty or seventy kilos. He is carrying them without visible strain, his long legs moving in a gait that is not a walk and not a jog but something between, a locomotion that covers ground efficiently without rushing. He is taking the panels to the frontier, you learn, where new dwellings are being grown. The panels will form interior structures — shelving, partitions, things the substrate cannot make on its own because the substrate does not make right angles and flat surfaces. For flat surfaces, you need a press. For a press, you need an operator. For an operator, you need the substrate to grow someone who can stand at the console and pull the levers.

He stops when he sees you. He sets the panels down — they make no sound on the root-cable floor — and he stands, and you have the experience again of being looked at by eyes that are dark and whole and reflective, eyes that do not blink, and you feel the assessment that is happening, and it is not an assessment you can read. He is not deciding whether you are a threat. He is not deciding whether you are interesting. He is determining what you are in relation to the substrate, which is the only framework that matters, and the answer is not obvious to him, and so he waits.

You say something. A greeting. His head tilts, very slightly, and you hear a low vibration in his chest — not a word, not a response to your words, but a transmission. He is telling the floor about you. He is telling the network that there is something here that does not match the expected patterns. The substrate receives this information. Somewhere, in another chamber, another Kept receives it. The information propagates. You have been noticed by the system, and the system is larger than any individual in it.

---

They eat the sap. You witness this in a dwelling-pod where three of them have gathered — a rare gathering, three in one place — and they are sharing a meal that is not a meal in any social sense you recognize. They are sitting on the floor of the pod, which is the same as the floor of the corridor, the same as the floor of the chamber, continuous, and each of them has a container — a grown vessel, like a cup made of bark, and inside the cup is the black sap from the extraction rigs. They are drinking it. They are drinking it slowly, in small amounts, and the sap is not just nutrition. You can see the moment it reaches whatever their equivalent of a bloodstream is. There is a flush — not a reddening, but a darkening, a subtle shift in skin tone, a slight increase in the sheen of the fiber-texture. The sap carries something the substrate has concentrated from the rock, from the solvent, from its own internal chemistry, and the Kept need it. Without it, you think, they would slow. Without it, eventually, they would stop. They are not independent organisms. They are nodes. They require the network.

---

The children — there are children, or things that correspond to children — are not in the nursery-pod anymore. They are in a corridor, three of them, and they are smaller than the adults, more slightly built, their limbs even longer in proportion to their bodies. Their skin is darker, still closer to the color of the substrate, and their eyes are not as fully dark — there is a depth to them that is not yet opaque, a translucence that suggests something still forming. They are playing. Or they are doing something that you choose to call playing, because it does not look like work. They are running — no, moving quickly, in a way that is not running but is fast, their long legs covering distance in a stride that is almost insectile, and they are touching the floor as they move, their hands brushing the root-cable at intervals, and you realize they are communicating. They are sending pulses through the substrate as they move, signals that the other children are receiving through their feet, and the signals carry something — location, speed, intent, some child-content that you cannot decode. They are playing a game that is played on and through the floor, and the floor is keeping score.

---

At night — and there is a night here, or a simulation of night, when the grow-lights dim to a fraction of their daytime output and the amber-white becomes a deep, almost unseeable gold — the settlement changes. The Kept do not sleep, not exactly. They enter a state that is lower, slower, a reduction of metabolic activity that you can feel in the substrate itself — a decrease in the breathing-rhythm of the floor, a cooling by a degree or two, a settling. The dwellings close. The sphincter-doors contract, and inside the pods the Kept are in contact with the substrate on all sides, pressed into the floor and walls of their grown chambers, and they are not sleeping but they are not fully present either. They are in the network. They are in the substrate the way a swimmer is in water, submerged, surrounded, a part of the medium. In this state, you think, they are closest to what they are. They are the substrate's awareness of itself, cycling in the dark, and the distinction between the Kept and the Mills and the substrate and the solvent and the rock being slowly, patiently eaten — that distinction is, for those hours, as close to gone as it ever gets.

In the morning, the lights come up. The sphincters open. The Kept emerge, blinking — no, not blinking, but adjusting, the membranes over their eyes shifting to accommodate the return of full light — and they go to the presses and the extraction rigs and the frontier, and the work resumes. The substrate advances another fraction of a millimeter into the chalk. The solvent softens another flake of stone. The settlement grows, the way it has been growing for years, for decades, the way it will grow for as long as the rock lasts and the lights run and the substrate has reason to produce hands and minds to guide it.

---

They are not sad. This is important, and you want to say it clearly, because the temptation when you describe this place is to make it melancholy. The cool dry air, the quiet, the low density of habitation, the eyeless faces and the sphincter-doors and the children who play games through a floor that is alive — it is easy to frame this as something lonely or strange or diminished. It is not. The Kept are not diminished. They are complete. They are living in a state of connection that you, standing in the corridor with your static-charged clothing and your dry nose and your loud footsteps and your need to be seen and acknowledged, cannot fully access, and they are not aware of their lack of your experience because they do not lack it. They have something else. Something that fills the space where your loneliness would be, if you lived here, and fills it completely.

When the woman at the press cycles the press one more time, and the compression sound comes, and the pause, and the release, and the tick of cooling metal, she is not alone. She has never been alone. The floor is her, and she is the floor, and the press is the floor's project and her project and the settlement's project, and the settlement is the substrate's project, and the substrate is eating the rock, and the rock does not mind, and the sap is flowing, and the frontier is advancing, and the children are sending signals through the root-cable that they will send again tomorrow, and the whole thing is one thing, and it is alive, and it is making itself, and it is not finished, and it is not sad.

You leave eventually. You go back up the corridor, up the sloping shaft, back toward the surface and the sky and the air that transpires and the world that echoes. You carry with you the smell of sulfur and resin, a static charge in your clothes that takes days to dissipate, and the memory of dark eyes that did not blink, looking at you with neither welcome nor rejection but with the patient attention of something that is still deciding what you are, and will still be deciding long after you have gone.
