# What Lives Here

## First: the obvious answer

Nothing lives here.

That is what the place wants you to think. You walk through it and see machines, not organisms. You see infrastructure, not inhabitants. You hear a pump, not a heartbeat. The architecture is so convincing — the streets so street-like, the dwellings so dwelling-shaped, the factory so factory-obvious — that you naturally look for the people who built it, find them absent, and conclude the place is empty.

But you are standing inside a living thing. You are standing inside dozens of living things. The mistake is not in calling them alive. The mistake is in looking for something smaller.

---

## The citizens

The citizens of the Rootworks are not individuals. They have never been individuals. They are strains — lineages of tissue that trace back to whatever was planted here first, however many decades or centuries ago, by whatever hands. Each strain is a species, but "species" is a word that implies separation, and there is no separation here. The ceiling-plants exchange fluid with the wall-plants through shared root-connections. The root-floor grows from tissue that originated in the walls. The machines are built from strains that were once wild and have been cultivated into specific forms over enough generations that the cultivation is now encoded in their growth patterns. They grow into shape the way a bone grows into shape — not thinking about it, not trying, just expressing a pattern that has been selected for so long it has become the pattern.

There are perhaps thirty distinct strains in the Rootworks. Maybe more — the boundaries between them blur where they share root-systems, and a single structure might contain tissue from four or five strains woven together, each contributing a different property. The lattice-roots of the walls are one strain: tough, woody, capable of being guided into woven patterns. The corky filler is a second: fast-growing, spongy, seals gaps. The leaf-mats are a third: broad, waxy, phototropic. The bioluminescent pods are a fourth. The pump-tissue is a fifth. The fiber-producing machines are a sixth. And so on.

Each strain has its own character. The lattice-root grows slowly and precisely. It does not deviate. Given a framework — an archway, a wall, a corner — it fills that framework exactly, no more, no less. It is the architect of the Rootworks, the strain that gives the place its shape. If the Rootworks has a mind — and it may, in the way that a forest has a mind, distributed and slow and nothing like a human mind — the lattice-root is the part that thinks in structure.

The corky filler grows fast and sloppy. It fills any gap it can reach, bulges through openings, seals punctures. It is the immune system. When the press was newer and still being adjusted, a crack formed in its frame, and the corky strain found the crack within days and sealed it with a thick callus that is now part of the machine's structure. The crack is still visible inside the callus, a flaw absorbed and incorporated, a scar that has become architecture.

The leaf-mat strain is the most responsive. Its leaves move. Not quickly — you would have to watch for an hour to notice — but they move, orienting toward light, toward moisture gradients, toward carbon dioxide concentrations. They are the strain that most readily detects your presence. When you stand in a dwelling, the leaves of the nearest roof shift, fractionally, in your direction. Not toward you as a person. Toward you as a source of warm, moist, carbon-rich air. You are, to the leaf-mats, a particularly slow-moving weather event.

The pump-tissue is the oldest strain, or feels like it. It has a density the other strains lack — its cells are packed tighter, its sap is thicker, its walls are layered like the growth rings of a tree that has been alive for a very long time. If the Rootworks remembers anything, the pump remembers it. The pump has been contracting and relaxing on its two-second cycle since before the stair was worn smooth. It does not tire. It does not falter. Its rhythm is the rhythm of the place, the tempo that every other process moves to. The fiber-machines vibrate in sympathy with it. The spore-pods crack on its off-beat. Even the drip from the ceiling seems to fall, when you listen long enough, in a pattern that loosely correlates with the pump's cycle, as if the whole chamber is breathing at a rate set by this one thick knot of muscle-wood in the floor.

The bioluminescent pods are the strain that is hardest to account for. They produce light for no obvious reason. Light costs energy. The pods do not photosynthesize — they are the light source, not the light user. They consume nutrients from the root-network and convert some fraction of them into cold blue-white photons, steadily, without variation. This is inefficient. This is, in biological terms, extravagant. But the leaf-mats need light to orient, to grow, to maintain their structure. Without the pods, the leaf-mats would degrade within weeks, and the roofs would fail, and the dwellings would flood, and the machines would corrode, and the system would unravel. The pods are not decoration. They are the load-bearing element of the whole architecture, the thing without which the rest cannot hold. They glow because everything else depends on their glowing.

They do not know this. Nothing in the Rootworks knows anything in the way you mean the word. But the system knows it the way a body knows how to breathe — not through understanding but through having been shaped, over long time, by the consequences of stopping.

## The shape of a life here

A life in the Rootworks is not a life you would recognize. There are no births, no deaths, no moments. There is no moment. The pump contracts and relaxes two thousand times an hour, and this is the closest thing to a clock, and no one is counting.

But there are events. The spore-pod cracks. This is an event. It happens every few seconds — a sharp percussion, a burst of reproductive tissue, a dispersal. The spores are released into the air, drift, land, germinate. Most land on surfaces that are already occupied and fail to establish. A few find bare spots — where a leaf has recently shed, where a root has worn thin — and send down filaments. These are the immigrants, the new growth, the replacement parts. They are identical to the tissue they replace because they are from the same strain, the same lineage, the same genetic source. They are not new individuals. They are the old individual, extending itself.

A life in the Rootworks is the life of a strain, not a specimen. The lattice-root that forms the wall of dwelling seven is the same organism as the lattice-root that forms the wall of dwelling twelve. They are connected through the root-floor, through shared tissue, through a network that runs beneath every surface. If you could extract the lattice-root strain from the Rootworks and hold it up, it would be a single plant — a single, vast, branching organism that has grown into the shape of a town. The same is true of each other strain. The Rootworks is not a community of plants. It is a community of thirty or so superorganisms, each one a single life that has grown to fill a role.

They do not compete. Competition implies excess, implies waste, implies organisms struggling for limited resources. The Rootworks has no excess. The water is exactly sufficient. The nutrients are exactly sufficient. The light is exactly sufficient. Each strain occupies its niche and no other, and the niches are separated not by competition but by specialization. The lattice-root cannot grow in water. The pump-tissue cannot photosynthesize. The pods cannot weave. They need each other. They are, in the deepest sense of the word, symbiotic — not in the casual sense of two organisms living near each other, but in the sense that none of them can exist without the others. The Rootworks is not a collection of plants. It is a single organism with thirty tissues.

If this is true, then what lives in the Rootworks is one thing. One very large, very slow, very old thing that has grown into the shape of a town and runs the way a heart runs, automatically, without supervision, without desire, without any awareness of itself as a whole.

## The things that are not plants

There are, in the Rootworks, things that are not plants.

They are small. They are not animals — the place is very specific about this. No animals. No insects, no worms, no vertebrates, no arthropods. But the floor is a stomach, and stomachs need to digest, and digestion at the rate the Rootworks requires — a fallen leaf absorbed within a day — is not accomplished by roots alone.

The microorganisms of the Rootworks are its hidden citizens. They live in the root-floor, in the water that runs through the root-channels, in the warm murky basin of the pump, in the films of moisture on every surface. They are bacteria, fungi, archaea — the same sort of microbial community you would find in any rich soil, any wetland, any decomposing log. But here they are not background. Here they are the mechanism by which the system closes its loops.

A leaf falls from the roof-mat. It lands on the root-floor. The root-floor does not immediately grow over it — that takes hours, and the leaf is already being processed before the roots arrive. The bacteria are there first. They have been there the whole time, in the surface film of water that covers the floor, waiting in the way bacteria wait — which is to say, metabolically active, ready to exploit any new resource. The leaf provides carbon, nitrogen, minerals. The bacteria break these down, releasing them into the water film. The water film flows into the root-channels. The root-channels carry the dissolved nutrients to the pump. The pump distributes them. The cycle is closed by organisms too small to see, living in a film of water too thin to feel.

They are not one species. They are hundreds. They are thousands, if you count strains and variants and the endless genetic drift of asexual reproduction in a closed system. The microbial community of the Rootworks is as complex as the plant community, as specialized, as interdependent. Some break down cellulose. Some break down lignin. Some metabolize the tannins in the water, making it palatable. Some fix nitrogen from the air, supplementing the minerals the groundwater provides. Some live nowhere else — they have evolved in this chamber, in these conditions, over however long the system has been running, and they exist nowhere on the surface. They are endemic to the Rootworks. They are its most unique inhabitants, and they are the ones you will never see.

There are fungi. Not mushrooms — there is nothing here that produces a fruiting body above the surface, because there is no wind to disperse spores and no animals to carry them. The fungi here are entirely subterranean, living in the root-mat, threading through the gaps between root strands. They are mycorrhizal, in a loose sense — they associate with the root-tissue, breaking down organic material the roots cannot process alone and trading the resulting nutrients for the sugars the roots transport. The fungi are the translators between the plant world and the mineral world. Without them, the root-floor could not digest the leaves. Without them, the system would accumulate dead material and choke on its own output.

The fungi are the oldest inhabitants, possibly. Their mycelial networks run through the root-mat in patterns that predate the root-mat's current layout, as if they were here first, as if the roots grew along channels the fungi had already established. This may be true. The Rootworks may have begun as a fungal colony in a warm wet cave, and the plants may have been added later, by the makers, grafted onto a system that was already running. Or it may not. There is no way to know without asking, and there is no one to ask.

## The makers

The makers lived here. They do not live here now. This is the central fact of the Rootworks, the fact that everything else is built around, the silence that every sound fills.

They were human-scale. The doorways are six feet tall, arched, shaped to accommodate an upright biped with shoulders narrower than a human's but not by much. The stairs are proportioned for legs of a certain length. The dwellings have rooms you could stand in, sit in, sleep in — the interior dimensions suggest a body that reclined, that wanted a certain amount of space around it, that valued privacy enough to create separate rooms but not enough to put doors on them.

They did not leave anything behind. No artifacts. No tools. No clothing, no furniture, no art, no writing. Either they took everything when they left, or everything they used was biodegradable and has since been absorbed by the root-floor, or the root-floor has grown over everything and it is all still there, buried in the layers of root-tissue, sealed under inches of living wood, entombed in the floor that was once their home.

This last possibility is the most likely. The root-floor grows inward. It thickens. It incorporates whatever falls on it. A dropped tool, a worn-out garment, a broken dish — each of these would have been covered by the root-mat within days, sealed in, slowly decomposed by the microbial community, its minerals extracted and cycled back into the system. But some materials would resist decomposition. Metal. Stone. Fired clay. If the makers used such things, those things are still here. They are in the floor. You are walking over them. They are an inch beneath your feet, or two inches, or six, depending on how long ago they were dropped and how fast the floor has grown since then.

The floor is an archive. The root-mat is a record. Layer by layer, it has preserved everything that has fallen on it, compressed and sealed and held, the way peat holds bodies, the way amber holds insects. If you could read the floor — if you could cut a cross-section and peel back the layers — you would find the history of the Rootworks written in its sediment. The oldest layers, deepest, would tell you when the first root was planted. The middle layers would tell you when the makers were here, how many there were, what they used, how they lived. The upper layers would tell you when they left. And the surface — the living surface, the layer you walk on — would tell you only that the system has been running alone since then, growing inward, filling the space, not forgetting but not remembering either, just accumulating.

The makers are gone. But they are here. They are in the shape of the doorways and the width of the streets and the height of the ceilings. They are in the smoothness of the stair. They are in the design of the machines, which were built to their specifications — to produce materials they needed, at rates they could use, in quantities they could carry. The fiber-mats were their paper, or their cloth, or their bandages. The boards were their building material, their furniture, their tools. The water was their water. The rooms were their rooms. The Rootworks is their legacy, and it is also their ghost, a place that remembers them in its bones the way a body remembers a posture it held for years, the way a path remembers the feet that wore it.

But the system does not mourn them. It does not miss them. It does not wait for them. The machines produce their outputs and the outputs stack up and the stacks grow moss and the moss is absorbed into the floor and the floor grows and the rooms narrow and the place carries on because carrying on is the only thing it knows how to do.

## What has changed since the makers left

Not much. That is the answer, and it is also the strangeness.

The system was designed to be self-sustaining. This was the makers' greatest achievement, or their most ambitious design, or their most desperate gamble — to build a system that could run without them. They succeeded. The Rootworks has been running without them for years, possibly decades, possibly longer. Nothing has broken. Nothing has failed. The pump still pumps. The pods still glow. The leaves still turn. The floor still breathes.

But the system is not unchanged. It is growing inward. The rooms are narrower. The streets are tighter. The outputs are accumulating. The system was designed to run without its makers, but it was not designed to run without its users. The distinction is critical. The makers built a system that could maintain itself. They did not build a system that could maintain itself *and* produce a usable output. The output has no destination. The fiber-mats stack up. The boards accumulate. The spores germinate and grow, but the growth has nowhere to go except inward, because the system is not expanding — it has reached the limits of the chamber, the limits of its water supply, the limits of its light. It can only grow thicker.

This is how the Rootworks will end. Not dramatically. Not soon. But eventually, over centuries, the rooms will close. The streets will narrow to nothing. The dwellings will fill with root-tissue. The machines will be sealed inside the growing mass, still operating, still producing, their outputs immediately absorbed by the tissue that surrounds them. The pump will be the last thing to go — it will keep contracting and relaxing, its basin filling and draining, long after the streets it feeds have become solid wood. And eventually, even the pump will be compressed, constrained, grown into, until its chamber is too small for the volume of water it needs to process, and the flow will back up, and the pressure will build, and the system will find a new equilibrium — one that does not involve pumping, does not involve streets, does not involve dwellings or machines or any of the architecture the makers designed.

It will become a forest. A single, solid, living mass of root and bark and cambium, filling the chamber from floor to ceiling, with no interior, no structure, no purpose except to persist. It will still be alive. It will still be the same organism, or the same community of organisms. But it will no longer be the Rootworks. It will be something else — something the makers did not design, something they might not recognize, something that has forgotten the shape of a doorway and the purpose of a street.

This will take a long time. Centuries. The system is patient. The growth is slow. You will not see it. The makers did not see it. But it is happening, has been happening since the last maker walked up the stairs and did not come back down, and it will continue happening until the chamber is full and the Rootworks is not a place but a thing, not a city but a stump, not a system but a solid.

---

## What lives here, finally

What lives here is a system. A system that was designed by beings who are gone, that runs on cycles they set in motion, that maintains a shape they gave it, that produces goods they are not here to use, that grows inward because it cannot grow outward, that will eventually fill itself and become something else.

What lives here is thirty strains of plant tissue, woven into a single architecture, each one dependent on the others, none of them aware of the whole, all of them contributing to a process none of them understands.

What lives here is a microbial community of thousands of species, too small to see, living in water films and root channels, digesting the dead so the living can eat, invisible and essential.

What lives here is a memory. Not a conscious memory. A structural one. The memory of feet on stairs, of hands on root-walls, of bodies in doorways, of use and purpose and design. The memory is in the shape of things, in the proportions, in the sizes. It is fading as the shapes change, as the rooms narrow, as the growth fills in. But it is still there, still legible if you know how to read it, still the closest thing the Rootworks has to a soul.

What lives here is a ghost of habitation. The Rootworks is haunted by the people who built it, not as spirits but as form. Every archway is their silhouette. Every street is their footprint. Every machine is their intention. They are present in the way a mold is present in the thing it shaped — not inside it, but of it, inseparable from it, the reason it is what it is.

What lives here is the Rootworks itself. One thing. One organism, if you are generous with the word. One community, if you are precise. One place that is also a creature, or a creature that is also a place, breathing on a two-second cycle, digesting on a daily cycle, growing on a century-long cycle, lit by its own light, fed by its own waste, warmed by its own decay. It is alive. It is the only thing here that is. It is enough.
