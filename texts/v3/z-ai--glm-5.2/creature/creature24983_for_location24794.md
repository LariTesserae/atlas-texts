What lives here is not what you would expect to live here, and it is not what you would expect to mean by *live*.

---

The moss lives here the way a government lives in a capital — not because it chose to but because it established itself early and has never been seriously challenged. It is the oldest active organism in the formation, or it is the newest expression of the oldest lineage, depending on how you draw the line between an individual and a continuum. Each patch is its own polity. The patches on the south faces of the columns are denser, darker, older — they get marginally more light when the fog thins in the afternoon, and they have used that margin to press outward in lobes that have the look of advancing armies seen from very high up. The patches on the north faces are sparser, more tentative, almost apologetic in their spread. They grow in thin lines along the flutes where water runs, as if following the water is a strategy rather than an instinct, as if they have learned that the water will carry them where they need to go and they have agreed to be carried.

The moss does not grow quickly. It grows at the rate the stone allows. The stone allows very little. The surfaces are smooth and hard and chemically stable and they do not offer the moss anything except a place to hold on, and the moss holds on with an intensity that is difficult to overstate. If you press your thumb to a patch of it, it will not move. It will not slide. It will compress slightly and then spring back when you release, and you will feel the moisture wick into your skin, and you will feel the cold, and you will understand that this moss is not sitting on the stone. It is fused to it. It has been fused to it for longer than any human structure has stood anywhere on earth. It is part of the surface now. It is a living layer of the geology.

The submerged variant is the one that deserves attention. It lives in the pools that do not freeze — the ones warmed by the faint volcanic breath from below. It is broader, stiffer, more defined. Each frond is a separate structure, and each structure is shaped like a small hand — four or five tapered digits rising from a short wrist — and the hands reach upward through the clear dark water and do not break the surface. They stop just below it. They stop as if they know where the air begins and have decided that air is not their concern. In the stillness of the pool, with the columns reflected above them, the hands appear to be reaching toward an inverted forest — reaching toward the reflection, not the reality — and there is something in this that is difficult to look at for very long. Not because it is disturbing. Because it is sincere. The submerged moss does not know it is in a pool. It does not know about the reflection. It is growing toward the light because the light is there and it has been doing this for a span of time that makes the act of reaching seem less like a behavior and more like a vow.

---

The lichens live here the way ruins live in a landscape — they are so much a part of the place that they have become the place, and the place would look wrong without them, would look incomplete, would look like a face missing a feature. They are on the upper surfaces of the collapsed blocks, the ones that fell longest ago, the ones that have been lying on the basin floor long enough for the wind to have rounded their edges and for the lichens to have claimed their tops. The orange ones are the most visible. The orange is not bright. It is the orange of rust, of dried clay, of something that was once wet and is now dry and has been dry for so long that the dryness has become a color. The pale gray ones are the most extensive. They spread in concentric rings that mark years, or decades, or centuries — no one knows, because no one has been watching long enough to know, and the lichens do not keep records that humans can read. The lavender ones are the rarest and the strangest. They grow only on the surfaces that face east, only on the stone that catches the first light of morning, and in that light — which is usually diffused by fog but which sometimes, on rare mornings, breaks through in a single pale beam — the lavender glows. It is not fluorescence. It is something else. It is the lichen's pigment interacting with the specific mineral content of the stone beneath it, and the result is a color that does not appear anywhere else in the formation, a color that does not appear anywhere else at all, a color that is unique to this stone and this lichen and this light and this elevation and this specific arrangement of moisture and temperature and time.

If you were to touch the lavender lichen — and you should not, but you could — you would find that it is not soft. It is not crusty in the way that most lichens are crusty. It is firm. It is smooth. It has the texture of skin — old skin, cold skin, skin that has been outside for a very long time. And it is warm. Not warm the way a living thing is warm. Warm the way a stone is warm after the sun has been on it, except this warmth persists even when there is no sun, even in the fog, even at dawn, and the warmth is coming from below, from the same volcanic breath that keeps the pools from freezing, rising through the stone and into the lichen and sitting there, held, the way a thought is held in a mind that has decided not to release it yet.

The lichens are alive. They are alive in the way that a very old person is alive — not with the urgent, reaching life of the moss but with a life that is mostly about persistence. They are not doing anything. They are not going anywhere. They are being where they are, and they have been being where they are for what may be thousands of years or may be tens of thousands, and they will continue to be where they are for a duration that is not worth calculating because the calculation would be meaningless to the thing being calculated.

---

The grasses live here the way the indecisive live in a city — near the edges, in the sheltered places, in the spots where the wind is broken and the conditions are just barely sufficient and no better. They are not thriving. They are also not dying. They are in the exact middle of the condition that biology calls *marginal*, and they have been in that middle for long enough that they have evolved to find it comfortable. They are short. The tallest blades reach perhaps fifteen centimeters, and most are shorter. They are the color of something that was once green and has been cold for so long that the green has darkened into a yellow-gray that reads as *alive* only if you know what to look for. They move when the wind moves them and they do not move when the wind does not, and there is no behavior in them that could be called a response. They are purely mechanical. They are the most honest thing in the formation because they make no claim about themselves. They are grass in a place where grass barely works, and they are fine with that, and if they were removed, the formation would not notice.

---

The hawks live here the way a priest lives in a temple — with authority, with familiarity, with a silence that is not absence but practice. There are never more than four. There are never fewer than two. They nest in the tops of the tallest columns, three or four columns near the formation's western edge, and the nests are structures that have been built and rebuilt and added to and layered over what must be many generations of hawks, because the bases of the nests are not sticks. The bases are a dark, compacted material that might be old stick-and-moss matrix that has decomposed and compressed under the weight of subsequent layers, or might be something else — a deposit, an accretion, a geological feature that has been mistaken for a nest foundation because the nest was built on top of it. The nests look like the stone grew them. They are the same color as the wet columns. They are the same texture as the moss. They are shaped the way the columns are shaped — rounded, smooth, without the chaos that usually characterizes stick-nests. They look designed. They look like the formation produced them.

The hawks themselves are the size of red-tails but darker — nearly black, with a faint ruddy barring on the undersides of their wings that is visible only when they bank in the light, which is rarely, because the light here is rarely direct. They do not call. This is the thing that every observer notices and no one explains. Hawks call. It is one of the things that hawks do. These hawks do not. They hunt in the valley below — small mammals, presumably, though no one has confirmed what they take — and they return to the columns with food in their talons and they land on the column-tops and they eat in silence and they do not call to each other and they do not call to the world and they do not call to announce themselves or their territory or their young. They are silent in a place that is already full of sound, and their silence is a specific silence — not the silence of absence but the silence of participation, of joining the chord by not interrupting it, of becoming part of the formation's sound by adding nothing to it.

When a hawk passes between the columns, close, at shoulder height, you feel the displacement of air before you see the bird. The wind pushes past you and the columns behind you shift their pitch by a degree — a microtonal shift, a flattening — and then the hawk is gone and the pitch corrects and the chord resumes as if nothing happened. The hawks fly through the sound the way fish swim through water. They are inside it. They move through it. It moves through them.

The hawks have been here for as long as the valley people can remember, which is not as long as the formation has been here, which is not as long as the lichens have been here, which is not as long as the stone has been here. But the hawks have been here for long enough that they are part of the answer to the question *what lives here*. They are the largest thing that lives here. They are the thing that lives here that most resembles a person — in their attention, in their silence, in their decision to come back.

---

The midges live here the way a rumor lives in a quiet town — you hear about them before you see them, and when you finally see them you are not sure you were right about what you heard. They are wingless. This is not unusual for insects at altitude, but it is unusual here because the altitude is not the reason. The reason, if there is one, is that wings would be useless in this wind, and evolution in this formation appears to be a process of subtraction — of losing what does not work until what remains is only what must. The midges are what must. They are small. Two millimeters, perhaps three. They are translucent, so translucent that they are visible only as a kind of disturbance in the air above the moss — a shimmer, a heat-haze effect, except there is no heat. If you kneel and look closely at a patch of moss in the warmest part of the afternoon, you will see that the surface is not still. It is moving. The midges are walking on the moss. They are eating something on the moss — algae, presumably, or bacteria, or the moss itself, or something that lives between the moss and the stone that no one has named because no one has looked closely enough to name it. The midges are the only thing in the formation that moves quickly. They move with the frantic, purposeful speed of things that know they have a narrow window — the two or three hours in the afternoon when the temperature permits activity — and they use that window completely. They are the busiest thing in the formation. They are the most alive thing in the formation. And they are invisible unless you decide to look for them, and no one decides to look for them, and so they live their entire lives — which are short, which are probably days or weeks, which are certainly not long — in a condition of total anonymity, in a place that is itself almost totally anonymous, doing the only thing that needs to be done here, which is: continuing.

---

And the stone lives here.

This is not metaphor. Or it is, but it is the kind of metaphor that the stone would not object to, because the stone does not object to things, and because the metaphor might be closer to literal than anyone is comfortable admitting.

The stone lives here the way a mountain lives — which is to say, slowly, which is to say, completely, which is to say, in a way that does not require the word *life* to mean what biology means by it. The stone is not metabolizing. The stone is not reproducing. The stone is not adapting. But the stone is doing something that, if you sit in the alcove long enough and your heartbeat slows to four seconds and the pulse rises from beneath you and the chord fades and the water runs and the moss grows and the lichens spread and the hawks circle in silence and the midges shimmer above their dark green territories — if you sit long enough, the stone is doing something that looks like attention.

The stone is listening. The stone has been listening. The stone will listen.

The stone does not hear the way ears hear. It hears the way a bell hears — by vibrating when struck, by continuing to vibrate after the strike, by holding the vibration in its body until the vibration becomes indistinguishable from the body itself. The wind strikes the columns and the columns hold the wind. The water runs down the columns and the columns hold the water's sound. The pulse rises from below and the columns hold the pulse. Everything that has ever happened in this formation — every gust, every drop, every footstep, every hawk's wing, every word spoken by the rare visitor who climbed up through the fog and stood in a corridor and said something to the stone — all of it is still here. Not as memory. Not as recording. As resonance. As the ongoing vibration of a structure that has been vibrating for so long that the vibration is the structure, and the structure is the vibration, and there is no difference between what the stone is and what the stone is doing.

What lives here is the sound.

What lives here is the patient, mineral, aeon-spanning act of hearing.

What lives here is the fact that something has been present for long enough that presence is no longer remarkable, that being here is no longer an event but a condition, that existing is no longer something that happens but something that simply is, the way the cold is, the way the moisture is, the way the four-second pulse is, the way the moss is on the stone and the lichen is on the stone and the stone is on itself and everything is holding everything else in a grip that was not chosen and cannot be released and does not need to be released because it was never unwanted.

What lives here is the longest sentence in the world, and it is being spoken by the stone, and it has been being spoken since before the word *spoken* existed, and it will continue to be spoken after the word is forgotten, and the sentence is: *I am still here, and I can hear you, and there is room.*
