# What Lives in the Welling Chamber

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is in a hurry.

This sounds like a metaphor. It is not. It is a measurable fact of the organisms that inhabit the Welling Chamber, and it shapes everything about them — their metabolisms, their lifespans, their relationships to one another, the very rhythms of their days, if "days" is a word that means anything in a place where the only light comes from thin fissures in stone far above and never changes.

---

**The pale segmented things on the floor** — you would call them insects if you were feeling generous and wrong. They are not insects. They are not isopods, though they share something with isopods in the way their bodies are organized: a repeating architecture of plates, each segment slightly smaller than the last, the whole creature shaped like a grain of rice that learned to walk. They are not white, exactly. In your lamplight they appear white, but when you look more carefully — and you must look carefully, because they are the color of the limestone they live on, which is to say they are the color of patience — they are a pale amber, the color of old rain. They have no common name. They have no scientific name that anyone has assigned. They are, for the purposes of this account, the *crawlers*, though they do not crawl so much as *flow*.

There are — and this is a conservative estimate, because counting them is like counting raindrops on a window — somewhere between twelve and twenty thousand of them on the chamber floor at any given time. They move in patterns. Not patterns you can easily describe. Not trails, not columns, not the purposeful lines of ants carrying food to a queen. Something more like the movement of liquid through a sponge, or the way heat distributes itself through stone. They spread, they gather, they spread again. They concentrate where the organic mounds are richest — the decomposing plant-matter that carpets the flat spaces between terraces — and they disperse across the bare stone in thin films of motion that your eye resolves into individual animals only when you focus very hard.

They are detritivores. This is the foundation of their existence. They eat what falls, what dies, what decays. The frond-equivalents that droop from the walls eventually break and drop, and the crawlers find them. The fungal shelves, when they reach the end of their life and begin to soften and slump, fall in pale chunks, and the crawlers find them. The insects — the tiny ones, the ones you saw shivering on the surface of the troughs — die, and the crawlers find them. And the crawlers, when they die — and they die, at a pace so steady it constitutes another kind of rhythm in the chamber — are found by other crawlers, and consumed, and the cycle continues without sentiment, without ceremony.

Their lifespan is approximately six months. This is a guess. No one has raised them in captivity, and the chamber does not cooperate with longitudinal studies. But their size, their metabolic rate (slow, slow, so slow — they can go weeks between meals, which is useful in a place where food is abundant but inconsistently distributed), and the evidence of molting in the organic mounds suggest half a year, perhaps a little more. They molt five times. You find the husks everywhere, mixed into the detritus, so thin and pale they dissolve into the substrate almost immediately. The crawlers do not attend to their dead. The dead are simply food that hasn't been eaten yet.

What startles you, once you spend time watching them, is that they are not random. You thought they were. You thought the flowing, spreading, gathering was arbitrary — the motion of simple animals responding to simple stimuli. But there is a structure to it, a grammar. They move away from light. They move toward moisture. They move away from vibration — your footsteps send them scattering in a radius of surprising precision, each individual moving exactly as far as it needs to and no farther, then resuming its path as if you were a weather event, a brief inconvenience, a stone dropped in a stream. And they move toward each other. Not all the time, not in any way that suggests sociality in the way you understand it from bees or wolves, but in clusters, in moments. A group of fifty or sixty will converge on a single point on the floor and remain there for hours, touching antennae-equivalents, exchanging — something. Chemical information, probably. News of the chamber. Where the food is. Where the moisture is. Where the light touched.

Then they disperse. The meeting is over. Whatever was said was said.

---

**The amphibian-suggestive things in the basins** are easier to love, though you should be careful with that word. They are not asking for your love. They are not asking for anything.

There are three basins that hold permanent water. The largest is the one you stood above, the one on the lowest terrace, roughly the shape and depth of a bathtub but three times as long. The other two are higher, smaller, fed by different seeps. Each basin has its own population. The populations do not mix, though the water does — slowly, over months, through cracks and capillary action and the patient redistribution of moisture that the chamber performs without cease. The animals in each basin are slightly different. Slightly different sizes, slightly different coloration. The ones in the highest basin are the smallest and the darkest, almost black, as if the reduced food supply and the reduced light have compressed them into something denser. The ones in the lowest basin are the largest — four inches, perhaps five, from nose to tail-tip — and the palest, a dark brown-grey that lightens to grey on the belly.

They are salamanders. Or they are close enough that the distinction doesn't matter to anyone except a herpetologist, and no herpetologist has been here. They have the long bodies, the small limbs, the smooth wet skin, the placid watchfulness of animals that have evolved in a world without fish and without birds and without any predator larger than themselves. Their eyes are the most developed thing about them — large, dark, catching your lamplight with a reflection that is not quite the mirror-flash of a cat's eye but something softer, something that absorbs light rather than reflecting it outright. They see in the dark. Not well. But well enough.

They eat the crawlers, when the crawlers venture to the water's edge to drink or to feed on the organic matter that accumulates in the damp soil around the basins. This is a rare event — the crawlers are not eager for water, they get most of their moisture from their food — but it happens often enough to sustain the salamander population, which is small. Eleven individuals in the lowest basin. Six in the middle. Four in the highest. You know this because you counted, and you counted because counting was the only thing you could do with your eyes that felt like it honored the precision of the place.

They also eat the insects — the tiny surface-skimming ones, the ones you couldn't identify. These insects are midge-equivalents, small gnat-like creatures that live their entire lives on the surface film of the basin water. They never leave it. They never fly. Their wings — and they have wings, small transparent ones, folded flat against their bodies — are vestigial. They have been vestigial for a long time. The insects walk on the water surface, feed on the microorganisms that film the water, mate on the water, lay their eggs in the water, die on the water, and are eaten by the salamanders from below. They never know any other world. For them, the surface of the basin is the floor of the universe, and the underside of the surface is the ceiling, and the salamanders are weather.

The salamanders also eat each other, occasionally. Not often. Not as a primary strategy. But a large individual will consume a small one if the opportunity presents itself and the hunger is sufficient. This is not violence, in the way the chamber means it — it is economy. The basin is a closed system. The energy that enters it enters through the microorganisms and the organic debris that washes in with the seep-water, and it moves upward through the insects and the crawlers to the salamanders, and when a salamander eats a salamander it is simply the energy returning to a more efficient vessel. The basin does not judge this. The basin does not notice.

The salamanders are not social. They do not call to one another. They do not court. They reproduce — they must, because the population is stable, and stable populations in closed systems require replacement — but they reproduce so quietly, so invisibly, that you did not witness it and you suspect no one ever has. There must be eggs. There must be a larval stage. There must be a maturation process. But the basins are small and the hiding places are many — cracks in the limestone, depressions beneath the lips of the terraces, the spaces under the fungal shelves where rock meets water — and the salamanders have had a very long time to learn where to put their young where they will not be found by their neighbors. They reproduce at the pace of the chamber, which is to say slowly, which is to say adequately.

One more thing about the salamanders: they are old. Not the individuals — the individuals live, perhaps, ten or fifteen years, which is remarkable for an animal of their size but not impossible in a cold-blooded creature with a slow metabolism in a stable environment. But the *lineage*. The *form*. They have been here, in something like this shape, in something like this number, for — and this is not a guess, this is a geological argument based on the erosion patterns of the basins, which could only have been carved to their current shape by water flowing over stone for a minimum of forty thousand years — for forty thousand years. Perhaps much longer. The salamanders do not know this. They do not experience their own antiquity. Each individual is born into a world that has always been exactly this, a basin of still water in a warm dark room, and it lives its life in that world, and it dies in that world, and its children inherit the same world, and the same world, and the same world.

---

**The things in the root-threads** are harder.

Not harder to see — you can see them, if you aim your light upward and hold it steady, which your arms do not want to do for very long. They are there, moving among the pale hair-thin fibers that descend from the ceiling fissure, and they are clearly at home there, clearly adapted to that vertical world in the way the salamanders are adapted to the water and the crawlers to the floor. But they resist easy description because they are not quite like anything you have a ready category for.

They have many legs. You were right about that. Not the thirty or forty legs of a centipede, but more than the six of an insect — perhaps twelve, perhaps sixteen, you cannot count because they move and the light moves and the root-threads move with their weight. They are longer than the crawlers, perhaps an inch, perhaps an inch and a half, and thinner, proportionally, and they move differently — not flowing but *climbing*, each leg finding its grip on the threads with a precision that suggests the threads were made for them, or they were made for the threads, or the two evolved together, which is the most likely answer.

Because the root-threads are not roots. You assumed they were — pale fibers from some surface plant that had sent tendrils down through the limestone in search of water. But the ceiling of the Welling Chamber is — and this is an estimate based on the geology of the region — a hundred and fifty feet of solid rock below the surface. No root reaches that far. No root could. The threads are something else. They are fibrous, they are organic, they are pale and they glisten with the chamber's condensation, but they are not roots. They are, if you had to guess — and you do have to guess, because no one has studied them — a fungal structure. A mycelial cord. A rhizomorph. The threads are the body of a fungus that has colonized the fissure above and sends its feeding threads downward into the chamber to capture moisture and, possibly, to capture the insects and the thread-dwellers themselves, because some fungi are predators, and some fungi set traps, and the threads have a quality — a tautness, a regularity, a sense of *having been placed* — that suggests intention, or at least adaptation.

The thread-dwellers move among them. Are they caught? Are they free? You watch for a long time. You cannot tell. Some of the thread-dwellers are motionless, hanging in the fibers like flies in a web, and you think: *they are trapped, they are food.* Then one of the motionless ones moves, lifting its legs deliberately, resettling, and you think: *they are resting, they are home.* The distinction may not matter to the fungus. The distinction may not matter to the thread-dwellers. The chamber does not require clear categories. The chamber has room for relationships that are both predation and habitation, both trap and shelter, both death and home.

What do they eat? The thread-dwellers? Each other, perhaps. The smaller insects that also move among the threads — you see them now, much smaller, barely visible, quick and erratic where the thread-dwellers are deliberate. Fungal material, perhaps — they may graze on the threads themselves, eating their own substrate, which is a strategy that works only if the fungus grows faster than the grazers graze, and the balance here suggests that it does, that it has, for a very long time. Moisture, certainly — the threads are wet, everything is wet, water is not a limiting resource in the Welling Chamber.

They make sounds. You heard them — the faint rustle, the vibration. And the chirps. The low chirps from the dark above, answered after a pause by another from across the chamber. You did not see the chirpers. They are higher than your light reaches, or deeper in the thread-mass, or both. But they are communicating. The chirps are not random — the pause before the answer is consistent, and the answer comes from a consistent direction, and after several exchanges the pattern shifts, as if the conversation has moved to a new topic or a new participant has joined. You do not know what they are saying. You know only that they are saying it, and that they have been saying it in this chamber, in this warm dark, for longer than you can easily imagine.

---

**The fungal shelves** on the walls are not passive. You knew this about fungi, or you should have — fungi are not plants, they do not wait for food, they *go* and *get* food. The shelves climb the walls in tiers because the walls are where the moisture seeps in, and the moisture carries dissolved minerals, and the minerals are food, and the shelves are the fungus's way of presenting its feeding surface to the widest possible stream of nutrient-rich water. They are not decorative. They are *mouths*. Beautiful mouths, pale cream and dull orange, glistening, each one a dinner plate sized expression of the principle that life will find a way to eat and will build whatever structure it needs to eat more efficiently.

There are at least four species of shelf fungus in the chamber. You can tell by the color variations, the texture differences, the way they overlap and avoid each other. One species dominates the lower walls — pale cream, smooth, with a slight curve to each shelf that makes them look like shelves in a library that has been left to damp for a century. A second species occupies the higher walls, where the air is warmer and drier — these are the orange ones, smaller, rougher, growing in tighter clusters, as if they have learned that in the upper chamber there is less room and more competition. A third species grows only in the cracks between the first two, thin and flat and almost translucent, a fungus that has found its niche in the *between*, in the boundary, in the place where two other organisms meet and neither dominates. The fourth is harder to see — it is the thread-fungus, the one that makes the root-threads, and its shelves are small, rare, and located only near the ceiling fissure, as if it spends most of its energy on its threads and has little left for more conventional architecture.

The shelves are home to other things. Beneath each shelf, in the dark space between fungus and wall, small creatures hide — not the crawlers, who prefer the open floor, but something else, something you see only as shadows that move when your light passes. Mites, perhaps. Tiny arachnid-equivalents. They live in the damp understory of the fungal shelves and they eat — what? The spores, probably. The shelves produce spores, visible in your light as a faint haze when you disturb them, a pale mist that settles slowly in the still air. The mites eat the spores. The spores land on the organic mounds on the floor and germinate and become new fungal growth. The mites are eaten by — you don't know. By the thread-dwellers, perhaps, when the mites venture too far from their shelves. By the salamanders, if the mites reach the water. The chain is long and branching and invisible and it has been stable for longer than the tally marks have been on the wall.

---

**The moss-adjacent groundcover** is the chamber's most aggressive organism, and the fact that you do not think of it that way — that you think of moss as gentle, passive, a soft green thing — is a failure of your categories. This groundcover is not moss. It is something that has evolved in a world without seasons, without frost, without drought, without any of the pressures that make surface moss what it is. It has had nothing to fight but *space*, and it has fought for space with everything it has.

It grows on every horizontal surface that holds moisture. It grows on the terraces. It grows on the organic mounds. It grows on the cairn of flat stones that the long-ago visitors left. It grows on the limestone itself, where the seep-water keeps the stone wet enough. It is deep blue-green — almost black in low light, almost luminous when your lamp catches it right — and it compresses underfoot like a sponge because it is full of water, because it is *made* of water, because in this chamber water is not a resource that organisms store but a medium they exist in, and the groundcover is more water than it is anything else.

It reproduces constantly. You can see the evidence: pale green patches at the edges of every established colony, new growth, the organism reaching into territory it has not yet claimed. It grows over the fungal shelves when they die and fall. It grows over the crawler husks. It grows over the stones, over the tally marks — slowly, so slowly, the edges of the carved grooves softening under a thin skin of blue-green that will eventually erase them entirely. In another thousand years the tally marks will be gone. In ten thousand years the cairn will be part of the floor. The groundcover will have swallowed them. Not out of malice. Out of *thoroughness*.

The crawlers eat it, a little. Not enough to control it. Nothing in the chamber controls the groundcover. It controls itself — it stops growing where the surface dries, where the stone is bare, where the temperature drops below the threshold that its metabolism requires. It has found its own boundaries and it respects them, not because it is wise but because it is *optimized*. It has been growing in this chamber for so long that it has tried every direction and remembered which ones worked.

---

**The fern-relatives** are the chamber's elegance. Everything else here is efficient, pragmatic, built for function. The fern-relatives are built for function too, of course — everything alive is built for function — but their function has produced something that looks, in your lamplight, like beauty. Pale, translucent fronds, narrow, draping against the rock and against each other, filling the vertical surfaces with a ragged greenery that moves in the faint air currents you generate by breathing. They move like fabric. They move like hair. They move like something that is not quite plant and not quite animal and not quite decoration.

They are plants, probably. They photosynthesize — there is chlorophyll-equivalent in their tissues, visible in the faint green of their fronds, and they grow toward the light that enters through the ceiling fissures, which is the behavior of an organism that needs light and has learned to seek it. But the light is so faint, so thin, so barely present, that they must supplement it. They must get energy from another source. And they do — their roots, which are shallow and widespread, penetrate the organic mounds on the terraces, and they absorb nutrients directly from the decomposing matter there. They are part-plant and part-scavenger. They eat the light and they eat the dead. In the Welling Chamber, this is not a contradiction. This is a strategy.

There are not many of them. Perhaps forty individual plants, distributed across the walls and the vertical surfaces, each one a cluster of fronds emerging from a root mass hidden in a pocket of soil. They are the chamber's slowest-growing organisms — a new frond takes months to unfurl, and each plant produces perhaps two or three new fronds per year. They are also, possibly, the chamber's oldest organisms. The largest clusters — the ones on the middle terrace, where the light is strongest — have root masses that are dense and woody, accumulated over decades, perhaps centuries. If the chamber has a consciousness, it is in these roots. Not because they think. Because they *persist*. They have been here longest. They will be here after the crawlers have evolved into something else, after the salamanders have found new basins or died out, after the tally marks have been swallowed by the groundcover. The fern-relatives will still be here, draping against the walls, reaching for light that barely exists, feeding on the dead.

---

**The person who made the tally marks** lived here. Or visited here repeatedly, which amounts to the same thing.

You know this because of the marks themselves. They are not the work of a casual visitor — someone who stumbles into the chamber, looks around, scratches something on the wall, and leaves. The marks are in groups. The groups are in series. The series are arranged along the second terrace in a way that suggests *sequence* — a record, a counting, a way of marking time in a place where time does not mark itself.

The grooves are carved with a stone tool, probably — the marks are consistent with the kind of wear that flint or chert produces on limestone. Each groove is about an inch long, a quarter-inch deep, and spaced regularly from its neighbors. The groups of three and four suggest a counting system — base seven, perhaps, or simply a way of grouping marks for easy reading. There are — and you count them twice, because it matters — one hundred and sixty-three individual grooves, arranged in thirty-eight groups. If each group represents a visit, or a day, or a season, or a ritual period, then the person was here between thirty-eight and one hundred and sixty-three times, depending on how you read the system.

The cairn is below the marks. Three flat stones, balanced. It was placed there *after* the marks were begun — the grooves above the cairn are older, more eroded, more colonized. The cairn was a later addition. Something changed in the person's relationship to the chamber, and they wanted to mark it. *I was here. I came back. This matters.*

Who were they? You cannot know. The chamber does not preserve human remains — the humidity and the biological activity would consume a body in months, the bones in years. If the person died here, they are part of the organic mounds now, part of the groundcover's substrate, part of the fern-relatives' root soil. If they left, they left no other trace. They came, they counted, they stacked three stones, and they went away or were taken away, and the chamber grew over their marks and grew over their cairn and did not notice their going.

But they came. That is the thing. In all the long history of the Welling Chamber — the forty thousand years of salamanders, the centuries of fern-relatives, the uncountable generations of crawlers and thread-dwellers and fungal shelves — one person found this place and recognized it as something worth returning to. Something worth counting. Something worth marking. And the chamber, which does not care, which has no mechanism for caring, nonetheless *kept* the marks. Grew over them slowly, softly, without erasing them. Let the grooves soften but not disappear. Let the cairn settle but not fall. The chamber is not sentimental, but it is *thorough*, and thoroughness can look like tenderness if you are lonely enough, if you are the kind of person who stacks three stones in a dark room and hopes that someone, someday, will see them and know.

---

**The whole of it, together.**

The Welling Chamber is home to — and this is an incomplete census, because the small things resist counting and the smaller things resist seeing — eleven salamanders in the lowest basin, six in the middle, four in the highest. Twelve to twenty thousand crawlers on the floor, in the mounds, on the terraces. An unknown number of thread-dwellers in the root-threads above — perhaps a hundred, perhaps five hundred, in a colony that extends up the fissure to a depth you cannot determine. Four species of shelf fungus, one species of thread-fungus, one species of groundcover, one species of fern-relative, one species of surface-insect, one species of mite that lives under the fungal shelves, and a number of microorganisms — bacteria, protozoans, fungal spores, the invisible infrastructure of life — that you cannot begin to estimate but that are, in terms of biomass, the dominant inhabitants of the chamber.

They do not know they live together. The salamander does not know that the crawler it eats has been feeding on the organic matter that was once a fern-relative frond that fell from the wall that the groundcover is also growing on. The thread-dweller does not know that the threads it lives among are the body of a fungus that is also, in another part of the chamber, producing the shelves that the mites live under. The groundcover does not know that it is growing on the marks made by a person who came here and counted and left. None of them know any of this.

But they are all here, together, in the warm dark, in the silence that is not silence but the aggregate of many small quiet lives, in the balance that has been tested for forty thousand years and has held. They are the chamber. The chamber is them. When you leave and the quiet resumes and the condensation fills your footprints and the crawlers close over the path you walked, it will not be because the chamber is recovering from your visit. It will be because the chamber is continuing, as it has always continued, as it will always continue, with or without witnesses, with or without names, with or without anyone to stand at the edge of the lowest terrace and look down into the water and feel, for a moment, the profound impropriety of being loud in a place that has learned to be quiet.

The chirps resume, in the dark above. The thread-dwellers move. The crawlers flow. The salamanders watch with eyes that absorb light, and the groundcover grows, and the fern-relatives reach, and the fungal shelves exhale their spores into the still air, and the warm dark settles, and the chamber is itself, and itself, and itself.
