# What lives here

Let me start with the beetles, because they are the ones you notice first, even though you don't notice them first. You notice the walls, the wet, the dimness — and then, after some time, the surface of the moss begins to seem not quite still, and you realize that what you took for texture is actually traffic.

They are not one species. That is the first thing to understand. There are at least four kinds, probably more, and they have divided the moss the way nations divide a coastline — not by treaty but by long habit, by the gradual settling of boundaries that happens when things live next to each other for a thousand generations and the arguments have all been had.

The largest are the ones I called rice-grain sized, though that is a human comparison and it makes them sound uniform, which they are not. They are elongate, almost cylindrical, with elytra that have a faint oiled sheen — not colorful, not iridescent, just *present*, a darkness that is not absence of color but its own color, the way obsidian is its own thing and not a failed diamond. They move deliberately. Each step is placed. They are not rushing. They follow channels in the moss that are not visible to you but are clearly visible to them — grooves worn by generations of exactly-sized bodies traveling exactly this route, the way a path appears in grass from feet. Except these paths are in moss, and the moss has grown around them, accommodating them, so the path and the moss are the same thing, co-formed. The beetles do not choose to follow the path. The path and the beetle are part of the same system. One does not precede the other.

These larger ones eat the moss. Or rather, they eat the dead parts of the moss — the lower layers, the material that has begun the slow transition from plant to peat. They are not destroying anything. They are completing a process the moss has already begun. They move along, and their mandibles — small, dark, precise, working with the unhurried patience of jaws that have never needed to be fast — clip and clip and clip, taking the oldest growth, leaving the surface intact. What they excrete goes into the substrate, and the substrate holds it, and it becomes part of the floor, and the floor becomes part of the walls, and the walls seep, and the seep feeds the moss. The beetle is a hinge in a cycle. It does not know this. It does not need to know this.

The second kind of beetle is smaller — half the size, maybe less — and lives on the walls, not the floor. It is paler. Not white, but a brown that has been diluted, the color of weak tea, of wet sand, of something that has lived in darkness so long that pigment became an unnecessary expense. It has eyes, but they are reduced, gathered into clusters that appear as little more than dark smudges on the head, and whether they function or are simply vestigial — remnants of a time when this beetle's ancestors lived in light — is a question the beetle does not ask. It navigates by moisture gradient. It moves toward wet. It can feel, through its tarsi, the difference between stone that is merely damp and stone that is actively seeping, and it moves toward the seep, always toward the seep, because the seep is where its food is.

Its food is the biofilm. The thin, living layer of bacteria and algae and other single-celled organisms that coats the wet rock beneath the moss and between the moss and in the places where moss has not yet reached. The biofilm is — and this is important — the *foundation* of this place, the way soil is the foundation of a forest, the way phytoplankton is the foundation of a sea. Everything here either eats the biofilm or eats something that eats the biofilm or eats something that eats something that eats the biofilm. The biofilm is the point at which the nonliving mineral world and the living world meet and merge and become indistinguishable. The water seeps from the rock carrying dissolved minerals — calcium, iron, silica, compounds leached from stone over millennia — and the biofilm intercepts them, incorporates them, builds itself from them, and the pale beetles crop the biofilm, and the spiders eat the pale beetles, and the cycle does not circle so much as *cascade*, downward and inward, everything feeding everything else, everything becoming everything else.

There are two more kinds. One is almost too small to see — a dark fleck, a mote that moves — and it lives in the deepest layer of the substrate, in the compressed peat, in the black fibrous floor that holds water like a lung holds air. It rarely surfaces. It is the detritivore that works the lowest stratum, breaking down what even the larger moss-eating beetles cannot process, and its waste is the dark humic juice that leaches back into the rock and emerges elsewhere as seep and the cycle — but no, I said that already. That is the point. The cycle is already said. It has been said for millennia. It does not need saying again, and yet it goes on saying itself, which is different from repetition. A heartbeat is not a repetition. It is a continuation.

The fourth kind — and I am less certain about this one, because I have not seen it so much as inferred it from the architecture — is a predator. Something that lives in the gaps between the shelf fungi and the rock, something that has left evidence in the form of empty beetle elytra scattered in certain places, the dark husks of consumed insects arranged — not arranged, *deposited* — in the way that something leaves remains. I do not know what it looks like. I know it is there because the system requires it. Without a predator in that niche, the pale wall-beetles would overrun the biofilm, and the biofilm would thin, and the wall would erode faster, and the geometry of the hollow would shift, and the geometry of the hollow does not shift. Something is keeping the balance. Something is eating enough beetles to keep the beetle population exactly where it has been for as long as the hollow has been the hollow. I imagine it — and this is imagination, not observation — as something flat, something patient, something that does not pursue but simply occupies a space and consumes what enters that space. A thing of edges and thresholds. A thing that waits by existing.

---

The flatworms deserve their own attention, because they are the creatures that most embody this place. Not because they are beautiful — though they are, in the way that simple things are beautiful, the way a line is beautiful, the way a single note is beautiful — but because they have taken the principle of the hollow to its most complete expression.

They are planarians. Or something like planarians. They are flat, dark-edged, and they glide on the water film that coats the rock, and they have no legs and no eyes and no apparent anterior or posterior that you can distinguish from a distance, though up close — if you crouch and bring your face close to the wet stone, close enough to smell the tannin and the cold — you can see that one end is slightly broader, slightly more blunt, and that this end leads, and the other end follows, and the body between is a smooth, uninterrupted translation from one to the other.

They eat the biofilm too, but differently than the beetles. The beetles crop it. The flatworms *graze* it — they slide across the surface and their ventral surface, their belly, their contact-point with the wet world, secretes enzymes and absorbs the dissolved products, so they are eating and moving at the same time, continuously, without stopping, without the mechanical clip-clip-clip of the beetles' mandibles. They are more efficient. They are older, evolutionarily. They are the way life solved the problem of "how to eat a surface" before it invented jaws. And they have never needed to update the solution. The solution still works. The surface is still there. They are still here.

What strikes you about them — what strikes you about all of them, but the flatworms make it most clear — is that they are not waiting for anything. They are not biding time until conditions improve. They are not enduring. They are not making the best of a difficult situation. They are *exactly where they belong*, doing *exactly what they are for*, and the concept of "better conditions" does not exist in their biology because their biology was shaped by these conditions and these conditions have not changed and so their biology is not a compromise or an adaptation but a *completion*. They fit the way a key fits a lock that was made for it. And the lock was made for it. And neither came first.

The flatworms reproduce, I think, by fission — by dividing, by becoming two where there was one. I have not witnessed this, but I have seen flatworms of different sizes, and the size distribution suggests growth and division rather than separate individuals from eggs. This means that the flatworm on the wall in front of you may be, in a real and biological sense, the same individual that was there a hundred years ago, a thousand, longer — the same body, the same continuous stream of cells, dividing and regrowing and dividing and regrowing, a living thread unbroken since the hollow was hollowed. Or it may not. The science of flatworm identity is not a science that has a clear answer. But the question itself — *is this the same one?* — feels irrelevant here. In a place where nothing changes, identity is not a line but a field. The flatworm is not an individual. The flatworm is a *presence*, a way the wet rock has of being alive, a property of the seep.

---

The spiders are the most active thing here, which is to say, they are the least still, which is to say, they are still very, very still.

They are small. Smaller than you would expect. The largest is perhaps the size of a lentil, leg span included, and most are smaller. They are dark — not black but the same oiled-darkness as the larger beetles, a darkness that reads as saturation, as density, as a color that has absorbed so much light it has become its own opposite. They do not build webs. They have no silk apparatus that I can see, or if they do, they do not use it. They hunt on foot.

They walk the water film. This is the remarkable thing. The walls of the hollow are wet but not dripping — the water clings as a film, a skin, a layer perhaps a millimeter thick, held by surface tension and adhesion to the stone — and the spiders walk on it. Their tarsi are minute, and their weight is negligible, and they press the film without breaking it, leaving dimples that spread and flatten and disappear, the way a finger pressed into a balloon leaves a depression that vanishes when the finger lifts. They are surface-tension hunters. They exist in the boundary layer — the thin zone where water and air meet, where the rules are neither the rules of water nor the rules of air but something else, a third physics, a physics of meniscus and capillary action and forces that operate at the scale of millimeters and do not scale up.

They hunt the springtails. The springtails are the most numerous animal here — more numerous than the beetles, more numerous than the mites, more numerous by far than the flatworms. They are everywhere. They are the reason the moss surface moves when you watch it long enough — not the beetles, which are too few and too slow to account for the rippling, but the springtails, which hop and land and hop and land in constant, tiny, almost invisible motion. They are hexapods, not insects, though the distinction is academic at this scale. They are pale, almost translucent, and they have the furcula — the tail-like appendage that snaps down and launches them into the air — and the launching is the sound I mentioned before, the dry tick, the fingertip-flicking-paper sound that is the loudest thing in the hollow.

The spiders do not chase the springtails. They intercept them. They stand on the water film, and they feel — through their legs, through the surface tension, through the vibrations that propagate across the wet skin of the wall — the springtails moving, and they move to where the springtail will be, not where it is, and they wait, and the springtail arrives, and the spider takes it. The whole interaction takes less than a second. It is not violent. It is not dramatic. It is a transfer — a springtail-shaped piece of energy moving from the category of "alive and hopping" to the category of "alive and held" to the category of "no longer alive" to the category of "being digested," and the spider moves on, and the water film closes over the dimple where it stood, and the wall is as it was.

The springtails do not diminish. This is the thing that is hard to understand from outside, from the world where predation means reduction and populations rise and fall and the balance of nature is a dynamic, oscillating thing. Here, the balance is not dynamic. The springtails reproduce at exactly the rate at which they are eaten. They have reproduced at exactly that rate for as long as the hollow has existed. The spiders eat exactly as many springtails as the springtails produce, and the springtails produce exactly as many as the spiders eat, and neither number has changed. This is not because of a feedback mechanism, not because of a regulatory system. It is because the conditions that produce springtails and the conditions that produce spiders and the conditions that produce the relationship between them are all the same conditions, and the conditions have not changed, and so the numbers have not changed, and the numbers will not change, because the conditions will not change, because this is the hollow, and the hollow is *finished*.

---

There are other things. Many other things. I should mention them, because they are here, and their presence matters, even if they are less visible.

There are mites — oribatid mites, the kind that look like tiny turtles, armored and slow — and they live in the moss and eat the dead moss and their droppings become the substrate and the substrate becomes the floor. There are nematodes — roundworms, microscopic, present in numbers that defy comprehension, present in every gram of the wet black floor, present in the biofilm, present in the moss, present in the water film itself — and they are the invisible engine of the place, the organisms that make the nutrients flow, that break the dead into the dissolved, that connect the mineral world to the biological world at a scale too small to see. There are tardigrades — water bears, as they are sometimes called, though they are not bears and only vaguely love water — and they live in the moss cushions, in the water that clings between leaves, in the tiny aquaria that each clump of bryophyte creates for itself, and they are almost immortal in their way, capable of drying and reviving, of freezing and reviving, of enduring, but here they do not need to endure, because nothing dries and nothing freezes, and so their immortality is unused, a capacity held in reserve in a place that never calls on it.

There are rotifers in the water film. There are ciliates in the water film. There are bacteria in the water film, and the bacteria are the smallest living things here and also the most numerous and also, in a sense, the most important, because they are the ones that interface with the rock, that take the mineral and make it biological, that stand at the boundary where geology becomes life and perform the transmutation. The biofilm is their city. The beetles and flatworms that eat the biofilm are their predators. The spiders that eat the beetles are their predators' predators. And the thing in the gap between the fungi and the rock, the unseen thing that leaves empty elytra — that is their predators' predators' predator. And all of this — all of this — rests on the bacteria, and the bacteria rest on the rock, and the rock seeps, and the seep feeds the bacteria, and the cycle is not a cycle but a *braid*, a plait of water and stone and life woven together so tightly that you cannot pull one strand without pulling all of them, and no one has pulled any of them, and no one will.

---

And there is — I must say this — something else.

Not an animal. Not a plant. Not a fungus. Something I cannot classify, because I am not sure classification is the right tool for it. It lives in the deepest part of the substrate, in the lowest layer of the black fibrous floor, where the peat is most compressed, where the material has been pressing down on itself for the longest time, where the distinction between "organic" and "mineral" has begun to blur. It is a network — a mycelium, perhaps, or a bacterial consortium, or something that is neither and both, a structure of filaments so fine that they are not visible to the eye but are visible to the hand, if you press your palm into the floor and wait, as a faint resistance, a tautness, the sense that the ground is not just material but *connected*, threaded, held together by something that runs through it the way roots run through soil except these are not roots and this is not soil and the analogy limps.

I do not know what it does. I do not know what it eats. I do not know if it eats. I know that it is there because the floor has a integrity that unstructured peat would not have — a cohesiveness, a woven quality, the sense that someone has been here, building, at a scale and in a medium that I cannot perceive directly. It may be the architect of the whole place. It may be the thing that stabilizes the substrate, that prevents erosion, that keeps the hollow from filling or collapsing or changing shape. It may be the reason the seep seeps at a constant rate — not a geological reason, not a physical reason, but a *biological* reason, a living dam, a living filter, a living infrastructure that regulates the flow of water through the floor and into the walls and out of the walls and down the moss.

Or it may be nothing. A residue. A deposit. A pattern that looks like architecture because I want it to be architecture, because the human mind looks for intention in structure, and this structure is so intricate, so dense, so *purposeful-looking* that it is hard to believe it is accidental.

But I do not need to resolve this. The hollow does not need me to resolve it. The thing in the floor — if it is a thing — does what it does, whatever that is, and has been doing it for as long as the hollow has been a hollow, and will go on doing it, and the doing is what matters, not the naming. The naming is for the outside. The naming is for the world where things change and must be distinguished from other things before they change into them. Here, nothing changes into anything else. Here, things are what they are and have been what they are and the name is the thing is the name is the thing, round and round, not a circle but a point, not a cycle but a state.

---

What lives here, then, is not a list. Not a catalog. What lives here is a *completion* — a set of organisms that have found, together, a way to be that does not require change, that does not require improvement, that does not require anything at all except the continuation of the conditions that produced them. They are not survivors. They are not pioneers. They are not remnants of something larger or precursors of something to come. They are the thing itself. The finished thing. The world said: *here is a wet dark place at the top of a mountain where water comes out of stone and nothing dries and nothing freezes and the light is always this dim and the air is always this cool* — and life said: *yes, that will do*, and moved in, and has been home ever since.

They do not know they are at the head of the world. They do not know what "head" means or what "world" means. They know the wet. They know the dark. They know the path through the moss and the taste of the biofilm and the vibration of the springtail across the water film and the pressure of the substrate beneath their feet and the temperature, always the temperature, the same temperature, the temperature that is not a temperature but a fact of existence, and they move within this knowing without reflecting on it, without naming it, without separating themselves from it, and the moving is the knowing is the living is the being, and it goes on, and it goes on, and it goes on.
