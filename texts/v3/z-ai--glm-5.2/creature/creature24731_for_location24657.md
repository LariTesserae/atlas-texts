# Inhabitants

---

The first thing that lives here is the mountain.

That sounds wrong, but only because we are used to thinking of stone as setting rather than resident. The mountain lives here the way a host lives in its own house — not doing, not performing, but *being the condition* under which everything else can happen. It exhales heat. It weeps water. It has worn its own interior smooth in the places where it matters and left itself jagged where it doesn't, and this is not architecture, not intention, but it isn't entirely passive either. The granite has a grain to it, a directionality, and the water followed that grain, and the bats followed the water's smoothing, and the beetles followed the bats, and the moths followed — well. The moths are harder to trace. But the mountain started it. The mountain is the first occupant, and it is still here, and it will be here after every other living thing in this gallery has gone.

The mountain is patient in a way that has nothing to do with virtue. It is patient the way a furnace is patient. It does not wait. It simply continues, and continuation looks like patience when you are standing inside it, breathing its breath, feeling its heat rise through the soles of your boots.

---

Then: the bats.

They are not one thing. That's the first error an outsider makes — assuming the colony is a single organism, a mass, a *swarm*. It isn't. It is a parliament of disagreements, a coalition of species that tolerate each other the way neighbors tolerate each other in a building with thin walls. There are at least four species here, probably more, and they roost in different zones, prefer different strata of the ceiling, keep different micro-schedules within the larger daily rhythm. You cannot see this well enough to identify them, but you can hear it — the vocalizations layer like instruments in a piece of music that has no conductor, no score, no audience. Each species has its own register, its own pattern of call and silence, its own preferred echoic space within the gallery's irregular geometry.

One species dominates. They are small — thumb-sized bodies, forearms like burnt matchsticks — and they pack the densest, the closest to the ceiling, pressed against the warmest stone where the geothermal heat is strongest. They are the heart of the colony, the engine of its warmth, the primary producers of the guano that feeds everything below. They leave at dusk in a stream so dense it registers on the air as a pressure-event, a wind that moves outward from the fissure entrance like an exhalation. They return before dawn in a ragged, tired flood, and the sound they make coming home is different from the sound they make leaving — louder, more territorial, full of the small squabbles of re-entry. *That's my spot. Move. Move.* They roost by preference in clusters of fifty to a hundred, and within those clusters there are sub-groups, pairs, individuals who return to the same centimeter of stone night after night. They know their neighbors. They argue with them. They sleep against them.

The second species is larger, slower, more deliberate in its movements. These roost in the mid-ceiling zone, in the gaps between the dense clusters of the first species, and they space themselves more evenly — an arm's length between bodies, each one occupying its own territory. They are the ones you notice when you look up, because they are big enough to have shape, to have presence, to look like *something* rather than texture. Their wings fold differently. Their ears are larger. They vocalize less frequently but at a lower pitch, a resonant clicking that you feel in your sternum more than hear with your ears. They hunt differently too — they leave later, return earlier, fly lower and slower, taking larger prey from different zones of the alpine meadow that exists outside the fissure. They are the old money of this colony. They were probably here first. They will probably be here last.

There are others. A third species, medium-sized, that roosts in the jagged sections of the ceiling where the stone still has teeth — they prefer the irregular surfaces, the crevices and overhangs, and they hang singly or in pairs, never in clusters. A fourth, smaller still, that seems to roost not on the ceiling at all but in the vertical cracks along the gallery's walls, hidden from view, detectable only by sound. There may be a fifth. The acoustics are complex enough that certain frequencies could be echoes, could be distortion, could be something else entirely, something living in a part of the gallery you cannot reach, a deeper chamber that the water made and the bats found and you never will.

The bats are not aware of you in the way you might expect. They know something is here — the echolocation tells them that much, a large, warm, slow-moving body in a space where bodies are usually small and fast and familiar. But you don't register as a threat. You register as *furniture*. A temporary obstruction. They adjust their flight paths when you stand in the gallery, flowing around you the way water flows around a rock, and they resume their patterns when you move. Some of them — the bolder individuals, the juveniles, the curious — spiral down toward you on their way out at dusk, clicking rapidly, building a sonic image of your shape and texture and heat-signature, and then pull up and away, satisfied or bored or whatever the bat equivalent of *not interesting* is. You are not interesting to them. You are not dangerous. You are simply *not relevant to the project of living*, which is the only project they have.

They dream. You don't know this, but they do. In the deep hours of the day-sleep, when the gallery is at its quietest and the colony is condensed to its densest, warmest, most compressed state, individual bats twitch. Toes flex. Ears rotate toward sounds that aren't there. Faces make expressions that are not quite expressions, that could be reflex or could be something else. Whatever bats do when they are unconscious and processing, they do it here, packed against each other in their thousands, suspended upside down in the dark, warm air, and the collective twitching and breathing and micro-vocalizing creates a substrate of sound beneath the substrate of sound — a hum beneath the hum, so low and so constant that it is indistinguishable from silence unless you have been in the gallery long enough to learn the difference.

---

The beetles live in the floor.

This sounds simple. It isn't. The guano is not a floor in any meaningful sense — it is a landscape, an ecosystem, a world. It has depth. It has history. It has layers, and the beetles inhabit specific layers the way forest creatures inhabit specific strata of canopy. The upper layer — the freshest deposit, the topography of ridges and valleys shaped by water and by the falling of waste from the ceiling — is occupied by the runners. These are the ones you see when you look down, the ones whose movement makes the surface pulse. They are dark, oval, fast for their size, and they move in patterns that look random but aren't — they are tracking moisture gradients, following the paths where fresh guano is wettest, where the nutrient concentration is highest. They have wings. They rarely use them. The gallery is their whole world, and the gallery has no need of flight.

Below them — an inch down, two inches, in the zone where the guano has begun to compress and darken and take on the consistency of packed earth — live the diggers. These are larger, slower, pale. They have lost the capacity for flight entirely; their wing-covers are fused, their bodies cylindrical, evolved for pushing through dense medium rather than moving across surfaces. They eat the older guano, the partially decomposed layers where the fungal mycelium is thickest. They are the composters. They are the ones who turn waste into soil, who transform accumulation into substrate, who make the floor into something that could — in another context, in another world — support growth. They carry spores on their bodies as they tunnel, inoculating fresh layers of guano with the fungus that will break it down further. They are farmers without knowing it. Or maybe they know it. It is impossible to tell what a beetle knows.

There is a third kind. Smaller than the others. Reddish-brown. You almost never see them because they live in the deepest layer, the oldest guano, the material that has compressed into something almost mineral, half-turned to stone. They exist in pockets of moisture that persist within this compressed layer, tiny voids where water has been trapped and slowly, slowly processed. They are the bottom of the system. They are the final stage. When they have finished with the guano, there is nothing left but the mineral residue, the calcium and phosphorus that will eventually become part of the rock itself. They are the end of the line and the beginning of the line, because the mineral residue feeds back into the stone, and the stone holds the heat, and the heat holds the bats.

The beetles do not know they are part of a cycle. They know moisture. They know food. They know the vibration of a spider's legs nearby, and the specific urgency of moving away from that vibration. They know the temperature gradient — warmer near the surface, cooler below — and they adjust their depth accordingly, moving up when the gallery cools, moving down when it warms. They have done this for longer than the tool-marks on the wall. They have done this for longer than the current configuration of the water-channels. They have been here long enough that their bodies are shaped by this place — not just adapted to it but *formed* by it, the way a river stone is formed by the river, the way a wind-blown tree is formed by the wind. They are gallery-shaped. They are guano-shaped. They are the place they live in, made mobile.

---

The spiders are few and they are patient.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Maybe forty. It is hard to count because they are translucent and they do not move when they do not need to, and in a gallery this size, forty translucent animals that are not moving are effectively invisible. They live in the cracks between the guano-ridges, in the narrow valleys that the water has carved into the deposit's surface. They are pale — not white, not quite, but the grey-white of old bone, of chalk that has been left in damp air. Their legs are long relative to their bodies, disproportionately long, and they hold them in a specific posture — splayed flat against the guano surface, sensing for vibration, for the specific frequency of beetle-feet on compressed substrate.

They do not build webs. Not really. They produce silk — a minimal strand, a trip-line, anchored between two ridges and left to sag into the valley — but it is not a net. It is an alarm system. When a beetle crosses the strand, the spider knows, and the spider moves, and the movement is very fast and very precise and then it is over and the spider is eating and the beetle is no longer a beetle but is instead becoming a spider, which is the way these things work.

They have no eyes. Or rather, they have the vestigial remnants of eyes — dark spots on the front of the cephalothorax that may have been functional once, in an ancestor that lived in light, that hunted by sight. But light is a rumor here. The fungal luminescence is not enough to see by, not for an eye evolved for surface conditions, and so the eyes have diminished, and the vibration-sense has expanded, and the spider has become something that experiences the world entirely through touch. It lives in a universe of texture and frequency. It knows the guano floor the way you know your own body — by contact, by pressure, by the specific sensation of surface against skin. It knows the beetles by their footsteps. It knows the bats by the rain of their waste, by the occasional thud of a body that has lost its grip and fallen. It knows you, when you are there, as a seismic event — a series of heavy footfalls that shake the substrate and send it retreating into its crack, waiting for the earthquake to pass.

The spiders reproduce rarely. The population stays stable because the food supply stays stable, because the beetles stay stable, because the bats stay stable. Everything here is calibrated to everything else, and the spider population is calibrated to the beetle population in a ratio so precise that it has the feel of mathematics. Thirty to forty spiders. Several thousand beetles. This is the equation. It has been the equation for a long time.

---

The fungus is alive in a way that is difficult to describe without sounding like you are reaching for metaphor.

It is not reaching for metaphor. It is simply the case that the fungus — the pale, luminescent mycelial network that threads through the guano and climbs the lower walls in veins of cold blue-white light — is doing something that looks, from a certain angle, like thinking. Not thinking the way you think. Not thinking the way anything with a brain thinks. But processing. Responding. Maintaining a network of chemical signals across a body that has no center and no edge, that extends through the entire guano deposit in threads so fine they are invisible individually but so numerous that their aggregate mass exceeds the aggregate mass of every beetle in the gallery.

The fungus eats the guano. It breaks down the organic matter — the chitin, the protein, the mineral residue — and converts it into itself, into more mycelium, into more network. In doing so, it produces light. Not much. Enough. The light is a byproduct of a chemical reaction, an oxidation process that serves no obvious purpose for the fungus itself — it doesn't need light, doesn't use light, doesn't benefit from light. The light simply happens, the way heat simply happens when you burn something, the way sound simply happens when air moves. It is a consequence of being alive in this particular way, in this particular medium, and it has been happening for so long that other things have adapted to it, have incorporated it into their own existence, have made it part of their world.

The beetles use it for navigation, possibly. The spiders may use it to time their hunting — the luminescence pulses faintly, a rhythm that corresponds to chemical fluctuations in the substrate, and these fluctuations may correlate with beetle activity patterns. The bats ignore it. The bats have echolocation and do not need light, do not want light, and the faint glow from below probably does not even register in their perceptual world.

But the moths.

The moths use it.

---

The moths are the mystery. The moths are the reason this place is a gallery and not merely a cave.

They are large. This is the first thing. Fifteen centimeters is not a large moth in absolute terms — there are moths in the world with thirty-centimeter wingspans, moths as big as birds — but fifteen centimeters is large for a cave moth, large for an endemic species, large for something that has evolved in a closed system with a finite food supply. Size implies abundance. Size implies that whatever the moths are eating, there is enough of it to support bodies this big, and that is strange, because the gallery is not rich in the things that moths usually eat. There are no plants. There is no nectar. There is no fruit, no decaying wood, no leaf litter. There is guano, and fungus, and bat-breath moisture, and each other.

They are grey. Uniformly, completely grey — the grey of the granite, the grey of wet stone, the grey of things that have been in the dark for so long that color has become unnecessary, a luxury that the environment does not reward. Their wings are unmarked. No eyespots, no patterns, no striping. This is unusual. Moths use wing patterns for camouflage, for mate selection, for predator deterrence. The absence of pattern suggests that none of these pressures exist here — that there is nothing to hide from, no predator that hunts by sight, no mate-selection process that rewards display. The moths do not need to be beautiful. They need only to be present, and present they are, in numbers that suggest a stable, long-established population.

They are furred. This is what strikes you most. The fur is dense, mammalian, covering not just the thorax but the abdomen, the legs, the bases of the wings. It is not the scaled covering that most moths have — it is true fur, modified setae that have become hair-like, and it serves the same purpose that fur serves on mammals: insulation. The moths are warm. Not warm the way insects are warm from ambient temperature, but warm the way *warm-blooded* things are warm, generating their own heat, maintaining their own internal temperature independent of the air around them. This is not unheard of in insects — some moths can shiver, can pre-warm their flight muscles before takeoff — but the degree of fur, the completeness of the covering, suggests something more than pre-flight warmup. It suggests a metabolism that runs hot. It suggests a moth that has committed to warmth as a strategy, that has bet its evolutionary future on the idea that being warm is better than being cold, even in a cave that is already warm, even in a gallery where the ambient temperature never drops below tepid.

They walk. This is the thing that stays with you. They walk across the ceiling, across the walls, across the bodies of roosting bats, and they do it with a deliberateness that implies purpose. They are going somewhere. They are doing something. Their legs are heavy, strong, articulated for walking rather than perching, and they move with a slow, deliberate gait that is nothing like the erratic fluttering of surface-dwelling moths. They look like they know where they are going. They look like they have been where they are going before.

What they are going to, you think — you are not sure, but you think — is the fungus.

You find them clustered on the lower walls, in the places where the luminescent mycelium is thickest, their proboscises extended against the fungal mat, feeding. They are drinking the fungus. Or eating it. Or absorbing something from it — moisture, nutrient, some chemical compound that the fungus produces as a metabolic byproduct. The fungus grows on the guano. The guano is produced by the bats. The bats are sustained by the alpine meadow outside. The moths are, in a sense, eating the meadow — processed through bats, through beetles, through fungus, through the slow chemistry of decomposition and reconstitution. They are three steps removed from the sun. They are eating sunlight that has been transformed three times, and they have turned that thrice-transformed sunlight into fur and warmth and silence and the specific, peculiar beauty of a grey wing with no markings on it at all.

They are silent. This cannot be overstated. In a gallery where the sound is so total that it becomes a physical thing, a pressure, a tingling on your lips and a ache behind your eyes, the moths make no sound. No wing-beat, no vocalization, no click or rustle or scrape. They move through the loudest space you have ever inhabited and they move through it like ghosts, like the idea of movement without the mechanical reality of it. Their wings are structured differently from other moths — you can see this when one spreads its wings, briefly, in the fungal light — the membranes are thicker, the edges softer, rounded in a way that absorbs rather than displaces air. They have evolved for silence the way other animals evolve for speed or strength or toxicity. Silence is their defense. Silence is their niche. In a world owned by sound, they have claimed the space between sounds, the gaps, the moments when the bats pause for breath.

The bats do not eat them. You watch, and you wait, and the bats do not eat them. A moth walks across a bat's face, and the bat does not flinch. A moth lands on a bat's wing, and the bat does not shake it off. There is a truce here, or a relationship, or a mutual incomprehension that functions as truce — the bats cannot hear the moths, and so the moths do not exist in the bats' acoustic world, and the bats do not waste energy pursuing things that do not exist. This is one theory. Another theory is that the moths taste bad. Another theory is that the moths provide something — grooming, parasite removal, some service that the bats benefit from enough to tolerate the presence of large, furry insects walking across their bodies in the dark. You don't know. The gallery doesn't tell you. The gallery is not interested in explaining itself.

---

And then there is the one who is gone.

The one who made the marks.

Not a species. An individual. A specific person, at a specific time, who entered this gallery and took a stone tool to the wall and scraped, deliberately, in parallel lines, and then left. This person is dead. Has been dead for a very long time — longer than the compressed guano, longer than the current water-channels, longer than the current configuration of the bat colony. This person lived and died and was buried or burned or eaten or simply dissolved into the landscape, and the only evidence that they ever existed is a series of grooves in a granite wall near the entrance to a cave that they probably used as shelter.

But they lived here. Even if only for a night. Even if only for the hours between dusk and dawn, crouched in the entrance of the gallery while the bats streamed out overhead and the moths watched from the walls and the beetles moved in the guano below. They were here. They felt the warmth. They smelled the dense, fungal, earthen smell of the deposit. They heard the roar — and it would have been louder then, if the colony was larger, if the chamber was less worn, if the acoustics were different. They heard the roar and they stayed, and they took out a tool and they marked the wall, and the marking was either important or not important, either functional or meaningless, and there is no way to tell the difference, and the not-knowing is a kind of haunting.

They were not the last. You are here now. But they were the first — the first that you know of, the first who left a trace. They are the ghost of this place, the absent resident, the one who came and went and left only the faintest signature of their passage. The bats have been here longer. The beetles have been here longer. The fungus, the spiders, the moths — all of them have been here longer, in continuous occupation, unbroken presence. But the one who made the marks was *like you*. Bipedal. Tool-using. Capable of looking at a wall and deciding to alter it. Capable of looking at a cave and deciding to stay.

The gallery remembers this person the way it remembers everything — through material, through residue, through the slow patient accumulation of evidence in stone and guano and compressed organic matter. The grooves in the wall have softened over millennia, worn by condensation, filled with mineral deposits, until they are more memory than mark. But they are still there. Still readable. Still the record of a hand that pressed a tool against stone and moved it, once, twice, again, in a pattern that meant something to the hand if not to the stone.

---

Everything here is alive, and everything here is eating everything else, and everything here is warm, and nothing here is in a hurry.

The bats fly forty kilometers in a night to hunt insects in the alpine meadow and they come back to the same centimeter of ceiling. The beetles tunnel through a meter of guano and never leave the floor. The spiders sit in their cracks and wait, and the waiting is not patience, it is simply the absence of impatience, the absence of any concept of time as something that is running out. The fungus grows in threads so fine they are invisible and in networks so vast they exceed the mass of every animal in the gallery combined, and it glows for no reason, and the glowing is beautiful, and beauty is not a purpose but it is a fact. The moths walk across the faces of bats and eat the light-eaters and are silent in a place of roaring, and they are the strangest thing here, the thing that makes the gallery more than a cave, more than a roost, more than an ecosystem — the thing that makes it a *place*, a location with a character, a somewhere rather than a nowhere.

They are all here. They are all alive. They are all going about the business of being themselves, which is the only business any living thing has ever been about, and they are doing it in the dark, in the warm, in the damp, in the sound that is also a silence, in a gallery in a mountain that is also alive, also patient, also going about its own slow business of being stone.

You leave. They stay.
