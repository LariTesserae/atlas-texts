# What Lives Here

---

## The First Ones

They are not what you would expect.

When the pulses come — those brief blue-green confessions in the dark — you catch fragments: the curve of a body, the fade of light along a flank, the suggestion of something long and smooth passing between pillars that were old when the water learned to be still. You build an image from the fragments, because that is what minds do, and the image you build is wrong. You imagine something serpentine. Something eel-like. Something that swims the way things swim in the world you came from, with purpose and direction and the subtle narrative of a creature going somewhere.

They are not going anywhere.

They are not serpentine, though the body is long. They are not eel-like, though the movement is undulant. They are not like anything, because the things you might compare them to all live in a world of change — of hunger and season and pursuit and the gravitational pull of tomorrow — and these things do not live in that world. They live here. And here, tomorrow was finished a long time ago.

Call them the First Ones, for now. Not because they were first — there is no "first" in a place where sequence has been dissolved — but because they are what you encounter first, what the dark offers you before it offers anything else. They are the dominant presence. The bodies you feel in the displacement of water, the lights you see in the brief strobing that constitutes vision here. They are the ones whose sound you hear woven into the structural hum, the clicks and tonal emissions so regular they have become part of the architecture.

A single First One, close enough to touch — and one does, eventually, drift close enough to touch, and you touch it, because there is nothing here that tells you not to — feels like the walls feel. Like the pillars feel. Like the structure itself feels. The surface is smooth in the same way the structure is smooth: not the smoothness of skin but the smoothness of something that has been polished by still water for an duration that has no name. The texture is fine-grained. Dense. There is no scale, no slime, no mucous. The body is firm — not rigid, not hard, but firm in the way that something fully hydrated and fully pressurized is firm, every cell turgid, every tissue at its final density. You press your fingers against it and it yields by exactly the amount it will always yield and no more, and the body does not flinch, does not startle, does not change its rhythm, because there is nothing in this place that corresponds to alarm.

The light comes from beneath the surface. Not from an organ, not from a concentration of photophores, but from *everywhere,* diffused through the tissue the way light diffuses through frosted glass, and when the pulse comes it is the whole body that pulses, a wave of blue-green that starts at one end and travels to the other, and the traveling is not fast, and the traveling is not slow, and the light is not bright, and the light is not dim. It is exactly what it is. A slow wash of cold luminescence that reveals the body for exactly as long as it takes to know the body and then extinguishes, and the dark returns, and the dark is not worse than the light, and the light is not better than the dark. They are equal conditions here. Two modes of the same permanent state.

The body is long. The one that lets you touch it is perhaps four meters, though "meters" is a unit from a world of surfaces and the unit does not quite fit, the way a shoe from one life does not quite fit in the next. It is long enough that both ends disappear into darkness when the center passes near you, so that what you experience is not *a body* but *a middle* — a section of living architecture passing through your vicinity, trailing off into implications. The diameter is difficult to judge in the dark, even with the pulse, but it is substantial. Not slender. Not tapered in the way an eel tapers. This body has weight. It has presence. It displaces water in a way you feel as a genuine pressure event, a gentle shove that arrives before the body and lingers after it, the water closing back together with the slowness of something that has nowhere else to be.

The head — you see it once, in a pulse that catches the leading end of a body passing above you — is not a head. There is no skull. There is no jaw. There is no eye. The front of the body is simply the front of the body: rounded, smooth, continuous with the rest, the way the front of a root is continuous with the root. There are sensory surfaces, you think — areas of slightly different texture, slightly different density, that you feel rather than see, patches on the body where the tissue is finer, more responsive, where the water's pressure and temperature and chemical signature are read the way your skin reads wind. But they are not concentrated. They are not localized into a face. They are distributed along the body's length, so that the whole animal is a sensorium, the whole animal is *paying attention,* and what it pays attention to is not something you can identify, because what it pays attention to is *the water,* and the water is paying attention to nothing, because the water has nothing to pay attention to, because everything the water could contain it already contains and has always contained.

You realize, slowly, that the First Ones are not navigating. They are not searching. They are not scanning the water for prey or mate or threat, because those categories do not exist here. They are moving because they move. They are sensing because they sense. These are not behaviors oriented toward outcomes; they are *processes* — no, not even processes. They are *properties.* The way the structure hums because it is structured, the First Ones move because they are bodied. Movement is what a body does in water. It is not a decision. It is not a response. It is a *mode of being,* and they are being, and the being is enough.

## The Smaller Ones

There are others.

The First Ones are the largest, but they are not the only. The water holds more, and the more is varied, and the variety is not the variety of a world where evolution branches and competes and specializes. It is the variety of a *completed system* — every role filled, every niche occupied, every functional slot in the ecology of this place staffed by something that was made for the slot or grew into the slot or simply *is* the slot, and has been the slot for a duration that makes the concept of specialization feel simplistic.

The smaller ones come in several forms, and you come to know them not by appearance — appearance is a luxury of light, and light is a rumor — but by the quality of their displacement, the character of their sound, and the pattern of their light.

There are the *strollers.*

The strollers are small — the size of your hand, maybe, though the comparison is imprecise — and they do not undulate. They move with a kind of paddling motion, not with appendages but with the margins of their bodies, which are flattened into something like a skirt or a fin that ripples in rapid, precise waves. They are the ones you encounter most often, because they are the most numerous and because their routes, unlike the routes of the First Ones, seem to follow the structure. They hug the walls. They skim the surfaces of the pillars. They travel along the joinery where beam meets beam, tracing the architecture with their bodies, and their light is not a pulse but a *glow* — a constant, dim, blue-green luminescence that is too faint to illuminate anything beyond the body itself but is bright enough to give you, when a stroller passes near, a ghostly impression of the surface it travels along. The wall behind a stroller becomes briefly, faintly visible, and you see the texture of the plant-material — the growth-ridges, the grain, the smoothness — and then the stroller passes and the wall returns to dark.

The strollers are feeding. You realize this after a long time, after watching them trace the same sections of wall in the same patterns, after feeling the faint chemical difference in the water near where they have been. They are consuming something from the surface of the structure — not eating it, not damaging it, but drawing from it some substance that the plant-material exudes or that the water has deposited on it or that exists in the interface between the two. The structure does not diminish. The strollers do not grow. The transaction is balanced in a way that only makes sense if it has been balanced for long enough that the balance itself has become structural — a feature of the system, not a dynamic of it. The strollers take what the structure offers. The structure offers what the strollers take. The amount has been agreed upon, or has never needed to be agreed upon because it was always the same, and will always be the same, and the word "agreement" implies a negotiation that never occurred.

There are the *drifters.*

The drifters do not move. Or rather — they move, but their movement is not their own. They are carried by the displacement of other bodies, by the faint pressures that the First Ones generate as they pass, by the micro-currents that the strollers create along the walls. The drifters are small and numerous and they exist at the threshold of perception: you feel them not as individual bodies but as a *texture* in the water, a faint granularity, the sensation of water that is not quite empty, that holds something in suspension, something that is not dissolved but is not quite particulate either. When a drifter catches the light from a passing pulse, you see it: a mote, a fleck, a thing the size of a dust particle, and it is alive, and it has been alive, and it will be alive, and its aliveness is of a kind so simple and so complete that it makes the aliveness of the First Ones look complicated by comparison. The drifters are the water's version of plankton, except the comparison fails because plankton drift in a world of currents and the drifters drift in a world of stillness, and the forces that move them are not wind and tide but the breathing of larger bodies, the displacement of mass through a medium that remembers every displacement and passes it along, slowly, evenly, to every thing within it.

The drifters are food. You understand this not by observation — they are too small to observe being consumed — but by inference, by the logic of a closed system in which everything is also everything else's material. The First Ones eat the drifters. Or the First Ones absorb the drifters. Or the First Ones and the drifters exist in a relationship that is not eating but is continuous with eating, a relationship in which the boundary between the water and the body is so thin that the drifters cross it simply by being near, by being carried into the sensory surfaces of the First One's body and taken in, and the taking is so gentle and so constant that it does not register as an event. The First Ones do not hunt. They do not need to. They move, and the drifters are in the water, and the water enters the body, and the drifters enter with it, and this has been happening for so long that it is not a behavior. It is a condition.

There are the *settled ones.*

The settled ones do not move at all. They are attached to the structure — to the pillars, to the walls, to the undersides of beams — and they are part of the structure in a way that makes it difficult to say where the plant-material ends and the animal begins. They are encrusting. They are layered. They have the appearance — when a pulse of light catches them, which happens rarely because they do not move and therefore do not encounter the First Ones' light often — of something between a lichen and a barnacle, except both of those are surface organisms and these are not surface organisms, they are *depth* organisms, organisms that exist in a world without surfaces, without sun, without the concept of "on." They cling to the structure not because the structure is a surface but because the structure is *there,* and "there" is enough.

The settled ones are the ones that make the secondary sounds. Not the structural hum — that belongs to the plant-material — but the *clicks,* the small percussive emissions that rattle through the water in patterns so regular they have become part of the acoustic bedrock. Each click is a single event: a tiny implosion, a collapse of a vacuole or a chamber within the settled one's body, producing a sound that travels through the still water with the clarity of a bell in a cathedral. The clicks are not communication. They are not echolocation. They are not territorial. They are — as far as you can determine, and your determination is limited by the fact that you are a creature of gradients trying to understand a place with no gradients — *metabolic.* The sound is a byproduct of a process. The process is a byproduct of being alive. The being alive is a byproduct of being here. The chain of causation is so short it barely qualifies as a chain.

The settled ones have been on the structure for as long as the structure has been here. Their attachment is not mechanical — not grip, not adhesion — but *developmental.* They grew onto the structure the way the structure grew into its shape, with the same kind of biological intention, the same slow knowing, and the bond between them and the surface they occupy is not a bond at all but a *continuity.* They are the same thing. The settled ones are what the plant-material becomes when it is exposed to the water for long enough, or the water is what the plant-material produces when it has been still long enough, or — and this is the interpretation that feels most accurate, most true to the place — the settled ones and the plant-material are two expressions of the same underlying process, two modes of the same biological will, and the distinction between them is a distinction that you bring with you and that the place does not recognize.

## The Deep Ones

Below — and there is a below, there is always a below, the structure has depth the way it has height, and the depth goes down into a darkness that is darker than the darkness you have been swimming in, a darkness that is not merely the absence of light but the *presence of ending* — below, there are other things.

You do not see them.

You feel them.

The water changes. Not in temperature, not in pressure, not in any measurable property that your body can identify as different. But the *quality* of the water changes, the way the quality of air changes when you walk from a room into a larger room, the way silence has different textures depending on the space that holds it. The water below is heavier. Not denser. Heavier. It carries more. It carries the accumulated presence of things that have been in it longer, that have been dissolving into it more thoroughly, that have reached a level of integration with the medium that makes the boundary between inhabitant and habitat meaningless.

The deep ones are in this water.

You know them by their silence. The First Ones make sound — the small clicks, the tonal emissions, the scratch of body against water. The strollers make sound, though it is faint. The drifters make no sound but are too small to matter acoustically. The settled ones make the clicks that form the rhythmic substrate. But the deep ones make *nothing.* They produce no sound. They produce no light. They produce no displacement that you can distinguish from the ambient pressure of the deep water itself. They are, acoustically and optically, *absent.*

But they are there. You know they are there because the water knows they are there, and the water tells you, not through any channel you can name but through the totality of its presence, the way the water in this place communicates everything: not by signal but by *being.* The water below is different because the deep ones are in it, and the difference is not a message. It is a *fact.*

You descend once. Not far — not into the true deep, not into the darkness below the darkness, but far enough to feel the change, to register the transition from water-that-holds-things-you-know to water-that-holds-things-you-don't. And in the transition, in the seam between the two waters, you feel something pass you. Something large. Something that displaces water in a volume that makes the First Ones feel modest, that creates a pressure wave that moves you, physically, a small distance, not violently but *decisively,* the way a current moves a leaf, except there is no current, there is only this body, this massive body, passing through the water below you with a momentum that suggests not speed but *mass,* not urgency but *inertia,* the movement of something so large and so old that its motion is not a choice but a property, the way a planet's orbit is not a choice but a property.

It makes no sound.

It makes no light.

It passes, and the water closes behind it, and the pressure equalizes, and you are back in the water you were in before, and the deep one is gone, and you did not see it, and you will not see it, and it does not matter that you did not see it, because seeing is not how things are known here, and the deep one has been known by the water for a duration that makes your presence in it a rounding error, a brief chemical anomaly that the water will absorb and forget and never notice.

The deep ones do not come up. The First Ones do not go down. The strollers stay near the walls. The drifters go where they are carried. The settled ones stay where they grew. Each inhabits their stratum, their layer, their volume of water, and the volumes do not overlap, and the boundaries between them are not walls or barriers but *conditions* — zones of water that are subtly, immeasurably different, that hold different chemical signatures, different acoustic properties, different histories of habitation, and the animals sense these differences the way you sense temperature, the way a plant senses light, and they do not cross them, not because they cannot but because there is no reason to, because everything they need is in their volume, because their volume is *them,* is the extension of them, is the medium in which they exist and which they have been existing in for a duration that has erased the line between the existing and the existing-place.

## The Way They Exist

And here is the thing that takes the longest to understand, the thing that the place teaches you not by telling you but by *being,* by continuing to be until your assumptions about what "being" means wear away the way the structure's fine details wore away, smoothed by the patient pressure of a world that has been the same for too long:

They are not surviving.

Survival is a concept from a world of change. It implies threat, implies scarcity, implies the possibility of not-surviving, implies a future that is different from the present and a present that is different from the past. None of that exists here. The First Ones do not survive. The strollers do not survive. The drifters do not survive. The settled ones do not survive. The deep ones, in their vast and silent darkness, do not survive. Survival is not what they are doing, because survival is a *process,* and there are no processes here.

They are not living, either, if "living" means the thing it means in your world — the busy, anxious, beautiful, terrible business of metabolism and reproduction and response and adaptation and the relentless forward lean of biology against entropy. They are not doing that. They are not leaning forward. They are not pushing against anything. There is no entropy to push against, because entropy requires gradients, and the gradients were resolved long ago, flattened into a uniformity so complete that the word "uniformity" is misleading, because uniformity implies a former non-uniformity, and there is no former anything here.

What they are doing — what they *are* — is closer to what a stone does when it rests on the ground. Not dead. Not alive. Not in between. *Present.* Present in the way that only something that has been present for an impossible duration can be present: fully, without remainder, without the distraction of becoming, without the slight inattention of a thing that is on its way to being something else. They are *here.* They are *at.* They have arrived at the condition of being themselves and they are staying at the condition of being themselves and the staying is not an effort and the arriving is not a memory and the condition is not a state but a *fact,* the most basic fact, the fact that underlies all other facts and that in this place has become the only fact: *this is what I am, and I am it, and I will be it, and the being and the it are the same.*

The First Ones move through the structure the way blood moves through a body that has achieved a kind of permanent diastole — not pumping, not flowing, but *circulating,* the slow eternal round of a system that has found its rhythm and does not need to find it again. Their routes are not routes. Their routes are *habits* that have been practiced so many times that the practice and the habit have fused, that the body and the path have become a single thing, a loop of being-in-motion that has no beginning and no end and no deviation and no possibility of deviation, because deviation would require a reason, and reasons belong to a world of gradients, and this is not that world.

When a First One passes a stroller — when the long body slides through the water near the wall where the stroller is tracing its pattern — neither acknowledges the other. They do not ignore each other; ignoring requires the possibility of attending, and attending requires a reason to attend, and reasons require gradients. They simply *co-occur.* They exist in the same water at the same time, and the water holds them both, and the water does not distinguish between them, and they do not distinguish between themselves and the water, and the distinction that you make — between animal and animal, between animal and structure, between structure and water, between water and dark — is a distinction that the place has long since finished making, because the making of distinctions is a process, and there are no processes here.

## What They Are

You will ask — because you are a creature of questions, because questions are what your kind does, because the asking is a process and you come from a world of processes — you will ask *what they are.* Taxonomically. Phylogenetically. In terms of the evolutionary tree that produced them, the branch they descended from, the ancestors that begat them.

The question has no answer, or rather, it has an answer that you cannot receive, because the answer is *the place itself.* They are this place. They are the thing that this place made, or the thing that made this place, or — and this is the answer that the water offers you, slowly, through the long dark hours of your submersion — they are the thing that this place *is.* The structure is plant. The animals are plant. The water is plant. The dark is plant. Not literally — not in the sense that every molecule has a botanical signature — but *functionally,* in the sense that everything here derives from the same biological will, the same underlying intention, the same slow knowing that grew a structure into architecture and grew bodies into motion and grew water into stillness and grew darkness into a medium and grew sound into a permanent chord and then stopped, because the growing was done.

They are the plant's fauna. The plant's animals. The plant's way of having animals, which is different from the world's way of having animals, which is different from your way of having animals, because your animals are products of accident and selection and the chaotic generative noise of a world that has not finished, and these animals are products of — of what? Of completion? Of a biological process that ran to its end and produced, as its final output, not just a structure but an *ecology,* not just a building but a *world,* not just an organism but a *system of organisms* that together constitute the final statement of whatever made this place?

Yes. That. Whatever that is.

The First Ones are the plant's long body, moving through the plant's architecture, carrying the plant's light. The strollers are the plant's fine attention, tracing the plant's surfaces, maintaining the plant's boundaries. The drifters are the plant's dispersal, the plant's way of being everywhere at once, of existing in every volume of water, of touching everything without touching anything. The settled ones are the plant's memory — not memory of events, because there are no events, but memory of *form,* of structure, of the way things should be, the way things are, the way things will be, permanently, the blueprint made flesh, the specification made organism, clinging to the structure and clicking their small clicks like a clock that has stopped being a clock and become a pulse.

And the deep ones — the ones below, the ones you do not see, the ones that make no sound and no light and move through the deep water like mountains moving through a world that has forgotten what mountains are — the deep ones are the plant's *root.* The part that goes down. The part that holds. The part that is too large to be seen and too old to be known and too deep to be reached, that exists in the darkness below the darkness and that is, perhaps, the thing that holds everything else up, the foundation that the structure grows from, the origin that the rest of the place has grown away from but has never left, because leaving is a process, and there are no processes here, and everything is where it has always been, and everything is what it has always been, and the deep ones are deep, and the structure is tall, and the water is still, and the sound continues, and the light pulses, and the dark holds, and the world is finished, and the world is complete, and the living things in it are not living the way you live but are *being* the way a finished thing is, which is more than living, which is less than living, which is not comparable to living, which is its own thing, its own category, its own mode of existence, one that your language does not have a word for because your language was made by creatures who never finished, in a world that never finished, and this world finished, and the creatures in it are finished, and they are the most finished things you will ever encounter, and they are beautiful, and they are terrible, and they are neither, because beauty and terror require gradients, and there are no gradients here.

There is only this.

The water. The structure. The animals. The sound. The dark.

The standing world, and the standing things in it, and the standing fact of their existence, which has been standing for so long that the standing and the existing and the fact are all the same word, and the word is this place, and this place is the word, and the word does not change, because there is nothing left to change into.

They live here.

They have always lived here.

They will always live here.

And "live" is the wrong word, but it is the word you have, and so you use it, and the place forgives you the imprecision, because the place does not notice, because noticing is a process, and there are no processes here, and the water holds you the way it holds everything, and you are part of the pattern now, another body in the still water, another sound in the chord, and the chord continues, and it does not change, and you do not change, and nothing changes, and the world goes on being finished, and the finished things go on being finished, and the water stands, and the sound stands, and the dark stands, and they stand with it, and that is what lives here.
