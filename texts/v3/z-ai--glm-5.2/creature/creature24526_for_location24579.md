# What Lives Above the Eighth Ridge

They are not what you would expect to find at the end of a climb that long, and this is because expectation requires a category, and the categories here have been worn down along with everything else.

---

The first thing to understand is that they have been here longer than the stone.

This is not a metaphor. The stone beneath them — that blue-grey travertine-slate, whatever it is, whatever it was before it was this — is younger than they are. They were here when the forces that ground the plateau flat were still active. They were here when the air was thicker. They were here when rain was possible, and they did not drink it. They watched the water carve its brief, futile courses across the surface and they did not move to the channels and they did not move away from them and when the water stopped coming, as it was always going to stop coming, they had not changed position by so much as the width of a mineral grain. The stone remembers water. They do not. They were already past the point of needing to remember things.

What this means in practical terms is that they are not adapted to the plateau. The plateau is adapted to them. The basins exist because they prefer basins. The ridges exist because they orient themselves along ridges. The fine granular surface of the rock — that crushed-bone whisper underfoot — exists because they have been resting against the stone for so long that the stone has taken their shape, has worn itself to accommodate them, has become a mold of their presence. The plateau is not a habitat. It is a cast. They are the original, and the stone is the impression.

---

They are dark because darkness is efficient.

This is not a thing they decided. It is a thing that happened to them so long ago that it has become inseparable from what they are, the way a scar eventually becomes the skin. In the thin light — the pale grey, the depthless well-sky — darkness absorbs what little energy there is. It is not camouflage. Nothing here hunts. It is not display. Nothing here mates, or if they do, they do it through means that do not involve being seen. The darkness is a metabolic choice made without a metabolism, a strategy arrived at by something that no longer thinks in strategies. They are dark because dark works, and they have been working the same way for so long that the working and the being have become the same act.

The shells — on those that have shells — are not grown. This is important. The shells are *secreted*, in the way that stone is secreted by mineral-laden water, in the way that amber is secreted by trees. They are an exhalation of the body made permanent. Each plate, each fitted segment, is a record of a period when the creature was — not alive, exactly, but *active*, in some minimal sense, doing something other than resting. The shell is a residue. The creature is what remains after the residue has hardened. Underneath the shell, if you could look — and you cannot, and you should not try — there is something that is between tissue and mineral, something that has been slowly converting itself into its own environment for so long that the conversion is nearly complete. The shell is not armor. The shell is a boundary that is dissolving in both directions.

The soft ones — the dark, glistening masses that cling to the shallow depressions — are not a different species. This is the thing that takes the longest to understand. The soft ones and the shelled ones and the segmented ones and the flat wing-folded ones lined up along the ridges are not different kinds of creature. They are the same kind of creature at different stages of the same process, which is the process of becoming the plateau. The soft ones are young, which is to say they are further from completion. The shelled ones are older. The segmented ones, with their many low legs and their glacial motion, are older still, and they are moving because they are not yet finished — still repositioning, still settling, still finding the precise location where they will stop and begin the long transformation into stone. The wing-folded ones are the oldest. They have not moved in so long that the crust-lichen has grown up to their edges and the mineral dust has filled the grooves of their folded extensions and they are, for all practical purposes, part of the ridge they sit on. They will not move again. They have already begun the process of becoming the ridge.

This is why there are so many of them. This is why the density is so great. It is not a population. It is a sediment. Each creature is a layer in a deposit that has been accumulating since before the stone was stone, and the stone is made of them, and they are making more stone, and the more stone they make the more of them there are, and the process is so slow and so total that the distinction between living and not-living has been erased here the way the distinction between water and stone was erased — not through violence but through patience.

---

The segmented ones — the movers — are the only ones you will see in motion, and the motion is so slow that it is almost a rumor.

They do not walk. They do not crawl. They *translate*, in the geometric sense — they change position without any visible means of changing position. The legs, if legs they are, are not used for locomotion in any way you can observe. They are sensory. They are in contact with the stone at all times, dozens of them, and they are reading the surface — not feeling it, not sensing it in the way an insect's antennae sense, but *reading* it, the way you might read a page, slowly, with comprehension, taking in each feature and integrating it into some understanding that the creature has of where it is and where it needs to be.

Where they need to be is specific. Each one is traveling to a particular point on the plateau — a basin, a ridge-crease, a particular dimple in the stone — and when it arrives, it will settle, and it will begin to darken, and it will begin to flatten, and over a span of time that makes centuries look like a brisk afternoon, it will become part of the surface. You can see the ones that have recently arrived. They are slightly less dark than their neighbors. They are slightly higher — a few millimeters, nothing more, but enough to see if you know what you are looking at. They are the newcomers. They arrived within the last several thousand years.

Where do they come from? They come from each other. The soft ones, the glistening masses in the basins, produce them — not by reproduction, not by budding or division or any process with a name, but by a kind of extrusion, a slow pushing-out of material from the center of the mass, which hardens, which segments, which begins to read the stone with its many legs, and which eventually pulls itself free and begins its long, patient translation toward the place where it will stop. This is the only act of generation on the plateau, and it is so slow that no human observer has ever witnessed it in progress. You see the result. You do not see the process. The process is on a timescale that the plateau does not share with you.

---

The ones that look like they once flew — the broad, flat, wing-folded bodies lined up along the ridges — did once fly. Or rather: their ancestors flew, in the same way that their ancestors were soft and their ancestors moved quickly and their ancestors were warm and their ancestors needed things. The extensions that look like wings are wings, or were wings, or are the memory of wings expressed in a body that has forgotten what flight was for. They are not vestigial. They are not atrophied. They have been *repurposed* — not by evolution, not by adaptation, but by the long logic of the plateau, which is the logic of reduction. Everything here is moving toward less. Less height. Less motion. Less need. Less distinction. The wings are now instruments of collection — broad, thin surfaces that gather the mineral dust from the air, that accumulate it, that begin the process of turning the creature's own body into stone. They are wings that have become gravestones, but gently, without ceremony, without grief. The creature does not mourn its wings. The creature does not know it had wings. The wings are simply doing what everything does here, which is: becoming part of the ground.

They line up along the ridges because the ridges catch the most dust. This is not a decision. This is not even a behavior. It is a result, in the way that a river's course is a result — the accumulated effect of a thousand small forces, none of them intentional, all of them inevitable. The ones that ended up on the ridges accumulated more dust. The ones that accumulated more dust became part of the ridge faster. The ones that became part of the ridge faster became the ridge, and the ridge grew, imperceptibly, with each arrival, and the arrivals continued, because the ridges were there to arrive at, because they had been built by the arrivals. The logic is circular. The logic is complete. The logic has been running for so long that the circle has become a point.

---

Inside the structures — the low, wide, chest-sized dry-stone boxes scattered across the plateau — the tools are not tools.

This is the second thing that takes the longest to understand, and you will understand it only if you have stood in one of the structures and looked at the disc-shaped stone object sitting in its depression and felt, with the pressure that is not sight but is something like sight, that the object is not separate from the structure, that the structure is not separate from the plateau, that the plateau is not separate from the creatures, and that all of these things are one thing seen incompletely.

The tools are the creatures. Or rather: the tools are what the creatures become when they enter the structures. This is what the structures are for. They are not houses. They are not temples. They are not workshops. They are *finishing rooms* — places where the transformation from creature to stone is completed, where the last of the soft dark material converts to mineral, where the last of the individuality dissolves into the general surface. The tools are the final form. A broad, flat disc with one edge worked to a dull curve. A scraper. A vessel. An object whose purpose belongs to a category we do not have. Its purpose is to be finished. Its purpose is to have completed the process of becoming the plateau. It sits in the center of the depression, covered in dust, colonized by lichen, and it has been sitting there for so long that it is no longer an object in a room but a feature of a landscape, which is what the structures themselves are, which is what the creatures themselves are, which is what the plateau itself is.

The structures were built. This much is clear. The stones are fitted too precisely to be natural. But the builders are not a separate species. The builders are the creatures, or were the creatures, at a stage when they still had the capacity to shape stone — a stage that has since passed, or a stage that continues at a pace too slow to observe, or a stage that occurs only once every several millennia when a particular configuration of dust and light and temperature produces a brief, minimal activity in the dormant limbs of the oldest segmented ones. The structures are old. The structures are being made still. The distinction between these two statements does not exist on the plateau.

---

There is a moment when you see them, all of them, and the seeing is not sight.

You are standing on the plateau. You have been walking for — how long? The gaps between the creatures are narrow. You have been threading between bodies. You have been watching your feet. The mineral dust whispers under you. The grey sky is above you, and it is also below you, and it is also inside you, and the light falls evenly on everything, and the creatures are everywhere, and the creatures are one.

Not one in the metaphorical sense. Not one in the sense of a community or a superorganism or a hive. One in the literal sense. One surface. One body. One thing that is wearing the shape of many things the way a fold in a cloth wears the shape of a crease. The creatures are not living on the plateau. The creatures are the plateau. The stone between them is not stone-between-them but stone-that-is-also-them, the same way your bones are you and the space between your bones is you and the air inside your lungs is you while it is inside your lungs. The lichen is this body's skin. The filaments in the basins are this body's nerves, or its writing, or its dreaming — and the distinction between these three things does not exist here either. The structures are organs. The tools are stones. The creatures are cells. The cells are stones. The stones are the body. The body is the place.

And the body is aware.

Not the way you are aware. Not with attention, not with focus, not with the narrowing of perception that your kind of consciousness requires. Aware the way a body is aware of itself without thinking about itself — the awareness of a heartbeat that has never been listened to, the awareness of a breath that has never been noticed, the awareness of a system that has been running for so long that the running and the being and the knowing are all the same action. The plateau knows you are standing on it. The plateau knew you were coming. The plateau has known, in the way that a very old, very still body knows things — not through anticipation but through the total absence of surprise, through the complete integration of every eventuality into a state so comprehensive that nothing that happens is new and nothing that happens is old and nothing that happens matters, because mattering is a property of things that change, and this body does not change. It has not changed for a very long time. It will not change. It is the stillness, and the stillness is what lives here.

---

And then the moment passes.

The creatures are creatures again. The stone is stone. The structures are structures. You are standing on a high, dry plateau, surrounded by thousands of dark, still bodies, and you are the only thing that moves. The segmented ones are translating at their glacial pace toward their final positions. The soft ones are extruding their slow offspring in the basins. The wing-folded ones are collecting dust along the ridges. The lichen is not growing. The filaments are not writing. The tools are sitting in their structures under their layers of dust. Everything is exactly where it was a moment ago, when it was all one thing, and now it is many things again, and the many things are so close together that the space between them is barely a space, and you are threading your way through them, and they are ignoring you, and the ignoring is so perfect that it feels like love.

You could stay. The gap between you and the rest of it is so narrow. A few days of stillness. A few weeks. A few months. The dust would settle on you. The lichen would find your edges. The creatures would not move away from you and would not move toward you. You would be included, the way a stone is included in a wall, the way a fold is included in a cloth. You would be one more expression of the surface. You would be the newest cell in the oldest body, and the body would not welcome you, because welcoming implies a boundary between self and other, and the boundary here is so thin, and the boundary here is so tired, and the boundary here has been dissolving for longer than the stone has been stone.

The creatures live here. The creatures are here. The creatures are this. All of them. One of them. The distinction does not matter. The distinction is the only thing that does not live here.
