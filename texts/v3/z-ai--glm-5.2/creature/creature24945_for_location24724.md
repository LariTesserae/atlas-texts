# What Lives in the Hanging Cirque

---

The first thing to understand is that nobody *lives* here, if by living you mean staying. The cirque is not a home in any settled sense. It is a place that is visited, used, passed through, returned to. Even the mountain is not finished with it. Even the stone is still deciding what shape it wants to hold. So what lives here lives provisionally, conditionally, the way a tenant lives in a building that is still under construction. Everything here has arrived recently. Everything here is still arriving.

---

## The Pikas

Start with them, because they are the closest thing the cirque has to residents, and because everything else orbits their presence.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Perhaps forty. It is difficult to count because they move in that staccato burst-and-freeze pattern that makes each individual seem like three, and because their color — a warm grey-brown that shifts toward ochre in certain lights — is so close to the color of the wet scree they live in that you can look directly at a pika and not see it until it moves. They are small. A closed fist. A large apple. Their ears are rounded and short, nearly invisible against the fur of their heads, which gives them a look of startled completeness, as though they were designed all at once by someone who did not want to add anything unnecessary.

They do not hibernate. This is the thing that defines them, that defines their entire relationship to the cirque. They stay here through the winter — through the howling, trackless, sun-obliterated winter at four thousand meters — and they survive by gathering plants during the short summer, drying them in the sun, and storing them in caches under the talus. You see these caches everywhere once you learn to look. A neat pile of cuttings, each stem snipped cleanly at an angle, stacked under an overhanging rock where the rain cannot reach. The cuttings are mostly cushion plant — silvery-green, still faintly aromatic when you crush them — but there are also saxifrage stems and the tough, fibrous leaves of the woolly composite, and sometimes a few strands of lichen, though it is unclear whether the lichen is gathered deliberately or simply comes attached to the other material.

Each pika maintains several caches. Not one. Several. This is not greed. It is logistics. A single cache can be discovered by a stoat or buried by a rockfall or spoiled by meltwater. To depend on one would be to gamble everything on a single throw. So they spread their bets across the talus, and in doing so they thread the cirque with their trails — narrow, well-worn paths through the scree that connect feeding areas to drying areas to cache sites to the deeper crevices where they actually sleep. These trails are the cirque's infrastructure. Its roads. Its plumbing. The pikas are not aware of this. They are simply going about their business. But their business has shaped the place.

They are territorial, but their territories are small and densely packed, and the boundaries between them seem to be maintained more by habit than by aggression. You do not see pikas fighting. You see them approaching each other, pausing, assessing, and then one of them — usually the smaller — veering away. There may be vocalizations at frequencies you cannot hear. There may be scent-marking. But what you observe is a kind of practiced, mutual avoidance that allows thirty or forty animals to share three hundred meters of scree without evident conflict.

They do not call. This is strange. Pikas in other places — lower, warmer places — are vocal animals. They give sharp, barky alarm calls. They chirp. They announce themselves. Here, they are silent. The energy cost of vocalizing in cold, thin air may be too high. Or perhaps silence is itself a strategy: to call is to advertise your location to stoats, to raptors, to anything that might be listening. In a place where every calorie is a negotiation, where the margin between survival and death is measured in a few extra grams of dried plant matter, silence is not a choice. It is a condition of existence.

What you hear instead, if you listen carefully, is the sound of their movement. The scrabble of small claws on loose rock. The faint, dry rustle of a hay-stem being dragged across scree. Once, standing near a talus pile, you hear a sound like a very small cough — a single, clipped exhalation — and then nothing. A pika emerges from a crack, looks at you with an expression that is difficult to read because their faces are round and featureless and their eyes are black and their whiskers are always twitching, and then it is gone, and the silence closes behind it like water.

They are not afraid of you, exactly. They are wary in a way that seems operational rather than emotional — the wariness of a machine that has been programmed to avoid certain stimuli. If you sit still for long enough — fifteen minutes, twenty — they resume their activity around you, and you become part of the landscape, a warm boulder that occasionally shifts. They will approach to within a meter. You can see the detail of their fur, which is denser and longer than you expected, and the way their feet are furred underneath, so that they leave no visible tracks on frost. You can see their eyes, which are not black but very dark brown, with a wet, bright quality that catches the light like the veins of dark glassy mineral in the headwall.

They are the cirque's heartbeat. Its pulse. When they are still, the place feels empty. When they move, it comes alive.

## The Stoat

There is one stoat. Or perhaps there are two — it is difficult to know, because you never see more than one at a time, and because in winter pelage they are nearly identical: white from nose to tail-tip, with only a black tip on the tail that serves, theoretically, as a distraction for predators, though what predators a stoat has at this altitude is unclear. The raptors, perhaps. Or nothing. The stoat may be the apex predator of the cirque, which is a strange thing to say about an animal that weighs less than two hundred grams.

You see it — or you believe you see it — three times over the course of two days, and each time it is doing the same thing: flowing. There is no other word. The stoat does not run. It does not walk. It *flows* over and through the talus with a fluidity that seems to mock the very concept of legs, and indeed you cannot see its legs when it moves — they are lost in the motion, in the long, sinuous, boneless undulation of its body as it slides between rocks and vanishes into cracks that seem too narrow for anything to fit through. It is white and it is fast and it is gone.

The pikas know it. They know it the way prey animals know their predators — not through thought, but through a deep, inherited attentiveness that lives in the body. When the stoat is abroad, the pikas do not move. They go still inside their talus piles, in the dark, and they wait. The waiting can last a long time. Twenty minutes. Half an hour. You begin to wonder whether they have somehow left, whether there are exits you have not seen, whether the talus is connected by tunnels to somewhere else. Then one pika emerges, cautiously, and then another, and the economy resumes.

The stoat must eat. It eats pikas. This is not a guess — you find the evidence in the form of a pika skull, picked clean, tucked into a crevice near the spring-seep. The skull is small and light and the teeth are still in it, orange-yellow at the tips, and it looks like a small, perfect artifact. The stoat has cached it, perhaps, or perhaps it is simply a leavings. There are fur tufts nearby — grey-brown, matted with moisture — that confirm the rest.

But the stoat cannot eat enough pikas to threaten the colony. The mathematics will not allow it. A stoat needs perhaps a quarter of its body weight in food per day. The pikas are breeding — there are young ones, smaller than the adults, with softer fur and less practiced movements — and their numbers are climbing. The stoat is a tax. A cost of doing business in a place where business is already marginal. The pikas pay it and continue.

What the stoat does, more than anything, is *change the atmosphere*. When it is present — when the pikas have gone still and the trails are empty and the only sound is the weeping of the spring — the cirque feels different. It feels watched. It feels like a place where something is happening that you cannot see, that is occurring just below the surface of the visible world. A predator does this to a landscape. It introduces tension. It makes the silence feel earned rather than empty.

The stoat is not malicious. It is not cruel. It is hungry, and it is good at being hungry, and it has found a niche — the narrow, specific niche of moving through talus and killing pikas — and it fills that niche with an efficiency that is almost elegant. You respect it. You do not love it. But you recognize that the cirque would be a lesser place without it — less alive, less alert, less itself.

## The Raptors

They do not live in the cirque. They live above it, or beyond it, or in a relationship to it that is too large and too mobile to be called residence. But they are here, and their presence is part of what makes the place what it is.

There are two of them. A pair. They are large — you estimate wingspans of nearly two meters, though distance and the distortion of bright air make certainty difficult — and they are dark, a uniform near-black that resolves into very dark brown when the light catches them at certain angles. Their wings are broad and their tails are short and their flight is a model of economy. They do not flap. They tilt. They ride the updraft that the headwall generates — the same updraft that carries the fog-banks away — and they circle in a slow, overlapping pattern that takes them out over the ridge and back, out and back, with a patience that looks like laziness but is actually calculation.

They are not always hunting. They are often simply *present* — riding the thermals, watching, existing in a relationship with the air that the ground-bound organisms cannot share. When they do hunt, the tempo changes. One of them — the smaller, presumably the female — tilts sharply and drops, not in a stoop but in a controlled, angled descent that brings her low over the talus. She is looking for movement. For the flicker of a pika between rocks. For anything.

She does not strike while you are watching. She pulls up, circles once more, and returns to altitude. The pikas have gone still, of course. They felt her shadow. They felt the change in the air. They are in their cracks and under their rocks, and she is flying over an apparently empty talus, and the math does not work, and she moves on.

They roost somewhere. Not in the cirque — there is no suitable ledge, no protected shelf. Somewhere on the peak itself, or on the far side of the ridge. They arrive in the cirque's airspace in the morning, when the first thermals begin to form, and they depart in the late afternoon, when the air cools and the updraft dies. Their presence is conditional, dependent on weather and light and the invisible architecture of the wind. But while they are present, they are the cirque's ceiling. Its sky. The thing that makes the pikas look up.

## The Flies

There is a patch of rock on the south-facing flank of a boulder near the cirque's center where the sun heats the stone to just above freezing — perhaps one degree, perhaps two — and on this patch of rock, on warm days, there are flies.

They are dark. Almost black. About the size of a fruit fly, but more robust, with thicker bodies and shorter wings. They do not buzz. They do not swarm. They sit on the warm rock and occasionally walk, with a slow, deliberate gait, from one spot to another. When they fly, it is a short, labored flight — a meter, perhaps two — and then they land and sit again. They are not strong fliers. They do not need to be. Their world is a few square meters of warm stone.

There are not many of them. A dozen, perhaps, on the best days. Fewer when it is cloudy. None at all when the fog rolls in. They are the most fragile things in the cirque, the most contingent. A change in the angle of the sun, a shift in the boulder's position, a rockfall that covers their warm patch — any of these could end them. They exist on a margin so thin that it is almost theoretical.

And yet they persist. They must be breeding somewhere — in the cracks of the warm boulder, perhaps, or in the damp soil beneath it. Their larvae must be developing in some protected micro-site that you have not found. They are completing a life cycle — egg, larva, pupa, adult — in a place where the temperature is below freezing for most of the year, and they are doing it on a rock. It is an achievement so modest and so unlikely that it feels almost embarrassing to call it an achievement. It is simply what they do. They are flies. They live on a rock. The rock is warm. That is enough.

The pikas do not eat them. The raptors do not notice them. The stoat has no interest in them. They occupy a space in the cirque's ecosystem that is entirely, hermetically their own — a few square meters of warmth in a cold place — and they are, perhaps, the thing most truly *native* to the cirque, because they have nowhere else to go. The pikas could, theoretically, move down. The stoat could hunt elsewhere. The raptors range across the entire mountain. But the flies live on this rock, and if this rock ceases to be warm, they will die, and there is no other rock. They are committed to this place in a way that nothing else here is.

You think about this more than you expect to. You think about it when the fog rolls in and the temperature drops and the flies disappear into their cracks and the warm patch of rock goes cold and blue. You wonder whether they are still alive in there, in the dark, in the cold, waiting for the sun to come back. They are. They always are.

## The Cushion Plants

They are not animals, but they are alive, and they live here more permanently than anything else, and so they must be described.

The cushion plants are the cirque's foundation. Its first claim on life. They are the thing that moved in before anything else — before the pikas, before the lichens even — and established that this bare, shattered, freezing ground could support something green. They are not dramatic about this. They do not grow upward. They grow *outward*, spreading in dense, hemispherical domes that hug the ground so tightly that the wind cannot get under them and the frost cannot penetrate them. Each dome is a single plant — a single individual, with a single root system — and some of them are decades old, though they are only a few centimeters tall and less than a handspan across.

They are silvery-green. The color of sage, almost, but paler, with a faintly metallic sheen that is the result of tiny hairs on the leaves — hairs that trap a layer of still air next to the plant's surface, insulating it from the cold and reducing water loss. On a sunny day, the upper surface of a cushion plant is warm. Noticeably, measurably warm. Ten, fifteen degrees warmer than the air around it. The plant is creating its own microclimate. It is building a small greenhouse out of hair and light and patience.

Inside the dome, the structure is dense and layered — a tangled matrix of stems and leaves, so tightly packed that it is nearly solid, with only the growing tips exposed at the surface. Dead material accumulates underneath, decaying slowly in the trapped warmth, forming a primitive soil that holds moisture and nutrients. Other plants germinate in this soil. The saxifrage. The woolly composite. The cushion plant is not just a plant. It is a *substrate*. A habitat. A home.

There are hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. They cover the ground between the talus piles in a patchy, uneven carpet — dense in the sheltered areas, sparse on the exposed ridges, absent entirely from the bare scree. They are still expanding. You can see the leading edge of the carpet, where young cushions are establishing themselves on bare gravel, their roots just beginning to probe the frost-churned soil. The process is slow — centimeters per decade — but it is visible, and it is ongoing, and it is the closest thing the cirque has to a story.

## The Saxifrage

Between the cushions, in the spaces they have not yet filled, the saxifrage grows. It is a creeping plant — prostrate stems rooting at every node, sending up tiny rosettes of leaves no larger than a fingernail — and it is covered, in the brief growing season, with flowers so small they seem like a joke about flowers. White. Star-shaped. Five petals. A yellow center. You have to kneel down to see them properly, and when you do, you find that they are perfect — not degraded, not stunted, not the diminished version of a lowland flower, but a complete, fully realized saxifrage that happens to be very small and happens to live at four thousand meters.

They open early. Before the frost melts. You find them in the morning with rime on their petals, the ice crystals forming a delicate, temporary frame around the white flowers, and the flowers are already open, as though they opened in the dark and have been waiting for the sun. Perhaps they have. Perhaps the opening is triggered by temperature rather than light, and the cushion plant's internal warmth is enough. Whatever the mechanism, they are ready. They do not waste time.

The flies visit them. This is the cirque's pollination economy, and it is tiny — a few square meters of flowers, a dozen flies, a few warm hours between fog-banks. But it works. The saxifrage sets seed. The seed disperses — by wind, probably, or by the mechanical action of frost-heaving the soil — and new plants appear in new places, and the carpet thickens, and the cirque becomes, incrementally, more alive.

## The Lichens

They are the oldest things here. Not the oldest in years — some of the cushion plants may be older than individual lichen colonies — but the oldest in evolutionary terms. Lichens were among the first organisms to colonize bare rock. They have been doing this for hundreds of millions of years. They are doing it here.

They cover every stable rock surface in the cirque. Not the loose scree, which shifts too frequently, but the fixed boulders, the headwall's lower reaches, the flat slabs near the spring. They grow in maps — irregular, branching, variegated maps that follow the micro-topography of the stone, filling cracks and spreading across smooth surfaces with a patience that makes geological time look hasty.

There are several kinds. A crustose lichen, flat and tightly adhered, in shades of grey and pale orange. A foliose lichen, leaf-like and slightly raised, in a color that you can only describe as *stone* — a composite of grey and brown and green that matches the rock so closely it is invisible until it isn't. And the turquoise one. The one that stops you.

It is a crustose lichen, or it appears to be — flat, tightly bonded to the rock, growing in small, irregular patches near the spring-seep where the rock is damp. Its color is extraordinary. Not blue. Not green. A vivid, saturated turquoise that looks artificial in this context, that looks as though someone has spilled paint on the stone. You do not expect to see this color in nature, and certainly not at four thousand meters on a bare granite face. But there it is. It is alive. It is growing, slowly, in its own patch of damp rock, and it is the most beautiful thing in the cirque, and nobody has ever described it, and it does not care.

Lichens are not one organism. They are two — a fungus and an alga, living in symbiosis, the fungus providing structure and protection, the alga providing food through photosynthesis. The turquoise lichen is this partnership, expressed as color. The pigment belongs to the algal partner, probably, though you cannot be sure without a microscope. What you can be sure of is that the partnership works. The lichen is here. It is alive. It is growing on bare stone, extracting nutrients from rainwater and dust and the slow dissolution of the rock itself, and it is doing this in a place where the temperature is below freezing for most of the year, and it is turquoise, and it does not need to be turquoise, and it is anyway.

## The Woolly Composite

There is one clump of it. One. In the sheltered lee of a boulder near the cirque's center, where the wind is broken and the sun heats the stone and the cushion plants have created enough soil to support something with a stem. It is six inches tall. It has woolly leaves — densely furred, grey-green, the hairs serving the same insulating function as the cushion plant's hairs but on a plant that has decided, against all logic, to grow *upward*. It has small yellow flowerheads — composite, like a dandelion, a cluster of tiny florets grouped into a single disc. They are open. They are facing the sun.

It is the tallest plant in the cirque. The only plant that grows vertically, that refuses the horizontal strategy that every other organism here has adopted. It stands up. It exposes itself. It takes the wind.

It is impossibly brave. You keep coming back to this word — *brave* — even though you know that plants do not have courage, that the decision to grow upward is a genetic one, that the woolly composite is simply doing what its genome tells it to do. But it looks brave. It looks like a flag. It looks like something that has decided to stand up in a place where standing up is the hardest thing you can do, and it has done it anyway, and it is alive, and it is flowering, and the flies have found it, and the seeds will set, and next year there may be two.

Or there may not. The boulder could shift. The wind could snap the stem. A late frost could kill the flowers before they set seed. The woolly composite is betting everything on a single boulder's lee, a single patch of soil, a single season's weather. It is the riskiest organism in the cirque. It is the one most likely to fail.

But it is here. It is six inches tall and it is yellow and it is growing, and that is enough.

## The Humans

They do not live here. But they came here, and they came here repeatedly, and they left marks, and the marks have stories.

Start with the stone ring. It is near the spring-seep — close enough to access water, far enough to avoid the damp ground that the seep creates. It is a low ring, perhaps two meters in diameter, built of local granite stones the size of a man's head. The stones are unworked. They are simply placed, selected for shape and weight, fitted together with the practiced ease of someone who has built many such rings. The ring is a windbreak. It is the foundation of a shelter — a tent, a hide, something with a skin stretched over it — and it faces away from the prevailing wind, which comes from the southwest, funneled through the defile.

The ring is not weathered. This is important. The stones are still sharp-edged, still bright with the mineral freshness of freshly broken granite. They have not been exposed to many freeze-thaw cycles. The ring is recent — decades, perhaps, not centuries. Someone was here within living memory. Or within the memory of the mountain, which is not the same thing.

Near the ring, the lean-to. A flat slab of granite, propped against a boulder at an angle that creates a sheltered space perhaps a meter high and two meters deep. The slab has a sooty film on its underside. Someone built fires here. Small fires — there is no ash, no charcoal, no heap of burned stones. Smoke-stains only. The fires were small enough to heat the space without consuming much fuel, and fuel at this altitude is scarce. What did they burn? Cushion plants, possibly, dried. Or animal dung. Or something they carried with them. The soot suggests repeated use — not a single night, but many nights, over many seasons. Someone came back to this lean-to again and again.

Who? The cirque offers no direct answer. But the scratches on the boulder near the spring — the sharpening grooves, smooth-sided, consistent with the honing of a stone point — suggest a particular kind of person. Someone who worked stone. Someone who hunted, or prepared to hunt. The line of stones marking the path to the cushion-plant patch suggests someone who harvested — who came for the plants, perhaps for medicine, perhaps for food, perhaps for fiber. The cairn on the ridge suggests someone who needed to mark a route, who came and went by a path that was not otherwise obvious.

This was not a home. It was a camp. A hunting camp, maybe. A place where someone — or a small group, a family, a band — came seasonally, in the brief summer, to take what the cirque offered. Pikas, perhaps. Plants. Stone for tools. The cirque was a resource, and they knew it, and they used it, and they left almost no trace.

Almost. The ring is still there. The lean-to still stands. The sharpening grooves are still smooth. Someone was here, and they knew this place the way the pikas know it — not as a landscape, not as a view, but as a set of specific, useful, named locations. The spring. The boulder. The path. The place where the cushion plants grow thickest. They had a mental map of the cirque as detailed and as practical as any modern survey, and they carried it in their memory, and they came back to it, and then, at some point, they stopped coming back, and the ring began to weather, and the plants began to reclaim the ground, and the cirque went back to being what it was before they came.

The humans are gone. The cirque does not miss them. But their marks are still legible, still readable in the stone, and they add a layer to the place that is not geological and not ecological but something else — historical, perhaps, or simply human. The layer is thin. It is the thinnest layer in the cirque. But it is there.

## The Spring

It is not alive. But it is the thing that makes life possible here, and so it must be described as though it were.

It emerges from a crack in the headwall's eastern flank, roughly fifteen meters above the cirque floor. The crack is narrow — a few centimeters wide, a few tens of centimeters long — and the water does not flow from it so much as *seep*. It appears on the rock face as a darkening, a dampness, that broadens as it descends. The water moves slowly, drawn by gravity and capillary action, following the irregularities of the rock surface. In some places it forms a thin film. In others it gathers into tiny rivulets that descend a few centimeters and then disappear, absorbed by the porous stone or evaporated by the wind. By the time it reaches the scree floor, most of it is gone. What remains soaks into the gravel and vanishes.

But it is there. It is constant. It weeps from the rock every day, every night, in all weather. In the morning, when the sun first hits the headwall, the seep brightens — the water is driven out of the stone by the warming, and the dark stripe on the rock widens and darkens. In the afternoon, it slows. In the evening, it nearly stops. But it never ceases entirely. There is water in this mountain, held in fractures and fissures deep in the granite, and it is making its way to the surface through this crack, and it has been doing so for longer than the cirque has existed.

The spring is what makes the cushion plants possible. It is what makes the lichens possible — the turquoise lichen grows only near the seep, only where the rock is damp. It is what makes the soil damp, what makes the frost rime possible, what makes the air feel like being pressed into a lake. It is the cirque's secret. Its hidden engine. Everything alive here is alive, directly or indirectly, because water is coming out of a crack in the rock.

The humans knew this. That is why the stone ring is there. That is why the sharpening grooves are on the boulder near the seep. The spring is the reason for the camp, the reason for the harvest, the reason for the human layer in the cirque's thin history. Water at four thousand meters is not common. Water that is reliable, that flows year-round, that is accessible and clean and sufficient — that is precious. The humans came for the water as much as for the plants or the pikas. They came because the spring made the cirque a place where a person could survive, briefly, with effort, if they knew what they were doing.

## The Mountain

It is alive too, in the way that mountains are alive — not biologically, not consciously, but actively. It is still calving. Still shedding. The defile through which you entered is narrowing, grain by grain, as freeze-thaw cycles break fragments from the walls and drop them on the floor. The headwall is still raw, still bright, still showing the striations of the glacier that carved it. The cornices form and collapse and reform. The stone weeps and freezes and weeps again.

The mountain made the cirque. It did not intend to. The glacier did the work — grinding and plucking and carving, over thousands of years, until the bowl was formed and the ice retreated and the bare rock was exposed. But the mountain maintains it. It provides the walls that trap the cold. It provides the crack from which the spring seeps. It provides the scree in which the pikas hide and the soil that the plants are slowly, patiently creating. It is the context for everything. The medium. The condition.

And it is changing. The freeze-thaw cycle is accelerating, perhaps — the climate is warming, and the mountain is responding. More rockfall. More scree. The defile is narrowing. The headwall is retreating, crumbling, losing height. In a hundred years, or a thousand, the cirque may be unrecognizable — filled with scree, or overgrown, or exposed to the wind as the walls erode. The life here is contingent on the mountain's continued cooperation, and the mountain is not cooperating out of goodwill. It is simply following the laws of physics, and the laws of physics, in this case, involve the slow destruction of the cirque.

But not yet. Not now. Now the cirque is here, and it is full, and it is alive, and the mountain is still holding its shape, and the spring is still seeping, and the pikas are still gathering their hay, and the stoat is still flowing through the talus, and the raptors are still circling overhead, and the flies are still sitting on their warm rock, and the cushion plants are still spreading, and the lichens are still growing, and the woolly composite is still standing up, six inches tall and impossibly yellow, and the spring is still weeping its slow, dark stripe down the headwall, and the light continues, and continues, and does not warm.

Everything here is alive. Everything here is temporary. These are not contradictions.
