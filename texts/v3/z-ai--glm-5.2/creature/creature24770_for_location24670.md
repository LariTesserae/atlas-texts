# Inhabitants

---

The first thing you need to understand is that nothing here knows it is small.

The chamber is the size of a walk-in closet, yes. But nothing in it has ever been anywhere else, and so the chamber is not a closet to them. It is not a room. It is not a cartridge or a node or a component. It is the world — the whole world, the only world, the total world — and its boundaries are the boundaries of existence. The inflow pipe is a feature of cosmology. The grating is a geological fact. The convection current is weather, is season, is the breath of god, is whatever the equivalent of those things becomes when you have no language and no concept of elsewhere.

Everything here is endemic. Not one species in this chamber exists anywhere outside it. They cannot leave — the inflow gap is too narrow for the worms, too fast for the crustacean-analogs, and the plants are rooted into the very walls. The outflow drops into a downstream chamber that might as well be outer space. And so they have evolved here, in this brass volume, in this specific warmth, in this specific light, in this specific rhythm, for so long that they are not adapted to the chamber so much as *made of* it. Their biology is a map of the room's conditions. Their bodies are arguments for the shape of the walls.

## The Verdigris Worms (so called for lack of anything better)

There are forty-one of them. You can count because they are territorial and their territories are fixed, and each territory corresponds to a section of the inflow pipe or the wall immediately adjacent to it, and the boundaries between territories are marked by subtle scrapes in the oxidation layer that they maintain with compulsive regularity.

They are not worms. The word is a concession to the limits of taxonomy. They are vermiform, yes — elongated, segmented, flexible — but their ancestry is impossible to trace. They may be descended from something that was once a polychaete. They may be descended from something that was once a larval insect. They may be something that began as a parasite on something else that no longer exists and outlived its host by adapting to fill every ecological role the host left empty. There is no way to know. The ocean outside has turned over its biota dozens of times since the chamber was sealed, and the fossil record of the original submersion event is buried under kilometers of sediment. These creatures have no relatives. They are a phylum of one, a family of one, a genus of one, a species that is also its own order and class and kingdom, if you are being honest about the distance between them and everything else alive.

Forty-one individuals. The population has been stable at approximately this number for a very long time. Not because they regulate it — they have no concept of population management — but because the chamber's energy budget supports approximately this many of them, and they have been culled to that number by starvation and territorial violence so consistently and for so many generations that their life cycle has *shaped itself around the number.* A female (they are sequential hermaphrodites, but let's use the word for convenience) will produce a clutch of eight to twelve eggs in her lifetime. Of those, statistically, two will reach maturity. The rest die as larvae, consumed by the filtering organisms on the walls or by their own siblings or by the simple metabolic failure of being born into a room that has exactly enough food for forty-one adults and not one more. The mathematics is not conscious. The mathematics is *structural.* It is built into their clutch size, their growth rate, their aggression thresholds. They are a species sculpted by the carrying capacity of a brass box.

They are the length of your forearm. This is a useful size — large enough to defend a territory against conspecifics, small enough to maneuver in the chamber's tight spaces, and precisely calibrated to the diameter of the inflow pipe's cracks, where they wedge their armored posterior segments to hold position against the pressurized flow. The chitin plates that armor them are layered like roof shingles, each one slightly overlapping the one behind it, and they are the color of verdigris not by coincidence but because the copper in the alloy walls has been bioaccumulating in their exoskeletons for so long that their plates are now structurally reinforced with copper salts. They are, in a literal sense, made of the room. The room is in them. They are the room's attempt to look at itself.

The clicking. You need to understand the clicking.

It is not communication. Or rather — it is, but not in the way you might assume. It is not language. There is no information transfer. The clicking is *display* — purely, exhaustively, relentlessly display. Each worm clicks its anterior plates against the inflow pipe at a rate and rhythm specific to that individual, and the sound serves two purposes: it announces occupancy of a territory (to rivals, to potential mates, to nothing in particular) and it establishes a kind of sonic boundary that other worms can locate and avoid crossing. The clicking is a fence. The clicking is a wall. The clicking is the way forty-one animals divide a closet-sized world into forty-one nations without ever coming to blows, because the boundaries were settled so long ago that the settling itself has become genetic. They are born knowing where their territory is. They are born knowing what their click sounds like. They are born knowing the clicks of their neighbors and where those neighbors' territories end.

When you entered the chamber, the clicking intensified. This was not alarm, exactly. It was *recognition.* You are large. You displaced water. You changed the convection pattern. The worms registered the change and clicked harder — reasserting their territories against a new variable, insisting on their own existence in the face of something unfamiliar. The protocol is simple: click louder until the new thing either leaves or becomes part of the background. You became part of the background. They have seen many things enter this chamber over the eons — debris from the inflow, dead organisms from upstream, the occasional mineral incrustation that breaks loose and tumbles in. They have a protocol for indifference, and you have been processed by it. You are now a feature of the room. You are now part of the weather.

They do not eat what you would recognize as food. Their diet is the bacterial mat that coats the inflow pipe — the same bioluminescent bacteria that light the chamber, or a close relative of them — and they graze this mat in slow, methodical passes along their territory, clicking between bites. The grazing is visible as a pattern: each worm's territory has a grazed section (recently eaten, matte, dark) and a recovering section (regrowing, glossy, faintly luminous). The worms manage their bacterial gardens the way a farmer manages a field — not consciously, not with foresight, but through the accumulated wisdom of behavioral algorithms refined over millions of years of eating the same thing in the same place. They rotate. They rest sections. They never strip a territory bare. If you watched long enough — months, years — you would see the grazed and recovering sections move in a slow rotation around each territory, a pattern so stately and so regular that it looks like agriculture and might as well be.

Their eyes are gone. Not reduced — *gone.* Where their ancestors had photosensitive spots, there is now a smooth plate of chitin, unbroken, opaque. They have not needed eyes for longer than they have been worms. The light in the chamber is bacterial and dim and carries no information they can use. They navigate by vibration, by chemical gradient, by the pressure of the water against their plates. They feel the pump-pulse through their whole bodies and they know where they are in the room the way you know where you are in your house with the lights off — by the accumulated map of sensation, by the texture of the familiar.

One of them — the one nearest the inflow gap, the largest, the oldest — has a territory that has been continuously occupied for longer than the species has existed in its current form. Not by the same individual. Individuals die. But the territory is always filled, always by a descendant of the previous occupant, always by the largest larva of the largest clutch, and the transition is seamless — the old worm weakens, the young worm grows, and there is a period of weeks or months when they share the territory without conflict, the younger one gradually taking over the grazing rotation, the older one gradually spending more time motionless, clinging to the pipe, clicking weaker and weaker until one day the clicking in that territory changes and the old rhythm is replaced by a new one and the old worm is gone. Consumed, probably. By the bacteria, by the smaller organisms, by the chamber itself. Nothing is wasted here. Nothing leaves.

## The Drifters

The crustacean-analogs — and again, the hyphen is a concession — are rice-grain sized and there are hundreds of them and they do not have territories because they do not have anything. They exist in the water column. They ride the convection. They are the closest thing in the chamber to a mindless presence, and yet they are not mindless. They simply have a mind you do not recognize.

They are translucent. You can see their digestive tracts through their carapaces — a thin dark line, often full of the particulate matter that they filter from the water column with feathery appendages that beat constantly, rhythmically, in time with the pump-pulse. They eat what the water brings: dead bacteria, fragments of worm chitin, spores from the ancient woody growth, dissolved organic compounds that enter through the inflow from upstream chambers where their own processes are happening. They are the chamber's cleanup crew. They are the chamber's circulatory system. They are the reason the water stays clear enough to see through.

Each one carries its bioluminescent bacteria on its dorsal carapace — a patch, roughly oval, that glows blue-white. The bacteria are not passengers. They are symbionts. They receive nutrients from the crustacean-analogs' metabolic byproducts and they provide, in return, light — but the light is not *for* the crustacean-analogs. The crustacean-analogs cannot see. The light is for the bacteria themselves, or rather for the bacteria's own metabolic purposes, which involve an electron transport chain that has evolved to take advantage of the faint electromagnetic field permeating the chamber walls. The bacteria glow brighter when the field peaks — when the pump-pulse reaches its apex — because their metabolism accelerates in the presence of the field. The light is a byproduct. The light is waste. The light is the most beautiful thing in the chamber and it is literally excretion.

But the aggregate effect. The aggregate effect is that the water glows. Hundreds of points of blue-white light, each one the size of a grain of rice, drifting in slow spiraling convection, brightening and dimming in unison with the pump-pulse, and the light reflects off the brass walls and off the chitin plates of the worms and off the dark surfaces of the vegetation, and the chamber becomes a kind of lantern, a kind of throat, a kind of lung, breathing light in and out in a cycle that has nothing to do with day or night or season or any rhythm the original engineers intended. It has to do with the pump. The pump is the sun. The pump is the tide. The pump is the heartbeat of this world, and everything in this world has arranged itself around it.

The drifters reproduce constantly. Their lifespan is short — days, perhaps, or hours. It is difficult to tell without marking individuals, and marking individuals this small in this environment is impossible. But there are always hundreds, and the number does not fluctuate, and so they must be reproducing at a rate that exactly matches their death rate, and this rate has been stable for so long that it has become a property of the chamber itself. You could measure the chamber's health by counting the drifters. You could diagnose the waterworks by the glow. If the pumps ever stopped — if the pulse ever ceased — the drifters would die within hours, and the light would go out, and the worms would lose their food source, and the plants would lose their convection, and the chamber would become a dead brass box falling slowly apart in the dark. Everything here is betting everything on the pump. Everything here has been betting everything on the pump for longer than the pump has had a name.

The drifters do not know this. The drifters do not know anything. They are a cloud of light in warm water, eating and dying and eating and dying, and they have been doing this for so long that the doing is what they are. They are not *alive* in the way that you are alive. They are *ongoing.* They are a process that happens to be made of animals.

## The Mat

The vegetation — and this word is even more of a concession than the others — is the thing you notice last and remember longest, which is appropriate, because it is the thing that was here first and will be here after everything else.

It began as terrestrial flowering plants. This is almost impossible to believe, looking at it now. There is nothing about the dark, velvety, matte-surfaced growth that covers every wall and surface that suggests a daisy or an oak or a blade of grass. But the cellular structure — when you look closely, very closely, at the way the cell walls are arranged — retains the ghost of angiosperm architecture. The xylem and phloem are still there, still organized in the ancestral pattern, but they have been repurposed so many times, across so many millions of years of adaptation to an environment that no terrestrial plant was ever meant to inhabit, that the original design is less a blueprint than a fossil. A memory of a memory of a time when these organisms grew in air and drank rain and turned their faces toward the sun.

There is no sun. There has been no sun for longer than the concept of sun has been meaningful to these organisms. They do not photosynthesize. They have not photosynthesized for millions of years. The chloroplasts in their cells are vestigial — present, still recognizable in structure, but functionless, the way an appendix is present in your body. They serve no purpose. They are relics. If you could somehow communicate with these plants, they would not know what light was for. They would not know what green meant.

They are green anyway. The green is not chlorophyll. The green is a pigment that serves a structural purpose — it stiffens the cell walls, it provides rigidity against the water flow, it is incorporated into the cellulose matrix as a cross-linking agent — and it happens to be green because the precursor compound that these plants synthesize from the sulfur compounds in the water happens to be green and the evolutionary path from precursor to pigment was shorter than any alternative. They are green by coincidence. They are green the way a fire is orange. Not because of what they are but because of what they are made of.

The mat is thick. Centimeters thick, in places. It covers the walls in a layer so dense that the brass underneath is not visible and may not, at this point, be structurally relevant. The plants' root systems — and again, *roots* is a word that does violence to the reality — have penetrated every joint, every seam, every rivet hole in the chamber's walls. They have colonized the space between the plates. They have, over deep time, become the structural reinforcement that holds the chamber together. Where the original rivets have corroded to nothing — and they have, they are gone, dissolved by the mineral-laden water over millions of years — the plant fibers that grew through the rivet holes have taken over the load. The chamber is held together by the plants. If you killed the plants, the walls would buckle. The chamber would deform. The inflow gap would change shape, the pressure regime would shift, the convection would fail, and the entire ecosystem — the worms, the drifters, the bacteria, everything — would collapse.

The plants are the room. The room is the plants. The distinction between infrastructure and biology has not existed here for a very long time.

And then there are the pipe-plants.

The vertical columns. The ones that rise from the floor grating toward the ceiling like organ pipes, swaying in the convection current. They are the most architecturally ambitious thing in the chamber — thick, fluid-filled, hydrostatic columns that stand a half-meter or more in height, tapering slightly toward the top, warm to the touch. They pulse. You feel this before you see it — a faint pressure change in the water around them, synchronized with the pump-throb, a dilation and contraction of the column's diameter that moves from base to apex like a peristaltic wave.

They have integrated with the infrastructure's rhythm. This is the only way to describe what has happened. The pump-pulse creates a pressure wave in the water column inside the chamber. This pressure wave is transmitted through the plants' hydrostatic skeletons — through the fluid-filled columns — and the plants have, over evolutionary time, developed structures that *respond* to this pressure wave. Valve-like internal partitions that open and close in sequence with the pulse, moving fluid through the column, circulating nutrients from base to apex and back again. The plants are using the pump's energy. They have built their circulatory system around someone else's heartbeat. They are, in some impossible sense, part of the machine now — extensions of the pumping infrastructure, biological components that the machine did not ask for and does not know it has.

You watch them sway. The convection pushes them gently, and they bend, and they return, and they pulse, and they have been doing this for so long that the metal grating they grow from has been worn into smooth grooves where their bases rub against the iron. They have shaped the floor. They have shaped the floor the way a river shapes stone.

## The Old One

The woody trunk in the corner. The one furthest from the inflow.

It is the oldest living thing in the chamber. This is not a guess. You can tell because everything else in the chamber has grown *around* it — the mat has thinned where it approaches the trunk, as if giving way, and the pipe-plants do not grow within a body-length of it, as if their roots have encountered something underground that discourages them. The trunk occupies a zone of its own. It has a territory, like the worms, but its territory is maintained not by clicking but by the simple fact of its chemical presence. Its roots extend under the entire chamber floor. Every other plant in the room is, in some sense, growing in its soil.

It is dark. Darker than the mat, darker than the pipe-plants — a dense, almost black wood that has been accumulating layers for so long that the outer bark has the texture of stone. It is scarred. Long gouges in the surface, some of them healed over with callus tissue that has itself scarred and healed, a record of impacts and abrasions that spans geological time. A piece of debris from the inflow hit it once, hard enough to leave a mark four centimeters deep. The mark is still there. The plant grew around it. The plant is still growing around it. The plant is still growing.

It has a single orifice near its apex. This is the strangest thing about it, and the strangeness does not diminish with observation. The orifice is not a flower. It is not a mouth. It is not a wound. It is a structure that has no analogue anywhere — a circular opening, three centimeters in diameter, ringed with a lip of tough, flexible tissue that is usually closed but that opens, slowly, rarely, to release a cloud of spores so fine they are barely visible. The spores drift in the convection. They settle on the walls. Some of them germinate. Most do not. The ones that germinate become new patches of mat, new sheets of vegetation that spread across the brass and copper and iron and begin the slow work of reinforcement and colonization that is, for these plants, the entire purpose of existence.

The Old One is the source. It is the origin point. Every patch of vegetation in the chamber — every mat, every root system, every pipe-plant — is descended from spores released by this trunk. It has been reproducing for longer than the worms have existed. It has been growing for longer than the convection current has flowed in its current pattern. It may predate the current configuration of the waterworks. It may have been here before the chamber was submerged, before the flood, before the world outside became what it is now. It is possible — and this is speculation, but informed speculation — that the Old One began as a terrestrial plant growing in a maintenance access shaft, a shoot that pushed up through a crack in the floor of a corridor that engineers walked through, and that when the water came, when the system was submerged, it did not die. It adapted. It kept growing. It became what it is now.

It looks like a monument. It looks like it is waiting. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply existing, the way a mountain exists — present, durable, indifferent to the scale of time that the shorter-lived organisms around it experience. The worms live for years. The drifters live for days. The Old One has lived for epochs. It has no sense of this. It has no sense of anything. But if the chamber has a center, a heart, a point from which everything else radiates — it is this trunk, in this corner, in the quietest part of the room, releasing its spores into the dark.

## The Things You Cannot See

There are smaller things. There are always smaller things.

The bacterial mat on the inflow pipe — the food source for the worms — is itself a community. Dozens of strains, hundreds of strains, layered on top of each other in a structure that has its own verticality, its own ecology, its own food web. The top layer is the bioluminescent species, the one that glows in time with the pump-pulse. Beneath that is a layer of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria that derive energy from the compounds dissolved in the inflow water. Beneath *that* is a layer of organisms that feed on the dead cells of the layers above — decomposers, recyclers, the undertakers of the microbial world. And beneath *that*, at the very bottom, in direct contact with the brass, is a layer of bacteria that are slowly, patiently, over centuries, dissolving the metal itself. They are eating the walls. They are eating the pipe. They are the reason the inflow gap exists — not because the gap was designed, but because the brass corroded and the bacteria were there, and the plants held the structure together when the metal failed, and the worms came to graze the bacteria, and the drifters came to eat what the worms left, and the entire chamber settled into its current configuration because of a process of decay that became a process of life.

There are things in the grating. Between the iron bars, in the spaces where the water drains, there are organisms that live in the interface between the chamber and the downstream system — small, flat, clinging things that hold on to the grating with hooked appendages and feed on whatever the current washes toward them. They are the chamber's border population. They exist in the threshold. They are the closest thing in the chamber to a species that knows there is an elsewhere, because they can feel the downstream pull, the drop, the flow into the unknown. They cling harder when the pump-pulse peaks. They are the only things in the chamber that orient against the outflow. Everything else goes with the current. These things resist.

And there are things in the root systems. In the spaces between the plant fibers and the brass, in the narrow channels that the roots have created as they penetrated the walls, there are organisms that exist in a world that is neither the chamber nor the wall but the boundary between them — a biological interstitial, a region of moisture and warmth and dissolved nutrients that has its own micro-ecology. These organisms have never seen the light of the drifters. They live in darkness so complete that they have not evolved any light-sensing mechanism at all. They navigate by chemical gradient, by touch, by the slow chemical signals that the plant roots themselves transmit. They are the chamber's unconscious. They are the part of the room that the room does not know about.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

If you were small enough — if you were the size of a grain of rice, if you could ride the convection current and see what the drifters see, if you could exist for a day in the body of one of the crustacean-analogs — what would the chamber be?

It would be warm. That is the first thing. A uniform, total warmth, coming from everywhere and nowhere, the way warmth comes from being held. The water is your medium. You are never not in water. The concept of *not being in water* does not exist for you. Water is what existence is made of. Water is the default state of reality.

The light would be your light. Your own body, glowing. The glow is not bright — it is a dim blue-white, enough to see a few body-lengths in any direction, enough to register the dark shapes of the pipe-plants and the armored bodies of the worms and the matte surfaces of the mat. The light pulses. You pulse. The world pulses. The pulse is the fundamental rhythm. You do not know that the pulse comes from a pump. You do not know what a pump is. You know that the world breathes, and that you breathe with it, and that when the world breathes you glow brighter and when it exhales you dim, and this is not a choice. This is not a response. This is what you *are.*

You would feel the vibration. Through the water, through your carapace, through the feathery appendages that filter your food. The pump-pulse is a low, slow throb that you feel in every part of your body simultaneously. It is not sound. It is not pressure. It is something in between — a physical fact so large and so constant that it is indistinguishable from existence itself. If the pulse stopped, you would know something had changed. You would not know what. You would not know why. But the absence of the pulse would be the most significant event in the history of your species, and you would experience it as a kind of vertigo, a kind of falling, a kind of silence so total that it would be, for you, indistinguishable from death.

The clicking of the worms would be your weather. You would hear it — feel it — as a constant, textural presence, like rain on a roof, like wind in trees, like the sound of a world going about its business. You would not know that the clicking is territorial. You would not know that the worms are individuals. You would know only that the room is full of sound and the sound has patterns and the patterns are stable and the stability is comforting. The clicking is the room talking to itself. You are part of what it is talking about.

You would drift. You would eat. You would glow. You would, at some point, die — quietly, without ceremony, without awareness — and your body would drift in the convection until it settled on a surface and was consumed by the organisms that consume such things, and your bioluminescent patch would dim and go dark and your nutrients would re-enter the chamber's cycle and your matter would become part of a worm's chitin plate or a plant's root fiber or the woody trunk of the Old One in the corner. You would not know this. You would not fear it. You would not experience death as anything other than the end of drifting, which is to say the end of everything, which is to say nothing, because for you there is no "after." There is only the drift.

And the room would continue. The pumps would pump. The water would circulate. The worms would click. The Old One would, eventually, open its orifice and release its spores. The light would breathe. The chamber would be what it has been for longer than anything in it can remember: a small room, full of life, held together by roots, lit by waste, shaped by a pulse that comes from nowhere and goes nowhere and has no purpose and no end.

The valve garden. It is the size of a closet. It contains a world.
