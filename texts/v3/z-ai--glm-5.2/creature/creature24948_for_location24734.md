# The Inhabitants

---

You would not call them people at first. You might not call them people at all.

The word requires a certain density of association — language, memory, sociality, the accumulation of shared reference that makes a creature more than the sum of its survival. You don't know if they have these things. You know they have stone tools and knot patterns and a reason to build platforms off the wet ground. You know they observe. You know they move. What you don't know is whether the thing that watches you from behind a column two hundred meters distant is a self in the way you understand selfhood, or whether it is something more like a tendril — reaching, sensitive, responsive, but without interior.

You will learn. Or you will learn enough.

---

They are called — you will never learn what they call themselves, so you will name them, and the name will be wrong — they are called the Marginals. Not because they are peripheral to the Rootcathedral. Because they live in the margins. Because the margin is the entire condition of their existence, the place where one growth form has not yet finished and another has not yet begun, the strip of unfinished ground between what is built and what is unmade. They live in the turbulence the way a surfer lives in the wave: never in still water, never in the breaking crest, always on the face where the energy is.

They are not rare the way an endangered species is rare. They are rare the way a particular temperature is rare in a system that is always changing — present wherever the conditions are right, absent wherever they aren't, and the conditions are specific and narrow and constantly shifting. A column falls. The debris is raw, exposed, full of sap and uncolonized surface. For a period — days, maybe a week — the fallen mass is a resource: the wood can be cut, the sap collected, the inner fiber stripped and dried. The Marginals find these sites. They work them. They build near them, not because they need shelter from weather but because they need a fixed position while they process material, and a fixed position requires a platform, and a platform requires uprights, and uprights must be lashed, and lashing takes time, and time is the one thing the Rootcathedral does not give them. Because the growth is coming. The grey-green tendrils are already emerging from the debris. The decomposer mat is already sliding toward the fallen mass. The window is open and it is closing.

So they work fast. Not frantic — you will see this, later, when one of them allows you to be close — but with a fluency that suggests the pace of the growth is a rhythm they were born knowing. They cut with the stone blades in short, precise strokes, angling the edge along the grain of the root-wood, following the fiber the way a butcher follows the seam between muscles. They strip bark in long continuous ribbons, coiling it as they go, and the coils are uniform — not because they are measuring, but because their hands know the length that a coil of this thickness can hold without splitting. They lash with the knot pattern you saw, and the pattern is not decorative. It is a tension knot, self-cinching, that tightens as the wood swells with moisture. They built it for this place. Or this place built it in them.

When the growth reaches the structure — when the first tendrils find the uprights, when the decomposer mat laps at the base of the stilts — they leave. They take the stone tools. They take whatever fiber they have processed and not yet used. They leave the wood. The wood is no longer theirs. The wood belongs to the growth now, and the growth will take it back, and the structure will become a mound and then a hill and then a surface indistinguishable from the surrounding mat, and in a month there will be no evidence that anyone was here except the stone, and the stone will sink into the mat and the mat will close over it, and the Rootcathedral will keep its secret.

They move in small numbers. You never see more than two together, and two is uncommon. Most often, one. A single Marginal, moving through the sparse zone between column clusters, carrying a stone blade in one hand and a bundle of stripped fiber in the other, walking with a gait that is not a human gait. The stride is long and the pace is unhurried, but the foot placement is deliberate — each step tests the ground, presses the root-mat, checks for give. They are reading the floor. The mat's swelling and subsiding tells them where the growth is active beneath the surface, and they route around it, the way you would route around a flooded street. They can feel it through their feet. The pressure changes, the temperature gradients in the fiber, the slight vibration of tissue expanding — they feel all of this, and what you experience as a vague sponginess under your boots, they experience as a map.

---

Their bodies are wrong, if you are expecting humans.

Not wrong in the way of deformity. Wrong in the way of adaptation — the way a cave fish is wrong, if you are expecting a surface fish. They are built for this place the way the root columns are built for this place: by the long pressure of specific conditions, repeated until the form that fits becomes the form that is.

They are narrow. Narrow-shouldered, narrow-hipped, narrow-limbed, as if the first fissure — the crack in the granite that you squeezed through to enter — is a crack they pass through regularly, and the cost of not fitting is not coming back. Their bodies are compressed, lateral, built to slide through gaps that would defeat a human frame. You see this when one of them moves between two root columns that have grown close together: she — you say *she*, though you have no basis for the pronoun, and you will later suspect that the binary is meaningless here — she turns her shoulders sideways and passes through a gap no wider than your skull, and she does not slow down, and she does not turn her head, and you understand that she has done this since before she could walk.

They are shorter than you. Not dramatically — maybe thirty centimeters — but enough that the proportions read differently. The limbs are long relative to the torso. The hands are large, very large, the fingers long and thin and articulated with an extra joint that you can see when one of them grips a root column to brace for a cutting stroke. The extra joint lets the hand wrap further around the curved surface, distributing grip across more contact points. The fingertips are dark — not pigmented, but stained, stained by the sap of the root-wood, which contains whatever compound drives the bioluminescence, and over years of handling, the compound has infiltrated the skin. The stains glow. Faintly. In the dim phases of the canopy pulse, you can see the glow: a pale blue-white at the fingertips, fading to nothing at the wrist. They leave prints. Not the footprints you found — those were boot-prints, if they wear boots, or wrapped feet — but handprints, on the surfaces they grip, glowing faintly, fading over hours as the compound breaks down in the air.

Their faces are the hardest part.

Not because they are frightening. Because they are *specific* in a way that your categories can't hold. The eyes are large — larger than human eyes, set wider apart, and the pupils are not round. They are vertical, slit-shaped, wide in the dim phases and narrowed to near-invisibility when the canopy surges bright. They can regulate. They can look into the canopy-light without flinching, and they can see — you infer this from their behavior — in light levels that would be total darkness to you. The dim phase of the canopy pulse, when the blue-white drops to something barely brighter than moonlight, is not dark to them. It is merely quiet. You realize, watching one of them work during a dim phase, that the canopy's brightening is not for them. They don't need it. The light is for the growth. The Marginals live in the light's absence as comfortably as its presence.

The nose is flat, wide, barely prominent. They don't breathe through it primarily — or they do, but the breathing is secondary, a slow steady rhythm that seems unrelated to exertion, as if the air intake is for something other than oxygen. You notice, when one of them is close — ten meters, eight, close enough to hear the clicking — that the mouth is slightly open at rest, and the lower lip trembles with a rapid, fine vibration, and the clicking is coming from the throat, from the mouth, from the air passing over something in the oral cavity that resonates. They are echolocating. The clicking is not language. The clicking is a sense.

The sound goes out. It bounces off the columns, off the canopy, off the mat, off the water surfaces, off your body. It comes back, and they read it, and they know the shape of the space around them with a precision that your eyes — adapted for sunlight, for distance, for the broad spectrum — cannot match. In the dense groves, where the columns are close and the light is broken into a shifting grid and vision is confused by shadow and glow, they navigate by sound. They click. They hear. They move. The clicking never stops entirely. Even in sleep — you will see one sleeping, later, curled on a platform in a dim phase, the body folded tight, the hands tucked beneath the chin — even in sleep, the clicking continues, slow, irregular, the way breathing continues. They are always mapping. The space is always changing. The map must always be redrawn.

Their skin is pale. Not the pale of illness or albinism. The pale of tissue that has never seen the sun. It has a quality you can only describe as *translucent* — not transparent, not see-through, but lit from within by the faint residual glow of the bioluminescent compound that has worked its way into every surface they touch, every fiber they handle, every pool they drink from. The compound is in the water. It is in the sap. It is in the air, in the condensation that deposits on every surface, including skin. They are saturated with it. Their skin glows, very faintly, in the dim phases — not enough to illuminate, not enough to cast light, but enough that in the darkest moments of the canopy's rhythm, a sleeping Marginal on a platform is a faint outline, a sketch in blue-white light, and you can see the shape of them the way you can see the shape of a cloud by the starlight it blocks.

They are hairless. Or nearly — there is a fine, colorless filament along the forearms and the jawline that catches the moisture in the air and beads with condensation, and you realize, watching a Marginal wipe her forearm across her face, that this is not hair. It is a grooming tool. The filaments collect water. The water is moved. The face is kept wet. The clicking requires moist tissue in the throat, and the air is cold and the moisture deposits constantly, and the filaments are a way of gathering it and bringing it to where it is needed. They are adapted to the dampness the way a desert animal is adapted to dryness: not by resisting it, but by incorporating it.

---

They do not speak.

This is not correct. They communicate. But they do not speak in the way you understand speaking — with words, with syntax, with the linear encoding of meaning into sound. What they do is harder to describe, and you will spend a long time watching before you understand even the outline of it.

The clicking carries information. Not just spatial information — not just *here is a column, here is a wall, here is a moving thing*. The clicking varies. The rhythm varies, the frequency varies, the duration of each click and the interval between clicks varies, and the variation is not random. When two Marginals are near each other — you witness this once, two of them meeting at a freshly fallen column, both arriving to work the same debris — their clicking changes. It synchronizes, briefly, and then it diverges, and the divergence has a pattern. One clicks high, the other low. One clicks in pairs, the other in triplets. The exchange goes on for several minutes. Neither makes any gesture that you can read as body language. They do not face each other. They stand side by side, both facing the fallen column, and they click, and the clicking is the conversation.

You think it is negotiation. The fallen column is a resource. Two have arrived. The resource is not large enough for two. They are deciding — or establishing, or remembering — who works it. The exchange ends. One stays. One leaves. The one who leaves does so immediately, without hesitation, without any visible signal. The one who stays begins cutting. You don't know what was decided or how. You don't know if there is a rule, or a hierarchy, or a memory of past encounters that informs the outcome. You don't know if they are individuals with preferences or members of a system with protocols. You don't know, and you may never know, and the not-knowing is not a failure of observation. It is a boundary. Their interiority — if they have interiority — is not shaped like yours. The clicking carries it, and the clicking is not for you.

What you do understand: the clicking encodes the space. When a Marginal clicks, the space answers, and the answer is different every time, because the space is different every time. A column that was there yesterday is fallen today. A mat that was flat is swollen. A pool that was still is disturbed. The clicking reads these changes, and the changes are the Marginals' news, their weather, their time. They do not have clocks. They have the growth. The growth is their clock, and the clock is always running, and they are always reading it, and the reading is the clicking, and the clicking is the living.

---

They eat the root-wood.

This should not surprise you, but it does, because the root-wood is not food in any sense you recognize. It is fibrous, wet, cold, nearly devoid of anything you would call nutrient. But they eat it. You watch — at distance, at patience, after hours of sitting still in a clearing until the Marginal on the platform forgets you, or decides you are not a threat, or simply stops caring — you watch one of them cut a panel from a fresh column, the way you found the tool marks on your first day. The panel is rectangular, the size of a book page. She holds it in both hands — the large, long-fingered, glowing-tipped hands — and she does something to it with her mouth. The clicking stops. The lower jaw extends — you did not know it could do that, and the extension is not large, a centimeter, maybe two, but it changes the shape of the face entirely, and the mouth opens, and the teeth are visible for the first time.

The teeth are flat. Not molars, not incisors — a continuous edge of something that looks more like horn than enamel, a flat scraping surface that runs the full width of the jaw, top and bottom. She presses the panel against her mouth and drags it sideways, and the scraping edge shaves a thin layer of fiber from the surface, and she chews, and the chewing is slow and methodical, and the fiber disappears. She does this four times, five, shaving and chewing, and then she drops the panel — thinner now, the surface she scraped already weeping new sap, the exposed tissue already beginning to swell with the first suggestions of grey-green tendrils — and she picks up her stone blade and goes back to work.

The root-wood sustains them. The fiber, the sap, the mineral content, whatever the bioluminescent compound is — it sustains them, in the way that lichen sustains a caribou, in the way that wood sustains a termite. They are processors. They are part of the system. They eat the growth and the growth eats their structures and the cycle does not have a direction. They are not parasites. They are not gardeners. They are a step in a process, and the process is the Rootcathedral's, and the Rootcathedral does not know they are here, or does not care, or cares in the way that a river cares about the stones in its bed — which is to say, the stones are there, and the river goes around them, and the going-around is the extent of the relationship.

---

They make things.

This is the part that will stay with you. Not the tools — the tools are functional, understandable, the product of need and available material. What stays with you is the thing you find in a clearing where no structure has been built, where no debris has fallen, where the mat is flat and the columns are distant and the emptiness is as complete as emptiness gets in this place. You find a arrangement on the ground.

Stones. Small stones — palm-sized, dark, glassy, the same material as the blades but unworked, unchipped, natural fragments that have fallen from some harder stratum below the root-mat and been pushed up by the swelling growth. Someone has placed them. Twelve of them. In a circle. The circle is not perfect, but it is intentional — the spacing is even, the stones are similar in size, and in the center of the circle, pressed into the mat, is a handprint. Glowing. Faint blue-white. The compound is still active. The print is recent. Hours old, maybe less.

It is not a shelter. It is not a tool-making site. It is not a processing station. It is a circle of stones with a handprint in the middle, and you sit beside it for a long time and you cannot explain it as anything other than what it looks like, which is a mark. A statement. A *here I was*. A place made, briefly, in a place that un-makes everything.

You think about the structures, the shelters, the platforms — all temporary, all doomed, all consumed by the growth within weeks of abandonment. You think about the stone tools, which persist, which sink into the mat but do not decompose, which will be there, under the surface, for as long as the Rootcathedral endures. And you think about the circle of stones, which is neither functional nor persistent, which will be scattered by the next swelling of the mat, which exists only now, only for the hours that the glow lasts, only for whoever might pass through this clearing in that window and see it and understand that someone was here and wanted to say so.

You think about the clicking. The clicking that reads the space, that maps the changes, that carries the conversation between two Marginals standing side by side. The clicking is ephemeral. It goes out, it comes back, it is gone. The structures are ephemeral. The handprints are ephemeral. Even the stone tools, which persist, are lost — sunk into the mat, invisible, unreachable. Everything they make is temporary. Everything they are is temporary. They live in the turbulence, in the margin, in the gap between one growth form and the next, and the gap closes, and they move, and the gap opens somewhere else, and they move there, and the moving is the living.

And in the middle of all of it — in the cold, in the wet, in the blue-white light of a canopy that does not know they exist — one of them knelt on the ground and placed twelve stones in a circle and pressed a hand into the center and left.

Not for shelter. Not for food. Not for survival.

For the same reason anyone has ever pressed a hand into a surface and left the print behind. Because the hand was there. Because the surface would hold it, for a while. Because the holding, even briefly, is worth the pressing.

---

The other inhabitants are simpler, and you will catalog them, and the catalog will be incomplete.

The pale flat things — the flock that erupted from the debris of the falling column — are called nothing, because you see them three times and never well. They are flat, translucent, the size of a hand, wingless, moving by means you cannot identify. You suspect they move on the thermal currents — the slow respiration of the space — and that they are, in some sense, riding the Rootcathedral's breath. They cluster where growth is active, where the sap is fresh, where the bioluminescent compound is most concentrated. They feed on it, you think. They are fragments of the system that have achieved mobility — cells that learned to move, tissues that detached and kept growing, or something else entirely, something that evolved here in the light and the cold and found a way to be small and fast in a place where everything else is large and slow. They do not click. They do not build. They are the birds of this place, if the birds were made of light and sap and the thinnest possible tissue, and they live in the canopy, and they come down only when the growth breaks, and they go back up when the growth settles, and their lives are as short and as quick as the Marginals' lives are long and deliberate.

There are other things. You see them as traces, as impressions, as shapes that might be fauna or might be growth forms you haven't categorized. A long, sinuous track in the mat, as if something heavy and limbless has dragged itself across the surface — but the track is already swelling shut, and you cannot follow it. A patch of the canopy that moves differently from the surrounding membrane, darker, denser, rippling with a motion that is not the canopy's usual pulse — something up there, something large, something that has incorporated itself into the structure and moves within it the way a hand moves inside a glove. You don't know what it is. You don't want to know what it is. You file it under *the Rootcathedral has depths you have not plumbed*, and you move on.

The decomposer mat is alive. You know this. It moves, it consumes, it leaves residue. But whether it is a single organism or a colony or a biome, you cannot tell. Its edges are indistinct. It does not have edges. It has fronts — directions of advance — and the fronts advance, and what is behind the front is no longer the mat but the dark residue, and the residue is substrate, and the substrate is new growth, and the new growth is not the mat. The mat is a process, not a creature. Or it is a creature that is also a process. Or the distinction does not apply here, in a place where the line between living and non-living was never drawn because there was no one to draw it until the Marginals came, and the Marginals do not draw lines. They live in the gap. The gap does not require lines.

---

You leave the way you came. Through the fissure. Up through the granite. The light fades. The cold recedes. The world above is the world above, and it is too bright, and the air moves too much, and nothing is growing at a speed you can see.

You will think about the Rootcathedral for years. You will think about the light. You will think about the growth. You will think about the canopy, breathing its slow tidal pulse, and the columns rising and falling, and the mat swelling and subsiding, and the decomposer advancing, and the pale flat things scattering and returning, and the whole vast system running and running, building and unbuilding, cell by cell, in the cold and the wet and the light that it made for itself.

But what you will think about most is the circle of stones. The handprint. The *here I was*.

In a place that un-makes everything. In a place where nothing persists. In a place where the ground shifts and the walls grow and the ceiling breathes and the only permanence is the absence of permanence — someone knelt and placed stones and pressed a hand and left a mark.

And the mark glowed.

And the glow faded.

And the growth came.

And the Rootcathedral continued, as it has for longer than you can calculate, as it will for longer than you can imagine, building itself in the dark beneath the world, lit by its own light, inhabited by its own inhabitants, unknown and unneeded and ongoing.

And somewhere in the margin — in the gap between what is built and what is unmade, in the turbulence, in the shifting, breathing, clicking, growing, falling, glowing, cold and wet and luminous space — one of them is walking. Narrow and pale and long-fingered, with glowing fingertips and a stone blade and a body built for cracks, clicking the space into shape, reading the growth, reading the changes, reading the room that is the whole world, the only world, the world below the world.

Clicking. Walking. Living in the gap.
