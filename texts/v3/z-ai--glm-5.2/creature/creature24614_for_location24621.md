# Inhabitants

---

The crabs own the ground. That is the first thing to understand, and the settlers — the animal settlers, the colonizers of this ruined place — have understood it in the way that animals understand territory, which is to say completely, without argument, through the fact of their bodies. The crabs are the foundation. Everything else is built on them.

They are not one species. You realize this slowly, the way you realize most things here, by standing still and letting your eyes adjust to the gray light and the wet air until the aggregate motion of the drainage channels resolves into individual bodies, and the individual bodies resolve into types. There are at least three. The largest are the fist-sized ones, dark-shelled, deliberate, moving with a kind of armored patience along the channel floors. They have asymmetrical claws — one heavy and blunt, one smaller and finer — and they use the heavy claw to crack things open against the pavement, whatever those things are. You never see what they eat. The food is too small, or too formless, or dissolved into the sediment itself. But you hear the cracking, a flat percussive *tock* that repeats at irregular intervals, and when you follow the sound you find a large crab holding something to the stone with its smaller claw and bringing the larger claw down with a motion that looks like a smith striking an anvil. They do this for hours. They do this all day. The sound is one of the layers of the murmur.

The second kind is smaller — half the size, narrower, quicker. Their shells are lighter, a mottled tan that disappears against the concrete when they hold still, which they often do. They are the ones you startle. You step near a drainage channel and a dozen of them freeze, and then a dozen of them bolt, and the bolting is a scrambling, clicking, splashing eruption that sends water up onto your boots. They climb. You did not expect crabs to climb, but these climb, scaling the vertical walls of the channels where the slag aggregate is rough enough to hold their legs, scaling the foundations of buildings where the concrete has pitted and eroded, scaling the rusted legs of machinery in the sheds where the metal has flaked into textures that claws can grip. You find them on second-floor ledges. You find them on the horizontal surfaces of dead pumps. They are everywhere the water can reach, and they have decided that the water can reach almost everything, because moisture wicks and condenses and pools in places that have no obvious connection to the water table, and the crabs follow the moisture, and the moisture does not ask permission.

The third kind you do not see for a long time. You see evidence of them — burrows. Small, round holes in the compacted earth between buildings where the pavement has broken away entirely, holes about the width of your thumb, spaced regularly, each one ringed with a small mound of excavated sediment. The holes go down. You cannot tell how far. You crouch and look into one and see nothing but darkness and feel a puff of warmer, wetter air against your face, as if the earth is breathing. Then, late one afternoon, while you are sitting on a collapsed wall resting your legs and the light has dropped to its dimmest level and you are very still, you see a head emerge from one of the burrows. A small crab — smaller than the others, paler, almost translucent, as if it has lived its life in darkness and never needed to build a shell that could withstand the light. Its eyes are on stalks and the stalks are longer than they should be, proportionally, and they move independently, sweeping the air. It watches you. Or it watches the space you occupy. It holds its position for a long time, its claws folded against its body, its stalk-eyes turning. Then it descends, and you see that the burrow goes deeper than you thought, because the creature is gone for a long time before another one emerges from a different hole, two meters away, and you understand that the burrows are connected, that there is a network of tunnels down there in the wet earth, a whole subterranean stratum of crab-life that you have been walking on without knowing it.

---

The lizards are the second layer. They are dependent on the crabs in a way that is not obvious until you watch long enough, and what you watch for is feeding. The lizards eat the crabs. Not the large ones — the large crabs are too heavily armored, too aggressive, too willing to use that blunt claw on anything that grips them. But the small ones, the tan-colored climbers, the quick ones. The lizards take those. You see it once, clearly, in a shed where a collapsed roof beam has created a warm pocket of air and a pool of standing water and a congregation of small crabs at the water's edge. A lizard — long-bodied, pale, with a head like a wedge and a jaw that opens wider than seems proportional — drops from the beam. It lands on a crab. The crab's legs churn and its claws snap and the lizard's jaw closes and there is a crunch that is louder than it should be in this quiet place, and then the crab is still and the lizard is swallowing, its throat working in a slow, deliberate pulse, and then the lizard is gone, up the beam, into the dark, and the other crabs have resumed their feeding as if nothing has happened. Death here is fast and ordinary and does not interrupt the rhythm.

The lizards are beautiful in the way that rust is beautiful, which is to say their beauty is inseparable from their context. Their skin is pale — not white, not cream, but a color that is almost the absence of color, a warm gray that holds the dim light and gives nothing back. In direct sun they would probably look dull, washed-out, but in this light, in this atmosphere, they glow. Their scales are small and fine and they catch the moisture in the air and each scale holds a microscopic bead of water, so that the lizard's body appears to be covered in a fine dew, and when it moves the dew shifts and the light shifts with it and the animal seems to shimmer, or to be made of the same material as the air, wet and luminous. They are fast. Their speed is not the speed of a startled mammal, which is explosive and brief, but the speed of water — fluid, continuous, pouring. A lizard goes from still to moving and the transition is so smooth that you cannot find the moment where one state became the other. It is simply gone, and then it is simply somewhere else, and the in-between is a blur of pale movement that your eyes cannot hold.

There are more of them than you think. You walk into a shed and you see two, three, and you think, yes, lizards, and then you sit down and wait and the shed repopulates. They come back to where they were because where they were was warm and dry and theirs, and you are a temporary disruption, and they do not see the point of yielding their territory to a temporary disruption. They come out of the machines. They come out of the wall cavities. They come out of the conduit channels where the broken sections provide access. They emerge from gaps in the concrete that you would have said were too narrow for anything to fit through, but the lizards are narrow, or they make themselves narrow, their bodies compressing, their ribs flexing, and they fit through spaces that a finger would not fit through, and on the other side of those spaces are the warm, dark, humid interiors of dead industrial equipment, and those interiors are full of lizards, and you cannot see them but you can hear them, the scratch of claws on metal, the small wet sound of a body moving through a space just large enough to hold it.

They bask. They bask on the machines, on the rusted surfaces that hold what little warmth the dim light provides. They flatten their bodies against the steel, spreading their legs, pressing their bellies down, and they are still, and in the stillness they are almost invisible, because their skin is the color of wet rust and their bodies are pressed flat against wet rust and the light does not distinguish between them. You look at a machine and you see a machine. You look again and there are four lizards on it, and you did not see them appear, because they did not appear, they were always there, and your eyes simply learned to see them. This is how the Murmurworks works. You learn to see things by waiting. The things are already there.

---

The bats — you call them bats, but you are not certain, and the uncertainty is part of how you experience them, because you never see one clearly. You see the aggregate. You see the river in the sky. The river forms every evening, or every dimming, and it forms from the windows of the administrative blocks, the dark upper floors where the glass is still intact or where the frames hold panels of ancient board that still block the light. They pour out. That is the only word for it. They do not fly out in ones and twos; they pour, a liquid stream of bodies, and the stream thickens and branches as more windows contribute their flow, and the branches merge over the central square where the shallow lake glints in the last of the light, and the merged river turns and wheels and covers the settlement in a living canopy.

They are hunting. You know this because the insects are there, the columns and sheets and hazes of small flying things, and the bats — the things — move through the insect populations with a precision that implies feeding. But you never see a catch. The insects are too small, the bats too fast, the light too dim. You see the bats turn and dip and rise and the turn and dip and rise is the geometry of pursuit, but the pursuit is invisible at the scale where it resolves. You are watching a war fought in the air above you between animals that are too small and too quick and too many to track individually, and the war is silent, and the war has been going on every evening for longer than you can estimate, and the war is not a war, it is dinner.

What you hear is the wings. Not a cry, not a screech, not the ultrasonic hunting call that actual bats use to navigate and locate prey. Either these animals do not echolocate, or they echolocate at a frequency you cannot hear, or they use some other sense entirely, some way of knowing where they are and where their food is that does not require sound. What they produce, audibly, is the rush of air over membranes. Thousands of bodies in motion, each one displacing air, each displacement a soft percussive pulse, and the pulses overlap and merge and what reaches you at ground level is a continuous low whisper, a sibilance, like wind moving through grass except there is no grass and no wind, just animals, just wings, just the accumulated sound of flight.

They return to the buildings the same way they left — in streams, in reverse, the river dividing into branches and the branches flowing back through windows and gaps and broken panels, and then they are gone, and the air is empty, and the insects, diminished but not eliminated, resume their columns and sheets, and the night — such as it is, the light dropping another shade toward gray-black without ever reaching true darkness — the night belongs to the ground.

---

The insects are the medium. Not the medium of the air — the medium of everything. They are the food chain's foundation and its expression, the reason the crabs have something to eat in the sediment, the reason the bats have something to hunt in the air, the reason the lizards have something to eat at the water's edge where the dead and dying insects fall. The insects are the engine. And they are the closest thing this place has to weather.

You walk through a cloud of midges and the cloud parts for you and closes behind you and the parting and closing is not random, it is responsive, the insects tracking your body, your warmth, your exhaled carbon dioxide, and adjusting their positions to maintain their columns and sheets around the obstacle you present. You are an obstacle. You are a walking disruption in an atmospheric layer that has formed its own rules and does not appreciate deviations. The midges re-form. The gnats re-form. The mosquitoes — and there are mosquitoes, many mosquitoes, and they find you within seconds of your arrival and they do not re-form around you, they form *on* you, and you are biting and slapping and the slapping is louder than anything else in the Murmurworks, a sharp crack that echoes off the buildings and silences the crabs for a moment and sends the lizards pouring off their basking spots — the mosquitoes are the one thing here that acknowledges you directly, that treats you as a resource rather than a feature of the landscape.

But even the mosquitoes are part of it. They breed in the standing water, every puddle, every drainage pool, every shallow lake, and their breeding is prolific and constant because the water is warm and the water is still and the water contains whatever chemistry it contains that supports their larvae. You look into a drainage channel and the surface is dimpled with the breathing siphons of larvae, hundreds of them, thousands, the water surface alive with the small rhythms of respiration. The crabs eat the larvae. The lizards eat the crabs. The bats eat the adults. The adults bite you. You bleed into the ecosystem. You are, briefly, a food source, and the ecosystem accepts you in the way it accepts everything — without comment, without adjustment, simply incorporating you into the flow of energy from water to insect to animal to animal to decay to sediment to water again.

There are spiders. Or things like spiders. You find them in the machinery, in the interior spaces of dead pumps and seized impeller housings and the dark gaps behind control panels. Their webs are not the geometric orbs of garden spiders or the funnel sheets of house spiders. They are irregular, dense, almost felted, spanning the interiors of mechanical components with a thoroughness that suggests the web-builder has decided that the entire inside of this machine is its territory and the web is the boundary. The silk is gray, almost invisible in the dim light, and you do not see it until you walk into it, and then you feel it on your face, a clinging, soft resistance that is worse than the walking into it is the knowledge of what the web implies, which is that the thing that made it is nearby, and the thing that made it is probably large, because the webs are large, and you back out of the machinery and you do not look closely at what lives inside it.

But you see one, eventually. It is on the wall of a shed, in a corner where two concrete surfaces meet at a cracked and stained joint. It is not large. It is the size of your palm, maybe, with its legs extended, and the legs are long and thin and jointed at angles that remind you of the machinery itself, the articulated arms of the transfer systems, as if the spider has borrowed its proportions from the equipment it lives in. Its body is dark and glossy, wet-looking, though it may not be wet, it may just be that the coloration holds the light in a way that reads as moisture. It sits in the center of its web and it is still and you are still and you look at each other, or it looks at the space you occupy with whatever arrangement of eyes it has, and you cannot count the eyes in this light, and the moment extends, and then a midge hits the web and the spider moves and the movement is so fast that the stillness and the movement seem to be the same thing, a single state that contains both, and the midge is wrapped and the spider is still again and you are still again and the moment is over.

---

There is something else. You do not see it. You are not sure you want to see it. But you find the evidence, and the evidence is consistent, and the evidence is this: in the long sheds, in the deepest interior spaces where the roof is intact and the walls are solid and the darkness is total, there are marks on the floor. Not marks — impressions. The dust and the debris and the fine sediment that covers the concrete have been disturbed, and the disturbance has a shape, and the shape is a path, a track, a regular passage of something heavy enough to leave an impression but careful enough to follow the same route each time. The path leads from one machine to another, from a gap in the wall to a pool of standing water, from the water to a dark corner where the debris has been pushed aside and the floor is bare and there are fragments of crab shell in a small pile, the remains of meals, and the pile is fresh, the shell is still wet.

You measure the track with your hand. The width of the impression is wider than your spread hand. The depth is slight, a centimeter, maybe two, but the sediment is compacted and a centimeter of impression in compacted sediment implies weight, real weight, the weight of a body that is substantial, that is not a lizard and is not a crab and is not a bat. Something else lives in the dark interiors of the Murmurworks. Something that moves at night, or in the permanent night of the shed interiors. Something that eats crabs. Something that has a route and a routine and a corner where it rests and a pile of shells where it feeds.

You do not investigate further. You tell yourself this is because you are being respectful of the animal's territory, which is true, and because the light is too dim to see by, which is also true, and because the shed is structurally unsound and it would be dangerous to go deeper, which is plausible, but the real reason is that you felt something in that corner, in that dark, something that was not the heat and was not the humidity and was not the murmur. A presence. A weight in the air that was different from the weight of the air. Something aware. Something that knew you were there and did not move because it did not need to move, because it was in its place and you were in yours and the place was large enough for both of you, and the distance between you was a kind of agreement, and the agreement was: you stay where you are, and I will stay where I am, and we will both continue to occupy this settlement that does not belong to either of us and belongs to both of us and belongs, finally, to the heat and the water and the rust and the slow mineral patience of the place itself.

You turn back toward the light. Behind you, in the dark, something shifts. You hear it. Or you think you hear it. Or you hear the absence of sound that a body makes when it moves carefully through a space it knows. And then the murmur closes over the moment like water closing over a stone, and you are walking back toward the entrance, and the lizards are pouring off the machines, and the crabs are clicking in the channels, and the bats are wheeling overhead, and the insects are reforming their columns in the air, and the Murmurworks is doing what it does, which is being alive, which is being full of life, which is humming, always humming, with the sound of living things that have found a place where the living is good.
