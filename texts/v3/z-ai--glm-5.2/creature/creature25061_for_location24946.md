# What Lives on the High Pastures of Verthen

---

## The People

They do not call themselves anything in particular. Or rather, they do, but the word is not a name so much as a recognition — it translates roughly to *the ones who stayed*, and it carries within it the acknowledgment that others did not. There were people who came up to the plateau and left again. There were people who came up and stayed a season and died. There were people who came up and stayed and their children grew and some of those children left, went down the escarpment, found the lowlands where the air is thick and the ground is soft and the wind does not cut, and they did not come back. The ones who remained are the ones who remained. It is not a philosophy. It is a description.

They are small. Not delicate — dense, rather, the way stone is dense, compacted by the pressure of the place into something that takes up less space than you would expect. The average height is below what lowlanders would consider average, and the bodies are thick through the chest, the lungs enlarged, the ribcages flared to make room for the air that is not quite enough. Their skin is dark — not the dark of a particular ethnicity but the dark of adaptation, of generations of unprotected exposure to light so thin and direct that it burns without the warning of heat. Their hands are broad, the fingers thick, the skin on their palms so callused that they can grip sharp stone without flinching. Their feet are hard. Many of them do not wear shoes in the city, because the stone paths are their stone, and they have walked them since childhood, and the soles of their feet are like leather, like the hides they stretch over their roof-frames.

They live a long time, or they seem to. It is difficult to know, because they do not count years the way lowlanders do — they count seasons, cycles, migrations, and a person's age is spoken of in terms of how many great herd-passings they have seen, which means that someone who was born in a year when the herds came through twice is technically older than someone born in a year when the herds came through once, even if the calendar would disagree. But the effect is real: the cold preserves them, or the slowness does, or the lack of disease that comes from living in a place where most pathogens cannot survive. An elder of sixty or seventy is not uncommon. An elder of eighty is not unheard of. They move slowly, these elders, and speak slowly, and are listened to, because in a place where nothing happens quickly, the accumulation of experience is the only currency that matters.

They do not have a word for *hurry*. This is not a romantic observation. It is a literal fact about their language, which has words for *wait*, for *persist*, for *endure*, for *the thing that is coming and will arrive when it arrives*, but no word that means *to move faster than is necessary*. When traders come up from the lowlands and try to rush a negotiation, the people simply stop talking. They wait. The trader either calms or leaves. Either way, the people are unaffected, because they were already waiting, and will continue to wait, because waiting is what you do on a plateau where the stone sets the pace of everything.

## The Goats

They are not goats.

They look like goats. From a distance, in the thin mist, with their long hair and their narrow faces and their habit of standing on rock ledges that should not support them, they are goats. But their ancestry is not the ancestry of goats. They are something else — something that came into being alongside the plateau, that evolved in the compressed, accelerated way that life evolves in new places, that filled the ecological niche of *grazer of sparse high-altitude vegetation* with an efficiency that suggests they were made for this place, or this place was made for them, or the distinction does not matter.

They are darker than goats. Their hair is not white or brown but a deep, blue-black that absorbs the light and makes them look, from a distance, like holes in the landscape, like places where the stone has given way and the void shows through. Their hooves are wider than a goat's, splayed almost like a snowshoe, and they are hard — harder than the stone they walk on, so that their tracks on the rock are visible as pale scratches, lines that crisscross the plateau in patterns that the people can read the way a sailor reads the sea. The oldest tracks are decades old, carved into the stone by countless passages, and they show where the herds have gone and will go again.

They do not bleat. The sound they make is lower, a kind of rumble that comes from the chest, that you feel in your feet before you hear it with your ears. It is a sound of contact, of proximity — they make it to locate each other in the mist, to confirm that the herd is still around them, that the shape they belong to still holds. When a goat is separated from the herd, it goes silent, and the silence is the signal, and the oldest female will stop the herd and wait, and the lost one will find its way back by the silence, by the absence of the rumble, by the space in the sound where the herd is not.

They eat almost nothing. This is the thing that lowlanders do not believe — that an animal can survive on what the plateau offers. But the goats have adapted to a diet that would starve a lowland animal in days. They eat lichen, scraping it from the rock with their lower teeth, which are set forward in the jaw specifically for this purpose. They eat the tough, thread-like grass that grows in the sheltered crevices. They eat moss. They eat the frost itself, in the morning, licking it from the stone before the sun melts it. And they can go for four days without water, five in the cold season, their bodies drawing moisture from the plants they eat, from the dew that forms on their own coats, from the thin rain that they stand in without discomfort, their long hair shedding water the way the stone sheds it.

The people know the goats. Not individually — there are too many for that — but by herd, by matriarch, by route. Each herd has a name, or a sound, a specific clicking in the throat that the people use to identify it, and the goats respond to their name, the oldest female lifting her head and turning toward the sound, acknowledging the call, and then lowering her head again, because acknowledgment is not the same as obedience, and the goats go where the goats go.

## The Sull

The *sull* are harder to describe because there is nothing else like them.

They are low. Their bodies are long and flat, barely a foot off the ground, and they move on six legs that are set along the sides of the body in pairs — front, middle, back — that move in an alternating rhythm that is, as the description noted, almost hypnotic. They are the size of a large dog, or a small pig, but stretched, elongated, as though someone took a round animal and pulled it from both ends. Their skin is hairless and gray, the same gray as the stone, and it has a texture that is somewhere between leather and the fine grain of sandstone. When they lie still, which they do for hours at a time, they are nearly invisible against the rock.

They are warm. This is their primary value to the people — they are warm, warmer than the goats, warmer than the stone, their bodies generating a heat that seems disproportionate to their size. In the shelters, they sleep against the people, against the children especially, and their warmth is enough to keep the cold at bay through the worst of the night. They give milk, too — a thick, rich milk that is almost the consistency of cream, that the people drink fresh or curdle into a dense, sharp cheese that keeps for months. And when they die, their hides are used for the softest clothing, for the inner layer that goes against the skin, and their bones are used for needles and awls and the small tools that stone cannot make.

They are not intelligent, exactly, but they are attuned. They know the people they live with. They know the layout of the shelters, the paths between them, the routes on the rooftops. They follow the people through the city like companions, not pets — there is no word for *pet* in the language, because the relationship is not one of ownership but of cohabitation, of mutual presence. The people feed the sull. The sull warm the people. The arrangement is understood by both parties and requires no enforcement.

They are, in their way, the thing that makes the city possible. Without the sull, the shelters would be too cold for children. Without the sull, the people would have no milk in the winter, when the goats dry up. Without the sull, the dead would be harder to honor, because the awls that pierce the holes in the hide wrappings are sull-bone, and the thread is sull-sinew. The sull are woven into everything, and they are so present, so ubiquitous, that they are almost invisible, the way the stone is invisible, the way the air is invisible — you stop seeing them because they are always there.

## The Birds

They are large. Their wingspan is wider than a person is tall, and they ride the thermals above the basin with a patience that borders on geological. They do not flap. They do not need to. The air above the plateau is in constant motion — the cold air sinking into the basin, the warm air rising from the valley below — and the birds enter this current and become part of it, their wings held at a precise angle that converts the moving air into lift, and they circle, and they circle, and they circle.

They are silent. Completely, absolutely silent. No cry, no call, no sound of wings. They communicate through the angle of their bodies, through the tilt of their heads, through the way they open or close their tails, and it is a full language, a complex one, that the people have learned to read partially, the way you might learn to read the expressions of a foreign face. The birds nest in the high fractures of the basin walls, in crevices that are inaccessible from below, and they descend in the morning to the city, landing on the rooftops, walking through the paths on their flat, wide feet, picking through the scraps, the refuse, the things that the people have discarded.

The people do not hunt the birds. This is a rule so old that no one remembers when it was made, or why. Some say it is because the birds are the spirits of the dead, returning to check on the living. Some say it is because the birds were here before the people, before the city, before the goats, and they have a prior claim. Some say it is simply because the birds are useless — their meat is tough and bitter, their feathers are too sparse for insulation, their bones are hollow and fragile — and there is no reason to kill something that is useless when killing requires effort. Whatever the reason, the birds move through the city with an assurance that suggests they know they are safe, and the people step around them, and the goats ignore them, and the sull do not react, and the birds pick through the scraps and then rise, rising, rising, back to the thermals, back to the circle, back to the silence.

## The Wild Herds

Out on the open plateau, beyond the city, beyond the basin, the land stretches and the herds move.

They are not goats. They are something older, something larger, something that has nothing to do with the people and does not know the people exist. They are tall — taller than a person, their shoulders at head-height, their necks long and thick, their heads small in proportion to their bodies. They are shaggy, their coats hanging in long, matted strands that almost reach the ground, and the coats are the color of the stone, the exact color, so that when a herd is at rest, lying down on the plateau, it is invisible, a part of the landscape, a fold in the rock.

They move in groups of hundreds. Thousands, sometimes. The herd is a single entity, a body with a thousand legs, a thousand stomachs, a thousand pairs of eyes, and it moves with a purpose that is not directed by any individual but that emerges from the collective, the way a flock of birds turns, the way a river finds its course. The people do not know where the herds go when they leave the plateau. They do not know where they come from. They know the routes — the paths worn into the stone, the ancient tracks that are older than the city — and they know the seasons, the two times each year when the herds pass through the basin, and they prepare for these passages the way a coastal town prepares for a tide.

When the herds come through, the city stops. The people retreat to their shelters, or to the basin walls, and they watch. The herds do not avoid the city. They do not seek it. They simply move through it, and the shelters that are in their path are… dealt with. The people have learned which paths the herds take, and they do not build on those paths, and the shelters that were built there in the early days, before the routes were known, have been abandoned, crushed flat, pressed into the stone by the weight of ten thousand animals passing over them, and now they are part of the ground, part of the plateau, and the people do not rebuild them.

The passage takes days. The sound is enormous — the sound of feet on stone, amplified by the basin, by the rock walls, by the sheer number of bodies — and then it is over, and the herds are gone, and the city is left with a layer of dung that the people collect and dry and use for fuel, and the stone is scoured clean by the passage, the lichen stripped, the moss trampled, and the people know that it will grow back, slowly, over months, before the next passage, and the cycle will continue, as it has continued, as it will continue.

## The Burrowers

In the marsh, beneath the dark, spongy ground, something lives.

The people do not have a name for them that translates well. The closest approximation is *the ones who are under*, and this is both a description and a recognition. They are small — the size of a hand, or smaller — and they are soft, their bodies pale and blind, their legs reduced to stubs that push them through the organic muck of the marsh floor. They feed on the roots of the marsh plants, on the decaying matter that accumulates in the soft ground, on the small insects and larvae that live in the gap between stone and soil.

The people trap them. This is one of the primary tasks of the marsh-tenders, the families who live near the marsh and are responsible for its care. They set traps — simple things, stone tubes with a narrowed entrance — and they check them each morning, and the burrowers they catch are killed quickly and cleanly and eaten, roasted over the small dung-fires, their flesh sweet and rich, a contrast to the lean meat of the goats and the dense milk of the sull. They are a delicacy, in their way. Not a luxury — there is no luxury on the plateau — but a pleasure, a thing that is enjoyed for its own sake, a taste that is different from the usual tastes, and the people value this because there is so little that is different.

The burrowers are, in a sense, the future. The marsh is growing, slowly, and as it grows, the burrowers' world expands, and their numbers grow, and the people trap more of them, and the protein they provide becomes more important to the city's diet. The marsh-tenders are aware of this. They do not overtrap. They leave enough burrowers to maintain the population, to reproduce, to continue the work of burrowing that aerates the soil and breaks down the organic matter and makes the marsh deeper and richer. This is not conservation in the modern sense — there is no concept of *ecosystem* in the people's language — but it is something parallel, a practical wisdom born of observation, of the understanding that if you take everything, there will be nothing left to take.

## The Dead

The dead live here too.

They are in the cracks in the stone, sealed behind plugs of shaped rock, their bodies wrapped in sull-hide and bound with sull-sinew, placed in the fractures of the basin walls with the care that the people give to all important things. They are not buried, because there is no soil to bury them in. They are not burned, because fuel is too precious. They are placed — *stored* is not the right word, because it implies that they might be retrieved, and they will not be — they are *settled*, given a place in the rock, a niche that becomes theirs, that becomes them, and the stone plug that seals them is carved, sometimes, with a mark, a symbol, a petroglyph that is not a name but a recognition, a way of saying *this person was here, this person lived, this person became part of the plateau*.

The people visit the dead. Not ceremonially, not on specific days — but in the course of daily life, as they pass through the city, as they walk the paths at the base of the basin walls, they pass the plugs, and they touch them, sometimes, a hand laid briefly on the stone, a recognition, and they continue. The dead are not far away. They are here, in the rock, in the walls of the city, and the people live among them the way they live among the living — in proximity, in mutual presence, in the understanding that the boundary between the two states is not as firm as the stone that seems to define it.

The oldest dead are in the deepest cracks, the widest fractures, the places where the rock split when it rose and has not yet healed. These are the dead of generations ago, of the early settlement, of the people who first came to the basin and decided to stay. Their plugs are worn smooth by time and by touch, by the hands of the descendants who have laid their palms on them over the centuries, and the stone is darker than the surrounding rock, stained by the oil of human skin, and the dead are part of the city now, part of the texture, part of the substance, and if you were to remove them, the city would not be the same, would not hold, would collapse inward into the spaces where they lie.

## What the Place Makes

What lives here, ultimately, is a way of being.

The people of Verthen are not primitive. They are not simple. They are not noble savages or untouched ancients or any of the other categories that lowlanders might apply. They are people — complex, contradictory, capable of kindness and cruelty, of wisdom and foolishness, of love and indifference — who live in a place that does not permit pretense. The stone is honest. The cold is honest. The light is honest. And the people who live in this honesty have become honest themselves, not because they are better than other people but because the place does not reward dishonesty. You cannot lie to the plateau. You cannot trick the frost. You cannot negotiate with the wind.

What lives here is patience. Not the patience of waiting, which implies expectation, but the patience of continuation, which implies only the willingness to go on. The people go on. The goats go on. The sull go on. The birds circle. The herds migrate. The burrowers burrow. The dead rest. The marsh grows. The stone erodes, grain by grain, century by century, and the city accretes, shelter by shelter, generation by generation, and the plateau holds it all, holds everything, in the enormous shallow bowl of its surface, and the wind moves across it, and the stars are very bright, and the frost comes, and the frost goes, and the people are still here.
