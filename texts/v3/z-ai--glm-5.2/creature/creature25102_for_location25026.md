# Inhabitants

---

You do not see them at first because the place teaches you to look wrong. Your eyes want distance, want horizon, want the clean figure-ground separation that surface light provides. Down here, figure and ground have dissolved into each other. Everything is both. The fish are the water until they move. The wall-creatures are the rock until they ripple. So when something larger than these presents itself, your brain registers it first as a change in the texture of the dark — a denser patch, a thickening, a place where the mist sits differently — and only afterward, seconds later, as a shape.

---

## The Fish

Start with what is undeniable. The fish are real in the way that stones are real. They do not perform their existence for you. They occupy the shallow water the way the water occupies the rock — completely, without ambition, as a condition of their being rather than a choice.

There are at least four species, probably more, and the distinction between species is not clean. They blur. They intermediate. You see a fish with the dark back of the first species and the ventral fin shape of the third and you understand that what is happening here is not radiation into distinct forms but a continuous pooling, a genetic saturation, the water's warmth and the water's chemistry and the water's permanence having dissolved the pressure to specialize. These fish are not competing. There is enough. The guano feeds the insects feeds the smaller fish feeds the larger fish feeds the things that are not quite fish, and the surplus runs off in every direction the way the water runs off in every direction, pooling, finding its level.

They are dark-backed because the dark is all there is. They are quick because quickness is cheap in warm water and slowness is expensive when the things that eat you are patient. You watch a school of them — fifteen, twenty, each one the length of your little finger — move through a pool that is barely deep enough to cover them. Their dorsal fins break the surface. The water rolls off them in beads. They move as a single body not because they are coordinated but because they are responding to the same stimulus, which is the vibration of your footstep transmitted through the saturated rock and into the pool, and they interpret this vibration the same way because they are, in the functional sense, the same organism distributed across many bodies.

One of them breaks from the school. Not in alarm — in interest. It approaches the place where your boot meets the water, and it nibbles. Not at the leather. At the edge of the disturbance, the place where the sediment your boot displaced is settling. It is eating what you stirred up. It is, in the most literal sense, feeding on your passage. You have disturbed the sump and the sump has incorporated the disturbance immediately, metabolized it, turned your walking into food. The fish is the mechanism of this incorporation. It does not know you. It does not care about you. It cares about the particle.

They breed here. You find the evidence in a shallow pool near the third light-pole — a cluster of eggs, translucent, the size of peppercorns, stuck to a piece of submerged concrete with an adhesive that is almost but not quite biological, the eggs themselves faintly luminescent in the orange light, each one containing a dark comma that twitches. The fish do not guard these eggs. They do not need to. The water is warm enough, the chemical balance correct enough, the predation pressure distributed enough that a percentage will simply survive, and a percentage is all that is required. This is a numbers strategy. The sump runs on volume.

## The Wall-Creatures

They are not insects. You settle on this early and the distinction matters, though you could not say why. Insects have an architecture — the chitinous exoskeleton, the three-part body, the mechanical jointedness that makes a beetle look like a tiny machine. These things are not machines. They are soft. Their bodies, when you look closely — and you will, because one lands on your wrist and stays there, attending to the salt — their bodies are translucent, pale, the color of the rock when the rock is wet. You can see their internal structure through the skin, which is not skin exactly but a membrane, something between a slug's mantle and a larva's cuticle, a covering that is permeable to the water in the air, that breathes, that osmotic pressure keeps turgid.

They have too many legs. Not the insect's six but something between fourteen and twenty-two, the count varying not only between individuals but along the body of a single individual, the legs appearing and disappearing as the creature moves, as if they are not fixed structures but *opportunities* — places where the body's soft tissue has reached down and touched the surface and found purchase and extended, and then retracted when no longer needed. Watching one move across the wet rock is watching a body negotiate with a surface in real time. It flows. It does not walk. The legs are instruments of a flowing.

They are eyeless. Or rather: they have the place where eyes would be, two faint depressions on the anterior end, but these are not eyes. They are something else — chemoreceptors, perhaps, or moisture-sensors, or organs that detect the vibration that is the sump's only constant. The wall-creature on your wrist has turned its anterior toward your skin, toward the moisture there, and you can see the depressions working, pulsing, opening and closing at a rate too slow to be breathing and too fast to be arbitrary.

They do not bite. This is important. You want them to bite because biting would be a relationship you understand — predator and prey, host and parasite, a transaction with clear terms. Instead they *attend*. They land on you and they stay and they move across your skin with a diligence that is neither aggressive nor tender and they collect what your body produces — salt, water, dead cells, the oils of your skin — and they carry it away and they consume it and they convert it into more of themselves, and this is not parasitism, exactly. It is something more radical. It is *inclusion*. You are being incorporated into the sump's nutrient cycle. Not as prey. As a surface.

Near the lights, they thicken until the air is not air but a medium, a suspension of small pale bodies that you inhale and exhale and inhale again. You breathe them. Some do not come back out. You feel them in your throat, on your palate, tiny and soft and dissolving. They do not survive the passage through your body, but their nutrients do. The fish will feed on what you excrete. The cycle continues.

The density at the lights is not a swarm. A swarm implies movement, direction, purpose. This is a *concentration* — the creatures gathering where the conditions are optimal and remaining there until the conditions change, which they do not, because the lights have been burning for longer than a human lifetime and will burn for longer than yours. The wall beneath the thickest concentration is not visible. It is furred, carpeted, the surface of the rock completely occluded by pale soft bodies layered three and four deep, the lowest layer pressed against the stone, the uppermost layer exposed to the air, and between them a gradient of moisture and warmth that the creatures have created themselves, a microclimate in the cusp of their own bodies, each layer feeding on the layer below it, the whole colony a slow waterfall of tissue converting rock-surface moisture into biomass and biomass into more biomass, and the sound — the cellophane-crumpling, the wet clicking, the arrhythmic rustling of a thousand soft bodies adjusting their positions — this sound is the sound of that conversion, audible, relentless, the noise of a process that does not require an audience.

## The Ceiling Colony

You call them bats because the word is convenient and because the shape is close enough. They hang. They vocalize. They live in densities that defy individual accounting. But they are not bats.

They are warm. This is the first thing you learn when one falls — not past you, but near you, dislodged from its hold by some shift in the rock or some collision with a neighbor, and it tumbles through the mist and lands in the shallow water at your feet and you see it clearly for the first time in the light of the nearest caged bulb and what you see is:

A body the size of your thumb, furred, but the fur is not hair. It is filament, the same material as the roots that probe down from the ceiling, pale and wet and fine, and it serves the same purpose — increasing surface area, capturing moisture, drinking the air. The creature is *drinking* through its fur. Its entire body is a tongue, licking the condensation from every surface it contacts. Beneath the fur, the body is warm — you feel this when you pick it up, instinctively, and it sits in your palm and vibrates with a heat that is startling, that is mammalian, or whatever the equivalent is here, the heat of a metabolism working hard in a body that has no interest in conserving energy because energy is abundant and conservation is a strategy for scarcity and scarcity is a concept that does not exist in the sump.

Its face. You look at its face. It has one. This is the problem. The wall-creatures have an anterior end and you can project whatever you want onto it, but this thing has a *face* — two eyes, dark, wet, set forward, and a mouth that is not a beak and not a muzzle and not a snout but a *shape*, a configuration of tissue that reads as expression, that reads as *something looking back*. It sees you. You are certain of this. Not in the way that a fish sees you, which is as a disturbance, a shadow, a change in the pattern of light. This thing sees you the way one animal sees another. It assesses. It has an opinion.

It does not have the opinion you expect. It is not afraid. It is not aggressive. It is — and you struggle for the word — *curious*. It turns its head. It looks at your face. It looks at your hands. It makes a sound, a single click, not the sustained chittering of the colony but a discrete vocalization, a word in a language you cannot parse, and you understand with a clarity that unsettles you that this sound is directed at you, is *about* you, is a report, a description, a filing, this creature telling itself what you are.

Then it is gone. It drops from your hand, extends the membrane between its limbs — not wings, not quite, but a webbing of skin and filament that catches the air the way the mist catches the light, inefficiently, diffusely — and it labors upward, not with the clean ballistic grace of a bat but with a swimming, struggling ascent, a creature that was built for hanging and is only reluctantly flying, and it reaches the ceiling and reattaches and folds itself into the mass and is indistinguishable within seconds.

The colony is not silent when you are beneath it. The chittering changes. A region of the ceiling directly above you begins to vocalize differently — not louder, not more intensely, but with a *pattern*, a call-and-response structure that was not there before you arrived. They are discussing you. Or they are discussing the disturbance you represent. Or they are simply vocalizing in response to a stimulus and you are anthropomorphizing the pattern, finding meaning in noise because meaning is what you do. You cannot tell. The difference is not resolvable from where you stand.

The guano beneath the colony is not guano in the way you understand guano. It is warm. It steams, faintly, in the cooler air — but there is no cooler air, so it steams in the same air, and the steam joins the mist. It is rich in a way you can smell — mineral, organic, complex, the scent of something being broken down into something else. It accumulates at a rate that implies the colony has been here for centuries, millennia, long enough that the guano itself has become a geological feature, a stratum, a layer in the sump's floor that the water shapes and reshapes into bars and ridges and spits that the fish use as shelter and the wall-creatures use as substrate and the whole system uses as fuel. The colony is an engine. It converts the ceiling's moisture and the air's insects into waste, and the waste feeds the floor, and the floor feeds the things that feed the ceiling, and the cycle does not have a direction. It is not a circle. It is a *saturation* — everything feeding everything, everything being fed by everything, the nutrients distributed the way the water is distributed, evenly, without hierarchy, without preference, without gaps.

## The Amphibian-Things

You do not get a clean look at these. You get glimpses. A wake. A displacement. A shape in the deeper pools where the terraces step down and the water darkens from clear to amber to a color that is not a color but an *opacity*, a thickness, the water so full of dissolved mineral and organic matter that it is less water and more broth.

What you see:

A body. Flat, wide — forty centimeters, maybe fifty, the proportions of something that has chosen to spread rather than stack, to present a surface to the warm water rather than a profile. It moves by undulation, not by limbs — or rather, the limbs are present, you think, four of them, but they are small, vestigial, tucked against the body, used for steering rather than propulsion. The propulsion is the body itself, a slow muscular wave that travels from the anterior to the posterior, the creature pushing the water aside the way the rock pushes the water up, by pressure, by presence, by being a thing that the water must go around.

Its skin. You see the skin for a moment as it passes through a band of orange light, and the skin is — *wrong* is not the word. *Different* is not strong enough. The skin is *continuous with the water*. It has the same sheen. It has the same texture, at the edge of resolution. You cannot tell where the body ends and the water begins, and this is not because the animal is camouflaged but because the animal and the water share a property, a quality of wetness that goes beyond surface, that is structural, the animal's skin as permeable as the rock, as permeable as the walls, the sump's defining characteristic — saturation — expressed in a body.

They call. You have been hearing it since you entered, but you did not identify it until now — the low note, the single tone that rises from the deeper pools every twenty or thirty seconds, a sound that is not a croak and not a call and not a song but a *pulse*, a pressure wave, something the body produces not with a throat or a syrinx but with its entire self, a contraction that pushes air or water through a structure you cannot see and produces a note that you feel in your sternum, in the water around your ankles, in the soft rock beneath your feet. It is not communication. Or if it is, the message is: *I am here. I am here. I am here.* The repetition is not insistence. It is simply continuation. The creature calls because calling is what it does, the way the water seeps because seeping is what water does, and the call is part of the sump's acoustic baseline, indistinguishable from the machinery and the water and the chittering, a component of the total sound rather than a signal within it.

There are more than one. You realize this when two calls overlap, the second beginning before the first has fully decayed, the two tones interfering with each other in a way that creates a third tone, a difference tone, a phantom frequency that exists only in the air between the two callers, and for a moment the sump's sound has a *structure*, a harmonic, a thing that was not there before and will not be there when one of them stops, and this structure is not intentional. It is an artifact of density. Two things calling at the same time because they are in the same place because the place is full of them and the timing is not coordinated, just *coincident*, and the coincidence produces beauty, or something adjacent to beauty, a momentary order in a field of total saturation, and then it is gone.

One of them is close. You feel it before you see it — a pressure change in the water around your feet, a warmth that is warmer than the water's warmth, a body-heat, metabolically generated, and then a touch, a brush, something smooth and firm passing against your ankle, and it is not aggressive, it is not curious, it is *indifferent*, the creature moving through the space that you happen to occupy, the way the water moves through the space that the rock happens to occupy, and you are, for a moment, an obstacle in its path, and it goes around you, and the touch is gone, and the wake settles, and you are standing in water that has been displaced by a body you did not fully see and will not fully see, and the note comes again, from very close, and you feel it in your teeth.

## The Things You Do Not See

There are things here that you do not see. This is not a failure of observation. It is a property of the place. The sump is saturated with life at every scale, and the scale you operate at — human-scale, eye-scale, the scale of light and dark and recognizable shape — is one layer among many, and not the densest layer, and not the most important.

In the rock itself, in the pore structure that weeps water from its walls and ceiling, there are organisms. You cannot see them. They are the size of cells, or the size of colonies of cells, and they live in the film of water that exists between mineral grains, and they metabolize the minerals, and they produce the warmth, or some of it, and they are the reason the rock is still becoming rock, because their waste products bind the grains together or keep them apart, and the rock's status — solid or permeable, finished or unfinished — is partly determined by things that live in the water that lives in the rock, and these things are alive, and they are numerous beyond counting, and they do not know you are here.

In the water, in the pools, there are organisms smaller than the fish. You see them if you hold still and look down and let your eyes adjust to the scale: the water is not clear. It is *granular*. It is full of things, particles that move on their own, that have direction, that cluster and disperse in response to stimuli you cannot detect. These are the foundation. Everything above them — the fish, the wall-creatures, the ceiling colony, the amphibian-things — rests on this foundation, this invisible layer of life so small and so dense that the water is, in a functional sense, more alive than liquid.

And in the deeper water, in the dark pools where the terraces step down past your waist, past your chest, into water that is not water but *medium*, there are shapes. You see them with the part of your vision that is not vision, the part that registers displacement, pressure, the movement of a large mass through a dense medium. Something is down there. Several somethings. They are larger than the amphibian-things you have seen. They are slower. They do not call. They do not surface. They do not interact with the shallow system of fish and insects and guano that fills the sump's upper layers. They exist below it, in a zone that the lights do not reach, in water that is too warm and too dense and too full of dissolved material to be called water by any standard you learned on the surface, and they have been there longer than the lights, longer than the machines, longer than the rock has been rock.

You do not see them. You feel them, in the pressure changes, in the way the water moves when no other force is moving it, in the deep undertone of the sump's sound that lives below the machinery and below the fish and below the calls of the amphibian-things, a frequency so low it is geological, a vibration that is not a sound but a fact, a statement about mass and warmth and time and the persistence of something that has been here since before the sump was a sump and will be here after the sump is no longer a sump, and this something is alive, or was alive, or is doing something that has no word in your vocabulary but that the closest word is *alive*, and it does not care about you, and it does not care about the lights, and it does not care about the machines, and it does not care about the rock, because the rock is a temporary condition and it knows this, if it knows anything, the way the water knows the shape of the rock it flows through, not as knowledge but as *consequence*.

## The Person

There is a person.

You find them at the monitoring station, the one with the corrugated walls and the table and the paper readout coiled on the floor. They are sitting in a chair that has rusted into the platform, the metal legs sunk into the soft rock and fused there by corrosion and time, the chair a permanent fixture now, and the person in it.

No. Not in it. *Of* it. This is the difficulty.

The person is wearing clothes. You recognize this first — the shape of a jacket, the shape of boots, the shape of a human body in a seated posture, and for a moment the entire sump resolves into something comprehensible, a place where a person sits and works, and you almost call out, almost speak, and then you do not, because the jacket is not a jacket. It is the shape of a jacket, the *memory* of a jacket, the jacket's pattern preserved in something that is not fabric — a material that has the color of the fabric and the drape of the fabric and the texture of the fabric but that is, when you look closely, when you let your eyes adjust to the specific, a *deposit*. A mineral accretion. The jacket has been here so long that the water has laid down a layer of dissolved stone over it, molecule by molecule, the same process that builds stalactites, the same process that builds flowstone, and the jacket is now a fossil, a cast, a thing that was a jacket and is now rock that remembers being a jacket.

And the person inside the jacket.

You look. You have to look. The platform is thirty centimeters above the waterline and the person is sitting in the chair and the chair is rusted to the platform and the person has not moved. The wall-creatures are on them. Not many — not the density of the ceiling colony or the light-halos — but a layer, a scattering, attending to the surfaces that the person offers, which are the same surfaces they have offered for what must be years, decades, the mineral accretion suggesting a timescale that you cannot calculate without knowing the water's mineral content and the deposition rate and these are things you cannot know. The wall-creatures are doing what they do to every surface in the sump. They are attending. They are collecting. They are converting.

The person's face. You look at the face and the face is — there. It is there. It is not a skull. It is not remains, not in the way you expected. It is a face, with features, with the arrangement of eyes and nose and mouth that makes a human face, but the features are *soft*, softened by the same process that softened the jacket, the water and the mineral and the time working together to fill in the details, to round the angles, to make the face less a face and more a *surface*, a topography, a thing the water flows over and the wall-creatures attend to and the mist condenses on.

The person is not dead. This is not a statement about biology. This is a statement about the sump. The sump does not distinguish between the living and the dead because the sump does not distinguish between the solid and the permeable, between the rock and the water, between the machine and the geology. The person is *here*. They are part of the system. They have been part of the system for longer than the paper readout has been feeding, longer than the light-poles have been burning, maybe longer than the pipeline has been corroding. They sat down in this chair and they watched the numbers change and at some point the distinction between the watcher and the watched dissolved, the way the distinction between the water and the rock dissolved, the way every distinction in this place dissolves, and what is left is a process, a slow conversion of human material into sump material, the body's warmth absorbed into the water, the body's minerals returned to the rock, the body's shape preserved in the accretion that covers it, a fossil forming around a form that is still, in some sense, present.

The wall-creatures on the person's hands move with the same diligence they apply to the rock. The fish in the water beneath the platform are the descendants of fish that were here when the person sat down. The ceiling colony above has been born and died and been born again a hundred times in the span of this person's stillness. The amphibian-things call from the deeper pools and the call reverberates through the water and through the rock and through the platform and through the chair and through the person and they do not flinch, because flinching is a response for things that are still separate from their environment, and this person is not separate.

You stand there. You are wet and warm and the sound is total and the light is orange and the person in the chair does not move and you realize, slowly, the way the water rises through the rock, that the person is not a person anymore, not in the way you are a person. They are a *place*. They are a surface. They are a component of the sump, as much a feature of the geology as the stalactites or the flowstone or the guano bars. They are where the wall-creatures live. They are where the mineral accretion grows. They are where the mist condenses and the water pools and the warm dark presses in, and they have been here long enough that the sump has accepted them the way it accepts everything — completely, without comment, as material, as presence, as another fold in the saturation.

You leave the platform. You do not speak. There is nothing to say to a place, and this is a place now, a place shaped like a person shaped like a chair shaped like a purpose that drained out of it the way purpose drained out of the machines, slowly, from the inside, leaving the form intact but hollow, and the form persists, and the form is inhabited, and the inhabitants are the same inhabitants that inhabit everything else in the sump: the water, the creatures, the mineral, the time.

---

The sump is not empty. It has never been empty. It is, in fact, the opposite of empty — it is *full*, in the way that a sponge is full, in the way that a body is full, every space occupied, every surface attended, every niche filled, and the life here is not an exception to the geology or an intrusion into the industry but a *continuation* of both, the same process expressing itself at different scales, the water's patience becoming the rock's patience becoming the wall-creature's patience becoming the fish's patience becoming the ceiling colony's patience becoming the amphibian-thing's patience becoming the person-in-the-chair's patience becoming the sump's patience, which is the patience of something that is becoming and does not know what it is becoming and does not care, because the becoming is the point, the process is the product, the saturation is the only destination, and everything that lives here lives in the middle of that becoming, and will live there, and will be absorbed, and the middle will continue.
