# What Lives in Verrault

---

The people came first, or the engines did, and by now the distinction has dissolved into the same wet stone.

---

They are not many. Three hundred and forty-one, according to the last census taken by someone whose name is written in the engineering library in a hand so steady it could be engraved. That was eleven years ago. The number has shifted since then — a few births, a few departures, a few returns — but it has not shifted much, because Verrault does not attract and does not repel. It holds. The population moves within a narrow band, the way a tide moves within a narrow band, and the people who live here have the quality of things that have found their level.

They are not old, particularly. That is a surprise. You expect a town like this — remote, industrial, perpetually damp, deafening — to be a town of the elderly, a place where the young have fled and the old remain out of inertia. But Verrault has children. Not many. Enough. They are born in the bonnes maisons with the windows shuttered and the cork walls doing their inadequate best, and their first experience of the world is vibration, and their second is moisture, and their third is the face of their mother leaning close, mouthing words they will learn to read on lips before they learn to hear. The infants do not cry the way infants cry elsewhere. They produce a sound — a short, compressed vocalization, more grunt than wail — and then they stop, because the sound they make is so small against the sound that surrounds them that the effort of crying is not worth the return. They learn this in the first week. They learn most things in the first week.

The children grow up reading mouths. They grow up reading faces, reading the angle of a shoulder, reading the way someone sets down a tool — with care, with frustration, with the particular weariness that means the tool will need replacing soon and the replacement will have to be fetched from the supply building and the supply building is on the far side of the square and the square is wet and the walk will take seven minutes. They read the town the way other children read weather. They are not silent children. They make sounds among themselves, in the insulated interiors, chasing each other through the narrow hallways of the bonnes maisons, and their voices are high and sharp and strange — strange because they are rare, strange because they are the only bright sounds in the acoustic landscape, and they carry through the stone walls and out through the vents and are swallowed by the roar within a few meters, like minnows entering a dark river.

They climb the tree. The tree is theirs, really — no adult bothers with it. It is too short to offer shade, too stunted to offer challenge, too wet to be comfortable, but it is the only vertical thing in the central square that is not a building or a pylon, and the children have claimed it with the territorial clarity of children everywhere. They sit in its branches, which are thick and rough and bend only slightly under their weight, and they hang from the lower limbs with their feet above the slick stone, and they do not fall. They do not fall because they have grown up in a world where everything is always vibrating and their inner ears have calibrated to it, and their balance is extraordinary, and their grip is extraordinary, and they move through the tree with a sureness that looks like recklessness but is the opposite. They are the most certain creatures in Verrault.

---

The adults are harder to describe, because they do not describe themselves and they do not perform themselves and they have very little interest in being seen. They are not shy. They are not withdrawn. They are *efficient* — with their gestures, with their movements, with their attention. A woman walks across the square and you notice that she does not look up, does not glance around, does not scan. She is going to the supply building. She knows this. You know this because her trajectory is a straight line and her body is angled into it and her hands, hanging at her sides, are slightly cupped, which means she is carrying something small that she will set down when she arrives. You know all of this without hearing a word, and so does everyone else in the square, and this is how the town communicates — not in language but in *trajectory*, in the grammar of purposeful movement through a space that everyone shares and no one needs to narrate.

Their hands are remarkable. You notice this in the engine-houses, where the maintenance work happens, and where the sound is at its most concentrated, most physical, most *present*. The engineers — and almost everyone in Verrault is, to some degree, an engineer — work with their hands in a way that seems from the outside like blindness. They do not look at what they are doing. They feel it. The components of the moteurs lentes are large and simple — massive pistons, iron rods, leather-and-rubber seals, brass valve-housings the size of a man's head — and the engineers handle them by touch, by weight, by the specific vibration that each component transmits when it is seated correctly or incorrectly. A loose seal has a different frequency from a tight one. A cracked housing has a different resonance. The engineers run their hands along the metal and they listen with their fingers, and the sound of the engines is so total that their ears are useless for this work, and so their hands have become their ears, and their ears have become something else — a vestigial sense, a background hum, a thing they use for noticing the fog.

Their hands are rough. The condensation and the metal and the mineral-rich water do something to skin over years — it thickens it, toughens it, but also stains it. The older engineers have hands that are dark in the creases, dark along the whorls of their fingerprints, as though the iron has entered the skin and become part of it. Their nails are thick and flat and blunt. Their palms are calloused in patterns that correspond to the tools they use most — a ridge along the base of the thumb where the wrench sits, a smooth patch on the heel of the palm where the hammer handle rests, a roughness across the fingertips that is not callous but texture, the skin having grown into a kind of fine grip, as though the hands themselves are becoming tools. They do not wear gloves. Gloves would mute the vibration, and the vibration is information.

---

There is a chief engineer. There has always been a chief engineer. The current one is a woman named Hélène Tourvel, or rather, she is named something that the sign language abbreviates as a flat hand brought to the sternum and then turned outward, which means *the one who is responsible*, and her name in the regional dialect is Hélène Tourvel, and she writes in her journal every evening in the chapel-library with a pencil that she sharpens with a knife, and the pencil shavings fall onto the stone floor and are swept up in the morning by whoever arrives first, and this has been happening for six generations of chief engineers, and the floor is slightly concave from the sweeping.

Her journal is not a diary. It is a log — of engine cycles, of maintenance schedules, of pressure readings from the aquifer bore-pipes, of the small variations in the sound that indicate changes in the system. But it is also, in its margins and between its entries, a record of a life. She writes: *Engine 14 running rough, third cycle lags by a half-second, probable seal wear, scheduled for Tuesday.* And below that, in smaller letters: *Fog came early. M. brought figs from the lowlands, too ripe, we ate them anyway.* And below that: *The aquifer pressure is unchanged. I check it every morning and every morning it is unchanged and this is either a comfort or a concern and I have stopped deciding which.*

She is fifty-three. She has been chief engineer for fourteen years. She will be chief engineer until she is not, and then someone else will be, and the journal will pass to them, and they will write in it with the same pencil (sharpened with the same knife, or its replacement), and the floor will continue its slow concavity. She has the Verrault accent — flat, compressed, subdued — and she uses it rarely, only in the insulated rooms, and when she speaks her voice has the quality of something being released under pressure, carefully, through a narrow opening. She does not raise her voice. No one in Verrault raises their voice. Raising your voice here is like pouring a cup of water into the ocean — the gesture is absurd, the result is nothing, and everyone who watches you do it understands that you do not understand where you are.

She knows where she is. She knows the engines the way you know your own breathing — not as information but as condition, as state, as the thing that is happening while everything else happens. She can feel when an engine is off-cycle the way you feel when you have stood up too quickly. It is not a thought. It is a physical sensation, a wrongness in the vibration, and she will stop mid-step in the square and turn her head slightly, not listening but *attending*, and then she will adjust her trajectory toward the engine-house in question and the problem will be addressed and the sound will resume its total consistency and she will continue walking.

She has a life outside the engines. This is important to say, because the town is not a monastery and the people are not monks. She has a partner, a quiet man named Renard who tends the general supply and who bakes a bread that is dense and slightly damp, like everything in Verrault, and that tastes of the mineral water he uses to make it. They have a daughter, twelve, who climbs the tree and who will not be chief engineer — that role does not pass by blood — but who will be something, who is already something, who sits in the branches of the stunted tree and feels the town through the bark and knows things about the engines that she cannot yet name.

---

The others. There are others. There is the man who walks the rail line every morning at first light, checking the ties, though no train has come from the lowlands in nine months and the last one carried only replacement seals and a crate of lamp-glass. There is the woman who teaches the children in a room above the municipal hall, teaching them the sign language and the regional dialect and the engineering schematics, because in Verrault these are all the same subject — to speak is to signal is to maintain is to live. There are the two brothers who work the night shift in the northern engine-houses and who are said to have a game they play, a competition of sorts, where each tries to identify which engines have entered maintenance rotation by the feel of the floor beneath their feet, and one of them is always right and the other is always close, and they have played this game for twenty years and it has never become boring because the sound is never exactly the same twice.

There are the old ones. Verrault has old ones, people in their seventies and eighties who have never left the plateau, who were born in the roar and will die in it, and whose bodies have taken on the quality of the place — dense, settled, slightly damp, moving with the deliberate patience of stone. They sit in the cafe, which is not a cafe in the way you imagine a cafe — no chatter, no clatter, no ambient music — but a room where people sit and drink a bitter local tea and communicate in small gestures and the occasional raised eyebrow and the reading of each other's breath, which is visible in the cool air. The old ones have a stillness that is not the stillness of age but the stillness of things that have found their place in a vibration and are content to resonate. They do not fear the engines stopping. They do not think about the engines stopping. The engines do not stop. This is not faith. This is observation.

---

And the other lives. The small lives. The lives that are not human but that have found the frequency and made it home.

The pale crickets in the ventilation shafts are the most successful. They are nearly translucent, the color of the condensation that forms on the metal, and they live in the warm air that flows out of the engine-house vents, and they eat the thin film of algae that grows on the damp interior surfaces. They do not chirp. They have lost the ability, or never had it, or had it and abandoned it as useless. They communicate through substrate vibration — tapping their legs against the metal ductwork in patterns that are, in their own way, a language, though no one has studied it, because no one has thought to, because the crickets are not a problem and Verrault does not attend to things that are not problems. They are everywhere in the engine-houses, in every vent, in every shaft, and if you stand inside an engine-house and close your eyes and try to subtract the engine sound from your perception — which is impossible, but the attempt is instructive — you can almost feel them, a fine tremor beneath the tremor, a life within the machine.

The worms in the drainage channels are less visible but more numerous. They are thin and dark and they live in the mineral-rich water that flows through the stone cuts, and they process the runoff — the oil, the iron oxide, the calcium deposits — and they have been doing this for so long that the drainage channels are, in a sense, their architecture. They built the channels. Not the stone part — the stone was cut by the founders — but the smooth interior surface, the biofilm that lines the channels and makes them flow efficiently, that is the worms' work, a century and a half of microbial masonry, and the channels would clog without them and the town would flood, and so the worms are, in their way, as essential as the engineers. No one mentions this. It is understood.

The bats — if they exist — live in the upper structures of the pylons, in the iron lattice where the cables connect, and they are said to be large and dark and silent. They are said to hunt in the fog, navigating by the scattered acoustic signatures of the engines, each fog droplet a tiny mirror for sound, and the bats reading the reflections the way the engineers read the vibration. No one has seen one clearly. They are a rumor that the children believe and the adults do not disbelieve, and at night, in the fog, when the sound becomes *lisible* and the town is readable, someone will sometimes point upward, toward the top of a pylon, and make the sign for *there*, and whoever is with them will look up and see nothing, or see something, or see a shape that might be a bat or might be a fog-shadow or might be the eye's attempt to find form in a place where form is always dissolving.

---

The tree lives. It should be said. The tree is alive. It is not dying, not stunted by poison or disease or neglect. It has simply reached the size that this place allows and it has stopped. Its roots go deep — deeper than you would expect, into the saturated basalt, into the fractures where the warm water rises — and it drinks constantly, perpetually, the way everything in Verrault drinks, and it has been drinking for forty years and it has not grown in thirty, and this is not failure. This is *arrival*. The tree has arrived at its form. It is a thing that has completed itself, and it stands in the square with the patience of something finished, and the children climb it because it is the only thing in Verrault that is done.

---

And the town itself lives. This is the strangest thing to say and the truest. Verrault is not a collection of buildings and people and engines. It is a single organism, a body with a circulation — the water, the power, the drainage — and a nervous system — the sound, the vibration, the reading of the fog — and a metabolism — the aquifer below, the heat rising, the engines cycling, the slow conversion of geological pressure into electricity and noise and moisture. The people are its cells. The engines are its organs. The crickets are its flora, its internal ecosystem, the things that live in the gut and keep it working. The fog is its breath. The tree is its... the tree is its stillness, the place where the body has decided to rest.

It does not grow. It does not shrink. It does not change, or it changes so slowly that the change is geological, measured in the concavity of the chapel-library floor and the staining of the engineers' hands and the slow weathering of the stone, and these are not changes but *patinations*, the way a river-smoothed stone is not a changed stone but a stone that has become more itself. Verrault is becoming more itself. It has been becoming more itself for a hundred and sixty years. It will continue. The aquifer will press upward. The basalt will hold. The engines will cycle. The chief engineer will write. The children will climb. The fog will come and go and come again. And the sound — the sound that is not a noise but a presence, not an event but a medium — the sound will continue, and within it, the people of Verrault will live their slow, damp, certain lives, and they will not leave, or they will leave and return, and they will not explain this to anyone, because explanation requires a voice raised above the roar, and no one in Verrault raises their voice, because they know what lives in the sound, and what lives in the sound is enough.
