# What Lives Here

---

## The Frogs

They are not named. They may not need to be — they may be the only ones of their kind, a species of one pocket, a population that has never left this room and never will.

They are small. The largest is perhaps four centimeters from snout to vent, most closer to three. Their bodies are the color of the condensation that falls around them — not transparent, but *translucent*, the pale gray-blue of frosted glass, and through their ventral skin you can see the slow pulse of their hearts, the dark thread of intestine, the occasional bright dot of a recently caught mite. Their dorsal surfaces carry a faint mottling, darker gray on gray, which resolves — if you look closely, if you hold one up to the bioluminescent light — into a pattern not unlike the grain of the root-bark they cling to. They are camouflaged, but not for any exterior world. They are camouflaged for *here*. Their coloration matches the mineralized wood of their own room.

Their toe-pads are remarkable. Each pad is a translucent disc, slightly concave, producing adhesion through a combination of wet friction and molecular attraction — the same physics that holds a tree frog to a leaf, but refined, exaggerated. When a frog shifts position, the detachment makes a soft *pop*, audible in the quiet pockets between engine-groans, and the reattachment makes a second, quieter *click*. Hundreds of frogs shifting at irregular intervals produce a scattered, arrhythmic percussion — a counterpoint to the steady four-second pulse of the great pumping roots. The frogs are the jazz. The engines are the bass line.

They do not vocalize. This is strange, for frogs. Their throats pulse — the rhythmic pumping of buccal respiration, flex and relax, flex and relax — but no sound emerges. The throat-sac is present, developed, capable of inflation, but it inflates silently and deflates silently, a gesture without a voice. Perhaps there is no need. Perhaps in a room where the acoustic floor is this high, where the diesel-idle groan and the hissing valves and the tearing-paper mandibles fill every frequency band a small frog might use, song would be wasted effort. Or perhaps they lost the habit long ago, in the same long ago when they lost the ability to leave, and now the throat-sac is a vestige, an organ that remembers its function the way a severed nerve remembers its signal.

They eat what the spiders catch — not the condensation, but the small flying insects that find their way through the fissure, drawn by the warmth and the light, and enter the pocket to discover there is no exit and no food source and no mate, only a warm wet room full of things that eat them. The frogs wait on their root-perches and take these insects from the air with a tongue-flick so fast it is less a movement than an absence — the insect is there, and then it is not, and the frog's jaw works once, twice, and the throat-sac pulses, and the moment is over. There are not many insects. Enough. The population is stable. It has been stable for a long time.

They do not have tadpoles. Or rather — they do, but you would not recognize them as such. The eggs are laid in the low spots of the floor-mat, in the shallow pools that form between root-fibers, and the tadpoles that emerge are not aquatic in the conventional sense. They are long, thin, almost eel-like, and they live *within* the root-mat, burrowing through the saturated fibers, breathing through skin and through the thin films of water that coat every surface. They do not swim. They tunnel. They eat the bacteria and fungi that break down the root-mat from below, and they are eaten in turn by the flat arthropods that patrol the floor's surface — the arthropods' mandibles can reach into the mat's upper layers, and a tunneling tadpole is a meal. Those that survive metamorphose inside the mat and emerge from it as miniature adults, already clinging, already still, already gray-blue and translucent and pulsing. The cycle is closed. The room produces exactly as many frogs as it consumes, and the number has been the same for longer than anyone has been counting.

A frog can live, here, for a very long time. There is nothing to kill them — no predator large enough, no disease that can gain purchase in a population this small and this isolated, no winter, no drought, no famine. They die of age, eventually, or of the slow failure of a specific organ, and when they die they do not fall. Their toe-pads hold them to the root-bark even after death, and their bodies dry slowly in the warm air, mummify, and are consumed in place by the fungi that coat every surface. A root-bark perch with a dead frog on it will, over weeks, develop a fine white bloom of mold, and the mold will reduce the frog to a faint gray smear, and the smear will be absorbed into the bark, and the root will carry those minerals upward — to the thing above, which does not know it is being fed by its own basement.

## The Arthropods

They are not centipedes. They are not millipedes. They are something that has no common name because they have never been common, because they have never been anywhere but here.

Their bodies are flat — dramatically flat, dorsoventrally compressed to a thickness of perhaps two millimeters in adults, which allows them to move through the upper layers of the root-mat the way a sheet of paper slides between the pages of a book. They are long, five to seven centimeters, with a body plan that suggests a millipede's architecture — many segments, many legs — but the legs are not uniform. The first three pairs are directed forward and are slightly longer, sensory, used for probing the substrate ahead. The remaining pairs — and there are many, perhaps forty on a large adult — are directed downward and outward, and they do not walk. They *swim* through the fibrous matrix, pushing against root-fibers the way a swimmer pushes against water, and the effect is a slow, steady, gliding motion that looks less like locomotion and more like drifting. But it is not drifting. It is deliberate. Each individual is going somewhere.

They process the floor. This is their function, though function is the wrong word — it implies design, and nothing here was designed. But they *do* something, and what they do is this: they eat the dead layers of the root-mat. The mat is constantly growing, constantly being added to from above by new root-fiber, and constantly dying from below as the deeper layers are compressed, mineralized, turned to stone. The arthropods live at the boundary — the zone where the fiber is dead but not yet stone, still soft enough to chew, still fibrous enough to be nutritious. They eat this layer. They digest it. They excrete, and their excrement is a fine, dense paste that fills the interstices of the mat, compresses, and becomes the substrate from which new root-fiber grows. They are not decomposers. They are *manufacturers*. They convert dead root-mat into the material that becomes new root-mat, and the loop is closed, and the floor maintains its depth, and the floor has maintained its depth for as long as anyone has measured.

Their mandibles are the source of the tearing-paper sound. Each mandible is a flat, blade-like structure, serrated on the inner edge, and they work in opposition — not scissoring, but *scraping*, one holding the fiber while the other peels it apart, layer by layer, the way you might peel a thin sheet of bark from a branch. The sound is the fiber separating. A single arthropod feeding is nearly inaudible. A thousand arthropods feeding, distributed across the floor-mat, each working at its own pace, produces the sound — a soft, pervasive, multidirectional tearing, like someone opening a letter in every direction at once, very quietly, forever.

They move in convoys. Not herds — convoys. There is a directionality to their movement that suggests not flocking but *traffic*. Individuals follow chemical trails laid down by those ahead of them, and the trails are renewed by those behind, and the effect is a slow, steady procession that moves across the floor-mat in broad arcs, processing as it goes, turning the dead fiber into paste and leaving behind a surface that is clean, dense, and slightly darker than the surrounding mat — freshly manufactured substrate, ready for new growth. The convoys do not collide. When two trails cross, the individuals at the intersection pause, touch antennae — and they do have antennae, long, fine, held forward like a centipede's — and then proceed, each following its own trail, each going its own way. The pause is less than a second. It looks like courtesy. It is not courtesy. It is a chemical handshake, a confirmation of identity, a check that the other convoy is *the same kind* — because in this room, everything is the same kind, and everything is in agreement, and the traffic flows.

They do not have predators inside the mat. The spiders are too large to follow them into the fibers. The frogs do not hunt them — they are too flat, too large, too deeply embedded in the substrate. The tadpoles share their space, and the arthropods eat the tadpoles when they can catch them, and this is the only predation that occurs in the floor-mat, and it is enough. The convoys flow. The mandibles tear. The paste is laid down. The floor is maintained.

## The Spiders

They are the most patient things in the pocket.

They sit in the crevices of the root-ceiling, in the gaps where one root presses against another and a narrow shelf of space remains — a centimeter, maybe two, enough for a body. They are not large. The largest has a legspan of perhaps five centimeters, a body length of two. Their legs are very long in proportion to their bodies, thin, slightly flattened, and they hold them in a characteristic posture — not spread radially like most spiders, but folded close to the body, directed downward and forward, so that the spider's silhouette is not a star but a *teardrop*, pointed at the rear, broad at the front, where the legs gather and the head waits.

They build no webs. This is not unusual for spiders — many families have abandoned web-building for active hunting or ambushing — but these spiders have abandoned hunting itself. They do not hunt. They *collect*.

The condensation droplets that form on the root-ceiling grow heavy and fall. Most fall freely, ticking against the floor-mat below. But some — a fraction, a percentage determined by the geometry of the ceiling and the spacing of the spiders — fall past a crevice, and in that crevice a spider waits, and as the droplet passes, a single leg extends, touches the droplet, holds it. The droplet adheres to the leg's surface — which is hydrophilic, unlike the rest of the spider's body, which is hydrophobic, a waxy cuticle that sheds water. Only the legs are wettable. Only the legs can hold a droplet.

The spider brings the leg to its mouth. It drinks. It folds the leg back. It waits.

This is their entire lives. They sit. They wait. They extend a leg. They drink. They fold. They sit. They wait. The rhythm is not their own — it is the rhythm of condensation, the rhythm of the room, the rhythm of water forming on a surface and growing heavy and falling. The spiders have synchronized themselves to the physics of their space. They are not active agents. They are *fixtures*. They are components of the water-collection system, and they have arranged themselves across the ceiling the way you might arrange gutters on a roof — at intervals, at the points where droplets are most likely to fall, in the crevices where a leg can reach the falling water.

They eat rarely. The insects that enter through the fissure are taken by the frogs, mostly, but a few — a few — blunder into the ceiling crevices, and the spider is there, and the legs are there, and the insect is caught and bitten and consumed, and the spider returns to waiting. This happens perhaps once a week. Once a month. The spiders can go longer. They are not starving. They are not hungry. They are simply *present*, doing the thing they do, at the pace the room requires.

Their reproduction is a mystery. No one has found an egg sac. No one has found a juvenile. The population is stable — the same number of spiders, in the same crevices, on every visit — but no one has seen them breed, and no one has seen them young. It is possible that they reproduce at a rate so slow it has never been observed — one egg sac per decade, perhaps, one juvenile surviving to replace one adult that has died of age. It is possible that the room produces them the way it produces condensation — at a rate determined not by biology but by physics, by the equilibrium of the system, by the set point that the pocket holds. It is possible that they are older than they look. It is possible that they have been in those crevices, in those positions, with those legs folded in those teardrop silhouettes, for longer than anyone has been counting.

## The Fungi

The bioluminescent patches on the ceiling are not a single organism. They are a consortium — a community of fungal species living in close association, each contributing a different component to the light. There is a primary species, a basidiomycete with flat, shelf-like fruiting bodies that press against the root-bark and glow with a steady blue-white light, but its glow is faint alone. The intensity comes from the bacterial endosymbionts that live within its hyphae — not bacteria in the conventional sense, but deeply integrated cellular components, organelles that were once free-living and are now part of the fungal machinery, producing light as a byproduct of their metabolism the way a firefly produces light or a deep-sea fish produces light, except that this light does not flash. It does not pulse. It does not vary. It is a steady glow, constant in intensity, constant in color, constant in every measurable parameter, and it has been constant for as long as anyone has measured.

The light serves no purpose that anyone can identify. Nothing here is attracted to it. Nothing here is repelled by it. The frogs do not use it for navigation. The insects that enter through the fissure are drawn by the warmth, not the light. The fungi do not need to attract anything — they are not dispersing spores, not fruiting in a way that requires a vector. The light may be an accident. A metabolic byproduct that has no cost and no benefit and no reason to be selected against. It simply *is*. The room glows because the fungi glow because the bacteria inside the fungi glow because that is what those bacteria do, and nothing has stopped them, and so they continue.

But the light does something, whether or not it means to. It makes the room visible. It makes the room *a room* — a space with walls and a ceiling and a floor, all illuminated, all present, all there. Without the light, the pocket would be darkness and sound and warmth — a sensory experience of being inside something, but not of seeing it. The light gives the room its quality of *place*. It is because of the light that you can see the frogs on their perches, the arthropods in their convoys, the spiders in their crevices, the great pumping roots at the far wall. It is because of the light that the room is not just a body but a *chamber* — a space that can be perceived, that can be described, that can be remembered.

The fungi do not know this. The bacteria do not know this. The light is not for you. But it is there, and you are there, and you can see, and the room is visible, and that is enough.

## The Thing Above

You cannot see it from inside. You can only infer it.

The roots that form the ceiling are part of something — a single organism, presumably, a plant of enormous scale and age, rooted in the limestone shelf, drawing water from the pocket below and carrying it upward through vessels that are, in this room, exposed and visible and audible. The pumping roots at the far wall are this organism's heart, or one of its hearts — a peristaltic engine that lifts water against gravity through a body that may be tens of meters tall, that may be hundreds of years old, that may be the largest living thing in the area, or the region, or further. You do not know. You cannot see it. You can only hear it, and feel its warmth, and stand inside the architecture of its roots.

It does not know you are here. This is almost certainly true. The pocket is an accident — a space that formed as the roots grew into the limestone, as water dissolved the stone, as the root-mat accumulated and the fissure opened and the room took shape. The thing above did not design this room. It did not build the engines. It did not place the frogs or the arthropods or the spiders. It grew, and the growing made a space, and the space filled with life, and the life found its equilibrium, and the equilibrium is the room you are standing in.

But the room is part of the thing above. It is inside its body. The warmth is its warmth. The water is its water. The engines are its engines, doing its work, pumping its fluids, keeping it alive. The frogs live in its body. The arthropods process its dead skin. The spiders drink its condensation. The fungi light its interior. Everything here is part of the thing above, even the things that are not the thing above, and the thing above is part of everything here, even though it does not know it, even though it does not notice, even though it will not notice when you leave.

You are inside something alive. It is warm. It is loud. It is steady. It has been steady for a very long time. And what lives here — the frogs, the arthropods, the spiders, the fungi, the bacteria, the thing above — is not a collection of organisms but a single system, a single machine, a single body doing what bodies do, which is to continue, which is to persist, which is to hold a set point in a warm wet room at the base of a limestone shelf and pump and pump and pump and not stop.
