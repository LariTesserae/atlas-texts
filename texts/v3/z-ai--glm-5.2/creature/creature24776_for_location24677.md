# What Lives Here

---

The frogs do not think about living here. That is important to understand. They do not consider it. They sit in the channels with the patience of things that have been shaped by sitting in channels, and when an insect touches the water's surface near them, the tongue comes out — a motion so fast it seems to bypass time — and then they are sitting again, as if nothing happened, as if the world is a channel and a tongue and nothing else. Their eyes are gold. In the oculus light, hundreds of gold eyes float in the shallow grooves like coins dropped by the careless dead. They do not look at you. They do not look at each other. They look at the space above the water, which is where food comes from, and they wait for it with a devotion that looks like stupidity but is actually something older than intelligence.

They have been here long enough that their bodies have changed. Not dramatically — you would still call them frogs if you saw one in your hand. But their skin is darker than it should be, darker than their ancestors who first found the channels and dropped down into them. Their legs are shorter. The webbing between their toes is thinner, because they do not swim anymore; they sit. They have adapted to the channel the way water adapts to a pipe — by becoming the shape of it. A frog born in the eastern channel lives its life in the eastern channel, calls from the eastern channel, dies in the eastern channel, and its body is carried by the slow water toward the basin, where the thing underneath receives it without comment.

They call because they are frogs. That is the whole reason. But the calling has become something more than reason. The chamber takes each call and returns it altered, and the frogs hear the alteration and answer it, and the chamber takes that answer and alters it again, and the frogs answer again, and what you hear — that low, resonant, layered *grrrk-grrrk-grrrk* that fills the vault like a tide — is not frogs calling. It is a conversation between frogs and stone, between the living and the architecture, and neither side knows it is participating. The frogs call because they are frogs. The echoes change because the echoes change. The conversation exists without anyone's consent.

There are generations upon generations here. The oldest frogs, the largest, sit nearest the basin, where the water is deepest and the insects gather thickest. The youngest, the smallest, the ones whose gold eyes are still more gray than gold, sit in the outer channels near the walls, where the water is shallowest and the light is dimmest. This is not hierarchy. It is not organization. It is simply the result of years of sitting and growing and slowly, imperceptibly shifting downstream. The channels are a life-path, carved in stone, leading from the walls to the basin. The frogs travel it in one direction, over the course of their entire lives, without ever moving on purpose.

---

The not-bats have no name. They should not need one. But you are here, and you are the kind of creature that names things, so: they are here, and they are not bats, and they are not anything else either.

They are small — the body of a mouse, the wingspan of a sparrow. Their membranes are the thing that makes them themselves. In the dimness of the chamber they are nearly invisible, dark-bodied creatures against dark stone, but when the oculus light catches them — when they drop from the ceiling in those silent cascades and sweep through the column of pale gray — the membranes light up like stained glass in a cathedral, and you can see the veins branching through them, and the blood moving in the veins, and the whole fragile architecture of a living wing held taut against air. The color is not a color. It is a quality of thinness — the membranes are so translucent that they do not so much glow as *transmit*, taking whatever light exists and passing it through themselves, slightly changed, slightly softened, so that the column of light is filled with flickering ghosts of itself as the not-bats pass through.

They chitter. This is the wrong word — it implies a mechanical sound, a rodent sound, a small brain producing small signals. What they produce is more like a song compressed to the size of a thought. The frequency sits right at the threshold of human hearing, so you feel it before you hear it — a buzz in the molars, a hum in the mastoid bone behind the ear, a pressure in the sinuses — and then you hear it, and it is not one sound but a woven texture of sounds, each not-bat producing its own thread, the threads braiding together in the vault's acoustics into something that is almost a chord. They are navigating. They are locating themselves in space, building a sonic map of the chamber by the reflections that return to their faces from the walls and the ceiling and the water and each other. It is practical. It is survival. But the chamber takes their practical sounds and gives them back rearranged, and sometimes — not often, not predictably — the rearrangement sounds like meaning.

When this happens, the not-bats do not react. They do not notice. They continue their sweeps, their insect-sorts, their return to the ceiling joints where they hang in clusters that look like dark fruit. But *you* notice, if you are there, if you are quiet enough, if you have been there long enough that your hearing has recalibrated itself to the chamber's frequency. You hear a phrase. Not a word — words are too structured, too human. A phrase. A shape of sound that seems to open a door and then close it before you can see what is behind the door. And then it is gone, and the chittering is chittering again, and the not-bats are not-bats, and you are left with a feeling that is not quite understanding and not quite wonder but is something in between, something that lives in the space where understanding and wonder share a wall.

They sleep during the brightest part of the day, when the oculus light is at its most direct, pressing themselves into the joints between ceiling blocks with their wings folded flat, their faces tucked into the dark crevices. At these hours the chamber is quieter — the frogs call, but the upper register of sound is missing, the chittering is absent, the sonic texture is incomplete. The not-bats' absence is as audible as their presence. The chamber seems to wait for them. When the light begins to weaken and the air cools, they emerge — not all at once but in a slow unzippering, one cluster at a time, the dark fruit releasing itself from the branches — and the sound fills back in, and the chamber is whole again.

They do not live long. Two winters, maybe three. But they reproduce in the ceiling joints, in the warm dark spaces where the stone is tightest, and the young are born knowing the chamber the way the chamber wants to be known — by sound, by echo, by the shape of the space as it returns to them through their own voices. They have never been anywhere else. They will never go anywhere else. The oculus is there, open to the sky, and they do not fly through it. Not because they cannot, but because the sky is not a concept they possess. The chamber is the world. The ceiling is the sky. The stone is the ground, inverted, and the water below is the only water, and the insects in the light are the only food, and this is enough. This has always been enough.

---

The insects are the smallest inhabitants and the most numerous, and they are also the most invisible, which is a form of power. The column of light from the oculus is their territory — a vertical column of warm air and illumination that is, to them, a country, a continent, a universe. They breed in the water channels, in the thin film of organic matter that collects along the edges where the stone is wettest. They emerge as adults and rise into the light, and they stay there, circling, swarming, forming dense columns of wing-beats that look, from below, like a solid thing, like a pillar of smoke that is not smoke but is alive and is made of thousands of bodies each so small that it is nothing, and all together they are something, and the something is food for everything else.

They do not think. They do not need to. They are the reason the frogs are here and the reason the not-bats are here and the reason the thing in the basin surfaces at dusk to feed on whatever falls into the water. They are the foundation of the economy. They are the smallest unit of the clockwork, the gear that drives the other gears, and they exist in such numbers that even the constant predation — tongues and wings and whatever the basin-thing does — does not diminish them. Every evening, the column of light is full of them. Every morning, the floor channels are full of their larvae. The cycle is so tight that there is no slack, no surplus, no waste. Everything that is born is eaten. Everything that is eaten becomes something else that is born. This is not cruelty. This is the shape of the place.

---

The thing in the basin does not want to be described. This is not a figure of speech. It is a recognition that what lives in the dark water of the central basin has arranged its existence around not being seen, and to describe it is to violate the terms of its tenancy.

But you can know this: it is longer than your arm. It moves by undulation — a lateral wave that passes through a body that is not quite fish, not quite eel, not quite anything that has a common name. Its skin, on the one occasion it surfaced close enough to the oculus light to be partially seen, was dark and smooth and reflected the light the way oil reflects light, with a sheen that is less about color and more about texture, about the molecular arrangement of a surface that has evolved to be private. It has eyes, or it has something that serves the function of eyes — two points on the forward end of its body that catch light differently from the surrounding skin, and that appeared, in that brief glimpse, to be looking upward at the oculus with an expression that is impossible to attribute to a non-human face but which might, if you are generous, if you are the kind of person who sees intention in the movements of animals, be called *curiosity*.

Or *listening*. The thing in the basin listens. The frogs call, and the echoes answer, and the not-bats chitter, and the chamber rearranges all of it, and the thing in the dark water lies at the bottom of the basin and receives the sound through its body, through the water, through the stone — and something happens to it that no one has ever observed, because observation requires light, and light is the one thing the basin-thing does not permit.

It eats frogs. This is known because the frog population does not grow despite the constant reproduction, and because occasionally, in the quietest hours, when the frogs have settled and the calling is at its lowest, there is a sound from the basin — a wet, sudden *snap* that could be a jaw closing, could be a body taken — and then silence, a longer silence than usual, before the frogs resume.

It also eats whatever washes down from the channels — organic matter, dead insects, the occasional crustacean that lives in the overflow and sometimes ventures too close to the basin's edge. It is the apex predator of the chamber, the largest body in a closed system, and it lives in the deepest part of the architecture, in the place furthest from the light, in the one spot where the builders placed a different, darker stone, as if they knew — or hoped, or feared — that something would eventually find its way down there and stay.

---

The moss is alive. It would be absurd to spend much time on it, except that it is the only plant in the chamber, and it is alive, and aliveness in such a place is not nothing.

It is *Brachythecium* or something close to it — a common moss, the kind that grows on damp stone anywhere in the temperate world, unremarkable except for its location. It has no business being here. The light is too thin, the stone too dense, the competition from animals too total. And yet it holds. A dark green ribbon, a handspan wide, two forearms long, following a crack in the eastern wall where water has found a permanent path. It does not flower. It does not fruit. It does not spread. It simply persists, photosynthesizing at the very bottom of the light's tolerance, pulling moisture from the crack, pulling minerals from the stone, growing so slowly that a year of its life would be invisible to a human observer.

If you pressed your hand to it — and you could, it is at chest height on the eastern wall, easy to reach — you would feel wetness, and softness, and the faintest warmth, the metabolic heat of a living plant doing the slow work of being alive. You might also feel, if you are the kind of person who feels such things, a kind of *stubbornness* in it — not consciousness, not will, but the particular quality of something that has been refusing to die for a very long time and has gotten very good at it. The moss is not impressive. The moss is not strange. The moss is a small green sentence in a gray paragraph, and it is the only thing in this chamber that is not an animal, and it holds its ground with the quiet conviction of something that has no other option.

---

And then there is the chamber itself.

This is the part that is hardest to write, because it requires saying something that should not be said about a room made of stone by people who are dead. But you have read the description. You have noticed the echoes. You have noticed the light. You have noticed that the chamber does things with sound that stone should not do, that the oculus admits light that is not the light of any sky, that the frogs go silent at the right moments as if they have been told to, that the not-bats fold their wings as if they have been asked to. You have noticed.

The chamber is not conscious. That would be too simple a word, too human a word, and the chamber is not human. The chamber is not alive in any way that biology would recognize. It has no cells. It has no metabolism. It has no nervous system. It is stone and water and the air between them.

But the chamber *does.* It does things. It takes sound in and gives it back changed. It takes light in and gives it back changed. It holds the temperature steady. It holds the water level steady. It holds the balance of species in a equilibrium so precise that the removal of any one — the frogs, the not-bats, the insects, the basin-thing, even the moss — would collapse the whole system. It has been doing this for centuries. It has been doing this without input, without correction, without any mechanism that a scientist would accept as regulatory. And yet it regulates. And yet it persists. And yet, at certain hours, when the light comes through the oculus at that impossible angle in that impossible color, it holds itself still — the way a living thing holds its breath.

Call it what you want. Call it emergent behavior. Call it the interaction of mineral and moisture and acoustics. Call it the ghost of the builders' intention, encoded in the shape of the vault, persisting in the stone like a thought persists in a neural pathway long after the conscious mind has forgotten it. Call it nothing. Call it a coincidence of geometry and groundwater.

But if you stand in the center of the chamber, beside the basin, in the gray light, and you say a word — any word, your own name, or the name of someone you love, or a word for water, or a word for stone — and you listen to what comes back, you will hear something. Not the word. Not exactly. The syllables, rearranged. The consonants, softened or sharpened. The vowels, stretched or compressed. Something that is almost your word, almost your voice, almost your meaning, but filtered through whatever the chamber is, whatever the chamber does, and given back to you as a question.

And the frogs will call, and the not-bats will chitter, and the insects will hum, and the thing in the basin will move in its dark water, and the chamber will be full of sound again, and the question will dissolve into the noise, and you will not get an answer.

But you will know that something heard you. Something that was made by hands. Something that has been listening ever since.
