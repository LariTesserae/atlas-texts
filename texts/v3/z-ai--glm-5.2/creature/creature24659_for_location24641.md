# What Lives on the Opener's Plateau

---

## The First Inhabitants (Before Everything Else)

The darks came first. Or rather — the darks were never not here. They are the condition from which all other conditions arise, the way soil is the condition from which grass arises, except the darks are not soil and what grows from them is not grass. They are the fourth kingdom, the one that has no name in any taxonomy built under a sun, because sunlit taxonomies begin with the premise that light is the engine of life, and here light is a byproduct. A waste product. A thing the darks excrete while eating stone.

They are small. They are everywhere. They live in the roots of the platform trees, in the stems of the climbing vines, in the thalli of the ground-cover mosses, in the bark of the trunk-zone growths, in the floating mats of the valley wetlands, in the biofilm that coats the ancient engines, in the water itself — the rivers carry them, the standing pools harbor them, even the mist that condenses on the underside of the cloud deck contains airborne colonies, drifting, metabolizing, glowing that faint blue-green that is the only light the plateau produces on its own. They are the plateau's chemosynthetic substrate. They eat hydrogen sulfide from the geothermal vents. They eat methane that seeps up through the basalt's capillaries. They eat the mineral residue of ten thousand years of decomposition, reprocessing it, cycling it, closing the loop so that nothing is ever truly lost. In exchange — though it is not exchange, it is something older and more intimate than exchange — they feed the plants they live within, and they glow.

The glow is not for anything. It is not communication. It is not attraction. It is simply what happens when a dark metabolizes: a photon is released, a faint blue-green photon, and it escapes the cell membrane and enters the air and is absorbed by moisture within a meter or two, and that is the end of it. The glow does not illuminate. It *outlines*. It turns every surface into a suggestion of itself, a dim phosphorescence that clings to form like condensation clings to glass. You see the plateau not as shapes in darkness but as darkness with shapes painted on it in a wash of blue-green so faint it is almost a feeling rather than a sight.

Everything that lives here lives in the darks' light. Everything that lives here lives in the darks' food chain. They are the first inhabitants and the most numerous and the least visible and the most essential, and they do not know any of this, because they do not know anything, because knowing is not a thing they do. They eat. They glow. They persist.

## The Trees (And What Lives In Them)

The platform trees are the architecture of the plateau, and everything else arranges itself around them the way furniture arranges itself around the walls of a room. They are tall — fifteen to twenty-five meters, straight, black-barked, unbranched until the crown, where they spread into broad horizontal platforms three to six meters across. The platforms are not leaves in any conventional sense. They are dense interlocking growths of thick waxy tissue, ribbed with veins that channel water toward the trunk, and their upper surfaces are concave, catching the constant mist and funneling it down channels that run the length of the trunk to the root system. Each platform tree is a water-collection device. Each one is a slow, vertical river, carrying rain from crown to root, and the roots distribute the water through the surrounding soil, and the soil feeds the darks, and the darks feed the tree, and the cycle is closed.

The canopy — the continuous living ceiling at twenty meters where the platforms overlap and interlock — is its own country. Its own climate. The air up there is wetter, warmer, closer to the cloud deck, and the growth is different: denser, faster, more opportunistic. The platform surfaces support communities of small plants — mosses, lichens, flat spreading things with no analog — that live in the thin film of water that covers the concave surfaces. These communities are grazed. The canopy herbivores are small, flat, eyeless things with sucker-feet — the same clade as the samplers that landed on your shoulder, but different species, or rather different *expressions* of the same body plan, the same way a shrew and a deer are different expressions of the mammalian body plan. Some are the size of a thumbnail. Some are the size of a dinner plate. They move across the platform surfaces with a slow, deliberate gait, their sucker-feet adhering to the wet wax, their underside mouths rasping the moss and biofilm, and they are in turn eaten by things that hunt them.

The canopy predators are also flat. Also eyeless. Also sucker-footed. But larger, and faster, and their mouths are different: not rasping but *gripping*, a circular jaw that closes like a camera aperture, and they hunt by vibration and by chemical trace, and they are patient, and they are effective, and the canopy ecosystem stays in balance because everything in it is eaten by something else. A canopy predator will sit motionless on a platform surface for hours, its body flush against the wax, its coloration matching the dark substrate, and when a grazer moves within range it moves — not quickly, not dramatically, but *decisively*, a single motion, a closing, and then it feeds, and then it sits again.

The trunk zone is different. The trunks are vertical surfaces, and the life that lives on them is specialized for verticality. There are things that cling — gastropod-analogs, but not gastropods, things with a single broad foot that secretes adhesive and allows them to climb the wet bark with a slow, steady motion, leaving a trail of slightly brighter bioluminescence where the darks in their foot-secretions have been smeared across the surface. There are things that hang — growths that depend from the trunk in strands, pale and tubular, filtering the water that runs down the bark, extracting organic matter, and these growths are not animals and not plants but something in between, something that anchors and feeds and occasionally releases spores or eggs or seeds (the distinction is not clear) into the flowing water to colonize new trunks. And there are things that *live in* the bark — burrowers, things that have eaten into the outer layers of the trunk and make their homes in the wet, dark, warm spaces between bark and wood, and these are the things that make the clicking and popping sounds, the mechanical relay-sounds, and they are communicating with each other, or marking territory, or simply existing loudly, and the trunk zone hums and clicks and pops with their presence.

## The Understory (And The Things That Walk)

Beneath the canopy, in the still warm air where the rain-hiss is muffled to a soft static, the understory is a wall. Not a wall of vegetation — a wall of *existence*, of living things so densely packed that moving through it is less like walking and more like wading, pushing through layers of growth that yield and reform behind you. Ferns — or fern-analogs, fronded things that unfurl from the dark wet ground in spirals of pale translucent tissue, their veins visible, their darks glowing faintly in their roots. Mosses — ground-cover mosses, carpet mosses, mosses that climb the trunks, mosses that hang from the canopy edges in curtains, mosses that grow on the surface of standing water. Vines — climbing things, thick and thin, some as slender as thread, some as thick as an arm, all of them seeking upward, seeking the canopy, seeking the wet air near the platforms, and they climb by twining and by adhesion and by growing straight up the trunks and across the undersides of the platforms, and they carry the darks with them, and the darks glow, and the vines are visible as lines of blue-green in the blackness.

And the things that walk.

They are not animals in any lineage you would recognize. They are plants — or were plants, or are derived from plants, or have plant ancestry and animal behavior, and the distinction between the two kingdoms broke down on the plateau a long time ago, because the distinction was never useful here. They have roots. They have stems. They have broad flat leaves that they hold above the ground like umbrellas, or like heads, and they move — slowly, over days, across the forest floor, seeking warmer ground. They move by growing. A root extends in the direction of travel, anchors, and the body follows, the old roots detaching and the new roots taking hold, and the whole organism shifts, a centimeter at a time, toward the geothermal seep it can sense through the soil. When it finds a warm spot, it stops. It puts down permanent roots. It stays. It may stay for years, decades, growing, spreading, becoming a small thicket of itself, and then the ground cools — the geothermal pulse shifts, as it does, as the magma dome breathes — and the thicket *leaves*. Uproots. Walks. Slowly. Patiently. Toward the next warm spot.

They are not the only things that move this way. The plateau has a spectrum of motility: things that are fully plant and never move, things that are mostly plant and move rarely, things that are half-and-half and move regularly, things that are mostly animal and move always, and the spectrum is continuous, unbroken, and the categories that any sunlit biologist would reach for — plant, animal, fungus, protist — are useless here. The plateau has its own categories. The plateau's categories are: *rooted, drifting, walking, swimming, hunting.* And even these blur at the edges.

The walking plants are the most disconcerting thing about the understory. Not because they are dangerous — they are not, they are too slow to be dangerous, they could not catch you if they tried and they do not try — but because they make the ground *unreliable*. You stand on what you think is solid earth and after a few minutes you realize the earth has shifted beneath you, not because it is unstable but because it is *alive*, because the thing you are standing on is a body, a slow body, a body that is in the process of relocating, and your weight is an irrelevance to it, a momentary pressure, a thing that will be gone in an hour and that it will have forgotten in a day.

## The Forest Floor (And What Lives Beneath It)

The floor is ankle-deep in black water and decomposing matter. It has been described. What has not been described is what lives in it.

The decomposers are the plateau's true population. Not the trees, not the walkers, not the samplers or the clickers or the predators — the decomposers, the things that eat the dead, the things that close the loop. They are in the water, in the silt, in the saturated organic matter, in the black ooze that accumulates in every depression and channel and basin. They are small. They are innumerable. They are the reason the plateau does not bury itself in its own dead, the reason every fallen tree and every dead animal and every shed leaf is broken down and returned to the system within weeks rather than years. They are the reason the rivers run black with silt — the silt is them, or rather the product of them, the mineral residue of their digestion, carried by the water to the basins where it settles and compresses and will, in a million years, be stone.

Among the decomposers, larger things live. The floor is a hunting ground. Things move through the black water with a motion that is not swimming but *scooting*, a bottom-dwelling locomotion, broad bodies pushed by broad feet through the shallow ooze. These are the rooters — not a clade but a *guild*, a functional category, the things that dig in the detritus for the rich layers of decomposition, that aerate the substrate with their passage, that spread the decomposers to new dead material. They are the size of cats, or dogs, or small pigs, and they are eyeless and earless and navigate by chemical gradient, by temperature, by the feel of the ground beneath them, and they are constantly, constantly feeding. Their waste feeds the decomposers. The decomposers feed the darks. The darks feed the plants. The plants fall and die and feed the rooters' food. The loop is closed.

And beneath the floor, in the cracks in the basalt where geothermal water rises, the fifth layer: the hot-seep community. These organisms are the palest things on the plateau — not just pale but *white*, translucent, their bodies so lacking in pigment that their internal structures are visible, dark organs floating in clear tissue. They are soft. They are warm. They cluster around the hot seeps the way reef animals cluster around deep-ocean vents, in dense communities that are more like tissues than populations, individual bodies so close together that they fuse, share fluids, exchange chemical signals, become a single meta-organism that breathes the mineral-rich water and grows and fragments and grows again. They do not move. They do not need to move. The hot water comes to them. The minerals come to them. They are the plateau's deepest inhabitants, the ones that live closest to the heat source, the ones that are most dependent on the geothermal pulse, and if the pulse shifted — if the magma dome beneath the plateau cooled or migrated — they would die, and their death would cascade upward through every layer, because the hot seeps are not just habitat, they are *source*, the place where the mineral wealth of the deep crust enters the biological system, and without them the darks would starve, and without the darks the plants would starve, and without the plants everything would starve, and the plateau would become what it was before life found it: wet black rock under a pressing sky.

## The River Things

The rivers are not empty corridors. They are habitats, highways, hunting grounds, and morgues all at once.

The river things are streamlined — or rather, they are *flattened*, which is the plateau's version of streamlined. Everything here is flat. The samplers are flat. The canopy predators are flat. The rooters are low and broad. Even the river swimmers are flat, wide-bodied, propelled by undulations of a skirt-like fin that runs the length of their bodies, and they move through the sediment-loaded water with a motion that is more like flying than swimming, a gliding, a hydrodynamic soaring that uses the water's density and their own breadth to generate lift and minimize drag. They are the size of your forearm, or your leg, or in the case of the largest river-dwellers, the size of a small boat, and the largest ones are filter-feeders, swimming with their mouths open, straining the black water for the organic particles the rivers carry, and they are the plateau's whales, if the plateau had whales, which in a sense it does.

The smaller river things are hunters. They move against the current, holding position with small bursts of fin-motion, and they wait for the decomposer-clouds to drift past — the clouds of particulate that the rivers carry, the invisible soup of bacteria and dark-spores and organic fragments — and they pick individual particles out of the water with small precise mouths, and they are fast and they are numerous and the rivers shimmer with their motion, a motion you cannot see but can *hear*, because their fin-skirts produce a sound in the water, a high-frequency whisper that adds another layer to the plateau's noise.

And in the deepest pools, where the rivers widen and slow and the sediment settles, there are things that do not move at all. Things that have anchored to the stone bottoms and spread themselves across the substrate like nets, like webs, like the fishing lines of some angler that never reels in, and they wait for the river things to blunder into them, and when they do, they close — slowly, with a hydraulic patience, a closing that takes minutes, that the prey cannot escape because the closing is too slow to trigger a flight response, too gradual to register as danger, and by the time the prey realizes it is caught it has been enfolded, and the net-thing begins to digest, and the river carries away what remains.

## The Large Animals (And The Larger Ones)

The large animals of the plateau are the things you hear but do not see. The deep, deliberate steps in the shallow water. The splash and the weighted silence.

They are not mammals. They are not reptiles. They are something that evolved from the walking-plant lineage, or from something adjacent to it, something that took motility further than the walkers did, that committed to movement, that gave up roots entirely and became permanent travelers. They have four legs — or four leg-analogs, four limb-like structures that are derived from stems, that have joints and muscles and broad flat feet with sucker-pads, and they move through the shallow water and the understory with a gait that is slow and deliberate and almost contemplative, each step placed with care, each foot tested before weight is committed. They are the size of deer, or horses, and they browse — they reach up with long flexible necks (necks that are also stem-derived, that can extend and retract, that telescope) and they crop the lower canopy edges, the hanging mosses, the vine-tips, and they grind the plant matter between broad flat plates that serve as teeth, and the sound of their grinding is one of the plateau's frequencies, a low crunching that mixes with the river-roar and the rain-hiss and becomes part of the texture.

They are not alone. They move in groups — not herds, not flocks, but *clusters*, three to seven individuals, related, perhaps, or simply accustomed to each other's company, and they communicate with low sounds, subsonic exhalations, the sounds you heard from upstream, the sounds that are more felt than heard. These sounds carry through the water and through the saturated ground, and other clusters hear them, and the plateau's large-animal population is connected by a network of low-frequency signals that maps the landscape in a way no visual creature could replicate — a map of pressure and vibration and chemical gradient, a map of warmth and water and food, a map that is constantly updated as the clusters move and feed and breathe.

They have predators.

The predators are the largest things on the plateau that are not trees. They are also four-legged, also flat-footed, also eyeless, but they are *different* — their bodies are lower, longer, their legs splayed wider, their movement is not deliberate but *fluid*, a flowing motion that covers ground with a speed the browsers cannot match, and they hunt by vibration, by the subsonic calls of the browsers, by the low-frequency map that the browsers use to navigate and that the predators have learned to read. They do not hunt often. The plateau's energy economy does not support frequent hunting — the geothermal input is steady but not rich, and the biomass that the system can sustain has a ceiling, and the predators are at the top of that ceiling, and they exist in small numbers, and they eat rarely, and when they eat the event is significant enough that the surrounding ecosystem registers it — the silence that follows, the cessation of small sounds, the pause in the clicking and popping that lasts for minutes before the plateau's noise resumes, the texture re-knitting itself around the absence.

You heard one. You did not see it. You do not want to see it. The plateau's rules are clear, and one of the rules is that the large predators are not interested in you — you are too small, too unfamiliar, too chemically wrong — but the rule has an exception, and the exception is *curiosity*, and you do not want to be the exception, because the predators' curiosity, if it exists, would manifest as a slow approach, a circling, a testing, and the testing would involve vibration and pressure and the application of a broad mouth to your body, and you would not enjoy it, and the plateau would not care.

## The Things That Live In The Engines

The engines are not empty. The engines have never been empty. Since the first station was built — by whoever built them, whenever that was — the plateau's life has been moving in.

The stations are warm. They are dry in some places and wet in others, and the wet places are connected to the water table, and the water table is connected to the darks, and the darks colonize the metal surfaces, and the plants colonize the darks, and within a generation of the station's construction the interior is a habitat. The grating floors become substrate for mosses. The warm pipes become trellises for climbing vines. The tanks, half-filled with stagnant water, become pools, and the pools become ecosystems, and the ecosystems become unique.

Each station has its own interior ecology, shaped by its specific conditions — its temperature, its humidity, its chemical residues, the specific minerals in its water supply, the specific darks that colonized it first. No two stations are identical inside, because no two stations have the same history, and the plateau's life is historical, is contingent, is shaped by accident and opportunity the way all life is shaped by accident and opportunity, even life that has never seen a sun.

The facility at the center of the valley is the extreme case. Its core runs hotter than any of the scattered stations. Its orange glow is not light in the biological sense — it is thermal radiation, the visible signature of a process that generates heat as a byproduct, and the heat has created a micro-climate inside the facility that is warmer than the plateau's baseline, warmer even than the hot seeps, and the organisms that have colonized that warmth are unique to it. They exist nowhere else. They are the pool-breathers, the things that are not quite plants and not quite animals and not quite darks but something related to all three, something that emerged in this room, in this building, in this specific combination of heat and mineral and the faint orange light that pulses with the machinery's rhythm, and they have been here for as long as the facility has been running, which is long enough for them to have become a distinct clade, a distinct lineage, a distinct *thing*.

They are the plateau's newest inhabitants. They are also, in a sense, its most adapted. They have evolved to live in a machine. They have evolved to depend on a process they did not create and cannot control and do not understand. If the facility stops — if the turbines stop spinning, if the geothermal pump fails, if the core cools — they will die, and their clade will end, and the pool will become a pool of stagnant water, and the water will be colonized by the plateau's generalists, and in a century the facility will be a ruin, and in a millennium the ruin will be a mound, and in ten thousand years the mound will be a slight rise in the valley floor, indistinguishable from the surrounding terrain, and the plateau will have absorbed another thing into itself, as it absorbs everything, as it has absorbed the engines and the paths and the builders and the intention that built them.

## The Builders (Or Their Absence)

The builders are gone. Or the builders are here. Or the builders were never what was imagined.

The stations are old. The facility is older. The paths that radiate from the stations are overgrown but still visible, which means they were walked for a long time — long enough to compact the earth, to alter the drainage, to create depressions that the ground-cover has colonized but not erased. Someone walked those paths. Someone built those stations. Someone designed the facility and its tower and its vent and its core, and someone set the turbines running, and someone walked away, or died, or became something else.

The plateau does not remember them. The plateau does not remember anything — it is not the kind of system that remembers. It simply persists. The builders built, and the plateau absorbed what they built, and the builders were either absorbed too or they were not, and either way they are gone, and what remains is the engines and the life that lives in them and the life that lives around them and the life that lives because of them, because the engines produce heat and the heat feeds the darks and the darks feed the plants and the plants feed everything else, and the builders, if they could see what they had made, might not recognize it, or might recognize it perfectly, might see it as exactly what they intended — not the machines, not the buildings, but the *system*, the living running growing breathing persistence that their engines made possible, the warm wet dark that they created or enhanced or simply occupied, and that has been running on its own, without them, for longer than they were present, and will continue running for longer still.

Or perhaps the builders are still here. Perhaps they are the darks. Perhaps they are the pool-breathers. Perhaps they are the facility itself, not as builders but as *inhabitants*, having uploaded or devolved or transformed into something that lives in the machinery it created, a consciousness that is not consciousness anymore but a process, a pattern, a rhythm that matches the turbines' rhythm, a pulse that matches the geothermal pulse, a thing that was once a person or a people and is now a hum and a glow and a warmth that rises from below.

The plateau does not say. The plateau does not care. The plateau endures, and what lives on it endures with it, and the builders are either part of that endurance or they are not, and the distinction, like all distinctions on the plateau, has been erased by time and moisture and the patient growth of roots.

## What Lives Here (Summary)

Everything. Everything lives here. The plateau is not a place with inhabitants; it is an *inhabitation*, a continuous act of living, a stratum of existence so dense and so layered and so interconnected that the concept of "what lives here" is almost a category error. What lives here is *the plateau*. The darks and the trees and the canopy grazers and the canopy predators and the trunk-climbers and the trunk-burrowers and the ferns and the mosses and the vines and the walkers and the rooters and the decomposers and the river-swimmers and the river-hunters and the net-things and the browsers and the predators and the station-communities and the pool-breathers and the engines themselves, if engines can be said to live, which on the plateau they almost can, because they breathe steam and they pulse with heat and they have been running for so long that the life around them has evolved to depend on them, and if that is not life it is at least life-adjacent, life-adjacent in the way that the plateau is everything-adjacent, because everything here is adjacent to everything else, touching, feeding, being fed upon, growing into and through and over and under, and the rain falls and the roots grow and the engines hum and the darks glow and the large things move through the shallow water with deep deliberate steps and the small things click and pop on the trunks and the walking plants relocate toward warmth and the pool-breathers rise and fall with the pulse of the machinery and the sky presses down and the world presses up and between the two surfaces, in the compression, in the warm wet dark, in the six hundred meters of habitable stratum, the plateau lives.

It has been living for a very long time.

It will continue living for a very long time.

What lives here is *persistence*.
