# What Lives Here

What lives in the Plenum lives slowly, and lives partly in the spaces where looking doesn't reach.

First, the filaments. They are alive by any definition that matters here, though "alive" may be one of those words this place has been quietly renegotiating. They are not plants. They are not fungi, though the comparison arises and then dissolves the way all comparisons dissolve in water this dense. They are themselves, and they have been themselves for longer than the stone remembers being stone. Each strand is a single continuous cell — you would know this if you could see at that scale, which you cannot, but the water knows, and the water has been generous with what it knows if you are patient enough to be read by it in return. The cell wall is not cellulose. It is not chitin. It is something that precipitated out of the water itself at some point so remote that the precipitation has become hereditary — the filaments now secrete their own walls the way the water once secreted them, and the memory of that original formation is encoded not in genes but in the shape of the molecules, in the angles at which they prefer to join. This is heredity without biology. This is inheritance without parents. Each filament grows from its tip, and the tip is the only part that is truly new, and the new part is always slightly more transparent than what came before it, because existence here is something that has to be committed to, that has to settle, that has to stop being a possibility and start being a fact, and the filaments do this reluctantly, the way a hesitant guest takes a seat.

They grow in the gaps between observation. This is not metaphor. This is the mechanism. When attention falls on a filament directly, the filament enters a state that is analogous to being watched — its metabolic processes, such as they are, align with the fact of being perceived, and in that alignment there is a kind of stasis. It holds. Not because it chooses to, but because the act of observation here is not passive. Looking at a thing in the Plenum is a kind of touch, a kind of holding. Your attention has weight here. It pins what it sees. And so the filaments grow when you look away, because your looking-away is a releasing, and in the release there is room for the next moment, and in the next moment the filament extends, and the extension is so slight and so quick that by the time you look back it has already become part of the established length, indistinguishable from what was there before except for the faintest difference in opacity, which you would only notice if you were keeping count, which you are not, because this place dissolves the kind of mind that keeps count.

There are, as far as anyone can determine, thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The chamber is small — skull-sized, heart-sized — but the filaments are hair-thin and they layer and they interlace and they form a volume of living matter that, if stretched end to end, would reach a distance that is meaningless in this context because distance here is not linear. The filaments do not compete. There is no struggle for resources because the resources are the water and the water is everywhere and the water is, in a sense that only becomes clear after long immersion, the filaments' ancestor and their food and their medium and their future. The water is what the filaments were before they were filaments. The filaments are what the water is learning to become.

The gill-growths — the darker, burgundy forms clustered in the lower chamber — are older. Not older in the way that a tree is older than a sapling, but older in the way that a memory is older than the moment that created it. They are what happens when a filament has been growing long enough that growth itself becomes a kind of sediment. They are denser, more structured, organized into tiers that follow a logic which is not geometry but something adjacent to geometry, something that would be geometry if geometry were allowed to be wet. They secrete, or perhaps they condense — the distinction is not firm — a substance that is thicker than the ambient water, and this substance collects in their folds and drips, and where it drips it feeds new filaments, and the cycle is complete, or rather it has no beginning and no end, it is simply the way this place metabolizes itself.

They respond to proximity. When your hand moves near them, they lean. Not toward, not away. They lean the way something leans when it is listening — and they are listening, or doing something so analogous to listening that the word is as good as any and better than most. They detect the heat of your body, or the displacement of the water, or something more subtle than either — perhaps the particular signature of your attention, which has its own frequency, its own texture, which is not the same as anyone else's attention and which the gill-growths can distinguish the way a tongue distinguishes salt from sweet. They have been leaning toward things for a very long time. They leaned toward whatever made the marks in the stone. They leaned toward the water before there were filaments. They leaned toward the stone before there was water. Leaning is what they do. It is their primary mode of relationship. It is how they know.

And there is something else.

The thing that moved — the displacement that traveled through the filaments the way a wave travels through wheat — is real, or it is as real as anything here, which is a standard that keeps shifting. It is not a creature in the sense of a discrete organism with boundaries and a body plan. It is a pattern. A pattern that lives in the filaments the way a tune lives in the notes that carry it, the way a wave lives in the water that rises and falls but does not travel. The filaments do not move on their own, not in the way that the gill-growths lean. But when the pattern passes through them, they move, and the movement is indistinguishable from the passage of something alive, and so the question of whether there is a creature or merely a coordinated response becomes one of those questions that this place has been gently dissolving since before you arrived.

The pattern has no name. It does not need one. It is not the sort of thing that responds to being named. It feeds on the phosphorescence — on the faint grey-blue light that comes from below and within — or rather, it is made of the same process that produces the phosphorescence, which is a chemical reaction so slow and so distributed that it cannot be localized, which is why the light always seems to come from somewhere else, from the periphery, from the edge of what you can see. The pattern moves through the filaments and intensifies the reaction as it passes, and the intensification is the trail of brighter light that fades over several seconds, and the fading is the pattern moving on, or the pattern ending, or the pattern beginning again somewhere else, or — most likely — the pattern doing all of these simultaneously, because time here does not insist on sequence.

If you were very still, and if you could be still in the way that the stone is still, which is not the stillness of inactivity but the stillness of something that has completed its becoming and is now simply what it is, you might perceive the pattern as a shape. It would not be a familiar shape. It would be the shape of a pause, the shape of a held breath, the shape of the moment before something becomes visible. It would be approximately the size of your smallest fingernail, and it would be approximately the color of the light, and it would be approximately alive, and the approximation would be close enough that the difference would not matter, or it would matter so much that you could not bear it, and either way you would not be able to hold the perception for long because your attention would fix it and the fixing would stop it and it would be gone, passed on, passed through, a wave that has moved to water you are not watching.

There may be more than one. There may be many. There may be one that is very large and that moves so slowly through the filament-mats that its passage takes days, weeks, the kind of duration that this place does not track because tracking duration requires a kind of sequential mind that the water has been dissolving out of you since you arrived. The large one, if it exists, would be the sum of the small ones, the way a chord is the sum of its notes, and it would have been here since the beginning, and the beginning would be the moment the water first learned to remember, and the water first learned to remember when the first filament grew, and the first filament grew in the gap between the stone's attention and its own existence, in the pause between being a stone and being a place, and in that pause there was room, and in that room there was water, and in that water there was the beginning of everything that lives here now.

What else. The marks. The terraced steps, the spiral, the fragment of wall, the dry cube. These were made by something. Something that organized, that shaped, that carved or formed or secreted with intention. The filaments do not make marks. The gill-growths do not make marks. The pattern that moves does not make marks. The marks were made by something that is gone.

Or something that is not gone but absorbed. Something that was here when the stone was newer and the water was thinner and the growth was younger and there was a kind of space in the Plenum that there is not now — the space of before, the space of intention, the space of a mind small enough to live in a skull-sized chamber and build things no larger than a thumbnail. Whatever made the marks was the size of the marks. It was tiny. It was precise. It had something like hands or something like patience or both. It built the spiral steps and it built the fragment of wall and it built the dry cube — the dry cube, the perfect two-centimeter absence of water in a world of water, the most impossible thing here, the thing that should not be, that insists on its own emptiness with a conviction that borders on defiance.

And then it was absorbed. Not killed. Not destroyed. Absorbed, the way the fallen tree is absorbed by moss, the way the original stone is absorbed by the growth that grows on it and through it and eventually becomes it. The mark-maker is part of the filaments now. Its carbon is in the cell walls. Its calcium is in the gill-growths' tiers. Its intention — and this is the hardest part to hold, the part that dissolves fastest under the water's patient reading — its intention is in the pattern that moves. The pattern that travels through the filaments like a wave through wheat is the residue of whatever made the marks, the way a word spoken in a room lingers in the room's resonance after the speaker has left, except the speaker has not left, the speaker has become the room, and the room is speaking itself, very slowly, in the language of growth that happens between observations.

The dry cube is what the mark-maker left. Not as a message. Not as a monument. As a *refusal*. The one place where the water cannot go. The one place where the growth cannot reach. The one place where the absorption did not complete. Whatever the mark-maker was, it wanted one thing to remain dry, one thing to remain empty, one thing to remain itself, and it invested in that emptiness whatever it had that was more than filament, more than water, more than stone — and the emptiness holds. The cube holds. After everything else has been colonized and layered and interlaced and grown through, the cube remains dry and empty and two centimeters on each side and it is the most alive thing in the Plenum, because it is the only thing here that is resisting.

You cannot reach it. You can only perceive it from below. But it knows you are there. The water has read you and the water has told the cube, or the cube has read the water that read you, or the cube does not need to be told because the cube is the eye of the place, the one still point, the one thing that watches without growing and holds without changing. And it is empty. It is waiting. It has been waiting for something, or it has been refusing something, and the difference between waiting and refusing is the difference between the filaments and the stone, which is to say: no difference that holds up under water.

So. What lives here.

The filaments live here, and they are the water becoming itself. The gill-growths live here, and they are the filaments remembering what they were. The pattern lives here, and it is the mark-maker still moving, still leaning, still listening with the whole of its absorbed body. The cube lives here, and it is the refusal, the pause, the held breath. And you live here, now, or you are beginning to, or the water has been reading you for long enough that the reading has become a kind of residence, and your warmth is in the water, and the water's coolness is in you, and the filaments are growing in the gaps between your thoughts, and the pattern has passed through you once or twice and left its trail of brighter light along your nerves, and the cube is watching you from its dry emptiness with the patience of something that has been waiting for exactly the kind of warmth you carry, the kind that makes growth happen, the kind that fills the gaps.

You are becoming what lives here. This is what lives here: the process of becoming. The filaments are becoming denser. The gill-growths are becoming older. The pattern is becoming more coherent, or it is becoming you, or you are becoming it — the distinction has been dissolving, as distinctions do, in the patience of water that has no appointment and no destination and no reason to distinguish between the thing that lives in it and the living itself, which is the same thing, which has always been the same thing, which is the only thing here that does not grow in the gaps because it is the gaps, it is the space between observations, it is the room where change is allowed, and it has been filling that room since before the stone was stone, and it will go on filling it after you have been read, after you have been absorbed, after your warmth has joined the water's coolness and the reading is complete and the cube is still empty and the pattern moves on through the filaments that used to be you.
