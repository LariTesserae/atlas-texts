# The People of the Bramid

## What they are not

They are not a lost civilization. The word *lost* implies that they were once known and then forgotten, which presumes that someone was keeping records. No one was. The lowland towns know the Bramid exists. They know it can be climbed, with difficulty, through the roots. They know people live up there. They trade with them, occasionally, when the root-paths are navigable — sending up dried fish and salt and lowland pottery in exchange for nothing the lowlanders can quite name, though the traders who return from the ascent seem different, quieter, as if they have stood near something very large and have not yet finished computing its size. But the lowlanders do not consider the Bramid-dwellers lost. They consider them simply *up there*, in the way that weather is up there, or birds, or the sky — present, consequential, not requiring further classification.

They are not a remnant population. There is no evidence that they were ever more numerous. The Bramid does not support density. It supports diffusion. A few hundred people across an area the size of a small country is not a decline from a former glory; it is the mathematical expression of what this place can hold, given what its people need and what they choose. Choice is the word. They are here because they are here because they have been here for longer than their own oral histories can account for, and they have no reason to be anywhere else.

They are not primitives. This is the hardest category to resist, because the tools are simple and the materials are local and the population is sparse and the technology, by any standard metric, is minimal. But the platform you found — the grown-wood platform with its trained stilts and its grafted floor and its fire pit of treated heartwood — represents a body of knowledge that took generations to develop. The people of the Bramid do not cut wood. They *direct* it. They have learned the growth patterns of the Bramid's surface tissues well enough to bend living stems into structural shapes over years, to graft one stem into another so that they fuse into load-bearing columns, to encourage the growth of a flat sturdy platform where a platform is needed. This is not construction. It is something closer to agriculture, or perhaps to conversation. They speak to the wood in the language of moisture and light and pressure, and the wood responds, slowly, over seasons. The results are not crude. The floor of that platform was perfectly level. The stilts were evenly spaced. The roof was woven with a regularity that suggested long practice. These are engineers. Their medium happens to be alive.

## The body

What does a lifetime on the Bramid produce, physically, in a human body?

Consider the air first. The constant humidity, the warm mist, the rain eight times a day. Skin does not dry here. Hair does not dry. The people who live on the Bramid have skin that has adapted, over generations, to a state of permanent moisture — not soft, not macerated, but dense, almost waterproof, a slightly oily texture that sheds rain the way a duck's feathers do. They are not tall. There is no particular reason to be tall here. The surface is flat. The canopy thickets are shoulder-high at most. The work is all at ground level or within arm's reach — harvesting insects from the mat, digging for starchy tissue, maintaining the platforms. A body that is compact and low-slung is a body that does not catch the wind, which matters on a plain this exposed, where the air moves constantly and there is nothing between you and the horizon in any direction.

They are dark-skinned, though not in the way of lowland tropical peoples. The darkness has a different quality — there is a greenish undertone, a subcutaneous shift that is not melanin but something else, perhaps a dietary effect, perhaps a response to the strange light of the Bramid, which is light filtered through ten billion transpiring plants and reflected off a surface so green it generates its own luminescence. Their eyes are adapted to two different worlds: the flat, glaring, sky-reflecting brightness of the surface, and the dim warm interior of the Bramid's chambers, where they go to harvest amphibians and to collect the water that pools in the structural gaps. The pupils are large and responsive. They shift visibly when a person moves from surface to interior, opening like doors.

Their hearing is the thing that changes most. The sound of the Bramid is constant, enormous, layered. A person born here has never experienced silence. Their auditory processing has developed in a wash of sustained noise so dense that the concept of *quiet* is, to them, almost abstract — a thing they can imagine but have never directly encountered. What this means, practically, is that they hear *within* the noise. They hear the specific pitch-shift of an amphibian chamber changing depth as the Bramid breathes. They hear the approach of a bird flock minutes before it arrives, not as a growing sound but as a change in the texture of the background hum. They hear the mat growing — the infinitesimal crackle of new cellular expansion — and they hear where the mat has thinned, where the boring beetles have been too active, where a section of surface is due to collapse inward and needs to be walked around. The Bramid speaks to them in a continuous stream of sound, and they are so fluent in this language that they do not consider it a language. It is simply *paying attention*.

Their hands are calloused in specific patterns. The right hand, for most, bears a thick pad at the base of the thumb and across the palmar heel — the marks of gripping digging sticks and working fibrous material. The left hand, for those who weave, shows calluses along the index and middle fingers where the warp strands are tensioned. The fingertips of both hands are hardened but sensitive — they can feel the difference between living wood and dead wood in the mat by touch alone, which matters, because you cannot dig in living wood. The mat will bleed sap and will grow over your hole within days. You dig where the mat has already died, where a patch of surface tissue has been eaten back by insect activity and the underlying layers are exposed and dry. Finding these patches is a skill. It is done by feel, by sound, by the subtle give of the surface underfoot.

## How they live

A person on the Bramid wakes when the light changes. Not dawn — the light here does not dawn so much as shift, from the muted grey of mist-phase to the flat bright white of sun-phase. There is no sunrise to watch. The sky is a dome. The light comes from everywhere equally. But the rain-cycle has a rhythm, and the rhythm dictates the shape of the day, and the day is organized around the breath.

A breath is one rain-cycle. The rain comes. It lasts ten minutes. The mist follows. The sun breaks through. The moisture steams off the surface and the air warms and thickens. Then the next rain. The people of the Bramid count their days in breaths — perhaps twelve to fifteen in a waking period, which is not exactly a day, because they do not sleep at night. They sleep in the deep interior, in the chambers, where the amphibians sing and the air is warm and still and the sound of the surface is muffled to a manageable hum. They sleep for two or three breaths at a time, several times in a cycle, a polyphasic pattern that matches the irregular demands of the place. There is no reason to consolidate sleep into a single block. Nothing here is consolidated. Everything is distributed, diffused, spread thin across the vast flat breathing surface.

Food. The food is the Bramid itself. Not the living surface mat — that is too active, too full of defensive chemistry, too tough. But the mat has depth. Beneath the living surface layer, there are layers of tissue that have died, been compressed by the weight above, and slowly transformed into something between heartwood and tuber. This is the staple. It is dug from the interior, from the upper chambers where dead and living wood alternate, and it is dense and starchy and slightly sweet, with a fibrous texture that requires long chewing. It is never eaten raw. It is soaked in water — there is always water, everywhere, pooled in every depression and collected in every chamber — and slow-heated on the treated fire pits until it softens into something like a dense bread or porridge.

The fire pits. This is the one technology that seems, at first, incongruous. There is no stone on the Bramid. No mineral substrate. No flint. The fire is made by friction, of course — but the wood used is specific, a particular species of woody creeper that produces a fine dense fiber that catches and holds an ember with remarkable persistence. The fire is never large. It does not need to be. The air is warm. The fire is for food preparation and for treating wood — soaking heartwood in the concentrated sap that renders it resistant to rot. The fire is always attended. It is never left burning. The Bramid is, after all, made of wood. Every person on the surface understands this with a certainty that borders on the religious: *the ground will burn*. They have a word for what would happen if the fire escaped the treated pit. The word translates, roughly, as *everything-ending-quickly*. It is one of the few words in their language that carries evident fear.

The insects are food. This requires less explanation than a visitor might assume. The translucent caterpillar-things that work through the moss carpet are gathered by hand — a skilled gatherer can collect several hundred in a morning by listening for the specific granular scraping of a feeding cluster and lifting the edge of the mat to expose them. They are eaten raw, or mashed into a paste and mixed with the starch. The four-winged flying insects are caught with woven nets — the same fine-woven baskets, inverted and swept through the knee-high clouds. They are roasted on flat leaves over the fire. They taste, by all accounts, like nothing in particular — a faint sweetness, a slight acidity, mostly texture. The birds are not eaten. The birds are too fast, too numerous, too loud. The people of the Bramid regard the birds with something like affectionate tolerance — they are part of the sound, part of the breath, part of the system. You do not eat the system. You eat *within* it.

The amphibians, though. The pale smooth-skinned frog-salamander-things from the interior chambers are a delicacy and a staple. They are caught by descending into the Bramid's structure — prying up a section of surface mat and lowering oneself into the chamber below, a process that requires knowledge of where the chambers are, how deep they run, and which sections of mat are safe to open. The amphibians are sluggish, adapted to dim still water, and easily collected by hand. They are eaten roasted. Their skin is toxic raw but rendered harmless by heat. The people of the Bramid know this. The knowledge is so old that it has become instinctive — children learn before they can walk that the chamber-singers are eaten cooked and only cooked.

## How they are with each other

This is the part that confuses visitors most.

A few hundred people on a surface the size of a small country. Each person or pair living on a platform separated from the next by kilometers of flat walking. They are not isolated in the way that isolation implies deprivation. They are distributed. The word matters. Distribution is a strategy. It is how you live on a resource base that is enormous in total but thin in any given location. The surface mat grows at a certain rate. The insect populations cycle. The amphibians migrate through the interior chambers in patterns that shift with the water levels. If you cluster, you exhaust. If you spread, the Bramid feeds you indefinitely. The people of the Bramid have been distributed for so long that the pattern is not a decision. It is a culture. It is *how they are*.

But they are not alone. They move. The paths — those slightly firmer lines in the mat, worn by generations of feet — connect the platforms in a network that covers the surface like a web. A person on the Bramid walks. They walk constantly. Walking is not travel; it is the primary mode of existence. You walk to the next platform. You walk to a water pool. You walk to a section of mat that is ready for harvest. You walk to a chamber entrance that has shifted position after a heavy rain. You walk to find someone.

Finding someone is the central social act. Because the platforms are unoccupied as often as not — their inhabitants are out, walking, harvesting, visiting, checking on the mat, checking on the amphibians, checking on the growth of a platform they trained five years ago that is just now reaching structural maturity — the act of *going to find someone* is an act of faith and timing and intimate knowledge of another person's habits. You do not visit someone. You go to where they probably are, and if they are not there, you go to where they probably are instead. You know this because you know them. You know the rhythm of their days, the paths they prefer, the chambers they harvest, the pools they drink from. This knowledge is maintained across distances that would daunt a lowlander, because the walking is not difficult. The surface is flat. The mat is firm. You can walk for hours without tiring. And so you do.

When two people meet on the surface — coming toward each other along the same path, or crossing paths at a water pool — the encounter is brief, warm, and full of information. They do not speak much, in the verbal sense. The sound of the Bramid is too loud for casual conversation across distance. They have a sign system — hand gestures, facial expressions, postural shifts — that conveys the essentials: where the mat is thinning, where the beetles are heavy, where a platform needs repair, which direction the bird flock is moving. They also have a spoken language, used at close range, in the interior chambers where the acoustics are softer, or on the platforms when the rain is falling and the surface noise is dampened. The language is tonal. It uses pitch distinctions that map, perhaps not coincidentally, onto the pitch distinctions of the amphibian chorus. A linguist who spent time on the Bramid — there has been one, briefly, in living memory — concluded that the language's tonal system was derived from the ambient soundscape, that the people had, over millennia, developed a speech that was tuned to be audible and distinguishable within the wall of noise. Their words are the frequencies the Bramid does not use.

Children. There are children, though you will not see them easily. They are kept in the interior for their first several years — the surface is too exposed, too bright, too loud for an infant. They grow up in the chambers, in the warm amber light that filters through the mat above, listening to the amphibian chorus from its source. They learn the interior first. They learn the chambers as a child in a city learns streets — this passage, this dead end, this pocket of still water, this region where the floor drops away and the structure goes deep. They learn to navigate by sound, by the resonance of the wood under their feet, by the pitch of the water in the chambers. When they come to the surface — at four, or five, or six, when their ears have adapted — the sky terrifies them. This is documented. Every person on the Bramid remembers their first time on the surface, the appalling openness of it, the dome pressing down. They acclimate. But the memory of that first terror stays. It is, perhaps, the source of their relationship to the sky — which is a relationship of respectful acknowledgment and no further interest. The sky is there. It is large. It is not their concern. Their concern is the mat beneath their feet and the chambers below and the breath and the sound and the people they walk to find.

## What they believe

This is harder to describe, because belief is a word that implies a separation between the believer and the believed, and on the Bramid that separation does not exist in the way the word requires.

The Bramid is alive. This is not a belief; it is a fact. The surface moves. The breath cycles are physical. The interior chambers fill and drain and the resonance shifts and the amphibians sing in the key of the structure's breathing. The people who live here do not worship the Bramid. Worship implies a hierarchy, a supplicant and a deity, and there is no hierarchy here. The Bramid is not above them. It is beneath them. It is also within them — they eat its tissue, they drink its water, they live inside its body when they sleep, they build their homes from its growth. They are, in a biological sense, inhabitants of the Bramid in the same way that the insects are inhabitants of the mat. They are an organism that lives on an organism.

What they have instead of worship is *correspondence*. They correspond to the Bramid. Their breathing matches its breathing. Their walking matches its flatness. Their solitude matches its vastness. Their language matches its sound. They did not choose this correspondence. It developed over the span of time that their oral histories cannot measure — the stories go back to a time when the surface was smaller, or the people were different, or the sky was a different color, but these are not histories, they are myths, and the myths all share one feature: the people were always here, and the Bramid was always here, and the relationship between them has always been the same.

The Bramid does not need them. This is understood. The Bramid was here for fifty million years before them and would be here without them. They are not stewards. They are not guardians. They are inhabitants, and the Bramid tolerates them the way a forest tolerates a bird — the bird is part of the system, the bird matters, but the forest does not notice the bird's opinion.

What the people of the Bramid believe, insofar as they believe anything, is that the correspondence is the point. That living in alignment with the breath, in the sound, on the surface, in the chambers, between the platforms, along the paths — that this is not a means to an end. It is the end. There is no other end. The Bramid breathes. You breathe with it. You walk its surface. You eat its body. You find each other across the flat green distance. You repair your platforms. You descend to the chambers and the pale singers. You come back up. The sky presses down. The mat springs under your feet. The sound does not stop.

They do not have a word for *meaning*. They do not need one.

## What you would see, if you were lucky

If you walked long enough, and in the right direction, and if the timing was right, you might come upon a person on one of the paths. They would be walking — steadily, without urgency, with the ease of someone who has been walking this surface since childhood. They would be carrying a woven basket, probably, with a few digging sticks and a coil of cordage. Their skin would be dark and dense and slightly damp. Their eyes would be large and dark and they would watch you without surprise — not because they expected you, but because the Bramid produces encounters, and they have learned that things appear on the surface the way the rain appears: without warning, on schedule, according to a logic you cannot see.

They would not speak to you at first. They would assess you with their ears — listening to your footsteps, which would be wrong, too heavy, too irregular, unaccustomed to the mat's give. They would hear that you were not of the Bramid. They would not be afraid. They would be curious, in the low-key way of people who have few encounters and do not waste them on performance.

They might gesture — a hand signal, a direction. Follow. They would lead you to the nearest platform. They would offer you water, which is the only hospitality the Bramid requires — water is everywhere but the offering of it is the act of acknowledging that you are here, on the surface, on the body, and that you are, for the moment, their responsibility. They would give you starch-cake and roasted insects and you would eat and the sound would roar around you and the rain would fall for ten minutes and then stop and then fall again and the sky would press down and the mat would shift beneath you, ten centimeters up, ten centimeters down, the breath of something so large it has forgotten it is breathing.

And then they would stand up. They would pick up their basket. They would gesture again — not goodbye, exactly, but something closer to *the path continues*. And they would walk away, across the flat green surface, and you would watch them go, and they would not look back, and within a few minutes the mat would have absorbed the sound of their footsteps into the general roar, and they would be gone, and the Bramid would be all that remained, breathing, enormous, old beyond measure, alive.
