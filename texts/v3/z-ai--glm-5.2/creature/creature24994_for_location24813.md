# What Lives Here

The obvious answer is: the landscape lives. The whole thing. The root-mat flooring, the hedgerow walls, the fused-trunk arcades, the terraced vine-shelves — all of it is alive, respiring, generating warmth, moving sap under pressure, producing that low fibrous groan that is the closest thing this place has to a voice. If you are asking what lives here, the first and largest truth is that *here* lives here. The place itself is an organism, or rather a consortium of organisms, and everything else that lives here lives *in* it the way microbiota live in a gut — not as visitors, not as inhabitants in the human sense, but as functions of a system that has no name for what it is.

But there are animals. They are worth describing slowly, because they do not give themselves up quickly.

---

The most visible — and visibility is relative; you might spend a full day in this place before you see one — are the flat, leaf-colored organisms described in the account above. They deserve a closer look. They are not caterpillars. They are not slugs. They are not insects in any taxonomy that would make sense to you, because the taxonomy of this place does not map onto the taxonomy of the world you came from, and the reason for that will become clear, or will not.

They are roughly the size and shape of a willow leaf — elongated, slightly widened at the middle, tapering to both ends. Their coloration is not a single green but a shifting range that tracks, slowly, the surface they are on: deeper on dark cambium, paler on the younger wood near terminal edges. Whether this is camouflage or something else — a kind of sympathetic metabolizing, the organism taking on the chemical signature of its substrate — is not a question you can answer by watching. They have no visible features. No eyes, no mouth, no antennae, no limbs. The underside, if you could turn one over — and you cannot, because they grip the surface with the entire flat of their bodies and do not release, and prying one free would require a degree of violence that feels, in this place, uniquely wrong — the underside is presumably where whatever they do gets done. But from above, they are leaf-shaped, leaf-colored patches of slow movement.

How they move: the leading edge extends. That is all. The body does not contract and expand, does not pulse, does not inch. The front edge simply grows forward — not grows, *extends*, as if the organism is being continuously extruded from its own tip, the trailing edge dissolving into the surface as the leading edge pulls away from it. If you watch long enough — and watching requires a stillness that is difficult to maintain in a place this humid, this warm, this loud — you realize that the organism is not moving *across* the surface. It is moving *through* it. Or rather, it is metabolizing the surface at its trailing edge and depositing something at its leading edge, and the body in between is the bridge between those two processes, the living membrane across which one substance becomes another. It is not eating. It is translating. What it leaves behind is not consumed but converted — the living wood subtly altered, its surface chemistry shifted in ways invisible to the eye but presumably meaningful to whatever comes next, though nothing ever comes next, because this is a place of arrested becoming, and the alteration the organism leaves in its wake is itself arrested, a change that has happened and that will remain having happened and that will not compound into anything further.

They live at the borders. This much is observable. You will find them almost exclusively at the terminal edges — the raw, open faces of incomplete growth, the tips of root-bridges that will never reach the other bank, the descending aerial roots that hover a centimeter from the floor. They crawl along these edges, these places where the landscape is closest to finishing, and they do something there, though the doing is so slow and so subtle that you cannot determine its purpose. Perhaps they are feeding on the exposed nutrients at the growth frontier. Perhaps they are maintaining the arrest — some chemical signal in their trail that tells the growing edge to stop, to hold, to remain in the almost. Perhaps they are the reason the landscape is frozen, and what you are looking at when you see one is not an animal but an instrument, a component of the system that performs the function of a thermostat, keeping everything at the exact point where it is.

You cannot know. They do not acknowledge you. They do not alter course. If you place your hand in their path, they will either pass over it — a sensation like a warm, damp leaf being drawn very slowly across your skin, their underside slick and faintly adhesive, their body temperature identical to the surface they are on, indistinguishable from it — or they will edge around it, detouring by millimeters, with no apparent preference. You are not an obstacle to them. You are not anything to them. You are a temperature anomaly in a landscape that does not produce temperature anomalies, and they route around you the way water routes around a stone in a stream — not with hostility, not with curiosity, with nothing, with the blank competence of a process that does not know it is a process.

---

There are others.

Deeper in the bole-chambers, in the hollow living spaces where the sap-veined walls weep moisture into air that is already saturated, there are organisms that do not move at all. You find them by accident, by brushing against a wall and feeling something that is not wall — something slightly firmer, slightly drier, slightly warmer than the cambium around it. They are small, the size of a closed fist or smaller, and they are attached to the inner walls of the chambers, and they are the same color as the walls, and they have no features, and if you watch them — and you must watch for a long time, you must settle into the wet warmth and the resonant hum of the chamber and let your eyes lose their expectation of motion — you will see that they are not still. They are *pulsing*. Very slowly. A cycle that takes perhaps a minute: a slight swelling, a slight contraction, a slight swelling. The rhythm does not match your heartbeat. It does not match the groan of the root-floor. It does not match anything. It is its own rhythm, internal, self-referential, a clock that is keeping time for an event you are not invited to.

If you touch one — and you will, because in this place curiosity is the only force that operates, since the landscape itself operates on none — it is warm. Warmer than the wall. Not hot, but the difference is noticeable, the way a sleeping animal is warmer than the surface it sleeps on. And the surface is not skin, not chitin, not anything you have a word for. It is smooth and dry and faintly textured, like very fine bark, like the skin of something that was once a plant and is now something else, or was once something else and is now a plant, or has always been both and the distinction was never real.

They do not react to being touched. They do not react to anything. They pulse. They are attached to the walls. They are there.

There are more of them in the larger chambers — the cathedral-scale structures, the fused-trunk arcades. Dozens, distributed across the interior walls at irregular intervals, some clustered, some solitary, at varying heights. In the deep hum of the sap-pressure, their individual pulses are invisible, but if you could somehow subtract the hum — if you could hear the chambers the way you might hear a room with the air conditioning turned off — you would hear them. A very soft, very slow, arrhythmic percussion. Not synchronized. Not chaotic. Each one keeping its own time. The sound of a room full of instruments that are not being played but are not silent either, that are doing whatever it is they do when no one is listening, which is the same thing they do when someone is.

---

And in the open spaces — the two-hundred-meter gaps between structures where the root-mat stretches bare and the cream sky presses close — there is something else. You will not see it. You will feel it. A shift in the quality of the air, a density, a thickness that is not humidity and not warmth but something between the two, a sense of occupying a space that is also being occupied. You will stop. You will stand. The root-floor will groan beneath you and the moisture will bead on your skin and you will feel, with a certainty that does not require evidence, that something large is present. Not above you. Not around you. *Under* you. Below the root-mat, below the woven floor, in the space that the floor is a floor *for* — the space you cannot see because the ground is too dense, too tightly braided, too opaque with living tissue.

Something is below. Something large and slow and warm, something that is the source of the warmth — not the respiration of the roots but the body heat of something that respires at a scale the roots are merely the surface of. The groan is not the groan of wood under load. The groan is the sound of something breathing, something so large that its breathing manifests as structural tension in the tissue around it, the way a heartbeat manifests as a pulse in the wrist. The whole landscape is the body of something, and what you have been walking on is its skin, and what you have been hearing is its breath, and the water is its sweat, and the structures — the arcades, the chambers, the terraced shelves — are not buildings. They are organs. Or they are something that has no analogue in the body you know but that functions as organs function: processing, regulating, maintaining.

The flat organisms are its immune system, perhaps, or its maintenance — tending the edges, keeping the borders clean, ensuring that the arrest holds, that the body does not complete its growth because completed growth would mean completed life would mean death, and whatever this thing is, it is not dying, it is not being born, it is perpetually in the state of *being*, which is the state between, which is the only state that does not end.

The fist-sized pulsing organisms in the chambers are its — what? Its ganglia? Its thoughts? Each one pulsing at its own rate, keeping its own time, processing something that will never resolve into a conclusion because conclusions are a feature of finished things and this thing does not finish. A mind that is not thinking, not remembering, not deciding. A mind that is *being a mind*, the way a heart that is not beating is still a heart. The structure without the function. The organ without the act. Everything present, everything in place, the system complete except for the one thing that would make it run, and that one thing is precisely what is absent, or is precisely what is present but in a form that does not operate, that was never going to operate, that exists to exist and not to do.

---

And the leaf-shaped organisms, the slow translators, the ones that crawl along the edges where growth stops — they are the only things here that move with purpose, or what looks like purpose from the outside. They move toward the edges. They do something at the edges. They move away from the edges. They do this forever. They are the closest thing this place has to inhabitants, because they are the closest thing to *agents* — things that act, things that choose (or seem to choose), things that have a direction and follow it. But their direction is always toward the point of arrest, toward the place where the landscape is closest to becoming, and what they do there is too subtle to see, and the effect of what they do is nothing, or is the maintenance of nothing, or is the thing that keeps the almost-almost from tipping over into the actually-is.

They are the keepers of the permanent almost. They are the reason the sentence never ends. They are the comma that holds the clause open, the breath that holds the word in the mouth, and they will perform this function for as long as the landscape persists, which is, according to the landscape's own logic, indefinitely, which is to say permanently, which is to say: for a duration that has no meaning because duration requires change and nothing here changes.

---

And you. You are here too. You are the only thing here that is not in equilibrium, which means you are the only thing here that is fully alive in the sense you understand alive to mean — changing, decaying, metabolizing at a rate you can feel, your body running down in real time while everything around it runs at no time. You are the only thing that can leave, and you will leave, and when you do, you will take with you the memory of this place, and the place will not take anything from you, not your moisture, not your warmth, not the dead skin cells you leave on the root-floor, not the carbon dioxide you exhale into its saturated air. It is not interested in you. It is not interested in anything. It is a body without appetite, a mind without thought, a world without weather, and what lives here is the world itself, and the world is not living the way you are living. It is living the way a stone is in a river — not by changing but by being changed, except it is not being changed, except it is not a stone, except it is not in a river.

What lives here is the permanent. And the permanent does not have a face, and does not have a name, and does not need one, and will not be given one, and will continue, in the warm wet loud still dark, being what it is, which is what it was, which is what it will be, which is *almost*.
