# What Lives on the Throne of Winds

---

The birds are not the first thing you see. The birds are the first thing you feel.

A shadow crosses your face. Then another. Then another, and you look up, and the sky is full of them — pale shapes turning in wide, slow gyres that overlap like the rings of water dropped from separate stones into the same still lake. They do not flap. You watch one for thirty seconds, a minute, two minutes, and it does not flap once. Its wings are extended, slightly curved, held in a configuration that suggests tension — not the relaxation of a gliding bird but the deliberate, engaged posture of a body that is working, constantly working, against and with the medium it moves through. The wind is doing most of it. But not all. There are micro-adjustments so small and so frequent that the eye reads them not as movement but as a quality of stillness, the way a tightrope walker's body is technically in constant motion even as it appears, from a distance, to be perfectly poised.

They are beautiful, but not in a way that invites sentiment. There is nothing soft about them. Their paleness is not the paleness of doves or swans; it is the paleness of bone, of sun-bleached stone, of things that have had color leached from them by long exposure to a light that does not compromise. Up close — and you will get close, because they are not afraid of you, because fear, as you will come to understand, is partly an acoustic phenomenon, and they have no acoustics — you can see that the feathers are not uniformly white but range from a chalky ivory at the tips to a darker, almost gray-blue at the base, where down covers the skin in a dense, close layer that seems to serve as insulation against the cold rather than against the wind. Their bodies are heavier than you expect. The bones are dense. They are not hollow in the way that the bones of flying birds are typically hollow, because the physics of flight here are different — the wind is so reliable, so constant, that the energetic cost of staying aloft is fundamentally altered. They do not need to be light. They need to be strong. The wind carries them; their bodies only need to resist.

Their faces are strange. The beak is there, but it has changed — broadened, flattened, the hard keratin extending sideways into a structure that looks almost like the grill of a filter. Inside, visible when one opens its mouth — which it does silently, without any accompanying sound, the jaw simply dropping open — there are rows of fine, bristle-like projections, pale and flexible, that interlock when the mouth is closed. Baleen. Or something that has arrived at the same solution as baleen through an entirely different evolutionary pathway. They are not seabirds. They have no relationship to the ocean. But they are filter-feeders, and the medium they filter is the thin biological scum on the plateau's wet stone, and the creatures within it, and the creatures within the creatures.

They come down to feed in rotations. This is not something you understand immediately. You watch for hours before the pattern becomes visible, and even then, it is not a pattern imposed by decision but by rhythm — a circadian beat that the flock as a whole maintains without any individual being aware of maintaining it. At any given time, perhaps two-thirds of the population is aloft, circling, and one-third is on the ground, walking across the wet stone on those flat, wide feet, sweeping their beaks through the scum. They move in a hunched, undignified posture, wings folded tight against their bodies, necks extended downward, and they are transformed by this — from the pale, soaring shapes that cut the sky into ribbons to something awkward and earthbound, almost reptilian, dragging their filtering mouths across the rock like strange pale storks working a mudflat. They feed for twenty minutes, perhaps thirty, and then they rise again, and others descend, and the cycle continues, and has continued, at this rate, in these proportions, for a span of time that has no beginning anyone could name.

They cannot hear themselves. They cannot hear each other. The roaring takes everything. Their inner ears have not atrophied — the structures are still there, still functional in a mechanical sense — but they serve no purpose, and the neural pathways that once processed acoustic input have been repurposed, over evolutionary time, into something else: a heightened sensitivity to air pressure changes so acute that a bird can detect the approach of another individual from the displacement of air alone, from the subtle shifts in the thermal column that another body produces. They feel each other coming. Not through sound, not through sight alone, but through pressure — a sense that you, standing on the plateau, have the physical apparatus for but will never develop, because you have not spent ten thousand generations in a place where the air itself is the only medium that carries information.

They read the wind the way you read a page. Every shift, every eddy, every pocket of turbulence or laminar smoothness is a word, and the sentences are long, and the grammar is ancient, and they have been reading this particular text for so long that they know every passage by heart. When a cloud-shadow passes over the plateau, the temperature drops by a fraction of a degree, and the thermals shift, and every bird in the sky adjusts simultaneously, a collective correction so synchronized it looks choreographed, though it is not. It is simply that every individual is reading the same change in the same medium at the same moment, and every individual responds in the same way, because the response is not learned but inherited, written into the body by a thousand seasons of the same wind blowing the same way.

They mate in the air. You do not see this often, but you see it once, and you do not forget it. Two birds, circling in the same thermal, gradually tighten the distance between them. The outer bird dips a wing — a specific angle, a specific duration — and the inner bird responds with a corresponding dip, and the two spirals become one spiral, and then, for a moment that lasts no more than three seconds, they are touching, wing to wing, body to body, still turning, still riding the wind, and then they separate, and the outer bird banks away, and the inner bird continues its circle, and the sky is full again of individual shapes that do not touch.

They do not mate for life. They do not pair-bond. The concept of partnership is absent from their social structure, which is not a structure at all but a distribution — a population spread across a thermal architecture that is fixed and permanent, each bird occupying the space it occupies because the wind put it there and the wind keeps it there. There are no flocks. There are no territories. There is a population, and the population is the flock, and the flock is the wind, and the wind is the plateau, and the distinctions between these things have dissolved so completely that to ask where the bird ends and the air begins is to ask a question the plateau answered long ago and no longer considers interesting.

---

The mammals are harder to see.

Not because they are hidden — they do not hide. They are simply the color of the stone they move across, and they move so slowly that the eye, scanning the plateau for life, skips over them the way it skips over the rock formations. You look, and you see stone, and undulations, and pale shapes in the sky, and the dark wet patches where the scum grows, and then you look again, and one of the dark patches has moved, and you realize that it was not a patch but a body, and it has been there the whole time, and it is still there, and it is still moving, and you do not know how long it has been moving or where it is going.

They are the size of large dogs, but proportioned nothing like dogs. They are long and low, almost ferret-like in their build, with legs so short that the belly nearly touches the stone. The fur is dense, remarkable in its density, each hair so fine that the pelt has a quality more like felt or velvet than like fur — it is a continuous surface, seamless, and it is the exact color of the granite, with the dark veins of obsidian-like material reproduced in darker streaks across the back and flanks in patterns that look, from above, indistinguishable from the stone's own patterning. You are not sure this is accidental. You are not sure that an animal that has evolved on a stone surface this specific, this constant, for this long, would not develop camouflage that mirrors that surface with an almost photographic precision. The ones that looked less like stone were seen. The ones that looked more like stone were not. The selection pressure is simple, and it has been running for a very long time, and the result is an animal that is nearly invisible on the only ground it has ever known.

Their faces are broad and flat. The eyes are small — not vestigial, but reduced, set close together on the front of the face in a way that suggests binocular vision, which is curious for an animal that seems to have no predators and no need to calculate distance precisely. But perhaps the eyes are not for depth. Perhaps they are for light. The plateau is a place of extreme brightness and extreme shadow, and the ability to read the passage of cloud-shadows — which are, as you have noted, constant and regular — may be as important to these creatures as the ability to read thermals is to the birds. The shadows are a clock. The shadows are a calendar. The shadows tell you where you are in the cycle of the day, and the cycle of the day tells you when the pools will be wettest and the scum thickest and the feeding best. The small, close-set eyes may be light-sensors first and imagers second.

The mouth is wide, a horizontal slit that runs nearly from one side of the face to the other, and when it opens, which it does with a mechanical, almost jointed motion, you can see the tongue. The tongue is dark, broad, and covered in a texture that is not quite papillae and not quite scales — a rasping surface, like a file, designed to be pressed against stone and dragged. You watch one feed. It approaches a wet depression — a shallow basin where water has pooled and scum has grown — and it lowers its face to the stone, and it presses its tongue to the surface, and it drags. The motion is slow, deliberate, almost tender. It leaves a clean, pale stripe on the stone where the scum has been removed, and this stripe will be visible for days, a record of feeding that other mammals can read and that you, once you learn to look for them, begin to see everywhere — long, curving pale lines across the dark wet stone, each one a meal, each one a track.

They patrol. That is the only word for it. They do not forage. They do not wander. They move in straight lines, and the straight lines have a quality of intention that suggests they are going somewhere specific, except that they do not appear to arrive. They walk for hundreds of meters — you track one across the plateau's surface for what must be a full kilometer, a dark shape moving so slowly that you must mark its position against a distant peak and wait — and then they stop, and feed, and rest, and then they walk again, in a new straight line, in a direction that does not seem to relate to the old direction in any way you can parse. They are alone. You never see two within fifty meters of each other. The distances between them are large, consistent, maintained not by aggression — you never see aggression — but by something else, something chemical, something laid down in the scum-trails they leave behind, a signal that says *I am here, I was here, this line is mine*, and the signal is respected, and the spacing holds.

They do not interact. They do not need to. The population is stable. The food is sufficient. The environment does not change. There is no pressure to cooperate, no pressure to compete, no pressure to do anything except continue, and continuation, here, is not a struggle. It is a process. The mammals are engaged in a process that has no goal and no endpoint and has been running, at this pace, in this pattern, for a duration that makes the concept of goal meaningless. They are not going anywhere. They are not becoming anything. They are being what they are, on the stone, in the wind, and they have been doing this for so long that the doing and the being are the same act.

---

The things in the scum you will not see clearly. Not because they are invisible — they are visible, barely, as movement, as a faint shift in the texture of the wet film, a quickening, a suggestion of liveliness in something that looked mineral. You kneel. You put your face close to the stone, close enough that the wind roars in your ears so loudly that you feel your skull is vibrating, and you look at the dark film on the wet granite, and you see that it is moving. Not all of it. Not the scum itself, which is as still as stone. But within the scum, on its surface, in the thin layer of water that covers it, there are things — pale, soft, legless bodies that pulse and contract and glide, and you cannot count them because they are too small and too many, and you cannot classify them because they do not resemble anything you have a name for.

They are not insects. They are not worms. They are not crustaceans or arachnids or any other category you carry in your mind as a framework for understanding small, many-legged or no-legged things that live in wet places. They are their own thing. They have arrived at solutions that evolution has arrived at elsewhere — soft bodies, because there is nothing hard in their environment that requires armor; pale coloration, because there is no light in the niche they occupy, the niche being the underside of a thin film of water on a stone surface; leglessness or near-leglessness, because the medium they move through is viscous, a gel-like layer that is neither liquid nor solid, and locomotion through it is achieved by peristalsis, by ciliary action, by methods that do not require limbs.

They are the foundation. Everything above them — the birds, the mammals — rests on this layer of life that is, in aggregate, a film no thicker than a coin laid on its side. The scum is their world and their food and their medium and their predator and their substrate, and they live in it the way the birds live in the wind: totally, without alternative, in a state of such complete adaptation to their environment that the environment and the organism are difficult to separate. The scum is biological. The creatures within it are biological. The stone beneath is mineral. But the boundaries between these layers are not sharp. The scum has etched the stone slightly, acidified it, and the stone has absorbed the scum, incorporated it into its surface structure, so that there is a zone — a few millimeters thick, a boundary layer — where rock and life are intermixed, where the mineral has become partially biological and the biological has become partially mineral, and this zone is where the pale soft things live, in a world that is neither stone nor scum but both, a liminal layer that exists nowhere else and that is, in its quiet, invisible way, the reason the entire plateau is alive.

They do not communicate. Or if they do, they communicate by the most primitive means possible — by touch, by the pressure of one soft body against another, by chemical gradients that propagate through the film like ripples through water. They have no senses that you can identify. No eyes. No ears. No organs of smell. They respond to light and dark, to wet and dry, to the chemical presence of each other and the biological presence of the scum, and these responses are enough. They have been enough for a very long time. They will continue to be enough for a very long time. The soft pale things in the scum are the oldest form of life on the plateau, and they are the most settled, and they are the most finished, and they do not know they exist, and this does not matter to them at all, because mattering is a concept that requires a kind of consciousness they do not possess and have never needed.

---

The mats of vegetation — the gray-green cushions clinging to the leeward sides of rock formations — are alive, but barely. They are so slow, so inactive, so close to the threshold between living and nonliving, that you are not certain, at first, that they are plants. They look like patches of lichen. They look like discolorations on the stone. But they have roots — threadlike, almost microscopic, penetrating into hairline fractures in the granite — and they photosynthesize, faintly, in the hard bright light, and they grow, though their growth is so slow that it is measured not in seasons but in geological time, in the slow accumulation of biomass that adds up, over centuries, to a cushion the size of your palm.

They are the only things on the plateau that predate the scum. They were here first. They colonized the stone when it was newer, when the fractures were fresher, when there was still some give in the rock that roots could exploit. They have been here so long that they have become part of the stone's surface — not growing on it but growing into it, integrated, mineral and vegetable fused into a single composite material that is as hard as rock and as alive as rock can be. They do not reproduce, or if they do, they do so at a rate that is invisible on any timescale you can hold in your mind. They simply persist. They are the most patient things on the plateau, and the plateau is a place where patience is not a virtue but a precondition of existence.

---

And there was something else. Once.

The smoothed stone. The worn surfaces. The paths that feet have made, paths so faint that you are not sure they are paths until you see that the stone along a particular route is fractionally more polished than the stone beside it, and that this polish extends in a line that does not follow the natural contours of the rock but cuts across them, which is what paths do, which is what intention does, which is what a body following a route it has chosen rather than a route the terrain has dictated does.

Something lived here that chose.

Not the birds, which ride the wind and feed and mate and die within a system that does not require or permit choice. Not the mammals, which patrol their straight lines and leave their chemical trails and exist in a state of distribution rather than decision. Not the things in the scum, which do not choose anything, which cannot. Something else. Something that came to the plateau — or arose on it, though this seems less likely, because the plateau does not seem like a place where things arise — and moved across it with purpose, with direction, with a kind of awareness that the current inhabitants do not possess and do not need.

They are gone. They left nothing behind that you can hold, nothing you can point to and say *this was made*. Only the wear. Only the faint polish on certain stones, the slight rounding of certain edges, the accumulated evidence of passage. They walked. They paused. They may have knelt — there is a place near the center of the plateau where the stone is smoother than it should be, a shallow concavity that could be natural and could be the result of bodies resting in the same spot, over and over, for a long time. They may have eaten here. They may have hunted the birds, or the mammals, or the things in the scum. They may have come for the stone itself — the dark veins of obsidian-like material, which is sharp, which is useful, which is the kind of thing that a creature with hands and needs would want.

But they did not stay. The sound drove them. The sound drives everything that can be driven. The plateau is not a place that tolerates the unequipped. You can stand it for hours, perhaps for a day, if you are stubborn and prepared. But the roaring does not stop. The light does not soften. The stone does not become comfortable. And eventually, the body understands what the mind has been trying to tell it: this place is not for you. Not because it is hostile. Because it is *finished*. Because it has completed its conversation with itself and has nothing left to say to anything new.

The things that wore the stone were here, and they left, and they have not come back, and the plateau has not noticed their absence. The birds still circle. The mammals still patrol. The scum still grows. The wind still blows. And in the smoothed stone, in the faint paths, there is the only record that something ever came here and chose to leave — a record so faint that it is less a trace than a rumor, less a memory than a stain, and the wind roars over it without acknowledgment, and the light falls on it without comment, and the plateau continues to be itself, entirely, without interruption, without interest, without end.
