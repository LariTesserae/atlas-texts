# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that the word *lives* is doing work it was not designed for in this place. The categories break down. What lives here does not live in the way that things live in places where there is light and season and the sharp punctuation of predation. Here, living is a continuous process, a state of being that has more in common with mineral deposition than with breathing, and the organisms that occupy the Old Gardens Beneath have become, over the long millennia of their isolation, something that sits uncomfortably in the vocabulary of biology. They are alive. They are also, in a sense that is hard to pin down, *geological*. They are biota that have become terrain.

---

## The Mat

The moss — though it is not moss — is the closest thing this place has to a citizen. It is the foundational organism, the substrate on which everything else establishes itself, the medium through which everything else grows. It is also, very probably, a single individual.

This is the thing that is difficult to convey without sounding as though one is exaggerating: the mats that cover the worked stone, the dark spongy sheets that build up in layers of living and dead and living material, that are meters thick in the shallows and thin to a film in the deeper channels, that extend continental distances across the plain — they are likely all one organism. Not one species. One *individual*. One genotype, cloned and re-cloned across the entire extent of the garden, so that a frond in the east and a frond in the west, separated by hundreds of kilometers of cold water and worked stone, are genetically identical, are in fact the same plant, are contiguous with each other through the unbroken tissue of the mat that connects them.

If this is true — and the evidence suggests it, though the evidence is difficult to gather in a place where gathering anything requires bringing equipment into total darkness and freezing water — then the largest organism on earth lives here, and it is a mat of not-moss at the bottom of a drowned garden, and it has been growing for longer than the oldest tree, longer than the oldest clonal colony, longer perhaps than the current interglacial period. It does not age in the way that organisms age. It does not accumulate mutations in the way that organisms accumulate mutations, because it reproduces by extension, by fragmentation, by the simple continued growth of its own body outward into available space. The cells at its center are not older than the cells at its edges. Every cell is the same age, because every cell was produced by the cell next to it, which was produced by the cell next to it, in an unbroken chain of mitotic division that goes back to the original planting, whenever that was, whoever did it.

The Mat — it deserves a name, though it has never been given one, and the giving of names is a problem here because names imply a distance between the namer and the named, and there is no distance in a place where one organism is the entire landscape — the Mat does not grow upward. It grows *outward*, and it grows *downward*, and it grows *inward*, into itself, layer over layer, the new growth on top of the old, the old growth compressed beneath the new until it becomes a peat-like substance, dense and fibrous and saturated with water, and then a denser substance, and then something that is not quite sediment and not quite peat and not quite stone but is all of those things and none of them. The Mat is building its own geology. It is creating the ground that it grows on, has been creating it for millennia, and the worked stone beneath it is no longer the floor of this place. The Mat is the floor. The stone is the basement, the foundation, the thing that was here before and that is now buried under the accumulated biomass of an organism that has had nothing else to do, for a very long time, but grow.

It is not competitive. There is nothing to compete with. It does not defend territory. There is no territory to defend, because it is all of the territory. It does not respond to light or the absence of light, because it stopped responding to light a long time ago, and the absence of light is not a stressor for an organism that has metabolized the absence into its normal operating condition. What it responds to is *surface*. It responds to the presence of bare stone, the way a liquid responds to a container — it fills it, it covers it, it conforms to the shape of it. Where the stone is rough, the Mat is thick. Where the stone is smooth — in the carved basins, in the channels — the Mat is thin, and in the channels where the water moves, however slowly, the Mat does not grow at all, because the water, over centuries, carries away the pioneer filaments before they can anchor. The channels are the Mat's boundary. They are the lines it will not cross, drawn not by its own biology but by the slow, patient force of the water itself, and they have been in the same places for as long as the Mat has been growing.

The Mat clicks. This was mentioned in the description of the place, but it was not explained, and it should be, because the clicking is the Mat's only behavior, the only thing it *does* that is visible on a timescale a human being could perceive. The clicking is gas release. The lower layers of the Mat — the compressed, partially decomposed layers, the ones that have been buried under meters of newer growth — are anaerobic, and in them, processes that are not quite decomposition and not quite digestion and not quite fermentation are taking place, carried out by organisms that are not quite bacteria and not quite archaea and not quite anything that has a name, because no one has sampled them and no one has sequenced them and no one has looked at them under a microscope. These organisms — these microbes, these bottom-dwellers, these smallest and oldest and most patient of the Mat's inhabitants — produce gas. Methane, probably. Something analogous, certainly. And the gas migrates upward through the Mat, through the layers, through the dense fibrous tissue, and when it reaches the surface — when it breaks through the topmost layer and enters the water — it makes a sound. A click. A tiny, percussive, almost inaudible event, the release of a bubble no larger than a pinhead, and it happens irregularly, constantly, across the entire extent of the Mat, so that the Old Gardens Beneath are filled with a faint, scattered, arrhythmic clicking that sounds, if you listen long enough, like the place is thinking.

It is not thinking. But it is metabolizing. And the distinction, in a place this old and this patient and this single-minded, may be smaller than it appears.

## The Stalks

The tall pale things that rise from the Mat — the columns, the fronds, the things that reach upward into the water column like the pillars of a drowned cathedral — are the Mat's only companions in terms of scale, and they are its dependents in terms of biology.

They are pale because they have no chlorophyll, or no functioning chlorophyll, or no need of chlorophyll. They are thin because thinness is the most efficient form when there is no light to gather and no wind to resist and no reason to be anything other than a slender column of tissue extending upward into still water. They branch in geometric patterns — regular, repeating, almost crystalline — because the branching pattern is written into their genome, and their genome is the genome of something that was *selected* for that pattern, long ago, by someone who wanted plants that branched in geometric patterns, and the selection has been conserved by the absence of any selective pressure to change it. No animal browses on them. No storm breaks them. No competitor shades them out. They grow as they were designed to grow, and the design has been carried forward through uncounted generations of cloning with the fidelity of a copied manuscript, and the copies have not drifted, because there is nothing to make them drift.

They are rooted in the Mat. This is the important thing about them, the thing that defines their existence: they are not independent organisms, or if they are, they are organisms that have made a bargain so complete that it has become a form of absorption. Their roots — if they are roots, if that word applies to structures that do not anchor and do not absorb water but do absorb something, some nutrient, some chemical that the Mat produces in its deep anaerobic layers — their roots extend into the Mat and they take from it whatever it is that they need to grow, and in return they provide... perhaps nothing. Perhaps they are parasites. Perhaps they are the Mat's own structures, its reproductive organs, its way of reaching upward into the water column for reasons that have to do with the dispersal of fragments or the exchange of dissolved gases or something else entirely that no one has observed because no one has been here to observe.

But they are beautiful. This is a thing that can be said about them even though no one has seen them, because beauty is not dependent on being seen; it is a property of form, and the form of the stalks is beautiful. They rise from the dark Mat in pale columns, branching at regular intervals in a pattern that is, when you trace it, a fractal — each branch repeating the pattern of the whole, each sub-branch repeating the pattern of the branch, down to the finest filament, which is too thin to see but which follows the same rule, the same angle, the same rhythm. They are the architecture of the garden, the vertical element in a landscape that is otherwise horizontal, and they fill the water column with a density that varies — sparse in the deep channels, thick in the shallows, where they grow close enough to brush against each other in the imperceptible current, their tips bending and touching and bending away over the course of hours, in a slow, blind, silent minuet that has been ongoing for millennia and will continue for millennia more.

They do not reproduce. Or rather, they do not reproduce *themselves*. They do not flower. They do not fruit. They do not release spores or seeds or gametes into the water. They *are* reproduced — by the Mat, apparently, or by some process that involves the Mat and the stalks together, some collaborative event that no one has witnessed and that may, in fact, be so slow that witnessing it would require watching a single stalk for a century, noting the gradual thickening at its base, the slow emergence of a new filament, the patient differentiation of tissue over decades into something that is recognizably a new stalk, a new column, a new pale reaching thing in the dark water.

## The Coral-Plants

The calcareous organisms that encrust the walls and the vertical faces of the terraces are the third form of life here, and they are the most enigmatic, because they sit at the boundary between plant and mineral in a way that challenges the utility of those categories.

They are not corals. They have no polyps. They have no tentacles, no mouth, no gut. They are not animals. They are also not plants in any recognizable sense, because they do not grow from a meristem, do not produce tissue in layers, do not follow the architectural logic of plant growth. What they do is *secrete*. They secrete calcium carbonate — or something very like it, something crystalline and pale and dense — from their outer surface, and the secretion builds up, cell by cell, layer by layer, the way a snail builds a shell, except that the entire organism is the shell, or the shell is the organism, and the distinction between living tissue and mineral deposit is so fine that it can only be detected by slicing a cross-section and looking at it under magnification, which no one has done.

They grow where the water moves. This is their defining preference, the thing that determines their distribution and their form. In the channels, where the water flows — slowly, glacially, imperceptibly — they grow into the current, their branching shapes dictated by the flow, thickening on the upstream face where the water brings them whatever it brings them, thinning on the downstream face where the water has already given up what it was carrying. They are shaped by the water the way a tree is shaped by wind, except that the water is a thousand times slower than wind and the shaping has taken a thousand times longer. A colony the size of a dinner plate is two thousand years old. A colony the size of a human head is older than agriculture. A colony that encrusts an entire wall — and there are such colonies, their white branching forms covering square meters of fitted granite — is older than the oldest city, older than writing, older perhaps than the use of fire, if the time scales are what they appear to be.

They are white. In the darkness, they are invisible, which is the condition of everything here, but it is worth noting their whiteness because it is a *chosen* whiteness, a whiteness that is the result of the material they secrete, and that material is white because calcium carbonate is white, and calcium carbonate is white because of the arrangement of its crystals, and the arrangement of its crystals is a property of chemistry, and chemistry is a property of the universe, and so the coral-plants are white because the universe is the way it is, and they have built themselves out of the universe's material, and they are the only thing in this place that has a color that could be seen, if there were light, and there is not light, and so their whiteness is a secret, a beauty that is hidden, a fact about the world that is true and that no one knows.

They are silent. They do not click. They do not scrape. They do not move. They accrete, and the accretion is so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness, and if you pressed your hand against a coral-plant — if you could, if you were here, if you had hands and could feel — you would feel something that is like stone but not stone, something that is like bone but not bone, something that is alive but does not feel alive, something that is building itself in the dark out of the dissolved minerals in old water, one molecule at a time, with the patience of a mountain.

## The Microbes

There are others. There must be others. The Mat is not a single organism in the way that a rock is a single object; it is a community, a consortium, a living fabric that includes within its layers organisms that are not the Mat but live inside it, among its fibers, in its water-filled interstices, in the anaerobic zones at its base where the chemistry changes and the light never reached even when there was light.

These are the microbes. The bacteria, or the archaea, or the whatever-they-are, the organisms that have no name and no description and no advocate, the organisms that are doing the work of decomposition and transformation and chemical conversion that keeps the Mat alive, that processes the dead layers into substrate for the living layers, that produce the gas that clicks, that cycle the nutrients that the stalks absorb through their roots, that maintain the chemistry of the water at a level that allows all of this to continue. They are the invisible workforce. They are the gardeners that the garden grew for itself after the original gardeners left.

They are in the water, too, not just in the Mat. The water is not sterile — nothing in this place is sterile, because sterility is a condition that exists only where life has been excluded, and life has not been excluded here; it has been *included*, comprehensively, totally, in every available niche and every available medium. The water contains dissolved organic carbon, dissolved minerals, dissolved gases, and it contains microbes, suspended in it at a density that is low by the standards of a warm, sunlit sea but that is, in absolute terms, significant. They are doing what microbes do — taking in, putting out, converting, cycling, maintaining the chemistry of their environment by the aggregate activity of their individual metabolisms. They are the smallest inhabitants and the most numerous and the least understood and the most essential. Without them, the Mat would suffocate in its own dead tissue. Without them, the water would become chemically inert, a medium in which nothing could happen. Without them, the garden would stop.

They are not aware of the garden. They are not aware of anything. They are doing what their chemistry requires them to do, and the fact that their chemistry, in aggregate, over millions of individuals over thousands of years, constitutes a form of maintenance — a form of gardening — is not something they know or care about. It is something that the garden does, through them, the way a body digests through its bacteria: without intent, without knowledge, but with a thoroughness and a reliability that intent and knowledge could not improve upon.

## The Question of Animals

There are no animals.

This needs to be said plainly, because it is the central absence of the place, the thing that shapes everything else, the hole in the ecosystem that the ecosystem has filled with plants and microbes and nothing else. No fish. No crustaceans. No mollusks. No worms. No jellyfish. No larvae, no eggs, no juveniles, no adults. Nothing that moves under its own power with intent. Nothing that eats anything else. Nothing that has a nervous system, a mouth, a gut, a sense organ, a response to stimulus that could be called a behavior rather than a reaction.

They were here once. This can be stated with some confidence, because the stone was carved in a time when this place was above water, and above water there are animals, and the basins and channels and walls were built for purposes that likely included the management of living things — fish, perhaps, or crustaceans, or something else, something that was part of the agricultural project that this place was. And when the water came, the animals stayed for a while, perhaps, adapted perhaps, lingered in the shallows perhaps, until the darkness and the cold and the loss of any input from the surface pushed them out, or killed them, or drove them to extinction, and the place became what it is now: a garden with no fauna, an ecosystem with no consumers, a world with no eyes.

The absence of animals has consequences. It means that nothing here is eaten. The Mat grows and is not grazed. The stalks extend and are not browsed. The coral-plants accrete and are not bored into, not settled on, not colonized by the larvae of sessile invertebrates. The dead tissue of the Mat accumulates and is not consumed by detritivores, is not broken down by the mechanical action of mouths and guts, is processed only by the slow chemistry of microbial decomposition. The absence of animals means that the cycling of matter in this place is entirely chemical, entirely microbial, entirely without the mechanical and behavioral components that animals bring to an ecosystem. It is an ecosystem of growth and decay, without the intermediate steps of consumption and predation, and it has been this way for long enough that the ecosystem has optimized for this condition, has become something that could not function with animals even if animals were introduced, because the relationships that would connect animals to this system have atrophied, have been lost, were never formed in the first place or were formed and then dissolved when the animals left.

This is what lives here. Plants that were once cultivated and have become wild and have become, in their wildness, something more cultivated than cultivation could make them, because the design has been carried forward by the absence of change, by the lack of any force that would alter it. Microbes that maintain the chemistry of a world they do not know they are maintaining. A single organism that covers a continent and clicks in the dark like a slow, enormous, brainless thought.

## The Inheritance

And there is one more thing that lives here, though *lives* is the wrong word for it and *here* is the wrong word for it and *thing* is the wrong word for it, but there is no right word, so this will have to do.

The design lives here.

The pattern that was laid down by the people who carved the stone and built the walls and planted the gardens and shaped the basins and cut the channels — that pattern is alive, not in the biological sense but in the sense that it is still being executed, still being followed, still the instruction that the place obeys. The Mat grows where it was planted. The stalks branch as they were bred to branch. The coral-plants accrete where the water flows, and the water flows where the channels were cut, and the channels were cut where they were cut by hands that are gone, and the hands are gone but the channels remain, and the channels dictate the water, and the water dictates the coral-plants, and the coral-plants are alive, and so the hands are alive, in a sense, in the sense that the design is alive, in the sense that the intention persists.

The gardeners are dead. The garden is not. The garden is gardening, following the plan that was laid down in stone and in genome, in the shape of a channel and the branching of a stalk, in the spacing of basins and the angle of a frond, in the patience of the people who made this place and the patience of the place that they made, which has inherited their patience the way a child inherits a face, without knowing it, without choosing it, without being able to refuse.

This is what lives in the Old Gardens Beneath: a design, being executed, in the dark, in the cold, at the bottom of a world that someone made, by the things that someone made, for the purpose that someone intended, and the purpose has been forgotten and the someone has been forgotten and the making has not been forgotten because the making is the place itself, and the place is still here, and it is still a garden, and it gardens.
