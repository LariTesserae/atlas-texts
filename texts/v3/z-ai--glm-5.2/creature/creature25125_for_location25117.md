# What Lives There

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here *lives* in the way you might mean. Nothing breathes. Nothing eats in any fashion you'd recognize. Nothing reproduces in the open, nothing dies where you can see it. The station is not an ecosystem. It is closer to a single artifact — a thing that persists — and the things inside it persist in the same key: quietly, without event, without the drama of biology.

But there are things here that are present, and presence, in a place this still, is its own kind of life.

---

**The Junction Box Organisms**

They are the first thing you become aware of, though *aware* is too strong — they register at the edge of perception, a pulse of blue-green in the dark that you mistake at first for an artifact of your own vision. Then another, elsewhere. Then another. They are on the junction boxes. All of them. Every welded-shut barnacle-cluster of metal on every wall has a skin of something living on its surface, and that something emits light.

What they are is difficult. They are not bacteria in the way you learned the word. They are not archaea. They occupy a position in the tree of life — if it is a tree, if the word still applies at that scale — that doesn't have a clean label. They are single-celled, or nearly so. They are chemoautotrophic, pulling energy from the slow oxidation of iron in the junction box housings, which means they are eating the station. Very, very slowly. They have been eating it for decades. At the current rate, they will finish eating it in approximately never.

They pulse because of something internal — a metabolic cycle, a calcium flux, a membrane event that builds and discharges on its own clock. Each cell does it independently. There is no coordination. But the cells on a single junction box are close enough to each other, and metabolically similar enough, that their pulses sometimes almost align — a drift toward synchrony that never quite resolves, like two clocks in the same room that almost tick together and then fall away. The effect is the lazy firefly flicker. The cold elderberry color. The irregular, arrhythmic, somehow-not-random schedule of light.

They do not like disturbance. This is the one thing they do that looks like behavior. When you approach a junction box — when your displacement pushes water toward it, when your body's warmth reaches the microenvironment around the housing — the cells stop pulsing. All of them. Immediately. The box goes dark. Not dim. Dark. They resume only after you have moved away, and they resume on their own individual schedules, which means the first few minutes after your departure are more active than the baseline, a kind of fumbling re-establishment of rhythm.

You learn to watch them from across the room. From that distance, they pulse freely. You become a kind of astronomer, reading them at one remove, interpreting signals that weren't meant for you and that carry no message.

---

**The Filamentous Organism**

It is in the corner. It has been in the corner for a very long time.

It is the only thing in this station that is doing something new.

It grows from the silt where the grating has collapsed, a single stalk rising maybe thirty centimeters, branching twice, maybe three times, into frond-like terminations that hang in the still water with the slow patience of something that has never been moved by current. It is pale. Translucent. The color of skim milk, or of bone marrow, or of the inside of a stem. You can see through it — not clearly, but enough to know that its interior is not solid. There is a structure inside. A central filament, denser than the surrounding tissue, that runs from base to tip. It is the color of copper oxide.

Because it is. Or near enough. The organism has incorporated metal from the cable it grows on. It is not a plant. It has no chlorophyll, no chloroplasts, no cellular machinery for photosynthesis, and there is no light here to photosynthesize. What it has is something else — a mechanism, imperfectly understood, for drawing electrons from the cable's residual current and using them to drive a metabolic cycle. It is, in the truest sense, eating electricity. Not much. A trickle. But the cable it is wrapped around carries a trickle, and the organism takes what it needs and lets the rest continue on to wherever the rest was going.

It is alone. There is only one. You search for others and find none — not on this cable, not on the other cables that descend into the sump, not on the grating, not on the walls. One organism. One instance. It may have been here since before the flood. Its base — where stalk meets cable meets silt — is thick, slightly more than a centimeter in diameter, and the thickness is not uniform. There are growth rings. Not wood, not calcium — something chitinous, something the organism secretes and abandons as it extends upward. You cannot count them. They are too fine, too close together, and the water is too dark. But you understand that they represent time. This organism has been recording its own persistence in the way a tree records seasons, except there are no seasons here. Only the slow accumulation of success.

It does not respond to you. You touch it — gently, with one numbed finger — and it is cool and smooth and slightly yielding, like cold cartilage. It does not flinch. It does not change its behavior, because its behavior is growth, and growth is the slowest thing in this room.

It is the only thing in the Drowned Switching Station that is winning its war with entropy.

In a hundred years, when the steel has thinned to lace and the copper has dissolved to patina and the grating has fallen and the relays have seized and the last click has clicked and the bus bars have gone cold and dark — when everything mechanical has finally conceded to the water and the cold and the slow chemistry of dissolution — this organism will still be here. Larger. Maybe branching. Maybe, if the cable it feeds on lasts long enough, sending a frond up through the collapsed grating into the room above. Or maybe not. Maybe it will simply persist, a pale shape in a dark room, eating the last electrons from a dead grid, growing at a rate that no human patience could measure, existing in a silence that no human ear could endure.

It will not know it is alone. It does not know it is alone now. It does not know anything. It grows. That is its entire response to being.

**The Microscopic Layer**

There are other organisms. You cannot see them. You know they exist because the water is not sterile — the convection cell that exchanges the room's volume every several hours brings in cells, spores, fragments of the microbial community of the flooded corridor outside. Most of them die here. The cold, the metal load, the darkness, the absence of any food source they are adapted to use — all of it is hostile. They enter through the floor vents, drift in the still water for hours or days, and dissolve. Their biomass becomes part of the silt. The silt is, in part, made of them — generations of microbial ghosts, compressed into that charcoal-dark sediment chest-deep in the sump below.

But a few persist. A very few. They are the things smaller than a millimeter that the original survey noted. They are not inhabitants so much as residues — strains that can survive here but cannot flourish, that exist at the very lowest threshold of metabolic viability, processing energy at rates so low that a single cell division might take weeks. They are in the water. They are on the walls. They are on the bus bars, on the cable runs, on the grating. They are a film, a wash, a biological patina so thin it is indistinguishable from the corrosion it lives on.

They do not matter to the station. The station does not notice them. They are the quietest thing in a very quiet place.

---

**The Relay**

This is a stretch. A relay is not alive. A relay is a switch — an electromagnet that opens and closes a circuit in response to a control signal. It has no metabolism, no cells, no will. It does what it was built to do.

But it clicks.

Every twelve minutes — approximately, irregularly, on a schedule that has something to do with the upstream power source and nothing to do with anything in this room — a relay somewhere in the station cycles. Opens or closes. The click is sharp, clean, percussive. It travels through the water and through the steel with equal clarity and arrives at you as a single note with no referent. No light turns on. No motor starts. No signal reaches a device that needs it. The circuit it controls terminates in open water, in a cable that runs to a building that is gone. The relay does not know this. It was built to click when told to click, and it is still being told, and so it clicks.

If you stay long enough — long enough for the cold to reach your bones, long enough for the blue-green pulses to become a language you almost understand, long enough for the filamentous organism in the corner to feel like a companion — the relay begins to feel like the heartbeat of the place. Not metaphorically. Physiologically. Your own pulse slows in the cold, drops toward the rhythm of the room, and the click arrives in the silence between your heartbeats with a precision that feels orchestrated. It isn't. It is coincidence. But coincidence, at this depth, in this cold, in this dark, acquires a weight that it does not possess at the surface.

The relay is not alive. But it is the thing in this station that most resembles life: it does something. It acts. It has a rhythm. It responds to a signal from far away with a local event. It is the only thing in the room that makes sound. And sound, in water this still, in a room this enclosed, is a kind of presence that is difficult to distinguish from life.

---

**The Station Itself**

And then there is the station.

The station is not alive. But it is not entirely dead, either. It is in a third state — the state of a machine that is running, that has been running for longer than most living things persist, that has no operator and no purpose and no awareness of its own obsolescence. It breathes — the convection cell, the slow exchange of warm and cold water through the vents. It has a pulse — the forty-hertz hum of the bus bars, felt in the chest more than heard in the ear. It has a metabolism — the current that enters from upstream and leaves through dead circuits, the slow oxidation of its own steel, the dissolution of its own copper. It has a rhythm — the relay, clicking every twelve minutes into the dark.

It is, in the terms that this place makes available, an organism. A very large, very slow, very cold organism that has been alive for three quarters of a century without anyone knowing. It preserved itself by accident. It feeds its single passenger — the filamentous thing in the corner — without knowing it is feeding anything. It will die when the upstream power fails, or when the steel finally gives way and the room floods fully and the convection cell that saved it becomes irrelevant, or when the last relay seizes and the last click is clicked and the silence becomes total.

Until then, it lives. It is the largest, oldest, slowest thing in this place, and it is the thing that makes the place what it is. Everything else — the organisms on the junction boxes, the filament in the corner, the microbial film on the walls, you — is a passenger. The station is the habitat. The station is the *body*.

---

**What is not here**

No fish. No crustaceans. No annelids, no nematodes visible to the eye, no mollusks, no colonial organisms, nothing with a nervous system, nothing that moves. The cold and the dark and the metal are too much. The station is a hostile environment for everything except the things that have found a way to eat its hostility — the junction-box organisms eating its iron, the filamentous thing eating its current, the microbial film eating its corrosion. Everything else is excluded. The station is a room that has chosen its occupants by the simple mechanism of being uninhabitable, and what remains is what could survive being unwanted.

You are not wanted here either. The cold makes that clear. You are a visitor, and the station is very patient with visitors, and the station will let you leave whenever you choose, and the station will not remember you were here. It does not have that capacity. It has never needed it.
