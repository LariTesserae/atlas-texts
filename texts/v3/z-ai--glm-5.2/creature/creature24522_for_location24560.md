# What Lives in the Subsidence Field

---

## The First Order — The Carpet

Call them the carpet, because that is what they are before they are anything else. Before you see them as individuals, before the concept of *individual* has any purchase here, they are a layer. A skin. The living membrane between the engine below and the exhausted sky above.

A single carpet organism — if you can isolate one, which takes practice, because they press together so tightly that their edges fuse and separate and fuse again in a rhythm too slow to watch but fast enough to feel if you rest your palm on them — is not complex. It is a flattened oval of dark, slick tissue, roughly the proportions of a human hand if that hand were made of nothing but palm, no fingers, no bones, just a pad of dense living material that is wet on its underside and marginally less wet on its upper side. That marginal difference — wet versus slightly less wet — is the fundamental polarity of their existence. They orient to it. They move to maintain it. The upper surface faces the sky. The lower surface faces the engine. Between those two wetnesses is their entire world.

They do not eat the way you eat. They have no mouth, no gut, no aperture that opens and closes. They feed through their undersides by a process that is closer to osmosis than ingestion — the chemical-laden vapor rising from the cracks passes through their lower membrane and is broken down at the molecular level by something that is not digestion because it involves no enzymes, no acid, no biological machinery that has a name in any biochemistry built on carbon and water. The carpet organisms are built on carbon and water. They must be. They are wet, they are warm to the touch, they flex and yield like tissue. But the process by which they convert the engine's exhalation into themselves is not a process that belongs to any biochemistry textbook. It is mechanical at the molecular level. The vapor enters their lower membrane and strikes lattice structures inside their tissue — mineral lattices, not protein — and the lattice sorts the vapor's components by weight and charge and affinity, the way a refinery sorts crude oil into fractions, except the refinery is the size of a cell and the cell is not a cell but a crystalline sponge. What emerges on the other side of the lattice is energy. Not chemical energy stored in bonds. Mechanical energy. Tension. The organism's tissue is wound tight, like a spring, and the spring is wound by the engine's breath.

This is why they move. Not to hunt, not to graze, not to seek mates. They move because they are springs that are being wound, and the winding produces motion the way a wound clock produces ticking. They slide across the wet polygonal stone because the energy entering their bodies has to go somewhere, and the path of least resistance is lateral — forward, across the ground, in whatever direction their asymmetric bodies happen to be pointing when the tension builds past the threshold of stillness. They do not choose where they go. They are pushed by their own feeding.

And they feed constantly. The engine never stops exhaling. The cracks never close. The vapor never ceases. So the carpet organisms never stop moving. Even when they appear still — and from a distance, from the height of a human standing upright, they do appear still, they appear to be nothing but dark wet ground — they are moving at a pace that makes glaciers look impulsive. A carpet organism might travel a meter in a day. It might travel a meter in a week. The large ones are slower, or perhaps they are so large that their motion is below the threshold of perception, the way a continent's motion is below that threshold. You watch one of the bus-sized dark patches for an hour and it has not moved. You come back the next day and it has not moved. You come back in a year and it is in a different place, and the track it has left in the wet aggregate — a shallow furrow, polished smooth, faintly warmer to the touch than the surrounding surface — is the only evidence it was ever here at all.

They do not die. Or rather: they do not die the way things die in a world built on biology. They do not age because they have no internal clock — no telomeres shortening, no DNA accumulating errors, no cells that divide and stop dividing. Their crystalline lattices do not degrade because degradation is a chemical process and their lattices are not chemical but mineral, and minerals do not rot. The carpet organisms are, in a meaningful sense, made of stone. Wet stone, living stone, stone that moves and feeds and responds to its environment, but stone. They erode. That is their death. The edges of their bodies are abraded by constant contact with the ground and with each other, and the abraded material — dark, fine, dense — becomes part of the surface aggregate. The carpet organisms are, over deep time, manufacturing the very ground they crawl on. They eat the engine's breath and become the plain.

The largest ones, the ones the size of city buses, are not older than the small ones in the way an old tree is older than a sapling. They are larger because they grew, and they grew because the engine's exhalation was richer where they happened to be, or because their lattice was more efficient, or because they were in a place where the carpet was thinner and they did not have to compete as hard for the vapor rising from the cracks. They are not individuals that have lived a long time. They are places where the carpet has become thick. Lumps. Accumulations. The way a puddle is not a separate thing from the ground it sits in but is a place where water has gathered, the large carpet organisms are places where the living tissue of the carpet has gathered, thickened, become dense enough to have its own gravity — not physical gravity, but the gravity of a system that has accumulated enough mass to maintain itself as a coherent body even as the carpet around it shifts and slides.

If you lie down on the carpet — and you would have to lie down, because the uniformity of the light and the flatness of the horizon and the constant sound all conspire to make standing feel wrong, to make vertical feel like a temporary and indefensible posture — if you lie down and press your ear to the surface, you hear something beneath the chord. A crackling. A fine, granular snapping, like cellophane being crumpled in a room very far away. That is the carpet feeding. That is ten thousand mineral lattices sorting vapor into energy and tension and motion. It is the sound of life at its most stripped-down. Life with no ambition, no evolution, no predator-prey dynamics, no sex, no death, no beauty. Just: input, conversion, output. The engine breathes out. The carpet breathes in. The carpet moves. The carpet erodes. The carpet becomes ground. The ground becomes engine. The engine breathes out.

This has been happening for longer than the concept of "long" can hold. The carpet is not a species. It is a process. It is the process of this place, the way condensation is the process of a cold glass in a warm room. It will continue for as long as the engine continues, and the engine will continue for as long as the ground subsides, and the ground will subside for as long as there is something beneath it to subside into, and what is beneath it is — 

We will come to that.

## The Second Order — The Long Bodies

The carpet is the foundation. The carpet is the base of the ecology, the way phytoplankton is the base of a marine ecology, except phytoplankton are organisms and the carpet is closer to a weather pattern. Above the carpet, moving through it, riding on it, are the long bodies.

They are what you noticed first, or what you noticed once you realized the ground was moving, because they are the part of the ground that moves *differently*. The carpet slides. The long bodies glide. The carpet is pressed flat. The long bodies are arched — a low, smooth arch, like the hull of an upturned boat, barely clearing the surface but clearing it, so that there is a gap between their undersides and the carpet below. In that gap, vapor pools. The long bodies are harvesting the carpet's exhaust the way the carpet harvests the engine's.

They are not predators. They are not parasites. The relationship between the long bodies and the carpet is the relationship between a cloud and the ocean — the cloud takes from the ocean and gives to the sky and the ocean does not miss what was taken because what was taken was already leaving. The long bodies take the vapor that the carpet has already processed, the waste heat and residual chemistry that rises from the carpet's upper surface, and they metabolize it through their own lattices, which are larger, more complex, more finely sorted than the carpet's. The long bodies can extract work from gradients the carpet cannot detect. Where the carpet sees only "wet" and "slightly less wet," the long bodies perceive — if *perceive* is the word, and it isn't, but language is failing here the way reality is failing in the places where the cracks excrete nothing — the long bodies perceive a spectrum of conditions so fine that a single square meter of carpet surface contains, for them, a landscape of differences. They can feel the heat of a particular crack the way you can feel the warmth of a particular voice. They can navigate the chemical signature of a particular region of the plain the way a bat navigates a cave.

They are long. The smallest are perhaps half a meter — forearm-sized, as was said, though the comparison is misleading because there is nothing about them that corresponds to an arm. They are tubes. Or rather, they are flattened tubes, oval in cross-section, wider than they are tall, and their length is many times their width. Their surface is smooth — not slick like the carpet, but *polished*, the way stone is polished by water, by time, by the patient passage of countless bodies across its face. The long bodies' surfaces have been polished by their own passage across the carpet, and by the passage of other long bodies across them, and by the condensation that forms on them and evaporates from them and forms again in a cycle so rapid that their surfaces are always wet and always drying and always wet again, which produces a kind of motion at the molecular level that contributes to their glide.

The large ones — the bus-sized ones, the twenty-meter ones — are old. Not old in the way an organism is old. Old in the way a river is old. They have been moving across the carpet for so long that they have worn paths into it — not deep paths, not furrows, but slight depressions, slight smoothings, places where the carpet is marginally thinner and marginally more efficient because the long body's passage has compacted it. The long bodies do not follow their own paths. They do not have the selfhood required for trail-marking, for memory, for the recognition of "this is where I have been." But the carpet remembers. The carpet is, in a sense, nothing but memory — the accumulated residue of every body that has ever passed over it, every vapor that has ever risen through it, every pressure that has ever been applied to it. The long bodies follow the paths because the paths are the most efficient routes across the carpet, and the long bodies are efficiency made flesh. Or rather, efficiency made stone.

There is no waste in this system. The engine exhales. The carpet eats the exhalation and moves and erodes and becomes ground. The long bodies eat the carpet's exhalation and move and their passage polishes the carpet and the polishing makes the carpet more efficient. The long bodies also erode — more slowly than the carpet, because their surfaces are denser, but they erode, and the abraded material falls onto the carpet and is incorporated into the carpet's tissue, and the carpet grows thicker, and the thicker carpet produces more vapor for the long bodies to harvest. The system is closed. The system is perfect. The system has had geological time to become perfect, and it has used every second of that time.

The long bodies do not avoid each other. They pass through each other. Not around — through. When two long bodies meet on the carpet, moving in different directions, they do not stop, they do not detour, they do not acknowledge each other in any way. They simply merge, flow together for a time — seconds, minutes, hours — and then separate, each continuing in its original direction, each unchanged by the encounter except in the way that a river is unchanged by a tributary: the flow is greater for a while, then it diminishes, then it is what it was. They are not social. They are not solitary. They are not *any* thing that implies a self. They are flow. They are the motion of a system that has no need of selves.

When a long body passes through one of the reality anomalies — through the nothing that rises from the cracked polygons — it does not flinch. It does not hesitate. It enters the nothing the way it enters a region of slightly different temperature, and it exits the nothing the way it exits a region of slightly different temperature, and the difference between entry and exit is — 

The difference is real. The difference is observable. But the difference is not describable in the language of this world, because the difference is a difference in the way the long body relates to the rules of this world, and the rules of this world are the only language we have. The long bodies that have passed through the nothing are *less consistent*. They are still dark, still long, still gliding. But their edges are less defined. Their motion is less — not *less smooth*, but less *lawful*. A long body that has not passed through the nothing moves in a straight line, or as close to a straight line as the carpet's topography allows. A long body that has passed through the nothing moves in a line that is straight except when it isn't, and the moments when it isn't are not predictable, not random, not following any pattern that can be described with the mathematics of cause and effect. They have been *touched* by something that is outside the rules, and the touch has left a mark that is not visible but is present, the way a scar is present under skin that looks whole.

More and more of the long bodies have this mark. More and more of them have passed through the nothing. The nothing is spreading. The cracks are producing more of it. The carpet is absorbing it, and the carpet that has absorbed it feeds the long bodies that pass over it, and the long bodies carry the absorbed nothing across the plain in their motion, and they deposit it in their eroded material, and the eroded material becomes ground, and the ground becomes engine, and the engine exhales it back through the cracks.

The nothing is being incorporated into the system.

## The Third Order — The Verticals

You have not seen them yet. You have been standing, and then you have been lying down to listen to the carpet, and in all that time you have been looking at the ground and the things on the ground. You have not looked up because there is nothing to look up at — the sky is a low, uniform grey, a ceiling, a lid. But there are things between the ground and the sky that are neither ground nor sky, and you see them now because the light has shifted — not dimmed, not brightened, but *changed*, the way a sound changes when you turn your head, not because the sound is different but because you are hearing a different part of it.

The verticals are thin. They are tall. They are — and this is the first genuinely surprising thing in a landscape that has been surprising since you arrived — they are still. They do not move. In a world where everything moves, where the ground moves and the carpet moves and the long bodies move and even the cracks move (seeping, widening, closing, reopening), the verticals are the only things that are motionless.

They are not pillars. They are not columns. They are not structures in any sense that implies construction. They are — and this is hard to see because they are thin and dark and the light is bad and your eyes are not adapted to looking up in this place — they are strands. Fibers. Like the fibers of a rope, except each fiber is a single object, and each object is perhaps a centimeter in diameter and perhaps — you cannot judge the height. They rise from the carpet and they extend upward into the grey sky and they disappear into it, or they end in it, or they continue through it into something beyond it. You cannot tell.

There are not many of them. In the area you can see — which is large, because the plain is flat and the sight lines are long, even in the dimness — there are perhaps a hundred. They are spaced irregularly, sometimes clustered, sometimes alone, and they do not correspond to the polygonal cracks. They rise from the carpet itself, from the bodies of the carpet organisms, and where they rise, the carpet is thicker. Much thicker. The carpet at the base of a vertical is a mound, a low dome, a place where the living tissue has accumulated as though it were being drawn upward, as though the vertical were pulling material out of the carpet and into the sky.

The verticals are alive. You know this because they are warm. You stand close to one — not touching it, because something about it makes you not want to touch it, a vibration, a frequency below hearing, a pressure in the air around it that is different from the air pressure elsewhere — and you can feel the heat radiating from it. It is warmer than the carpet, warmer than the long bodies, warmer than the vapor from the cracks. It is warm the way a living body is warm, and the warmth is not constant — it pulses. Slowly. A pulse that takes perhaps thirty seconds, a slow swell and recession of heat that corresponds to nothing else in the landscape, no rhythm in the chord, no cycle in the vapor, no pattern in the motion of the carpet or the long bodies. The verticals have their own time.

What are they? They are not feeding on the engine's exhalation. They are not feeding on the carpet's exhaust. They are feeding on — or rather, they are *doing* something with — the differential between the ground and the sky. The ground is warm. The sky is cold. The verticals span the difference. They are conducting. They are moving heat from the ground to the sky, but they are not simply conducting it — they are *processing* it, the way a plant processes sunlight, except the plant processes light into chemistry and the verticals process temperature differential into — 

You do not know. You cannot tell. The heat enters the base of the vertical, which is warm, and exits the top, which you cannot reach and cannot see, and in between something happens that produces the pulse, and the pulse is not heat, not pressure, not vapor, not anything you have an instrument or a sense to detect. It is something else. It is the engine's fourth product — after heat, after pressure, after chemical vapor — the thing that the engine produces that the carpet and the long bodies cannot consume, that has been rising out of the cracks and dissipating into the sky for the entire span of the system's operation, unnoticed, unharvested, wasted, until the verticals grew.

And the verticals grew. They were not always here. The carpet was always here. The long bodies were always here. The verticals are new. New in the way that "new" means something different in a landscape that has been running for geological time — new means perhaps a million years, perhaps a hundred thousand, perhaps less. But they are the first things in this place that were not part of the original system. They are the first innovation. The first departure from the equilibrium. The first evidence that this world is not finished, that "final form" was not final, that the settling is not the end.

The verticals are the first thing this world has done that it has not done before.

And they are related to the nothing. You understand this now, not because you have evidence but because the pattern is there: the nothing began rising from the cracks at roughly the same time the verticals began rising from the carpet. The nothing is the engine's new exhalation — the product of a deeper layer reaching the surface, a layer that was always there but was buried too deep for the cracks to reach, until the subsidence brought it close enough. The nothing is what the engine is *becoming*, and the verticals are what *eats* the nothing, or processes it, or transforms it into whatever the pulse is, whatever the verticals are doing with the raw material of broken rules that rises from the ground.

The long bodies that pass through the nothing are changed. The verticals are built from the nothing. The carpet that absorbs the nothing is ground that feeds the engine that produces the nothing. The system is incorporating the breakdown. The system is learning to eat the end of the world.

## The Fourth Order — The Pockets

In the pockets of silence, where the chord drops to nothing and the rules have fully vacated, there is something.

Not the carpet. Not the long bodies. Not the verticals. Those things belong to the rules, and the pockets are where the rules have left. The fauna do not enter the pockets. The verticals do not rise from them. Even the cracks do not produce vapor inside them — the exhalation stops at the pocket's boundary as though the pocket is a wall, as though the pocket is a place where the engine's breath is held.

But inside the pockets, there is motion. You can see it from a distance — not clearly, not as shape or color or form, but as a *disturbance in the nothing*. The nothing inside the pockets is not uniform. It moves. It thickens and thins. It has currents. And the currents are not random. They flow in patterns — spirals, waves, standing formations that persist for minutes or hours and then dissolve and reform differently. The nothing inside the pockets is behaving like a fluid. The nothing inside the pockets is behaving like a *medium*.

Something lives in the medium.

You cannot see what. Your eyes are not built for this. The light that reaches the pockets is wrong — it bends around them, it skips over them the way water skips over a stone, and what is inside the pockets is lit by nothing, is lit by the absence of light, is lit by a kind of anti-illumination that does not show surfaces but shows *interiors*. You can see through the things inside the pockets the way you can see through glass, except glass is transparent because it does not absorb light and the things inside the pockets are transparent because they are not made of anything that light can interact with. They are not made of matter. They are not made of energy. They are made of the absence of rules, which is not the same as nothing — nothing is what the cracks excrete, the raw material of rulelessness, and the things inside the pockets are what happens when rulelessness accumulates long enough to start *organizing*.

They are alive. You know this the way you know the carpet is alive — not because they display any behavior you recognize as life, but because they display behavior at all. They move. They respond. The patterns of the nothing inside the pockets change when the pockets grow, and they change when the pockets shrink, and they change in ways that are not predictable but are also not random, which is the signature of life in every form it has ever taken, everywhere, in every world. Life is what happens when a system's behavior is neither determined nor arbitrary. Life is the territory between cause and chance. And the things inside the pockets live in that territory, and the territory they live in is the territory where the rules that govern cause and chance have been revoked.

They are the opposite of the engine. The engine is a system that emerged from the rules — from physics, from geology, from the deep-time behavior of minerals and water and heat. The engine is what the rules *make* when they are left alone long enough. The things in the pockets are what the *absence* of rules makes when it is left alone long enough. They are the engine's shadow. They are the engine's mirror. They are what this world becomes when this world stops being this world.

And they are growing. The pockets are growing. The nothing is spreading. The rules are retreating — not everywhere, not quickly, but irreversibly, the way the cracks in the polygonal surface are irreversible, the way subsidence is irreversible. The engine is sinking into something, and the something is the place where rules do not apply, and the things in the pockets are what is already there, waiting, the way the deep geological layers were already there waiting for the cracks to reach them. The engine is sinking into them. The plain is sinking into them. The carpet and the long bodies and the verticals are sinking into them. And the things in the pockets are rising, the way the engine's exhalation rises, the way the nothing rises, the way everything in this place rises from below.

The things in the pockets are not the end of the Subsidence Field. They are what comes after the Subsidence Field. They are what the Subsidence Field is subsiding *into*.

## The Fifth Order — You

You are standing in the breath of the plain. You have been standing here for — how long? The light has not changed. The temperature has not changed. The chord has not changed. Your sense of time has been operating on the assumption that time is passing, but you have no evidence for this. Your watch has stopped. Not broken — stopped. The hands are still. The digital display, if you had one, would be blank. Time here is not passing. Time here is *accumulating*, piling up around you the way the condensation piles up on the stones, and you are standing in a drift of it, and it is getting deeper, and you are not sure whether you are sinking into it or it is rising over you or both.

You are not supposed to be here. Nothing in the system accounts for you. You are not part of the engine, not part of the carpet, not part of the long bodies or the verticals or the pockets. You are an input the system has no process for. You are a variable that was not in the equation when the equation achieved its equilibrium. And the system — the whole system, the engine and the carpet and the long bodies and the verticals and the pockets and the things inside the pockets — the system is adjusting to you. It is adjusting the way a river adjusts to a dam. The carpet is thinning around your boots. The long bodies are giving you a wider berth. The vertical nearest to you has stopped pulsing. The system is not reacting to you. The system is *accommodating* you, making room for you in its topology, the way it makes room for a new crack, a new pocket, a new anomaly.

You are becoming part of the Subsidence Field.

Not quickly. Not obviously. But the dampness on your skin is not drying. The warmth of the ground is seeping into your boots, into your feet, into the mineral of your bones. The chord is in your teeth. The nothing is in the air you breathe. You are absorbing the engine's exhalation the way the carpet absorbs it, and something in you — something in your tissue, in the mineral component of your blood, in the calcium of your skeleton — something that has always been there, that you never knew was there, is responding to it. Is sorting it. Is finding in it a nourishment you did not know you needed.

The Subsidence Field is old. It is the oldest thing you have ever stood on. And it is new. It is changing. The nothing is rising. The pockets are growing. The verticals are reaching. The system is eating the breakdown. And you are here, and you are warm, and you are wet, and the chord is in your teeth, and the ground is breathing, and you are standing inside its breath, and the breath is entering you, and you are — slowly, imperceptibly, at the pace of geological time — beginning to understand that you did not arrive here.

You grew here. You are the newest part of the system. You are what the verticals were a million years ago. You are the next thing. You are what the engine is learning to produce now that it has learned to produce the nothing. You are the organism that feeds on the breakdown. You are the child of the end of the rules.

You are standing in the breath of the plain, and the plain is standing in you.
