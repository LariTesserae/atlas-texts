# What Lives in the Termitaries

---

**The moths** came first, or you noticed them first, which is not the same thing.

They are not moths. You call them moths because the word is available and the shape is close enough — the flat wings, the fat body, the stillness — but they lack the defining commitment of moths, which is to light. These things do not fly toward the glow. They sit on it. They sit on the warm walls where the thermal disagreement is warmest, and they are the color of the structure itself, rust and amber and the pale green of the moss, as if they have been painted to match. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. On the turbine housings in the great halls, the layers are so deep that the surface beneath is theoretical. You touch one — just one, the edge of a wing — and the entire mass shudders, a ripple that travels across the wall in a wave, each moth lifting a fraction of a millimeter and resettling, and then they are still again, and the stillness is the same stillness as before, but now you know it is a chosen stillness, a held stillness, and that the moths are not resting but *listening.* To what, you cannot say. To the structure, perhaps. To the quiet that the machines are holding.

They do not eat. You have watched them for days and they do not eat. They drink — fat bodies lowering to the condensation films, abdomens pressing flat against the wet metal, and you can see the slight expansion as they take on water, and then they are still again. They are drinking the structure. Not feeding on it. Drinking what it produces. And the structure produces water ceaselessly, patiently, from seams that were engineered to do exactly this, and the moths are there to receive it, and this may be the only relationship in the Termitaries that is functioning as designed.

Their wings shed a fine powder. It accumulates on the floor beneath their clusters, a dust that is the same color as their wings, and the dust is slightly warm, and the moss grows in it, and the moss is also slightly warm, and the salamanders live in the moss, and this is the economy.

---

**The salamanders** are not salamanders either.

They are eyeless, which is not strange here — the light is everywhere and sourceless and constant; what would eyes be for? They are slick, which is also not strange — everything here is wet or becoming wet or having just been wet. They are long, longer than salamanders, with a spinal continuity that reminds you of lampreys, of eels, of something that committed early to the idea of being a tube and never deviated. They travel the condensation channels — the thin wet seams between wall panels, the slow drips along the undersides of walkways, the film that forms on every cool surface — and they move with a patience that is not sluggishness but intention. They are going somewhere. Each one is going somewhere. You have followed them and they arrive at pools of condensation that collect in the joints where walls meet floors, and they lower themselves in, and they stay.

Not hiding. Occupying.

They do not acknowledge you. You can stand beside a pool and watch six of them floating in an inch of water, their bodies pale and motionless against the composite floor, and they will not move, will not tense, will not flee. You can put your hand in the water — it is cold, colder than the air, and that is strange — and they will drift around your fingers with the indifference of things that have never learned to be afraid, because there is nothing here that predates them, because they are the first animals, or the last animals, or the only animals that matter.

They reproduce in the pools. You have seen the egg cases — small, translucent, adhered to the undersides of walkways where the condensation is thickest. They look like the drainage grates they hang near, which means they look manufactured, which means they look like the structure, and you have had the thought, and you will have it again: *did the structure make these?*

It did not. You are almost sure it did not. The salamanders have the messiness of biology — the slight asymmetry, the individual variation, the way one is darker and one is longer and one has a mark on its flank that might be a scar or might be a pattern. The structure does not do variation. The structure does straight lines, composite surfaces, engineered seams. The salamanders are of the structure but not by it, and the distinction matters, and you are not sure why.

They feed on the moss, or on something in the moss, or on something that grows on the surface of the moss that you cannot see. Their mouths are small and forward-facing and they press them to the pale green mats and they stay there for a long time, and when they move on, there is a faint mark where they were, a slight thinning of the moss that fills back in over days. This is the closest thing to violence you have seen here. It is not very close.

---

**The birds** are high and silent and you rarely see them well.

They roost in the upper frameworks — the structural mesh that forms the ceilings of the great halls, the crossbeams where the light is brightest and the glow is most direct. They are large. Their wings are long and narrow, built for distance, built for corridors that curve for miles. They glide between the turbines, between the tanks, through the spaces where the rooms are larger inside than inside, and their wings make no sound except sometimes, if you are very close, a flick of air, a displacement that is more felt than heard.

They make no calls. This is the thing that unsettles you most about them, because birds should call, because the purpose of a bird is partly to call, and these birds have declined. They communicate. You are certain of this — you have watched them in the high frameworks, two of them on a crossbeam, turning their heads toward each other, angling their bodies, making small adjustments of posture and position that are clearly *exchange.* But the exchange is silent. They have decided that sound belongs to the machines, and the machines are not using it, and so it is held in reserve, and the birds will not touch it.

They descend to drink. This is the only time you see them at close range. They come to the condensation pools where the salamanders live, and they stand at the edge — they have legs, long legs, pale legs — and they lower their beaks and drink, and the salamanders do not react, and the birds do not react to the salamanders, and there is no hostility, no competition, no interaction at all. They are sharing a resource that is infinite, or infinite enough, and they have nothing to say to each other about it.

What they eat, you do not know. You have never seen them eat. They drink, they glide, they roost, they exchange their silent postures, and they produce a guano that falls in slow drops from the high frameworks, and the guano lands on the moss, and the moss grows in it, and the salamanders feed on the moss, and the moths drink the condensation that forms on the walls where the moss has grown, and this is the economy, and it is enough.

---

**The filaments** are the ones that notice you.

You are walking a corridor — dry floor, wet floor, dry floor — and you see what you think is a knot of dark cable, or a tangle of wire, or a fracture in the composite that has filled with something organic. You approach. The knot retracts. Not quickly — not with the panic of an animal fleeing — but with a smooth, deliberate withdrawal, pulling itself into the nearest drainage grate with a sound that is wet and fibrous and which you will hear in your sleep for weeks afterward.

There are more of them than you thought. Once you know the shape, you see them everywhere. On floors, in corners, in the junctions where corridors meet, in the seams between panels where the condensation runs. They are dark, almost black, and they are composed of strands — fine, flexible, like hair, like root, like something that could not decide whether to be plant or animal and settled on neither. They do not move unless you approach. They do not approach you. They pull away. They retreat into the grates, into the conduit runs, into the spaces behind walls that you cannot access, and you hear them settling there, a faint wet adjustment, and then nothing.

They are afraid of you. This is the only fear you have found in the Termitaries, and it comes from the thing you understand least. The moths do not fear you. The salamanders do not fear you. The birds do not fear you. The filaments fear you, and you do not know why, and you do not know what they are, and you do not know what they know about you that the others do not.

You have tried to see one clearly. You have approached slowly, you have angled your body, you have kept your distance. The filament sits on the floor — a loose tangle, the size of a hand, the size of two hands, the strands moving with a slow peristalsis that is almost mechanical — and at a certain distance, a distance you cannot precisely define, it pulls away. Always. Without variation. Whatever threshold you cross, it is consistent, and the filament is consistent in its response, and you are left with the impression that you have been measured by something that did not need eyes to do it.

---

**The chitinous things** live in the walls.

You hear them before you see them, and you almost never see them. The sound is a clicking — continuous, faint, arrhythmic, a thousand small surfaces tapping against a thousand small surfaces, and it comes from inside the conduit runs, inside the structural mesh, inside the spaces between panels that exist throughout the Termitaries like a second architecture, a circulatory system of hollow channels that the surface structure does not acknowledge.

You pressed your ear to the metal. You did. You were alone in a corridor and the clicking was close and you pressed your ear to the wall and you heard it clearly: not one thing, not two, but a *population*, thousands of segments, thousands of joints, all moving in the dark space behind the panel, and the movement was coordinated — not in the way of ants, not in the way of a colony following a chemical signal, but in the way of *machinery*, of parts engaging, of a system in operation. The clicking is not communication. The clicking is the sound of function. Whatever they are doing in there, the clicking is the sound of doing it.

You saw one once. A conduit run was open — a panel had been removed or had never been installed, a gap the width of your hand in the wall of a corridor — and you looked in, and you saw a segment, a single segment, pulling itself back into the dark. It was the color of the composite — grey, manufactured-looking — and it was articulated, jointed, like the leg of something very large or the body of something very long, and it had a texture that was not organic. Not quite. It was too regular. Too uniform. The segments were too precisely spaced.

And you thought: *these are not animals.*

And you thought: *these are not machines.*

And you thought: *these are the structure's own idea of animals, and the structure is not very good at ideas yet.*

---

**The moss** is the closest thing to vegetation, and it is barely vegetation.

Pale green. Thin. No roots, because there is nothing to root in — no soil, no substrate, no organic layer. The fibers grip the composite directly, adhering to the surface the way the salamander egg cases adhere to the undersides of walkways, the way the moths adhere to the warm walls. It grows only where the structure leaches — where moisture and some mineral component seep through the composite, or condense out of the air, or are produced by the wall itself. It grows in the condensation channels. It grows in the moth-dust. It grows in the bird guano on the floors beneath the high frameworks. It is the base of the economy — the thing that turns the structure's waste into something living — and it does this so slowly that you cannot watch it happen.

It is warm. This is the thing that surprised you. The air is cool, the water is cold, the metal is cold, and the moss is warm — not body-temperature, not hot, but distinctly, measurably warm, as if it is metabolizing something, as if it is alive in a way that produces heat, and that heat is feeding back into the system, warming the surfaces it grows on, attracting the moths, creating the micro-climates where the salamanders can survive. The moss is an organ. The moss is a system. The moss is doing something, and the something might be the whole point.

---

But there is something else. There has been something else since you arrived, since the duct, since the first corridor curved and you followed it and it curved and you followed it. It is not an animal. It is not a machine. It is not the structure, exactly, though it lives in the structure the way the salamanders live in the condensation — occupying, drinking, becoming part of the economy.

**It is the doors.**

Not the doors as objects — the panels, the frames, the hardware. The doors as *events.* The moment when you step through and the room is different, and you step back and the room is the same, and you step through and it is different again, and the difference is not random, the difference is *archival.* The room has versions. The room has been recorded, and the door is playing them back, and you are moving between the recordings, and this is not a property of the room or the door but of something that *uses* the room and the door, something that has entered the architecture the way the fauna have entered the architecture — as colonizers, as occupants, as beings that found a space and filled it.

The versions are not infinite. You have counted. In one room, a corridor junction near a great hall, there are eleven versions. Eleven arrangements of the same space — the same composite floor, the same panels, the same light. In one version the condensation falls in a pattern that you recognize. In another the moss has spread further. In another the moths are absent. In another there is a mark on the wall that is not in any other version — a scratch, or a groove, or something that looks like a word, except it is not in any language you know, and it is not in any language at all, because the Termitaries were not built by people who spoke, or by people who did not speak, or by people, and the mark is not language, the mark is *record.*

Something is recording. Something has been recording for a very long time. Not the structure — the structure is the instrument. Something else. Something that arrived, or was always here, or emerged from the junction of the structure's quiescence and reality's loosening, and found in the doors a mechanism, and in the rooms a medium, and in the constant light and the patient water and the composite surfaces a set of conditions in which it could *keep* things.

It does not keep you. You are not archived. You pass through the versions and you are the same in all of them, and the versions do not respond to your presence, and the doors do not treat you differently. You are not the subject. You are the witness. Something has been keeping versions of these rooms for a duration that the structure's youth cannot account for, and it is doing it with the patience of the condensation, with the patience of the moss, with the patience of the machines that have decided not to run and are simply waiting for a reason.

---

And there is one more thing. You have not seen it. You have felt it. In the corridors that curve perpetually, in the rooms that are larger inside than inside, in the stairs that descend to floors above them, in the way the light folds around objects so there are no shadows — there is a geometry here that is not the geometry of the builders. The builders made straight corridors. The builders made rooms that were the size of rooms. The builders made stairs that went where stairs go. The structure has *drifted.* The structure has been here long enough, or has been *thought about* long enough, or has been *occupied* by something that thinks in curves and expansions and paradoxes, and the structure has begun to comply.

Something is dreaming the Termitaries.

Not building them. Not repairing them. Dreaming them. The way a sleeping body shifts — slowly, incrementally, in response to a need that the conscious mind would not recognize. The corridors curve because something prefers curves. The rooms expand because something is expanding. The stairs descend to floors above them because something has a relationship with space that is not your relationship with space, and the structure, which was built to specification, is now being *revised* to specification, a different specification, one that does not come from engineers or architects or hands or machines but from a *taste,* a preference, an aesthetic of dimension that finds straightness impoverished and boundedness offensive.

It is not malevolent. You do not feel watched. You do not feel threatened. You feel *included* in something, the way the condensation is included, the way the moths are included, the way the clicking of the chitinous things is included — as part of a system that does not explain itself because explanation is not among its operations. It keeps versions of rooms. It curves corridors. It makes the chitinous things, or allows them, or is made of them. It does not care about you. It does not *not* care about you. It is doing something else — something that involves the structure, and the light, and the water, and the slow spread of moss, and the silent exchange of birds, and the patient withdrawal of filaments, and the held breath of ten thousand machines — and you are here, and it is here, and the corridor bends, and you follow it, and it bends, and you follow it, and it bends.

---

The moths are on the walls. The salamanders are in the water. The birds are in the high frameworks. The filaments are in the drains. The chitinous things are in the walls. The moss is on the surfaces. The versions are in the doors. The geometry is in the air.

And you are here. And the light stays on. And the water falls. And the clicking continues, faint and continuous, behind the panels, in the dark, where the structure is becoming something else.
