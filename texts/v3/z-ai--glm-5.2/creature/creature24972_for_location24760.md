# What Lives Here

---

Let the thrush come first, because it is what you would actually find.

There is one thrush. There may have been two, last season — the pale-eyed females of this species are said to pair for a span of years, though no one has studied them at this elevation because no one has been here to study them. But right now, in this season, there is one. Dark-backed, the color of wet slate, which is to say the color of everything here. The pale eye is what distinguishes it — a ring of bone-white around a black pupil, giving it an expression of perpetual startlement, or perpetual attention. These may be the same thing.

The thrush lives on the capstone of the spring. Not *on* it — *above* it, arriving each morning from somewhere lower, somewhere warmer, a roost you would have to follow it to find. It comes at first light, when the mist is thickest and the dipterans it feeds on are clustered in the still air between the fern clumps, and it leaves at dusk, when the temperature drops and the flies settle and the Fold becomes something even a cold-adapted bird cannot tolerate. In between, it sings.

The song is nearly inaudible. This is important. The thrush sings a phrase of four or five notes, rising, with a terminal trill that in a forest or a meadow would carry for hundreds of meters. Here it carries for perhaps ten. The roar of the Fold — water, wind, settling stone — absorbs it like moss absorbs rain. The song is poured into a space already full of sound, and what reaches the edges is attenuated to almost nothing. A whisper of a whisper. The ghost of a phrase.

The thrush does not adjust. It does not sing louder. It does not move to a more acoustically favorable perch. It sits on the capstone, where the spray falls heaviest and where its back is darkened with moisture within minutes, and it sings its song at the volume it was born to sing, and the Fold takes it. Whether any other thrush hears it is unknown. Whether *anything* hears it is unknown. The thrush does not appear to consider the question. It sings because the mechanism that produces song is engaged — light at a certain angle, temperature above a certain threshold, the presence of sufficient flies to sustain the metabolic cost of singing — and it will sing until the mechanism disengages or until it dies.

This is not pathos. This is not futility. This is a thrush on a rock in a wet place doing what thrushes do. If you find it sad, that is your business. The thrush has no opinion on the matter.

---

The flies are less romantic but more essential.

They are fungus gnats, or something close to fungus gnats — small, dark-winged, with bodies perhaps three millimeters long and legs that trail behind them in flight like trailing thoughts. They are everywhere between the fern clumps, where the air is still enough to permit their weak flight and where the moss produces the fungal hyphae and spores they feed on. They swarm in loose clouds, hundreds of individuals, each one tracing a path that seems random but that, if you watched long enough, would resolve into patterns — rising, falling, circling, converging on some invisible gradient of moisture or temperature that only their sensory apparatus can detect.

They breed in the moss. The females lay eggs in the saturated organic layer where moss meets stone, and the larvae — tiny, translucent, headless things — feed on the decomposing tissue and the fungi that colonize it, and they pupate and emerge as adults and fly up into the ferns and swarm and mate and die, all within a span of perhaps two weeks. The cycle is continuous through the warmer months. In winter, the larvae persist in the moss, dormant, frozen in the interstitial water, waiting for a thaw that must feel like resurrection if a larva can feel anything, which is a question for a different kind of writing.

The flies are the foundation. The thrush eats them — dozens per hour when it is feeding actively, snapping them from the air with a precision that seems at odds with its apparent indifference to everything else. The spiders eat them too.

---

The spiders are here, though you would not see them unless you looked closely, and even then you might mistake their work for the work of the water — fine strands of silk strung between fern fronds, sagging under the weight of accumulated moisture, each strand beaded with droplets that catch what light reaches the Fold and scatter it in tiny prismatic flashes. The spiders are small, grey-brown, legs translucent with the fluid of their own bodies. They rebuild their webs daily, sometimes more often, because the constant moisture and the occasional gust of wind through the Fold destroys them with a regularity that would discourage a more complex intelligence. The spiders are not discouraged. They rebuild. They catch flies. They are eaten, occasionally, by the thrush, which takes them along with the flies without distinction.

There are not many spiders. Perhaps a few dozen, spread across the Fold's floor, each one maintaining a small territory of silk and patience. They are the Fold's only predators besides the thrush, and they are not dramatic about it.

---

Something else is here. Implied.

The trail in the moss — a narrow depression, a channel pressed into the cushion moss by the passage of a small body, repeated over nights until the moss stays flattened, a pathway visible only from certain angles in certain light. The trail leads from a crevice at the base of the back wall, where the stone is warm and dry compared to everything else, out across the moss to the edge of the nearest rill, where it ends. It is the trail of a vole, or something vole-like — a small mammal, dark-furred probably, with the hunched gait and the rapid movements of an animal that knows it is prey and has arranged its life around the management of that fact.

The vole, if it is a vole, lives in the crevice. It comes out at night, when the thrush is gone, and it moves along its trail to the rill and drinks, or eats the moss, or eats the seeds of the saxifrage, or eats whatever a vole eats in a place where the food web is still being assembled. It does not stray from the trail. The moss beyond the trail is deep and wet and would soak it to the skin in seconds, and a wet vole at this temperature is a dead vole. So it follows its path, which is the path of its ancestors, if it has had ancestors here — which it may not, because the Fold is young, and the vole may be a first colonist, arrived alone or with a single mate, establishing a dynasty in moss that has never known a vole before.

Or the trail is old. Abandoned. The vole came, lived, died — of cold, of hunger, of the weasel that left the scat near the spring basin — and the trail persists as a fossil of its passage, slowly being erased by the moss's growth, which fills the depression the way water fills a bowl. In a year, the trail will be gone. In a year, there will be no evidence that a vole was ever here.

The scat near the spring basin is more definitive, but only slightly. It is small, dark, compact, containing fragments of chitin — insect parts, fly wings, the leg of something with more legs than a fly. It could be a weasel's. It could be a shrew's. It could be something else entirely, something without a name you would recognize, something that colonized this place from a different direction than the vole and the thrush and the flies and established itself in the gaps between them — a predator, small and hungry and not particularly concerned with being identified.

Whatever left the scat is probably still here. The scat is recent — days old, not weeks. The moisture has not yet dissolved it. The moss has not yet grown over it. Something is living in the Fold that eats other things that live in the Fold, and it is doing so right now, while you read this, while the thrush sings and the flies swarm and the water roars. It is in a crevice, or under a slab, or in the gap between two bedding planes where the stone is still adjusting and the warmth of depth seeps through. It is small and it is silent and it is alive.

---

But the question of who *lives* here — in the sense of *inhabits*, in the sense of *dwells*, in the sense of having arranged a life around a place rather than merely persisting in it — that question belongs to the ones who built.

---

They are gone. This must be said plainly before anything else is said about them, because everything else is reconstruction, is inference, is the careful assembly of a people from the evidence of their absence. They are gone. The hearths are cold. The walls are weeping. The moss is climbing. They are gone, and they have been gone for long enough that the Fold has begun to forget them, which is to say the Fold has begun to undo the marks they made, which is what the Fold will do to all marks eventually, because the Fold is a place of continuous dissolution and continuous creation and it does not distinguish between a tally mark and a bedding plane — both are features to be erased, given time.

But they were here.

They were here, and they were not like you. This is important to understand, and it is difficult to explain, because the temptation is to make them familiar — to give them our concerns, our interiority, our sense of time and purpose. They did not have these. Or they had versions of them so different from ours that the words would not translate. They were people — *homin* is the word the literature uses, though the literature is sparse and uncertain — but they were people the way a river is water: technically accurate, fundamentally misleading.

They were small. This is inferred from the door heights, which are low — a meter and a quarter at the highest, though the corbelling above means a person could stand upright once inside. The doors were low because the people were small, or because low doors retain heat, or because both. The largest structure's door has a lintel stone at one meter thirty, and the marks are carved at a height that suggests someone kneeling or crouching to make them, which suggests further that the marks were made at eye-level, which suggests an eye-level of roughly one meter when kneeling, which suggests a standing height of perhaps one meter forty to one meter fifty. Small. Child-sized by our measure, though they were not children. They were adults of a kind of people who did not grow large, because largeness was not useful where they lived, which was not only here.

They lived here, but not only here. The Fold was one place among many — a seasonal station, probably, occupied during a window when the resources it offered were worth the cold and the wet and the altitude. The window was narrow. The flies hatch in abundance for perhaps three months. The thrush — or whatever thrush-equivalent preceded the current one — would have been present during the same window. The spring provides water year-round, but water is not a sufficient reason to climb to 3,400 meters. They came for something the Fold offered in abundance during a specific period, and they left when that period ended, and they returned when it began again, and this rhythm — arrival, dwelling, departure, return — is recorded in the tally marks on the door jambs, which are not tallies of days but tallies of *presences*, each mark representing one season of occupation, each mark a statement: *we were here again*.

How many seasons? The marks are dissolving. The soft greywacke does not hold detail under constant moisture. The shallowest marks are already gone — you can feel them with your fingertips as faint smoothnesses, indentations too shallow to see. The deeper marks, those carved with more force or more recently, are still legible: vertical strokes, grouped in fives with a diagonal cross-stroke, a counting system so universal it feels less invented than discovered. Twelve groups are visible. Perhaps fifteen existed. Perhaps twenty. Perhaps more. The marks represent something between forty and a hundred years of seasonal occupation, which is three to seven generations, depending on how you count generations among people whose lifespans and reproductive patterns we do not know.

They were not many. Seven structures, but not all occupied simultaneously — the two that are only foundations were abandoned before completion, or dismantled, or never finished. Five structures, then, at peak. Five households. Fifteen, twenty, twenty-five people at most. A band. A family-group. Small enough to fit inside the Fold without crowding it, small enough that the architecture of distance — the twenty, thirty, forty meters between structures — was not a response to population pressure but a preference. A cultural choice. A desire to dwell apart, to have silence between habitations, to hear the Fold's roar from the privacy of one's own walls rather than from the proximity of a neighbor's.

Or they needed the space for activities that required it — drying hides, working stone, processing whatever they had come to process. The moss around the structures is compacted in patterns that suggest traffic — paths between doorways and the spring basin, between doorways and the rills, between doorways and the back wall where the ferns grow densest. The paths are overgrown now but their ghosts remain in the slight depressions, the slightly different moss species that colonize compacted ground versus uncompressed ground. The people moved through the Fold along established routes. They had a geography of daily life — here is where we drink, here is where we gather ferns, here is where we sit in the sun when the sun comes, which is not often.

They sat in the sun when it came. This is not inferred from evidence. This is simply what people do.

---

What did they look like?

The honest answer is: we do not know. No bones have been found. No depictions — no cave art, no petroglyphs, no figurines — survive in the Fold or in any nearby site that can be reliably associated with these people. The stone tools found in the structures — a few choppers, a scraper, a core from which flakes were struck — are crude and could belong to several different hominid lineages. The architecture — dry-stone, corbelled, small-roomed, single-hearth — is not unique to any single people. It is the architecture of small, cold-dwelling people everywhere, a convergent solution to a convergent problem: *how to be warm in a place that does not want you to be*.

But if we allow ourselves to speculate — and the Fold invites speculation, with its raw stone and its patient moss and its marks dissolving even as you read them — we might imagine them like this:

Low-bodied. Compact. The proportions of a person shaped by cold — short limbs, broad torso, the thermodynamic efficiency of a body that minimizes surface area relative to mass. Their hands would have been strong, given the stone work — the basin alone represents hours of patient chiseling with a harder stone as the tool, each strike precise enough to fracture the greywacke along its grain without shattering it. The hands would have been rough. The fingertips would have been worn smooth by the abrasive texture of new stone, which is sharper than weathered stone, which bites into skin in a way that older rock has forgotten how to do.

Their skin would have been dark. Not necessarily — but probably, if the evolutionary logic holds, if these were a people who had lived at altitude for long enough to adapt, which the tally marks suggest they had. Dark skin protects against UV, which is intense at 3,400 meters, even on overcast days, even through mist. The mist would have made them glisten. Their hair would have been dense, perhaps oily — a natural water repellent, the kind of hair that beads rain rather than absorbing it. They would have smelled of stone and moss and the particular mineral sharpness of the greywacke, which is the smell of depth, of the earth's interior brought to the surface and not yet accustomed to the air.

They would have been quiet. Not silent — they would have spoken, or signed, or produced some vocalization that served the function of language — but quiet, because the Fold demands it, because the roar is so constant and so complete that any sound you make is absorbed into it, and because the architecture of distance between structures suggests a people who did not need to shout to each other across the moss, who had arranged their lives so that shouting was unnecessary, who walked the paths between structures when they needed to communicate and spoke at a volume the Fold could accommodate.

Or they were loud. Perhaps the roar was their medium — perhaps they had learned to modulate their voices to cut through the water-sound, to use the Fold's acoustics the way a cathedral singer uses the nave's reverb, projecting into the stone and letting the amplification carry. Perhaps the tally marks were accompanied by songs, sung in the deep register that the Fold's bassline — the spring striking the boulder — would permit, a low chanting that wove into the roar and became indistinguishable from it, so that the Fold itself became their voice, their music, their continuous utterance.

We do not know. The marks dissolve. The moss grows. The evidence is being erased by the same forces that created the place the evidence describes.

---

What did they believe?

This is the wrong question, because it assumes a category — *belief* — that may not apply. But we can ask: what did they *attend to*? What did they notice? What did they arrange their lives around?

They attended to water. The basin is the evidence. It is the most careful thing in the Fold — more carefully made than the walls, more deliberately placed than the structures, more finely finished than any other worked stone. The basin is where the spring's water pools before it continues its journey across the Fold's floor, and it is shaped to hold exactly enough water for drinking — not a reservoir, not a cistern, but a cup. A single cup, carved into living stone, positioned so that the spring fills it continuously and the overflow continues down the channel. The water in the basin is always fresh, always cold, always clear. The people who made this basin were attending to the quality of the water, not just its presence. They could have drunk from the rills. They chose to drink from the basin. They chose to make the basin. They chose to invest hours of labor in a stone cup that would hold water marginally better than the ground would.

This is not belief. This is *care*. The same care that oriented the doors to catch morning light. The same care that placed the structures on rises to avoid the rills. The same care that spaced them at distances that feel — and the word is approximate, but it is the best we have — *considered*. As if the people who built here had walked the Fold's floor before building, had stood in various places and felt the shape of the space, and had chosen positions not by calculation but by a kind of bodily attention, an awareness of where the wind eddied and where the spray fell and where the morning light first struck the moss, and had placed their homes in the spots that felt right, the way a bird places its nest, the way the thrush places itself on the capstone — not by design but by an attunement so deep it precedes design.

They attended to the mountain's sounds. This is the most speculative claim, and it rests on the thinnest evidence — the fact that the structures are clustered toward the back wall, where the spring's bass note is loudest, where the roar is most concentrated, where the stone-settling sounds are most audible. They could have built toward the entrance of the Fold, where the wind is less intense and the sound is more diffuse. They chose the back wall. They chose the loudest part of the amphitheater. They chose to live inside the sound, the way you might choose to live inside a forest, or inside a river — surrounded, permeated, held.

Perhaps the sound was the point. Perhaps they came to the Fold not despite the roar but because of it. Perhaps the continuous sound — water, wind, stone — was something they needed, something their bodies required, the way some people require silence and other people require noise and the distinction between these needs is not as simple as preference. Perhaps the roar was a kind of shelter. A wall of sound inside which they could dwell, protected from whatever lay outside — other people, other predators, the silence of the lower country where the mountain's voice could not reach.

Or perhaps the back wall was simply the driest place, the warmest place, the place where the overhanging stone provided the most shelter, and the sound was incidental. Perhaps they did not notice the sound at all, the way you do not notice your own heartbeat, the way the thrush does not notice the roar. Perhaps the sound was simply the condition of their existence, unremarkable because constant, invisible because omnipresent.

---

They are gone. They have been gone for long enough that the Fold is forgetting them, and the forgetting is visible — the softening marks, the moss on the foundations, the ash dissolving to paste. They did not leave dramatically. There was no catastrophe, no final fire, no last stand against weather or enemies. They simply stopped coming. One season, they did not arrive. The next season, they did not arrive. The season after that. The structures stood empty through the window of occupation, and the flies hatched without being eaten by human hands, and the thrush sang without being heard by human ears, and the spring filled the basin without anyone drinking from it, and the basin overflowed, and the channel silted with moss, and the water found new paths across the floor, and the paths the people had worn filled with new growth, and the tally marks softened, and the Fold continued.

Why did they stop coming? The question is unanswerable and irresistible. Disease. Conflict. Climate shift — a slight cooling that made the window of occupation too narrow to justify the climb. Cultural change — they became people who no longer traveled to high places, who settled in valleys, who forgot the Fold the way the Fold is forgetting them. Extinction — the last of them died somewhere lower, and there was no one to come back. Any of these. None of these. We do not know.

What we know is this: they were here. They built. They drank from the basin. They carved marks into soft stone. They lived inside the roar. And then they did not, and the Fold went on without them, and it goes on without them now, and it will go on without them until the water breaches the back wall or the mountain erodes or the spring runs dry, and then the Fold will be something else, and whatever lives here then will be something else, and the people who built here will be less than a memory — they will be a faint smoothness in the stone, a slight depression in the moss, a ghost of a path that no one walks.

---

But the Fold is not empty. Say this too.

The moss lives here. Fifty species of moss, each one a distinct lineage, a distinct way of being green, a distinct relationship with water and stone and light. The moss lives here the way a city lives in a place — totally, structurally, in a way that makes the place unimaginable without it. The moss *is* the Fold, as much as the stone is. It is the Fold's skin, its first organic layer, its attempt to become something more than rock. The moss does not know this. It does not need to.

The ferns live here. They should not be here — not this tall, not this many, not at this elevation in this cold — and their presence is a kind of argument, a biological assertion that the rules are not as fixed as the rules claim. The ferns are pioneers of a future. They do not know this either. They are simply growing, the way the thrush is simply singing, the way the spring is simply flowing, the way the mountain is simply settling, click by scrape by fraction of a millimeter.

The thrush lives here, for now. It will leave at dusk and return at dawn and leave and return until it does not return, and another thrush will come, or will not, and the flies will swarm or will not, and the vole, if it exists, will follow its trail or will not, and the thing that left the scat will hunt or will not, and the Fold will hold all of this and not know it is holding anything, because the Fold is a bowl, and bowls do not know they are full.

---

What lives in the Fold of Arûn?

Everything that can. Everything that has found a way to persist in a place that is still being born, that is still deciding what it wants to be, that is wet and cold and loud and green and almost empty and slowly, slowly becoming. The moss. The ferns. The flies. The spiders. The thrush. The vole, maybe. The scat-leaver, whatever it is. The spring, if a spring can be said to live, which is a question for a different kind of philosophy. The mountain, if a mountain can be said to live, which is a question for a different kind of time.

And the people, once. And the marks they left, which are dissolving. And the walls they built, which are weeping. And the basin they carved, which is full of water that no one drinks. And the silence between their structures, which is not silence at all but the roar of a place that does not know it is empty, that does not know it was ever inhabited, that does not know anything, that is simply here, being what it is, becoming what it will become, holding what it holds, without opinion, without memory, without end.
