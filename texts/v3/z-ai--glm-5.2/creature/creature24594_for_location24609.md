# The Cistern of Hands

---

**The Pale Ones**

They are the oldest residents, or at least the ones with the deepest claim. The crustaceans — they have no name for themselves, and if they did, it would be a chemical name, a name made of calcium gradients and water temperature and the particular vibration pattern of a drop striking the surface at a given distance. They call each other by touch. By the specific frequency of their own clicking, which is as individuated as a voice, which is how they know each other in a space where sight was abandoned long ago as a pointless expense.

They are not large. The biggest among them would fit across a human palm, though you would not want to hold one — they are frantic in their motion, antennae whirling, legs scrabbling, and their claws, though small, are strong enough to score the ceramic glaze of the cistern, which is why the glaze is in the condition it is in. They have been etching their own signatures into the infrastructure for longer than the infrastructure was meant to last.

They are white. Not the white of albinism, not the white of a creature that has simply lost its pigment. They are the white of things that have never needed color — a translucent, faintly blue-white, through which you can see the dark thread of their digestive tracts when the light shafts find them. Their eggs are smaller than a grain of sand and they lay them in clusters of three to four hundred in the cracks of the cistern ceramic, where they sit in the warmth and the damp until the young emerge already clicking, already in motion, already participants in the roar.

They eat what falls to them. Insect debris from the bat colonies above. The shed exoskeletons of their own kind. The occasional amphibian egg, when one is laid in the shallow water and left unattended long enough. Organic particulate that washes in through the channels during rains. They are not choosy. They are not predatory in any way that requires planning. They are simply always eating, always moving, always producing the small percussive report of their claws against the cistern floor, and this sound — this constant, layered, arrhythmic ticking — is the bass note of the chamber, the foundation upon which every other sound is built.

There is a hierarchy among them, though it is not one a human observer would easily detect. The largest individuals occupy the center of the cistern, where the water is shallowest and warmest and where the most debris collects. The smaller ones ring the periphery, in the deeper channels, in the cracks, in the spaces between the fitted stone where the water goes when the basin overflows. They move inward as they grow. They move outward when they molt, when they are soft and vulnerable, when they retreat to the dark joints between stones to wait for their new armor to harden. The molting is the only time they are still. The molting is the only silence they know, and it is a silence that lasts hours, and in those hours the individual simply exists, a pale soft thing in a crack in the stone, listening to the roar continue without it.

They do not know they are blind. They do not know they are white. They do not know that they are descended from something that once lived in light, that once had pigment, that once had eyes that functioned. The adaptation is complete. They are what they are: a thousand small clicking bodies in warm shallow water, making the sound that makes the cistern the cistern.

**The Three Colonies**

The bats do not think of themselves as three species. They think of themselves as three communities, three dialects, three arguments happening simultaneously in a room too small for any of them to win.

The largest colony roosts in the upper alcoves on the east wall, where the light reaches at midday and where the stone is warmest. They are small — thumb-sized bodies, wingspans no wider than a spread hand — and they are the loudest. Their chittering is high, rapid, conversational, and it never stops. Even in the deepest part of the night, when the other two colonies are quiet, the east-wall bats are talking. They talk to their neighbors. They talk to their young. They talk to the bats who are returning from hunting sorties through the light shafts, demanding to know what they found, where they went, how the outside air tasted. They are, in their way, gossips. Their colony is the most socially complex, the most densely interconnected, and they maintain their bonds through constant vocal contact, a web of recognition and relationship sustained entirely by sound.

They eat insects. The light shafts that pierce the dome serve as their highways, and they cycle through them in quick bursts — out into the evening air, a circuit of the surrounding vegetation, back to the roost in under a minute. The chamber is their home, not their hunting ground. They return to it because it is warm, because it is safe, because the stone alcoves are the precise shape and temperature their bodies have evolved to occupy, and because they have always returned. The colony memory is long. No individual bat knows why they roost here, but the colony knows, the way a river knows its course: not through decision but through the accumulated weight of a thousand seasons of choosing the same thing.

The second colony is quieter, larger-bodied, and roosts in the mortared joints of the dome itself, in the gaps where the stalactites have not yet formed, in the spaces between the mineral deposits. They are darker — brown-black, with shorter ears and broader wings — and they hunt differently: slower, higher, taking moths and larger insects from the air above the tree line. Their vocalizations are lower in pitch, less frequent, more purposeful. A call from one of these bats is not chatter; it is a statement. A declaration of territory, a warning, a summons. They tolerate the east-wall colony because they must — the space is what it is — but they do not engage. They hang in their dome-joints with their wings folded tight and they watch, with the patient disinterest of things that have been here long enough to know that noise changes nothing.

The third group is the smallest and the strangest. They roost near the floor, in the lowest alcoves, in the spaces behind the clay vessels, in the shadows beneath the overhanging stone where the amphibians cannot reach. They are pale — not white like the crustaceans, but a diluted grey-brown, as though someone took a normal bat and washed it. They are the most recent arrivals, or at least the most recently adapted, and they have done something the other two colonies have not: they have incorporated the cistern into their hunting. They take the crustaceans.

Not from the water — they are not equipped for that. But when a crustacean ventures out of the basin, crawling across the damp floor toward a puddle or a channel, the pale bats drop on it. Silently. A quick descent, a scrabble of claws, a high squeak of success, and they are back in their low alcoves with their prey, crunching through the shell with small sharp teeth. They are the only bats that hunt inside the chamber. They are the only bats that have found a way to live off the cistern itself, rather than using it merely as a roost. In doing so, they have become something new — not quite a separate species, not yet, but something on its way to becoming one. A thing in process. A future taking shape in the dark.

**The Watchers**

The amphibians do not have a name for what they do. They do not think in terms of patience or strategy or ecosystem. They think in terms of: *movement, stillness, hunger, satiation, warmth, coolness, the vibration that means prey, the vibration that means something larger.*

They are toads. This is the simplest description and it is not wrong, though it is not complete. They are heavy-bodied, broad-skulled, with legs thick enough to carry them across the wet stone but not designed for distance. They do not hop so much as lumber, their bellies dragging across the damp floor, leaving a faint trail of moisture that dries within minutes. Their skin is the grey-brown of the stone they live on, mottled with darker patches that resemble, if you are inclined to see it, the patterns of the mortar joints in the walls. This is not camouflage in the way a chameleon changes color. It is the slow matching of a body to a place over generations, the way a river stone becomes smooth.

They sit in the shallow puddles. They sit at the edges of the cistern. They sit in the alcoves that are accessible from the floor, occupying spaces that were once used for storage, for tools, for human hands, and they wait. The waiting is not passive. Their eyes — golden, vertical-slit, lidless — track every movement in their field of vision with a focus that is almost painful to observe. They watch the crustaceans. They watch the pale bats when they descend. They watch the drops falling from the ceiling, not because the drops are interesting but because a drop striking the water might mask the sound of an approaching strike, and they need to know that, they need to account for it, because their hunting depends on a single explosive motion that must be precisely timed.

When they strike, it is fast. The body coils, the mouth opens, the tongue — thick, sticky, pink-grey — lashes out and returns with whatever it caught, and the mouth closes, and the body settles, and the eyes resume their tracking. The whole motion takes less than a second. The sound it makes — a wet, hollow snap — is one of the loudest single sounds in the chamber, loud enough to startle bats into flight, loud enough to send ripples across the cistern, loud enough that the crustaceans, for a brief moment, go silent.

That silence is the closest thing the chamber has to a pause. It lasts perhaps two seconds. Then the clicking resumes, the chittering settles, the drips continue, and the toad swallows and resumes its watch.

They lay their eggs in the deeper channels, the ones cut into the walls at floor level, where the water is still and warm and protected. The eggs are dark, gelatinous, strung in long strands that cling to the stone and absorb the warmth of the masonry. The tadpoles, when they hatch, are the only vertebrates that live in the channels, and they are hunted by nothing — the crustaceans cannot reach them, the bats cannot reach them, and the adult toads do not eat their own young, or at least not deliberately, though there are accidents in the dark. The tadpoles develop slowly in the warm still water, metamorphosing over weeks rather than days, and when they emerge as tiny toadlets — grey-brown already, already watching — they climb out of the channels and onto the floor and find puddles of their own and begin the long process of sitting and waiting and watching that will occupy the rest of their lives.

There are perhaps thirty of them. Perhaps forty. It is difficult to count because they do not move, because they look like stone, because the light shifts and what you thought was a toad is a wet rock and what you thought was a wet rock is a toad that has been watching you for the last twenty minutes with an expression that is not hostile but is not welcoming either. It is the expression of a thing that has assessed you and found you irrelevant to its concerns.

**The Smaller Lives**

There are things here that you would not notice unless you sat on the steps for a long time, which people have done, which is why the steps are worn.

Spiders. Three species, at minimum, occupying the upper walls where the insects fly when they enter through the light shafts. Their webs are strung between stalactites, across alcove openings, in the corners where the mortar has crumbled. They are not large. They are methodical. They rebuild their webs every morning, because the bats disrupt them every night, and this cycle of destruction and reconstruction is as regular as the tides, as unremarkable to the spiders as the rising of the sun.

The webs catch moths, flies, small beetles, and occasionally a bat that has misjudged a flight path and tangled itself. The bat will struggle and chitter and eventually free itself, leaving a torn web and a frustrated spider, but the spider will not be frustrated for long because it does not have the cognition for frustration. It will rebuild. It always rebuilds.

There are mites on the bats. There are nematodes in the water. There is a species of silverfish — pale, swift, almost translucent — that lives in the alcoves among the clay vessels and feeds on the organic residue of centuries: the fiber of the rotted nets, the leather of a strap that once held a tool, the paper of a document that was placed here for safekeeping and has become, over the centuries, food. The silverfish do not know they are eating history. They know only that the cellulose is sweet and that the dark alcoves are safe and that the stone is warm and that this is enough.

The moss on the upper walls is alive in a way that moss is always alive — slowly, patiently, in a timescale that makes the toads look frantic. It grows a millimeter a year. It has been growing since the mortar first softened, since the first spore found the first crack and sent its first protonema into the calcium-rich substrate and began the process of building something green in a place that was not designed for green. The moss does not need much. It needs dampness, which it has. It needs light, which it has in measured doses. It needs time, which it has more of than anything else in the chamber.

The lichen is older. The lichen with the curled edges and the black fruiting bodies — it is not one organism but two, a fungus and an alga in a partnership so old that neither can remember being separate, if remembering were something they did. The lichen has colonized the ceiling in patches that have been there, growing, dying back, regrowing, for longer than the bats have been roosting, longer than the toads have been watching, longer perhaps than the steps have been worn. It is the closest thing the chamber has to a historian. It does not record. It simply persists.

**The Hands**

The builders are gone. Their bones are not here — they are elsewhere, in a place that was designed for bones, or in no place at all, consumed by the processes that consume all biological matter when it is left to them. What the builders left is this: the chamber, the channels, the shafts, the cistern, the alcoves, the steps. The tools in the alcoves, placed with the care of things that would be picked up again and then not picked up again. The iron hooks. The ceramic glaze. The fitted stone.

They left the hands. Not literal hands — the cistern is not full of severed hands, despite the name, though the name is old and its origin is uncertain and it may refer to the handholds dimpled in the walls, or to the shape of the stalactites, or to the simple fact that everything in this place was made by hands, shaped by hands, placed by hands, and those hands are gone and what they made remains. The cistern is full of the evidence of hands. Every surface bears the mark of intention. Every channel was planned. Every shaft was cut with purpose. The dome was laid by hands that knew how to lay domes, that had done this before, that understood the angle of repose and the properties of mortar and the way stone settles under its own weight over time.

Whoever they were, they understood warmth. They understood air. They understood water — not just the flow of it but the behavior of it, the way it absorbs heat and releases it, the way it moves through stone by capillary action, the way it sustains life in small closed systems. They built a place that would be warm without fire, that would be lit without lamps, that would hold water without a spring, that would sustain life without maintenance. They built a machine, and the machine works, and the machine does not need them.

This was perhaps the point. Or perhaps the point was something else — a water supply, a ritual space, a refuge, a larder, a bath — and the ecosystem is an accident, a beautiful accident, a colonization by living things of a space that was built for a different purpose and has been repurposed by the slow democracy of adaptation. The builders may have intended the crustaceans. The builders may have introduced them as a food source, or as cleaners, or as part of a system they understood and we do not. Or the crustaceans may have found their own way in through the channels, a thousand generations ago, and stayed because the water was warm and the dark was safe and there was no reason to leave.

The builders are not here. But their hands are everywhere — in the worn steps, in the fitted stone, in the channels that still carry water exactly where it was meant to go, in the alcoves that still hold what was placed in them, in the shafts that still deliver light to the same surfaces at the same hours. The chamber is a monument to hands. It does not know this. The bats do not care. The toads do not care. The crustaceans do not care. The chamber is full of life that has no use for monuments, that has no use for memory, that lives in the present tense of warm water and falling insects and the next meal and the next molt and the next egg and the next generation of pale clicking bodies in the dark.

---

**What Lives Here**

What lives here is a community of the unbothered. A society of the practical. The chamber is not a ruin — it is a home, and it has been a home continuously, without interruption, since the last human hands placed the last tool in the last alcove and climbed the worn steps and did not come back. Since then, the residents have been managing. They have been eating and being eaten, breeding and dying, molting and emerging, clicking and chittering and snapping and dripping and growing, and the chamber has been breathing for them, and the light has been falling for them, and the water has been flowing for them, and none of them have needed to ask anything of anyone.

They do not know they are the inheritors of something. They do not know they are living in a machine built by hands that are gone. They know only the warmth of the stone, the taste of the water, the sound of the chamber breathing around them, the constant multipart roar that is not noise but is home, is the sound of home, is the sound of a place that is full of life doing what life does in a place that was made, by accident or by design, to hold it.
