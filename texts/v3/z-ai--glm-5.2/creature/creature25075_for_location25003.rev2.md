# What Lives at Veyl

---

The people first, because they are the easiest to see and the least interesting to think about, though this is not a judgment of them. There are perhaps thirty. They are maintenance staff. They were not always only maintenance staff — there are traces of more, a second-story window in the operator's residence with a window box whose brackets have rusted through, a faded painted border on the control room wall where a calendar or a map once hung, the particular way the path between the pump house and the residence has been worn by feet that were not always hurrying — but now they are maintenance staff. They keep the engines running. They clear the leaf litter from the iron walkways. They note the level of the sump and the state of the pipes and the fourth engine's cycling frequency in a logbook that is kept in a waterproof bag hung on a nail inside the pump house door, and the entries are brief and functional and do not include observations about the white substrate because no one has asked them to observe the white substrate in over a decade and the habit of not observing has become, itself, a kind of training.

They are not unfriendly. They will talk to you. They will tell you which engine tends to run hot in the afternoon, which section of the retaining wall has developed a new seep, which of the ficus roots has begun to cross the main path and will need to be trimmed back before it lifts the brickwork. They will not tell you about the white thing in the sump. Not because they are forbidden — the instructions say only *do not disturb* — but because they do not have a way of talking about it. It is not a secret. A secret is a thing you hold. This is a thing they live next to. You live next to the sound of the engines too, and you do not describe the sound of the engines to people. It is simply the condition of being here.

They sleep in the humid dark with the drone in their teeth. They eat in the operator's residence, which has a kitchen whose window looks out on the nearest ficus trunk, so close that the leaves press against the glass in the early morning when the condensation is heaviest and the thermal updrafts have not yet started. They cook with gas brought in from outside. They do not grow food here. Nothing edible grows here. The plants are lush and relentless and none of them are for eating. The soil is rich and wet and would grow anything you planted, but no one plants, and has not planted for as long as anyone can remember, and the reason for this is not stated but is, like the substrate, a thing that is understood without being discussed: this is not a place that feeds you. It is a place that runs.

There is a woman named Tahl who has been here longest. Twenty-three years. She is not the oldest, but she has been here longest. She can tell the state of the fourth engine by the vibration in the floor of the residence, without going outside, without looking. She says the sound changed once, years ago, when the third engine threw a bearing, and she woke from a dead sleep knowing something was wrong before the alarm light on the control panel had finished cycling on. She says the sound is not something you hear. She says it is something you are. She does not say this poetically. She says it the way you would say *the floor is solid* or *water is wet*. A material fact.

There are younger people. They come for seasons, or for a year or two. They are paid. The work is simple and repetitive and it is not unpleasant if you can tolerate the damp. Some of them develop a rash on their forearms and shins from the condensation that forms on the skin and stays there, and the rash goes away when they leave and does not come back. Some of them do not develop the rash. There is no apparent pattern. Tahl has never had the rash. She does not mention it. When the younger ones mention it she looks at the rash the way you would look at a crack in a wall — noting it, not alarmed, not surprised — and does not say anything, and the younger ones learn from this that the rash is not a topic, or not a problem, or the same thing as a topic that is not a problem, which is most topics here.

They do not go near the sump except along the iron walkway, and they do not go to the sump at night. This is not a rule. There is no rule. No one has told them not to go to the sump at night. They simply do not. The sump walkway is lit by a single lamp at the north end, a caged bulb on a post, and at night the light falls on the first three meters of the walkway and stops, and beyond that is the dark, and in the dark the white mat is, and they do not go there.

---

The trees. The *Ficus veylensis*. They are not what you would call inhabitants; they are the habitat, the architecture, the frame on which the place is hung. But they are alive, and they are here, and they are the largest living things in the basin, and if the question is *what lives here* then the trees are the first true answer.

They are not like other ficus. Or they are, in the way that a person who has lived in one room for their entire life is like other people: structurally the same, behaviorally specific. The *Ficus veylensis* has adapted to the permanent saturation, to the low light, to the constant heat. Their leaves are broader and darker than their relatives' — the waxy cuticle thicker, the drip tips longer and more pronounced, the whole leaf shaped like a long teardrop that funnels water with an efficiency that seems engineered. Their root systems are shallow and vast. A single tree on the west edge of the basin has roots that extend across thirty meters of ground, the main cables as thick as a human thigh, branching into finer and finer tendrils until at the margins they are the thickness of wire and disappear into the moss-carpet and the dark earth. They do not grow down. There is no reason to grow down. The water is here, on the surface, in the air, everywhere. The roots grow out. They grow around. They map the ground in a slow web that is always shifting, always extending, always in the process of becoming something that, from above, would look like a circuit board or a nervous system or a river delta seen from very high up.

The trees are old. No one knows how old. They were here before Veyl was built — before the pumps, before the sump, before the drainage channels were dug. They were here when the river still ran through the depression, or near enough through it that the basin was a floodplain, and they were fig trees then, ordinary, growing in the wet. The basin formed around them, or the basin was always there and the trees found it, and the two — the geography and the biology — entered into the long negotiation that has produced what you see now. The trees are not passive in this. They shape the basin as much as the basin shapes them. Their canopy holds the humidity in. Their roots hold the soil. They are the reason the air is breathable, the reason the heat is not simply heat but is instead a warm wet envelope that plants and humans can survive in. They are the lungs of the place, except they are not lungs because they do not breathe in and out; they breathe in and in and in, drawing moisture from the air, condensing it, sending it down their trunks to their roots, and the roots release it to the soil, and the soil releases it to the sump, and the sump sends it to the pumps, and the pumps send it out of the basin, and the cycle is not a cycle but a directed flow, a one-way movement of water from sky to root to ground to sump to pump to channel to phantom lake, always outward, always east, following the ghost of the river.

There are fourteen trees. Fourteen that are large enough to be called trees. There are smaller ones, saplings and volunteers, but the fourteen are the ones that form the canopy, and they are the ones that matter. Each one has been given a designation by the maintenance staff — not a name, a designation, a letter and a number, A-1 through D-4, based on which quadrant of the basin they occupy. Tahl can tell you which tree's roots have crossed the path most recently. She can tell you which tree dropped a branch last winter, a branch as thick as a man's torso that fell across the north end of the sump and lay there for three days before the staff cut it up and dragged it to the south wall, where it is now a long dark shape covered in the same moss-imitating vascular plant that covers everything horizontal and wet. The branch is still there. It is rotting. It will take years to rot completely. No one is in a hurry about it.

---

The plants between the trees. The ferns, the moss-that-is-not-moss, the climbing vines on the south wall. They live here in the ordinary sense. They grow and they hold and they spread and they compete in their slow way for the flat wet surfaces and the thin films of soil that collect in the joints of the brickwork. They are not remarkable except in their specificity: each one where it can thrive, each gap between plants a site where, for now, nothing has succeeded in establishing. This is the way of plants. They are the second true answer to the question of what lives here, and they are the answer you would expect, and they do not need much description because they are doing what plants do, which is to fill every available surface with green and to do it without hurrying and without stopping.

But there is a thing about the ferns. The stand of ferns against the east wall of the pump house — the tallest ones, waist-high, their fronds beaded with condensation — they are the oldest colony in the basin. They were here before the pump house was built. Tahl says they were here before the sump was poured. She says someone, sixty or seventy years ago, decided to build the pump house on the one spot in the basin where ferns were already growing, and she does not know if this was deliberate or accidental, and she does not think it matters. The ferns grow against the pump house now as if the pump house grew against them. The relationship is that old.

---

The white thing.

You look down into the sump from the iron walkway and you see it on the bottom, and you know it is alive in the same way you know the engines are running — not because you can see it moving, but because it is doing something. It is there. It is growing. It has a pattern. The pattern changes over time, though not in any way you could see in a single visit. The maintenance staff say the substrate has not changed. The instructions say to note any changes. The substrate has changed — of course it has changed; everything alive changes — but the changes are so slow and so consistent with the overall form that they do not register as changes. They register as the thing being itself, more fully, the way a person's face changes over decades and remains recognizable.

It is white. Milk-white, bone-white, the white of chalk dissolved in water until it is not solid but not quite liquid either. It grows in radial patterns, branching fractal filaments that cover the concrete floor of the sump in a mat that is, from the walkway, the shape of a river delta or a lung or a tree — a branching thing, a thing that repeats its form at every scale, large branches dividing into small branches dividing into fine branches dividing into threads that are as thin as the ficus roots at their margins and that may, in fact, be thinner than that. You cannot see the finest detail from the walkway. The water is clear but dark-tea-colored, and the depth is three meters, and the light is low, and the white mat is at the bottom of all of it, and what you see is an impression of white, a pattern of white, a white that is doing something.

It pulses.

This is the thing that no one explains and that everyone who has stood on the walkway has seen. When the pumps cycle — when the fourth engine kicks in and the water level in the sump shifts, rising half a centimeter and then falling — the mat pulses. It expands. It contracts. The rhythm is slow, slower than breathing, slower than a heartbeat. It is the rhythm of something that does not have a heart. It is the rhythm of something that is responding to pressure, to the weight of water above it, in a way that suggests a membrane — a single membrane, a single surface, not a colony of individuals but one thing, one continuous sheet of white that covers the entire floor of the sump and breathes when the pressure changes.

The water that flows over it is clearer than the water that flows in. The maintenance staff know this. Tahl knows this. She has seen the inflow and the outflow and she knows that the substrate is doing something to the water, taking something out of it, filtering it, processing it in some way that no one has investigated because the instructions say *do not disturb the substrate* and because the water is clearer on the way out and there is no problem to solve. The substrate solves it. Whatever *it* is.

What lives in the sump is the white thing. It is alive. It is alive in a way that does not require eyes or legs or wings, in a way that does not require a mouth or a gut or a nervous system, in a way that does not require any of the apparatus that the rest of the living world uses to maintain itself. It lives at the bottom of a concrete cistern in a humid basin in a place called Veyl, and it grows in fractal patterns, and it breathes when the pumps cycle, and it cleans the water, and no one knows what it is.

It is not a bacterium. It is not a biofilm. It is not a slime mold, though it resembles one the way a whale resembles a fish — superficially, from a distance, by convergence. It has been looked at, or samples have been taken, or the instructions to leave it alone were preceded by a period of investigation — the details are not clear, and the records are in the wet-and-dried folder in the control room, and the folder is there and the instructions are in it and the rest of the folder's contents are illegible, the ink bled and rebled until the pages are a palimpsest of brown stain. Whatever was learned has been lost to the wet, which is the way of this place. The wet takes everything, eventually, and what survives is what can be remembered by the people who stay.

Tahl has been here twenty-three years. The substrate was here before her. It will be here after her. She does not think about it. She walks the iron walkway twice a day, morning and evening, and she looks down at it the way you would look at a clock — checking, confirming, noting that it is still doing what it does — and she moves on. She does not go to the sump at night.

---

And then there is the place itself, which lives.

This is not a metaphor. Or it is, but it is also not. The basin is a system. The engines are the heart, or the kidneys, or whichever organ you prefer for a thing that pumps and moves and cycles. The trees are the lungs, or the skin, the membrane that holds the inside in and mediates the exchange with the outside. The water is the blood, or the lymph, the fluid that moves through everything and carries and deposits and carries again. The plants are the flora, in the biological sense — the bacteria that live in the gut, the moss on the skin, the things that live in and on the larger body and are part of it without being identical to it. The white substrate is — the white substrate is the thing you cannot name, the organ whose function is not clear, the part of the body that the anatomy textbook describes as *function unknown* or *may play a role in* and moves on from.

The place lives the way a coral reef lives, the way a hot spring ecosystem lives — not as a single organism but as a process, a sustained activity, a thing that is happening rather than a thing that is. It is happening now. The engines are running. The water is moving. The trees are drinking the air. The ferns are unrolling. The white mat is breathing at the bottom of the sump. The thirty people are inside it, in the kitchen, in the pump house, on the paths, and they are part of the process too — the maintenance, the small corrections, the logging of levels and the clearing of walkways — the way that certain fish are part of a reef, not living on it but living in it, their presence a function of its presence, their absence unthinkable from the inside though from the outside it would be trivial to imagine the reef without them.

What lives here is Veyl. Veyl lives. It is a low, wet, humming, green-and-white thing that lives in a depression in the earth and manages water and does not care about you and will be here, breathing, when you are not.
