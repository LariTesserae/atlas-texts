# Inhabitants

---

There is no single answer to the question of what lives here, because here, living is not a thing that singular things do. But there are forms, and the forms have habits, and the habits have shapes, and if you are patient — and you must be patient, because patience is the metabolic currency of this entire realm — the shapes will show themselves.

---

## The Flat-Bellies

You see them first as texture. The surface of a shelf-pool, which you took for still water, resolves under continued attention into a mosaic of small bodies — each one the size and thickness of a human palm, though the analogy is misleading because they are not symmetrical in any way you are accustomed to. Their bodies are broad, flat ovals, wet-skinned, colored exactly like the water they float on, which is to say the color of light that has passed through ten meters of green membrane. They have no heads. They have no faces. What they have is a margin — a thin rim of tissue that rings the oval body and moves in continuous undulation, a motion so subtle it looks like the surface tension of water disturbed by air. This is how they move: by wearing the water. The rim grips the surface film and pulls them forward in increments too small to track, so that a Flat-Belly crossing a pool the width of your hand appears not to move at all and then, ten minutes later, is on the other side.

They breathe through the skin. Their entire dorsal surface is a lung. If the air dries — which it rarely does, but sometimes a pocket of lower humidity moves through like a warm wind — they submerge, and you see their undersides for the first time: pale, translucent, crossed with a network of dark lines that might be blood vessels or might be nerve trunks or might be both, because in this place the distinction between sensing and circulating has not been drawn with any firmness. The undersides are also covered in small cilia, fine and dense, that beat in coordination and allow the creature to swim with a lazy, sculling motion when it must. They prefer not to. They prefer to float, to breathe, to watch.

They watch. The rim is not only a locomotory organ. Along the inner edge of the margin, spaced at irregular intervals, are structures that are not eyes exactly but are light-sensitive patches so numerous and so widely distributed that the creature possesses what amounts to panoramic vision — not sharp, not resolving detail, but exquisitely tuned to movement, to change, to the displacement of a shadow that does not exist here because there are no shadows. What they see, then, is not shape but *shift*. They detect the difference between a still column and a column with something leaning against it. They detect the compression of the ground-mat under a footstep, transmitted through the water as a pressure wave. They know you are there before you arrive at the pool's edge. They have known since you entered the canopy's breathing range, which is to say they have known for a long time.

They eat what falls into the pool. Pollen-analog, insect-equivalents too small to see, fragments of curtain-tissue that detach and drift down. They are filters. They are also, when the opportunity presents, predators — a Flat-Belly will, if a climber descends to drink and misjudges its footing, roll its margin over the creature with a motion so slow it seems preordained, enfolding the prey in its dorsal surface, which secretes something that is not quite a digestive enzyme and not quite a sedative but functions as both. The prey stops struggling. The Flat-Belly does not move differently. It floats, and it digests, and the pool goes on being a pool.

There are thousands of them. Every shelf-pool holds dozens. Every still surface in the undercanopy is, on closer inspection, a parliament of Flat-Bellies, waiting, floating, breathing through their backs, watching with their edges. They are the most abundant large organism in this place, and they are invisible unless you stop moving for long enough that they forget you are novel, at which point they resume whatever it is they do when they are not being watched, which is: nothing different. They do the same thing watched or unwatched. They float. This is either a sign of profound stupidity or profound wisdom, and the undercanopy does not distinguish between the two.

## The Climbers

You notice them as motion in the curtains — a shiver, a displacement, a section of lichen-sheet that hangs differently than it did a moment ago. The climbers live in the hanging growths. They are the reason the curtains drip more than physics alone would explain: the climbers move through the lichen matrices, displacing the water held in their interstices, sending small cascades down the sheet's length in sequences that, if you watched long enough, would map the creature's path the way a seismograph maps a tremor.

They are small. The largest is the length of a human forearm but half the width, flattened dorsoventrally, so that it can slide between layers of curtain-tissue the way a hand slides between sheets of paper. Their bodies are segmented — not in the clean arthropod manner but in a softer, more ambiguous fashion, each segment blending into the next, the boundaries marked only by slight changes in coloration and by the placement of legs. They have many legs. Eight, ten, twelve — the count varies between individuals, and you begin to suspect that the number is not fixed, that these creatures grow legs as they grow segments, that their body plan is modular and open-ended, a system that adds components as needed rather than determining them in advance.

The legs are delicate, jointed in ways that do not correspond to any limb you are familiar with — each one bends in three places, none of which is obviously an elbow or knee, and the feet are not feet but small adhesive pads, wet, slightly tacky, capable of gripping the smoothest surface. The columns. The shelves. The root-vines. The surface of a pool, briefly, if the stride is fast enough. The climbers can go anywhere in the undercanopy. They are the generalists, the commuters, the creatures that move between zones and niches with an indifference to substrate that amounts to a kind of freedom.

Their eyes are the thing that unsettles. Along the flattened dorsal ridge, two rows of dark, wet,凸lusterless spots run the length of the body — not paired, not symmetrical, but staggered, offset, so that the field of view from each eye overlaps with its neighbors in a way that produces not binocular depth perception but something else, something you do not have a word for. A *strip* of vision. A continuous image, unbroken, that wraps around the body's cross-section and provides the creature with a 360-degree awareness so seamless that it does not, perhaps, experience the world as having a front and a back. It experiences the world as a sphere of sensation with itself at the center, and everything that enters that sphere is noted, classified, and responded to with a deliberation that matches the undercanopy's pace.

They descend to the shelf-pools to drink. They are cautious about this. They have reason to be. They approach the pool's edge, extend the foremost segment of their body over the water, and lower a mouth — a mouth you still have not seen clearly, a mouth that is perhaps not a localized structure but a function of the body's anterior, a softening and opening of the ventral surface through which water is drawn — and they drink for thirty seconds, or a minute, and then they withdraw, segment by segment, back into the curtain. If the water moves wrong, they withdraw faster, the legs blurring for the only time you will see them move at speed, and they do not come back for hours.

They eat the lichen-curtain itself. They eat the algae-films on the columns. They eat each other, occasionally, when a smaller climber is caught in a section of curtain that a larger one is consuming, and the larger one does not distinguish between curtain and curtain-dweller, because the curtain-dweller is, from a nutritional standpoint, simply curtain that moves. This is not predation. It is imprecision. The undercanopy does not waste energy on precision.

## The Coil

You find it because you stop to rest against a root-vine and the root-vine is warm and you lean into it and it moves.

Not the vine. Something on the vine. Something wrapped around it in a spiral so tight and so still that it had become, to your eye, simply a thickening in the vine's contour, a ridge, a feature. The movement is minimal — a tightening, a contraction, the way a fist closes slowly — and then it is still again, and you step back, and you wait, and over the next ten minutes the thing unwinds.

It is limbless. It is long — longer than you expected, three meters, maybe four, a body of consistent diameter, roughly the thickness of a human thigh, tapering at both ends to points so gradual that you cannot identify which end is which. It is not a snake. It is not a worm. It is not anything you have a category for, because its body has no external features: no scales, no segments, no eyes that you can find, no mouth that you can see. Its surface is smooth, wet, the same grey-green as the root-vine it was wrapped around, and the skin has a quality you can only describe as *matte* — it absorbs light rather than reflecting it, so that the creature seems slightly darker than its surroundings, a shadow without a source.

It unwinds and it does not leave the vine. It moves along the vine's length by a motion that is peristaltic — a wave of contraction that travels from one end to the other, pushing the body forward in a slow, continuous glide, the vine passing beneath it as if through a soft tunnel. It is headed upward, toward the canopy, and you realize that this is what it does: it climbs. It wraps around root-vines and column-structures and it climbs, slowly, over hours or days, from the ground level toward the light, and when it reaches the canopy — or near enough — it feeds. On what, you cannot determine. Perhaps the photosynthetic tissue itself. Perhaps the condensed moisture and the dissolved nutrients it carries. Perhaps something else, something that exists only in the upper layers, a resource you have no way of knowing about from down here.

It does not react to you. You stand close enough to touch it, and it continues its climb, and its body passes within a meter of your face, and it does not pause or alter course. You are not a predator. You are not prey. You are not a feature of its environment that it has evolved to notice. You are, to the Coil, a temporary obstacle in a landscape of permanent obstacles, and it will go around you or over you or through you if necessary, with the same patient, mindless determination with which it goes around and over and through everything else.

There is something about the Coil that disturbs you in a way the other creatures do not, and it takes time to identify what it is. It is not the limblessness. It is not the size. It is the *smoothness*. The other creatures have features — eyes, legs, mouths, margins. The Coil has nothing. It is a body and nothing else. It is the undercanopy's vision of what life becomes when it has been simplified by success: a tube that moves and eats and grows, and that has done so for so long that it has lost everything it no longer needs. It is not primitive. It is *finished*. It is the end-state of a lineage that found its optimal form early and then stopped changing, because the environment never demanded anything more. You watch it climb until it is out of sight, a dark line ascending into the green, and you feel as though you have watched something complete itself.

## The Groaner

You hear it before you see it, and you hear it through your feet. A low vibration in the root-mat, a tonal pulse that travels through the fiber-and-water substrate the way sound travels through a drumhead — not as a noise in the air but as a pressure in the ground, felt in the bones of the ankle, the calf, the knee. It comes from a direction you cannot pinpoint, because the root-mat transmits omnidirectionally, and by the time you have oriented yourself toward what you think is the source, the groaning has stopped, and you are standing in silence, and the silence is so complete that you doubt the sound happened.

It happened. You find evidence: a section of ground-mat that is compressed differently than the surrounding surface, a broad depression, as if something very heavy had rested here for a long time and then moved on. The depression is already rebounding — the fiber-mat lifting, the water returning — and in another hour it will be gone. But for now, you can read it. The impression is roughly circular. It is four meters across. The depth at its center is perhaps thirty centimeters. Something rested here that weighs as much as a large animal — a horse, a cow, something in that range — and it was here recently, within the last few hours, and it moved in a direction you can determine by the slight elongation of the depression's leading edge, the smear where the weight shifted forward before lifting.

You follow the smear. You follow it for a long time, because the Groaner is slow and you are slow too, now, the undercanopy having calibrated your pace to its own. The trail is not a trail in the sense of broken branches or displaced earth. It is a series of depressions, each one slightly different in size and depth, spaced at intervals of roughly ten meters, each one already healing, already being reclaimed by the mat's resilience. You are following a creature through the traces of its weight, and the traces are temporary, and you understand that the Groaner could be ten meters ahead of you or a hundred and you would not know the difference from the trail alone.

You find it because it stops.

It is resting against a column — or rather, it is resting *on* a column, having leaned its mass against the fluted surface with enough force to deform the column's outer tissue, creating a shallow bowl in the trunk's side that matches the creature's contour. The column has accommodated it. The tissue has grown to fit, the down has thickened in the areas of contact, and you realize that this is a habitual resting spot, used so often and so consistently that the plant has adapted to the animal's presence, incorporating the relationship into its own growth pattern. They are not symbionts. They are not partners. They are simply two organisms that have been leaning on each other for so long that they have shaped each other's form.

The Groaner is massive. It is the size of a small rhinoceros, but low-slung, broad, its body a flat heavy loaf that rests close to the ground on four legs so thick and so short they appear to be stumps of muscle rather than proper limbs. Its skin — or its surface, because you are not certain it is skin in the way you understand skin — is covered in the same absorbent down that covers the columns, and growing in that down are colonies of the lichen-analog that forms the curtains, and in those colonies live small colonies of the climbers, so that the Groaner is not one animal but a community, a mobile ecosystem, a walking shelf-pool, a living substrate. It is a place. It is a creature that has become a place.

Its head, if it is a head, is the lowest part of its body — a broad, blunt wedge that rests on the ground-mat when the creature is stationary, pressing into the fiber, pushing water out in a small ring of moisture around its face. There is a mouth. There are openings that might be nostrils. There are no eyes. The Groaner is blind. It navigates by pressure and vibration and chemical sense, reading the root-mat the way you read a map, feeling the density of the ground ahead, the water content, the proximity of columns, the presence of other organisms through the vibrations they transmit. It groans because the groan is a probe — a low-frequency pulse that travels through the substrate and returns as reflected vibration, giving the creature a sense of the space around it, a sonar of the ground. It is mapping the undercanopy with its body. It has been mapping it for years, for decades, for a lifespan you cannot estimate because you have no basis for comparison.

It eats the ground. This is the part that takes longest to understand. Its mouth is pressed against the fiber-mat, and it is feeding — slowly, continuously, ingesting the decomposed plant material that constitutes the floor, processing it through a gut that is extraordinarily long and extraordinarily efficient, extracting nutrients and moisture and microbial life, and depositing the remainder behind it as a compacted waste that is denser and firmer than the surrounding mat and that becomes, over time, a path — a slightly raised, slightly more solid ribbon of ground that other creatures prefer to walk on because it does not give under their weight the way the uncompressed mat does. The Groaner is a road-builder. It does not intend to be. Its waste is simply more convenient to walk on than its food, and so the undercanopy's few thoroughfares are the work of this animal's digestion, laid down over years of slow, circular, purposeless wandering.

It knows you are there. You can tell because it shifts — a minute adjustment of weight, a tightening of the muscles along its flank, the sort of preparatory motion that precedes either flight or aggression. But it does neither. It relaxes. It resumes feeding. You are not a threat. You are not interesting. You are a vibration in the ground, a pressure signature it has not encountered before, and it has filed this information in whatever passes for its memory and returned to the business of eating the floor. In a few hours it will groan again, and the groan will carry your footprint-vibration as part of its map, a small anomaly in a landscape it has been reading for longer than you have been alive, and it will move on, and the ground will rebound behind it, and the path it leaves will slowly be overgrown and softened and absorbed back into the mat, and in a year there will be no evidence that either of you was here.

## The Markers

They are not here.

That is the first thing to understand, and the most important. Whatever made the carvings on the columns — the diamond inside the diamond inside the circle, the overgrown spiral — is gone. Not recently. Not in any timeframe that a human mind comfortably holds. The carvings are old. The tissue of the columns has grown around them, distorted them, incorporated them. The markers lived here, or passed through here, in an era when the undercanopy was already ancient, and they left their marks, and the marks are disappearing, and the markers are gone.

But they were here, and they were *something*, and the question of what they were is the question that the undercanopy poses most insistently to anyone who walks through it with the capacity to ask.

They were bipedal. This much is inferable from the height of the carvings — all of them are between 1.2 and 1.8 meters from the ground, a range that corresponds to the reach of an upright creature of roughly human proportions. They had hands, or appendages capable of fine manipulation, because the grooves are too precise, too varied in depth and width, to have been made by anything that was not gripping a tool. The tools are gone. Whatever they were — stone, bone, thorn, sharpened shell-analog — they were not made of the undercanopy's permanent materials, and they have been absorbed back into the biomass.

They could see. The carvings are placed on columns that are visible from a distance, in the undercanopy's limited sense of distance — they are on the sides facing the more open spaces between column-clusters, where the light is marginally brighter and the sight-lines marginally longer. The markers chose their surfaces. They were not carving blindly. They looked at the landscape and selected their canvas.

They thought in symbols. The patterns are not random. They are not decorative, or if they are decorative they are also something else, because decoration does not require the consistency of depth and spacing that these carvings exhibit. Each mark is a deliberate arrangement of lines in a specific configuration, and the configurations differ from column to column, which means the marks are not stamps, not reflex actions, not the output of a single behavioral subroutine. They are *generated*. Each one was composed. Each one represents a choice.

And they are alone. There is nothing else. No shelters, no tools, no remains, no middens, no paths, no evidence of sustained occupation. The markers passed through the undercanopy the way light passes through water — present, detectable, leaving a faint signature, but not staying. They did not live here. They visited. They walked through this vast warm dim wet body of a place, and they looked at it, and they touched it, and they carved into its bones, and they left.

What were they? Not human. The carvings predate any plausible human presence by orders of magnitude. The tissue growth that has distorted the spiral — you can estimate the rate, given the known growth speed of the column-tissue, and the estimate places the carving at a minimum of several hundred thousand years old. The diamond-circle mark, less distorted, is younger, but still ancient beyond any human timeline. Whatever made these marks was here before your species existed, and it was making symbols, and it was choosing surfaces, and it was thinking about what it saw.

The markers were not of the undercanopy. Everything that lives here is born from the undercanopy, shaped by it, colored by it, paced by it. The markers were not colored like the plants. They were not slow. They did not blend in. They were the sort of creature that stands out — that arrives from elsewhere, that carries its own rhythms and its own colors and its own pace, that walks through a landscape and does not become the landscape but marks it instead, because marking is what you do when you are a creature that moves through places without becoming them. You mark. You say *I was here and I was not from here and I saw what this place looks like and I had a thought about it and the thought took this shape*.

They are gone. Whatever lineage produced them, whatever world they came from above the canopy or beyond the plateau, it has ended, or changed, or moved on, and the markers are as finished as the Coil is finished, as finished as the undercanopy itself is finished — not incomplete, not failed, just *done*. What remains is a few grooves in a few columns, softening, greening, filling with water, being grown around. In another hundred thousand years they will be gone. The columns will have healed. The marks will have been absorbed. And the undercanopy will continue its ancient business of being alive, warm, wet, dim, patient, and utterly uninterested in the fact that something once walked through it and thought about what it saw and left a record that no one would read for longer than the record was designed to last.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here. The Flat-Bellies and the Climbers and the Coil and the Groaner and the lichen-curtains and the algae-films and the columns themselves and the root-vines and the ground-mat and the water that runs through it all — they are not separate organisms in the way that you are separate from the chair you sit on. They are zones of intensity in a continuous field of life. The undercanopy does not have inhabitants. It *is* the inhabitants. Every creature here is an organ of the larger body, and the larger body is the plateau, and the plateau is the root, and the root is the world, and the world has been growing inward for so long that the distinction between the living and the lived-in has dissolved entirely.

What lives here is the place itself, expressing itself in forms — flat, climbing, coiling, groaning, marking, growing, decomposing, regrowing — and the forms are temporary, and the place is not.

You are temporary. You are a form walking through a form, a warm body in a warm body, a pulse of movement in a landscape that measures movement in geological time. The undercanopy does not notice you. It will not notice you when you leave. It will not notice you when you die. It did not notice the markers, except in the way that a body notices a scratch — by growing around it, by healing over it, by continuing.

This is what lives here: continuation. Patience. The slow, warm, wet, green business of going on.

And it is enough. It has been enough for longer than the word "enough" has meant anything. It will be enough after the word has been forgotten, along with everything else that was ever said or carved or thought inside the body of the world.
