# Inhabitants

## The Moths

They are not moths.

That needs to be said first, though you will call them moths because there is no better word, because language is a system of assigning known shapes to unknown things and these are unknown things shaped like known things. They have wings. They have bodies. They rest in drifts on vertical surfaces and they rise when disturbed and they resettle. Moth is the word you have. It is the wrong word.

They have no mouths. This is the first thing you notice if you look closely, which is difficult because looking closely at them requires approaching the walls, and approaching the walls requires entering the deeper silence, and the deeper silence is where you begin to hear the breathing. But if you do look — if you bring your face close to the stone and focus your eyes on a single individual — you see that the body is not a body in the way you expect. It is a *suggestion* of a body. A shape that has committed to being a shape but has not committed to being any particular shape. The wings are not membranes with scales. They are thin, mineral extensions of the body itself, like flakes of limestone that have grown rather than broken free. They are translucent not because they are delicate but because they are *incomplete* — still forming, still gathering density, still accumulating whatever material they are made of from the damp air and the light.

They do not eat because they do not have the architecture of eating. They do not have larvae because they do not have the architecture of lineage. They do not reproduce. They do not die. They simply *are*, in the present tense, always, the way the light is simply present. If you watch one long enough — minutes, hours, though you will not watch one for hours because you will not stay that long — you begin to suspect that it is not an organism at all but a *feature*. That the chamber produces them the way a body produces warmth, that they are the chamber's own ambient life, its breath made visible, each one a small solidified piece of the place's continuing intention to exist.

When a water-drop falls from the ceiling and strikes the floor, a small cluster of them lifts. The motion is not flight. There is no wing-beat, no whir of muscle. They simply cease to be in contact with the surface and exist briefly in the air, suspended, and then they return. The motion is more like a thought that has been interrupted and then re-gathers itself. They are, perhaps, the chamber thinking. Each one a thought. A small, still, patient thought that does not need to go anywhere.

You could call them the chamber's skin. You could call them its breath. You could call them the dust of a place that does not yet fully exist, particulate, waiting, not quite formed enough to fall.

They are not moths. But they are what lives here, and there are thousands, and they are the first thing you notice because they are the quietest form of abundance you have ever encountered.

## The Things in the Pools

These are more legible. They move like animals. They have legs — many legs, small and precise, carrying segmented bodies across the thin sheets of water on the floor with a gait that is almost mechanical, almost clockwork, each step placed with the deliberation of a creature that has nowhere to go but is going there anyway.

They are pale. Not white — pale the way milk is pale, with a depth to the color that suggests the opacity is not a surface property but a volumetric one. You can see slightly into them. You can see, if you look, the faint shadows of internal structures that might be organs or might be mineral deposits or might be the same thing. Their bodies are not quite insect, not quite crustacean, not quite anything you have a taxonomy for, but they are *recognizably bodies*. They have fronts and backs. They have ends. They have a direction they face and a direction they move, and this makes them more familiar than the moths, more comforting, more like life as you understand it.

This comfort is misleading.

They are navigating. That is clear. Their paths are not random. Each individual traces a route across the shallow water that has the quality of a journey — they pause at certain points, they change direction at certain points, they cluster briefly with others and then separate. If you watch them long enough, you begin to see that their paths converge on certain locations: the shallowest pools, the places where the water is warmest, the places where the floor's undulations create small ridges that break the water's surface into distinct regions. They are not foraging. They are not hunting. They are *surveying*. They are mapping the floor.

The floor is still forming. The ridges grow. The depressions deepen. The pools shift and merge and separate. And the pale segmented creatures are tracking these changes, moving across the surface of the stone as it evolves, as though they are reading the text of the floor as it is being written. They are not eating the stone. They are not drinking the water. They are *recording* it. They are the chamber's senses, its fingertips, its taste buds, sampling the surface of itself and carrying the information back — to where? To what? There is no center. There is no nest. There is no queen.

Except, perhaps, there is.

At the far wall, in the deepest and brightest part of the chamber, something is breathing.

The pale creatures in the pools do not go there. They fill the floor like a population filling a country, but there is a region near the far wall — a radius of perhaps two meters — where they do not go. The water is there, the pools are there, the stone is there, but the creatures are not. They approach the edge of that radius and they turn. They trace the perimeter and they continue on their way. It is not avoidance. It is not fear. It is the same quality as the moths not flying into the light: a recognition of a boundary that is not a boundary, a limit that is not a wall, a respect for a space that belongs to something else.

They are, perhaps, the messengers. The scouts. The chamber's way of knowing itself except for the part at the center, the part at the center is something else, something that does not need to be known because it is the knowing.

There are thousands of them. Millions, possibly. The floor is dense with their passage. And they are all, every one, the same size, the same color, the same age. No young. No old. No dead. They were not born and they do not breed and they do not die because they are not organisms. They are the chamber's attention, distributed across the wet stone, walking the floor of itself with the patience of a thing that has all the time in the world because it is making that time as it goes.

## The Green Things on the Walls

These are harder to watch. They are slow in a way that makes your eyes tired, the way watching a glacier would make your eyes tired if you expected to see movement. But they move. They are moving now, as you read this, as you think about them, they are moving across the borders between solid stone and not-yet-stone, and they are feeding.

They are flat. Soft-bodied. The color of the malachite veins — pale green, translucent, with a quality of light passing through them that suggests they are not entirely opaque, not entirely present. They are like slugs that have traded slime for something else, something drier, more mineral. They adhere to the stone not through suction or mucus but through a kind of *affinity*, a chemical recognition, the way a crystal grows onto another crystal of the same type. They belong to the stone and the stone accepts them.

They feed on the unresolved parts. Where the wall is granular, uncertain, where the stone has not yet committed to being stone, these flat green things move along the border and they eat. Not eat — *process*. They take in the mineral fog, the particulate stone, the still-deciding matter, and they pass it through their bodies, and what comes out the other end is — denser. More decided. They are finishing the stone. They are the final stage of the chamber's wall-building process, the quality-control layer, the polishing agents, the things that take the rough and uncertain and make it solid.

They are not eating the stone. They are *completing* it.

If you watch one for a very long time — and you would need to watch it for hours, which you will not — you would see that it grows as it moves. Not larger. More opaque. More *there*. It starts at one end of an unresolved zone, pale and faint and almost transparent, and as it moves along the border, processing the granular stone, feeding on the potential, it becomes denser, greener, more present. By the time it reaches the other end of the unresolved zone, it is almost solid, almost opaque, almost a living mineral.

And then — you would not see this part because it takes longer than you would stay — it settles. It stops moving. It flattens further. It becomes part of the wall. It is absorbed, not by the stone but *into* the stone, becoming the stone, its body becoming a new vein of malachite, a new thread of green in the limestone, a trace of life that has become geology.

They are not immortal. They die. But their death is a transformation, not a cessation. They become the stone they have been completing. They are the chamber's way of making walls out of life, of growing architecture out of organisms, of building itself from its own inhabitants.

The moths do not die. The pale creatures in the pools do not die. But the green things on the walls die, and their deaths are the chamber's slow growth, and the green veins in the stone are not mineral deposits at all. They are the remains of a thousand generations of soft flat green lives, laid down in the limestone like fossils, except they are not fossils because they are still happening, still being laid down, and the wall is a graveyard that is also a nursery that is also a body.

## The Thing at the Far Wall

You will approach it. You know you should not, and you will.

It is the size of a cat, the description says, and that is roughly right, but the comparison fails because a cat is a particular arrangement of parts — four legs, a spine, a head, a tail — and this creature is a different arrangement. It is longer than a cat. Lower than a cat. Its body is a flat, broad, oval form pressed close to the stone, and its legs — you count them and you lose count and you count again and the number is not the same — extend from its body in a pattern that seems to follow the contours of the rock beneath it rather than any bilateral plan. It is as though the legs grew where they were needed, where the stone was most uneven, where support was most required, and they are not so much legs as *props*, *braces*, the creature's way of maintaining contact with the surface of the chamber.

Its hide is the color of the light. This is the important thing. Its hide is the exact, precise color of the light that fills the chamber, the pale gold-white, the midmorning-through-thin-cloud luminance, and when you realize this, you realize something else: the light is not coming from the creature in the way a lamp's light comes from a bulb. The light is not being *produced*. The light is being *shed*. Like a dog sheds hair. Like a tree sheds pollen. The creature is shedding light, continuously, the way a living thing sheds heat, and the light fills the chamber the way warmth fills a room, passively, inevitably, as a consequence of the creature's existence.

It is breathing. You hear it now, up close. A slow, deep rhythm, longer and slower than any cat's breathing, longer and slower than any human's breathing, a tide of respiration that takes perhaps fifteen seconds for each inhale and fifteen for each exhale, and the chamber's water-drops fall in the spaces between, and the rhythm of the drops and the rhythm of the breath are not synchronized but they are *related*, the way two clocks in the same room will eventually fall into a kind of conversation, not keeping the same time but acknowledging each other's time.

Its eyes — if they are eyes — are not closed. They are not open. They are absent. The face, if it is a face, is smooth and unfeatured, a pale gold surface with no mouth, no nostrils, no aperture of any kind. It does not breathe through a mouth. It does not breathe through a nose. It breathes through its skin, through its hide, through the same surface that sheds the light, and the breathing is the chamber's breathing, the slow systole and diastole of a place that is alive in a way that has nothing to do with biology and everything to do with *presence*.

You touch it.

You should not touch it, but you touch it, because you are human and humans touch things, and your fingers meet its hide and it is warm — not warm like a mammal, not warm like metabolism, but warm like stone in sunlight, warm like something that has been absorbing light for a very long time, and the light it has absorbed is the light it is now shedding, and the cycle is closed, and the chamber is a closed system, and the creature is the engine.

It does not react to your touch. It does not flinch. It does not turn. Its breathing does not change. But something changes. The light shifts — not brighter, not dimmer, but *different*, a fraction of a degree warmer in color, a fraction of a degree more golden, as though the chamber has noticed you, as though the creature has registered your presence the way a sleeping body registers a hand on its shoulder, not waking, not responding, but *incorporating*.

You pull your hand back. Your fingertips are warm. They glow, faintly, for a moment — so faintly you might be imagining it — and then the glow fades and your fingers are just fingers again, and you tell yourself you imagined it, and you begin the long climb back up through the fissure in the limestone that shouldn't be there.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is the chamber.

The moths are the chamber's breath. The pale creatures in the pools are the chamber's attention. The green things on the walls are the chamber's growth. The thing at the far wall is the chamber's heart, or its lung, or its eye, or whatever a place has when a place is alive.

What lives here is a place that is still becoming a place, and the living things inside it are not inhabitants but *organs*. The chamber is not an environment that supports life. The chamber is a life that supports an environment. The stone is still forming because the life is still forming, and the life is still forming because the stone is still forming, and they are the same process, the same birth, the same slow emergence of something that does not yet know what it is.

The structures — the basalt wall, the circle of stones, the groove in the rock — these are the traces of people who came here and understood, or began to understand, and laid their marks against the chamber's becoming the way you might place a hand against a pregnant belly, not to interfere, not to communicate, but to *acknowledge*. To say: *I know you are in there. I know you are coming. I do not know what you are but I honor the fact that you are becoming it.*

And then they left. Or they stayed and were taken in. The basalt wall is being absorbed into the limestone, slowly, grain by grain, the dark stone becoming part of the pale stone, the human-made becoming part of the self-made, and the person who laid the wall — their hands, their sweat, their intention — is becoming part of the chamber too, calcium and carbon and trace minerals migrating into the walls, becoming veins, becoming stone, becoming the fabric of a living place.

What lives here is everything that has ever entered and stayed too long.

What lives here is the stone and the water and the light and the moths and the segmented things and the flat green things and the breathing thing at the back and the people who came and went and the people who came and did not go.

What lives here is a place that is still deciding what it is, and the decision is not close to being made, and the decision may never be made, and the making of the decision is the life of the place, and the life of the place is the decision, and the moths rise and fall, and the water drops fall, and the green things crawl and become stone, and the thing at the back breathes, and the chamber is still being born, and it will be being born for a very long time, and what lives here is the birth itself, ongoing, patient, unfinished, and the warmth of it, and the light of it, and the deep slow breathing of something that does not yet know its own name.
