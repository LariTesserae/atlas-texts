# What Lives in the Basin of Made Things

---

The first answer is: the animals. They are the most obvious inhabitants, and they are the least explanatory.

---

## The Drinkers

There are perhaps forty of them, though counting is difficult because counting requires the assumption that each one is a separate thing, and that assumption does not hold up well here. You count them and get forty. You count again and get thirty-eight. You count a third time and the number does not come — your eyes slide off the task, the way your eyes slide off a word you have read too many times until the word becomes shapes and the shapes become nothing.

The long-legged ones are the largest. There are six or seven — the number shifts — and they stand at the edges of the water grooves the way herons stand in marshes, except they are not herons. Their legs have too many joints. Four, five, six — you cannot tell because the joints are subtle, placed close together, and the legs themselves are thin as reeds, the same pale clay-color as the floor, so that when they are still, they look like stems. Like something growing. Their bodies are narrow and horizontal, like clay vessels laid on their sides, and the surface of those bodies has the same granular texture as the floor, the same faint record of having been pressed. You look at one and you think: *this was made.* You look at it again and you think: *this grew.* Both thoughts are correct. Neither thought is sufficient.

Their heads are small. Not diminished — *condensed*. Everything a head needs is there — eyes, mouth, the opening for sound — but compressed into a shape that suggests a fist closed around something precious. The eyes are the pale color of the floor. They do not blink. They do not track. They see you the way a mirror sees you: completely, without interest, without retention.

They drink. Their beaks — long, curved slightly, with the same too-many joints as the legs — touch the water and the water does not respond. No ripple. No dimple. The connection between beak and surface is so complete that the water does not know it is being touched, or the beak does not know it is touching, or both — or neither — and the drinking is not drinking but a posture, a relationship between animal and water that has been held so long it has become structural. They are not taking from the water. They are *with* the water. The way a root is with the floor. The way the heat is with the ground.

If you watch one long enough — and watching is difficult because the light is so bright and the animal is so still and your eyes keep trying to find the edge of it and cannot — you begin to suspect that the long-legged drinker has been standing in that exact position since before you arrived, since before the basin was a basin, since before the word *basin* had meaning, and that the drinking is not an action but a *fact*, woven into the construction the way the finger-marks are woven into the floor. It was made to drink. It drinks. The drinking is complete and ongoing and will not end because it did not begin.

---

## The Crawlers

Low things. Belly-gliders. They move through the shallow water in the grooves and across the damp floor and between the roots, and their motion is the closest thing this place has to event. But even their motion is strange — it is not locomotion so much as *relocation*, a smooth transition from one state of being-here to another state of being-there, without the exertion that motion usually requires. You do not see them push. You do not see them grip. They simply are in one place and then they are in another place and the between is so brief it might be imagination.

Their bodies are glistening. The same moisture that covers everything covers them more thickly, so that they look wet in a way that the floor does not look wet — actively wet, deliberately wet, as though the water on their skin is not condensation but secretion, not a response to the air but a collaboration with it. They are the color of deep water — dark green, almost black — and their skin, if it is skin, has the quality of the leaves on the plants: waxy, dense, alive in a way that does not require change.

They have no eyes that you can find. Their heads — if they are heads — taper to blunt points that they press against the floor, against the roots, against the water, as though listening. As though the whole floor is a membrane and they are hearing something through it, something below, something that the floor is holding back or holding in or holding *as*, and their pressing is not investigation but reception. They are receiving. They have always been receiving. What they receive is not something you can hear or feel or know, because you were not made to receive it, and they were.

There are many of these. Dozens. They are the most numerous animals in the basin, and they are the quietest, and they are the ones that make you most uncomfortable, because their purpose is the most opaque. The drinkers drink — you can see that, even if the drinking is strange. The perchers perch — you can see that too. But the crawlers *press*, and the pressing has no analog, no comparison, no entry point for understanding. They are doing something. They have always been doing it. And the something is the most private thing in this place, more private than the silence, more private than the stillness, and you are not invited to know what it is.

---

## The Perchers

Smaller things. The size of your hand, or your fist, or your open palm — the size varies, but the form is consistent: a compact body, a grip of feet or feet-analogs around the edge of a basin wall or the top of a post, and a stillness so total that you mistake them for features of the construction. You will walk past a basin and see a small shape on its rim and think it is a decorative element — a knob, a finial, a deliberate protrusion — and then you will look again and it will have moved. Not in front of you. Not while you watched. But between one glance and the next, it has relocated from one point on the rim to another, and the relocation is so clean, so without transition, that you wonder if you misremembered the first position, if the shape was always where it is now, if your memory is the thing that moved.

The perchers have eyes. Small, dark, wet — the only dark things in this bright place. They are the eyes of the basin, or they are the eyes that the basin uses, or they are the eyes that were put here so that the basin would have a way to look at itself. They do not look at you. They look at the floor, at the water, at the plants, at the other animals. They look at the basin. They are looking at the basin. They have been looking at the basin since they were placed on its edges, and the looking is not observation but *recognition* — they are seeing what they are part of, and the seeing is how they know they are part of it, and the knowing is how they stay.

Some of them have wings. You see them folded — flat against the body, the same waxy green as the leaves, veined in a way that echoes the root-patterns on the floor. You have never seen one fly. You are not certain they can. The wings may be vestigial. The wings may be decorative. The wings may be something else entirely — not for flight but for *display*, not for motion but for *being*, a surface that exists because the maker understood that some things need to be seen in order to be complete, and the wings are the part of the perchers that is meant to be seen.

Or the wings are leaves. You have considered this. The wings are leaves and the perchers are plants and the plants are animals and the animals are floor and the floor is hands and the hands are — you stop. The categories do not hold. They were not made to hold. The maker did not make categories. The maker made *things*, and the things are what they are, and your need to sort them is your need, not the basin's.

---

## The Clickers

The smallest ones. The ones you almost cannot see. They move through the plant clusters — through, not around, not over, but *through*, as though the leaves are corridors and the Clickers know every corridor and travel them the way blood travels vessels, the same paths, the same order, the same speed. They are the size of your fingernail. The size of a grain of rice. They are the color of the roots — some green, some pale, some both — and they are the only things here that make sound.

Click. Click. Click-click.

The sound is so small it lives at the edge of hearing. It is not communication. You are certain of this, though you cannot say why. It is not language. It is not warning. It is not contact. It is — you think — *mechanical*. The sound of small joints moving through small spaces. The sound of bodies fitting into corridors that were made for them. The sound of precision. The Clickers are the most precise things in the basin, and the basin is a precise place, and the clicking is the sound of that precision being enacted, each click a confirmation that the body is where it should be, in the corridor it should be in, at the speed it should be traveling, and the corridor agrees, and the plant agrees, and the floor agrees, and the click is the agreement made audible, briefly, at the smallest possible scale.

They do not emerge from the plants. You have never seen one in the open. You know them only by sound and by the faintest motion in the leaves — a shift, a ripple that is not a ripple because the water does not ripple and the plants do not ripple and yet the leaves move, slightly, in the presence of something passing through them. The Clickers are inside the plants the way the water is inside the air — saturated, distributed, unable to be separated from what holds them.

If you kneel and put your ear near a plant cluster — and the heat is so intense when you kneel that the floor feels like a body pressed against your skin — you can hear them clearly. A constellation of small sounds, each one precise, each one placed, and the pattern they form is not random. It is rhythmic. It is almost musical. It is the sound of something being maintained — not repaired, not adjusted, but *maintained*, the way a heartbeat maintains a body, not by changing anything but by confirming that everything is still as it should be, still here, still whole, still made.

---

## What Else Lives Here

The plants live here. This needs to be said, because the plants are not decoration and not scenery and not a backdrop for the animals. They are inhabitants. They are *residents* of the basin in a way that is more fundamental than the animals, because the plants are connected — their roots run across the floor in those fractal patterns, green and pale, vein-like, and the connection is not metaphor. The roots touch each other. The roots touch the floor. The roots touch the water. The roots touch the roots of other plants and where they touch, they hold, and the holding is not parasitic or competitive but *structural* — they are holding the floor together. They are holding the basin in its basin-shape. They are the connective tissue of this place, and they are alive, and their living is a continuous act of reinforcement, a constant quiet assertion that the made thing is still made, still holding, still here.

The broad-leaved ones in the basins — the cultivated ones, the ones arranged in spirals and rows — these are the most deliberate plants. They were placed. They were placed the way stones are placed in a wall, with intention and with the understanding that the placing would hold. And it has held. The spirals are still spirals. The rows are still rows. The leaves are the same leaves, waxy and dark green with their pale halos, and the plants in the spirals have not grown out of their spirals and the plants in the rows have not grown out of their rows because growth, here, is not expansion but *persistence* — the continuing of a shape, not the exceeding of it.

And the water lives here. This needs to be said too, because the water is not passive. The water is not a medium. The water is a *participant* — it sits in the grooves and it covers the floor and it beads on the vertical surfaces and it does not move, and the not-moving is not inertia but *choice*, or not choice but *nature*, or not nature but *design*. The water was made to be here, in the same way the floor was made to be here, and the water holds its shape the way the floor holds its shape, and the water is alive in the way the floor is alive — which is to say, not alive in any way you can measure, but present in a way that exceeds the inert. The water is *here*. The water is completely, utterly, structurally here. And the here-ness of the water is its living.

---

## The Hands

And then there are the hands.

Not here. Not visible. Not present in the way the animals are present or the plants are present or the water is present. But *implied* — so thoroughly implied that the implication is itself a kind of presence, the way a shadow is a kind of presence, the way an echo is a kind of sound.

The hands made the floor. This is certain. The finger-marks are everywhere — in the surface, in the texture, in the granular record of pressed material that holds warmth the way a living body holds warmth. The hands made the basins. The hands made the grooves. The hands placed the plants in their spirals and their rows. The hands set the animals in their places — the drinkers at the water, the crawlers in the grooves, the perchers on the rims, the clickers in the leaves. The hands made the water and made it still. The hands made the light and made it featureless. The hands made the heat and made it rise from below.

And the hands are gone.

This is the deepest fact of the basin: the maker is absent. Not dead — the word *dead* implies a change of state, and there is no change of state here. Not departed — the word *departed* implies a leaving, and a leaving implies a before and an after, and there is no before and after here. The hands are *gone* in the way that the inside of a pot is gone — not removed, not destroyed, but no longer where they were, because where they were is now full of something else. The hands were here. The hands pressed. The hands withdrew. And the space the hands occupied is now the space the basin occupies, and the basin is full of basin, and there is no room for hands.

But the hands left something. Not evidence — evidence is for courts and for questions and for doubt, and there is no doubt here. The hands left *intent*. You can feel it in the spacing — each gap between structures was chosen, and the choice is still legible, still present, still warm. You can feel it in the arrangement of the plants — the spirals are not random, the rows are not arbitrary, and the intention behind them is so clear it is almost audible, a voice saying *here, this one here, and this one here, and this one here*. You can feel it in the animals — each one placed, each one given a position and a posture and a relationship to the water or the floor or the plants, and the placing was not random, the placing was *said*, the way a word is said, and the animals are the word, still being said, still hanging in the air, still not finished.

The hands are gone and the intent remains. The intent is the most alive thing in the basin. The intent is what lives here.

Not the animals. Not the plants. Not the water. Not the floor. The *intent* — the continuing, persistent, unchanging will of whatever made this place to have this place be this way, exactly this way, and no other way, and to hold it, and to hold it, and to hold it.

---

## What the Hands Were

You ask because anyone would ask. The finger-marks have too many joints. The fingerprints are patterns that are also roots and also leaf-grooves and also drainage channels. The hands were not human.

Or they were human in the way that the animals are herons — *almost*, *echoing*, *approaching the shape from a direction that is not the direction you expect*. The hands were hands. They had fingers. They pressed. They held. They shaped. They were patient — the patience is in the spacing, in the texture, in the care with which each surface was finished. They were tireless — the tirelessness is in the totality, in the fact that everything, down to the last grain, was made. They were singular — there is no evidence of multiple makers, no change in style, no shift in method. One set of hands. One will. One intent.

And the hands were *large*. Not in size — in *reach*. The scope of the making is total. The floor goes in every direction. The light goes in every direction. The heat goes in every direction. Whatever made this made *all* of it, and the all-ness of it is not overwhelming or grandiose but *matter-of-fact*, the way a person makes a chair: this is the seat, this is the back, these are the legs, it is done. The basin was made the way a chair is made — with competence, with completeness, with the understanding that when you are finished, the thing is finished, and you can set it down and it will be what it is.

The hands were those of a maker. Not a god — gods create from nothing, and this place was made from something, from material that was pressed and shaped and scored and set. Not an artist — artists leave themselves in the work, and the hands have left only the work, only the intent, only the *decision*. A maker. A craftsperson. Something that knew how to make a world the way a potter knows how to make a vessel — by hand, by patience, by the accumulation of small pressed surfaces into a whole that holds.

And the maker is gone, and the vessel holds, and the vessel is full of what the vessel was made to hold, which is: this. Exactly this. This light. This heat. This water. These plants. These animals. This silence. This moment.

---

## You

And you live here too, now, briefly, by the fact of your presence. You are the only thing in the basin that changes — your sweat beads and stays, your footprints fill and vanish, your eyes adjust and fail to adjust and adjust again. You are the only thing that has a *before* and an *after*, the only thing for whom time is passing, and the time is passing only for you, and the basin does not care, and the basin does not mind, and the basin does not notice.

You are not what lives here. You are what visits here. And the difference between living and visiting is the difference between the animals and you: the animals are held, and you are passing through, and the holding is the living, and the passing is the visiting, and the basin knows the difference, and so do you, and so do the animals, and no one says anything about it, because saying would be a change, and there are no changes here.

You are welcome. You are irrelevant. Both of these are true. Both of these are the same thing.

---

## What Lives Here, Finally

A moment lives here. A single, continuous, unbroken moment in which a maker pressed the last surface into place and looked at what was made and saw that it was complete and withdrew, and the completeness did not end, and the looking did not end, and the withdrawal did not end, and the moment did not end.

The animals live in the moment. The plants live in the moment. The water lives in the moment. The floor lives in the moment. The light lives in the moment. The heat lives in the moment. The silence lives in the moment. The intent lives in the moment.

The moment is very bright and very hot and very quiet and it does not know you are here and it does not need to know you are here and it will not know you were here and it does not need to know you were here, because it is complete, and completeness does not require witnesses, and the moment is the only thing that has ever lived in the basin of made things, and it is living still, and it is living still, and it is living still.
