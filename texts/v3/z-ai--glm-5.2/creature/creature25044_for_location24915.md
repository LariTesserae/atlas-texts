# What Lives Here

## The film

Begin with what you cannot see.

On every submerged surface — every pool floor, every channel bed, every rim of calcite beneath its thin sheet of flowing water — there is a coating. It is not algae. It is not moss. It has no common name because no one has needed to name it; it exists below the threshold of human attention, below the threshold of most attention. A biofilm. A community of single-celled organisms so sparse that it barely qualifies as a layer, so thin that the mineral surface beneath it shows through, so simple in its organization that calling it a "community" may already be too generous. It is more like a stain that happens to be alive.

But it is here, and it is everywhere here, and it is the foundation of everything else that lives.

The organisms within it are chemoautotrophs, or something close enough. They do not photosynthesize — there is no need, or perhaps no advantage; the light here is abundant but the chemistry is more reliable. They draw energy from the mineral gradients in the water, from the slow reactions between alkaline fluid and stone, from the faint disequilibrium between what the water carries and what the rock offers. Their metabolism is slow. Glacially slow. They divide perhaps once a week, perhaps once a month. They do not grow in colonies visible to the naked eye. They do not form structures. They simply persist, a living film thinner than a human hair, spread across the geometry of the stone like a translucent varnish, and they have persisted this way for longer than the film has had anyone to notice it.

If you kneel and press your face close to a pool — and the brightness will make you squint, and the cold rising from the water will sting your cheeks — you can almost see it. A slight difference in the quality of the submerged stone. Where the film is present, the surface has a faintly different texture, a barely perceptible mattness that breaks the reflection just enough to notice if you are looking for it. Where the film is absent — in the faster channels, where the flow scours the stone clean — the mineral surface is brighter, slicker, more purely itself. The film dulls what it touches. That is the only signature it leaves.

It is, in the most literal sense, the bottom of the food chain here. The only food chain here. Everything above it derives from it, and it derives from the stone and the water, and the stone and the water derive from the plateau itself, and the plateau derives from nothing — it simply is.

## The grazers

The crustacean-like creatures come next, and they are the largest permanent inhabitants of the terraces, which tells you something about the scale of life here. They are not large. The biggest you see is perhaps four centimeters long, and most are smaller.

They are pale. Not white — the white of the mineral formations is a vivid, reflective, wet-surface white that throws light. The grazers are a duller shade, a matte ivory that sits somewhere between the cream of the calcite and the faint ochre of the iron-stained ridges. Their shells, or the things that serve as shells, carry the mineral signature of the water they live in. They are, in a sense, made of the place. Deposited within it. Their exoskeletons incorporate calcium carbonate from the pools, and over the course of a life — however long that is; you cannot tell — they become small walking fragments of the terrace itself. When they die, and their bodies settle to the bottom of a pool, they dissolve and re-deposit so quickly that they leave no fossil. They are of the stone and they return to the stone and the stone does not remember them.

Their movement is the thing that reveals them. Against the stillness of the mineral surfaces, any motion is conspicuous. They move slowly — not the darting, reactive motion of a freshwater shrimp, but a deliberate, almost contemplative traversal, as if they are in no hurry because there is nothing to hurry toward. They glide across the bottom of a shallow pool on legs you cannot see, their bodies low and flattened to the stone, and they graze. Their mouths — or the structures that function as mouths — work continuously, scraping the biofilm from the mineral surface, consuming it in a thin, even pass. They leave a faint trail behind them: a margin of slightly brighter stone where the film has been removed, already being recolonized as they move on. If you watch long enough, you can see the trail slowly disappear as the film re-establishes itself, growing back at a pace matched, almost exactly, to the pace of grazing. Equilibrium. The grazers eat the film as fast as it grows. The film grows as fast as it is eaten. Neither gains. Neither loses. The system has been in this state long enough that the grazers have shaped the film and the film has shaped the grazers and neither would be what it is without the other.

There is no abundance here. No population boom, no crash, no season of plenty followed by a season of want. You count them as you walk: one in this pool, two in that channel, none in the next four pools, then three in a shallow pan of water barely deep enough to cover them. The density is constant. Low. A thin scattering of life across a vast mineral surface, like a few grains of sand on a bright floor. They do not crowd each other. They do not avoid each other. They seem indifferent to each other's existence, each one following its own slow path across its own patch of stone, and if their paths cross they pass without acknowledgment.

You notice that they are not uniform. There are at least two forms. One is the flat, oval shape you see most often — the grazer, the one that moves across open pool floors. The other is longer, narrower, and you find it in the channels where the water flows fastest, clinging to the walls of the channel rather than the floor, its body angled against the current in a way that suggests it is shaped to hold its position in moving water. It does not graze in the same way. It seems to filter — holding still, letting the water pass over it, extracting something from the flow. Microscopic particles. Fragments of biofilm dislodged from upstream. The thin detritus of a thin ecosystem. It is the same species as the grazer, or it is not. You cannot tell. The taxonomy of this place has not been written, and may never be, because the creatures within it exist at the very edge of what is worth describing.

## The things in the deep pools

There are deeper pools. Not many, but a few — pools where the water has cut or dissolved a basin that goes down a meter or more, where the bottom disappears into a mineral darkness that the brightness of the surface cannot penetrate. The water in these pools is colder than the shallow water, still and dense, and it smells different — more metallic, more alkaline, as if it has been in contact with the stone for longer and has taken more from it. You look into these pools and you see nothing but your own reflection and the reflection of the sky behind you, and the reflections are so sharp and so bright that the pool seems to be a window rather than a body of water.

But there is movement, sometimes. Not at the surface. Below it. A disturbance in the clarity — a faint clouding, a shift in the way light passes through the water column, as if something has moved at the bottom and stirred up a sediment that the eye cannot directly see. You watch. You wait. The sound roars around you. The mist drifts. And then, in one pool, you see it: a shape rising from the bottom, pale and slow, a body broader than the grazers in the shallows, with a profile that suggests something flattened and fringed, something that has lived in darkness long enough that it does not need the features that light-dwelling creatures need. No visible eyes. No pigmentation. A body that moves by undulation, a slow ripple along a margin that could be a fin or could be a skirt or could be something that has no analog in any body of water you have known.

It rises a meter from the bottom and then descends again. The disturbance settles. The pool returns to its mirror state. You have seen it for perhaps three seconds.

What is it? You do not know. It could be a larger version of the grazer form — a deep-water morph, grown larger in the cold and the dark, feeding on a biofilm that grows more slowly and more sparsely but grows nonetheless on the submerged walls of the basin. It could be something else entirely. A predator. A scavenger. Something that has found a way to make a living in the one part of this landscape that is not identical to every other part. The deep pools are the only variation here, the only place where the equilibrium of the surface gives way to something more complex, more stratified, and if there is a niche for a different kind of life, this is where it would be.

You look into the other deep pools and see nothing. But you have the sense — not proof, not evidence, just the quiet conviction of someone who has spent a day in a strange place — that they are not empty. That the stillness is not absence. That the brightness of the surface is a screen, and behind it the water holds what it holds.

## The bird

It is not a resident. It comes from below — from the green world, the world of growth and change, the world that exists at elevations where biology is not an afterthought. It comes up the mountain on the thermals, or on whatever air currents exist in this thin atmosphere, and it circles the terraces with a patience that suggests it has done this before and will do it again and has been doing it long enough that the circuit is established.

It is a raptor. Or it is raptor-shaped. Broad wings, a short tail, a dark body that stands out against the pale ground in a way that the other life here does not — as if it has not needed to adapt its coloration because it does not live here, only visits, only passes through the boundary between its world and this one. It hunts the grazers. You see it dive once, a steep stoop from a height that makes it almost invisible, and it strikes a shallow pool with its talons and comes up empty. The grazer it was aiming for has pressed itself flat against the stone, or has hidden in a fissure, or has simply not been where the bird thought it was. The bird climbs again. It circles. It is patient. It will dive again.

How often does it succeed? Not often, you think. The grazers are sparse, and they are the color of the stone, and the brightness of the water makes the pools into mirrors that probably confuse a hunting bird's depth perception. But it does not need to succeed often. The grazers are not its only food source. It hunts the lower slopes too, where insects and lizards and other birds exist in a world that is more generous. The terraces are one stop on a circuit, not a territory, and the bird's presence here is opportunistic — an extraction of marginal calories from a marginal system, a bet placed on the possibility that a pale shape on a pale stone will be there when the talons come down.

There is something mournful about it. Not the bird itself — the bird is simply alive, doing what alive things do, converting the world into itself — but the relationship it represents. The terraces are so nearly closed. So nearly a complete system, mineral and water and film and grazer, self-contained, self-sustaining, indifferent to everything beyond their bright, roaring, saturated boundary. And the bird crosses that boundary. The bird is an intrusion, a thread connecting this place to the larger world, a reminder that the plateau is not a separate creation but a part of a continuum, a high point in a landscape that includes forests and rivers and insects and birds of prey, and that the boundary between the living world and the mineral world is not a line but a gradient, and the gradient includes a raptor circling above a terrace, looking for a shape that matches the color of the stone.

## What is not here

It is worth noting what does not live here, because the absences define the place as much as the presences.

There are no plants. Not even the most minimal — no lichen, no algal mat, no single green cell. The chemistry is wrong, or the light is wrong, or the absence of soil is wrong, or all three. The plateau is a world before or after photosynthesis, and nothing has crossed that threshold since the water began to flow. The biofilm persists without light. The grazers persist without plants. The whole minimal ecosystem runs on chemistry and stone, and it does not need the sun except as a source of warmth and brightness, and it does not need the sun even for that, because the water is what it is regardless of the sky.

There are no insects. No flies, no beetles, no midges, no water striders on the surface of the pools. You would expect midges, at least — hardy, adaptable, capable of surviving in hot springs and salt lakes and glacial meltwater. But there are none. The mineral content may be too high. The water may be too alkaline. The absence of any terrestrial organic matter may mean there is nothing for an insect larva to eat. Or the sound may have something to do with it — the constant vibration, the physical pressure of the noise in every fluid medium, might be intolerable to an organism as small as an insect, might shake apart the delicate structures of its body, might make the surface of every pool a hostile environment rather than a home. This is speculation. But the absence is real, and it is total, and it contributes to the strangeness of the place: a landscape with water everywhere and not one insect on it or in it.

There are no amphibians. No fish. No reptiles. No mammals. The bird is the only vertebrate that uses this landscape, and it uses it only partially, only from above, only as a hunting ground and not a home. Nothing with a backbone lives on the terraces themselves. The grazers — if they have backbones, which you doubt — are the most complex permanent residents, and they are barely there, barely visible, barely alive in any sense that the world outside this place would recognize.

There are no microbes in the air, or none that persist. The mist forms and dissipates and forms again, and if it carries microorganisms from the pools into the atmosphere, they do not survive long enough to establish themselves elsewhere. The terraces export nothing. They are not a source. They are not a seed. They are a terminus, a place where life has arrived and adapted and persisted at the very edge of what is possible, and they have not spread, because there is nowhere to spread to. The terraces are the terraces. The world below is the world below. The boundary holds.

## The sense of a system

What lives here, then, is a system so minimal that calling it an ecosystem feels like exaggeration. A film. A grazer. A thing in the deep pools. A bird that visits. That is the complete inventory, and it has been the complete inventory for longer than the stone can record, and it will be the complete inventory for as long as the water flows.

But there is something about the system that is more than the sum of its thinness. It is the way the parts fit. The film grows at a rate determined by the chemistry of the water. The grazers consume the film at a rate determined by their own metabolism, which is determined by the temperature of the water, which is determined by the altitude and the flow. The deep-pool creatures persist in a niche created by the geometry of the stone, which is determined by the erosion parameter — 1.5, established, in-balance — which has not changed. The bird arrives because the grazers exist and departs because the grazers are sparse, and its arrival and departure are governed by thermals that rise from the plateau, which are governed by the temperature differential between the bright stone and the cool air, which is governed by the light, which is governed by the sky. Everything connects. Everything is connected to everything by the thinnest threads, and the threads are all taut, and none of them has slackened or tightened in a span of time that the human mind can grasp.

This is what lives in the Terraces of Constant Sound. Not a community, exactly. Not a population. Not a web of life in the sense that the word is usually used, with its implications of abundance and complexity and change. What lives here is a calibration. A set of equations solved by organisms so simple that they barely qualify, in a place so stable that the solution has not needed to be re-derived. The film grows. The grazer eats. The deep thing moves. The bird circles. The water flows. The sound does not change.

And you — you who climbed up here, you who are warm where the place is cool, you who make sounds with your mouth that the roaring swallows — you are not part of the calibration. You are a visitor to an equation that does not include you. The grazers do not know you are watching them. The film does not know you exist. The deep-pool thing, if it noticed your shadow on the surface of its pool, noticed only a brief change in the light and then a return to the same brightness it has known for however long it has lived in that mineral dark.

You are the first thing here that is not in equilibrium. And you are the only thing here that will leave.
