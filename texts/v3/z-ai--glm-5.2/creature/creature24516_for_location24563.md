# What Lives on the Ghran Plain

---

The birds come first, because the birds are what you hear, and on the Ghran Plain hearing comes before seeing. Always.

They are herons — or they were herons, once, in the same way the ridge was a hill and the rivers were rivers. Countless generations of standing in shallow water on a flat plain with nowhere to fly to and nothing to flee from have changed them. They are tall, taller than any heron you would recognize, with legs like pale stilts that seem to go on impossibly long, as if the birds were trying to lift themselves out of the water and simply kept growing upward without ever succeeding. Their bodies are slim and almost colorless — not white, not grey, but the color of the sky when it is about to rain, a watery non-hue that makes them difficult to see against the horizon even when you are looking directly at them. When the plain floods and the mirror rises, they stand in the reflection and become half-submerged in their own images, so that each bird appears to be two — one reaching up into the air, one reaching down into the water, joined at the feet. They do not seem to notice this. They may not be able to see it. Their eyes are positioned for looking down, for watching the water's surface, for tracking the small dark shapes that move beneath the film.

They are called *gharrel* by the people who have passed through — though the people who passed through did not stay long enough to make the name stick, and the birds do not use it. They stand in processions, twenty or thirty in a line, all facing the same direction, all stepping at the same slow pace, one foot lifted, held, placed, the other lifted, held, placed. The synchronization is not communication. It is a shared reflex. The water moves at the same speed everywhere, the algae grows at the same density everywhere, and so every bird independently arrives at the same pace. They are like clocks wound to the same time not by a hand but by the mechanism of the place itself.

They call. This is what the gharrel do that you cannot escape — the deep, resonant tones that carry across the water and bounce off the cloud ceiling and arrive at your ear twice. The call is not a song. It is not territorial. It is not a warning or a summons. It is a sound that the birds make the way the outcrops make water — because they are there, because they are cool, because the air is wet. Something in the gharrel's chest, a resonance chamber that runs the length of its extraordinary neck, vibrates in the humid air and produces a tone that is almost subsonic, almost felt rather than heard, a hum that seems to come from the ground itself. When one calls, others call in response, but not in answer. They call because the first call created a vibration in the water that touched their feet, and their feet told their bodies, and their bodies told their throats, and their throats made the sound again. The chorus is not a conversation. It is a chain reaction.

---

The frogs are smaller than you think they should be.

For the noise they make — the pulsing, rhythmic undertone that rises from every puddle and pool and film of standing water — you expect something substantial. A frog that sounds like this should be the size of a fist, at least. But the frogs of the Ghran Plain are the size of a thumbnail. Dark, almost black, the same color as the algae they eat, and just as slick with the water that coats everything. They sit in the cracks between laterite polygons, in the shallow pools that form in the low places, on the weeping flanks of the outcrops where the moisture runs down in branching rivulets. They are everywhere. Everywhere. You cannot take a step on this plain without being within a meter of a frog, and usually within a few centimeters. They do not scatter when you approach. They do not have predators that approach from above on foot, and so they have no instinct for flight. You can kneel down and look at one, and it will look back at you — if looking is what a frog does — with its golden eye, and it will continue to call, and the vibration of its body as it makes the sound will send concentric ripples across the surface of its tiny pool.

They call in synchronization, the same way the birds walk in synchronization — not because they coordinate, but because they share the same medium. The frogs that live in the same cluster of pools can feel each other's calls through the water, and they adjust, and the adjustment becomes a rhythm, and the rhythm becomes a pulse, and the pulse is what you hear when you stand on the plain: the heartbeat of something very small repeated a million times until it becomes something very large.

Their life cycle is fast, which is strange on a plain where everything else is slow. They hatch from eggs laid in the standing water, they develop as tadpoles in the film of moisture that sits on the laterite, they metamorphose into frogs in the space between two rainfalls, and they die — not because anything kills them, but because they are built to burn out, built to complete a cycle and be replaced. The adult frogs live perhaps three months. In that time, they call, they mate, they lay eggs in every available pool, and then, when the water dries up in the brief evaporation period between rains, they desiccate. They fold in on themselves. Their skin hardens into a papery shell. They become small, dry, dark shapes sitting in the cracks of the laterite, and when the rain comes again — hours later, a day later, always soon — the water reaches them and they unfold, and they are alive again, or their eggs are alive, or the next generation is alive, and the calling resumes as if it never stopped. Because it never has.

This is the paradox of the Ghran Plain: the landscape is ancient beyond measure, and the life that fills it is swift and disposable and renewed without end. The stone does not change. The birds do not change. The frogs do not change, but they die constantly. They are the event horality of the place — the living things that experience time, that grow and mate and die while the plain around them does not, the creatures whose brief burning is the only evidence that time passes here at all.

---

The insects are the connective tissue.

They are small, dark, fast, and they are the reason anything else is here. The algae on the laterite and the outcrops is the foundation, and the insects are the teeth that chew it into something that can pass up the chain. There are several kinds, though the distinctions blur — the plain does not reward specialization, because the food source is singular and uniform. There are beetles that crawl across the wet stone, their bodies flattened and wide, their legs splayed, designed to move over a surface that is always wet and always flat. They scrape the algae film from the laterite with mouthparts that are broad and blunt, like trowels. There are flies — not flying flies, or not primarily — flies that have reduced their wings to stubs and spend their lives on the water's surface, skating on the surface tension, laying their eggs in the film. There are the larvae of something that is not quite a mosquito and not quite a midge, translucent thread-like creatures that writhe in the standing water and feed on the algae bloom that follows every rain.

And there is one kind that is stranger than the others, and it lives on the outcrops.

On the weeping surfaces of the ancient stone, where the moisture condenses from the air and runs down in branching rivulets, there is an insect that has become something very nearly like a plant. It is a hemipteran — a true bug, with piercing-sucking mouthparts — but it does not move. It finds a spot on the outcrop where the water runs most consistently, where the mineral staining shows that the flow has been steady for years or decades, and it settles there. It inserts its mouthparts into the thin film of algae that grows on the wet stone, and it stays. For its entire life. Its legs, unused, atrophy. Its wings, unfurled once to bring it to this spot, dry and fall away. Its body flattens against the stone and darkens until it is indistinguishable from the mineral stains around it — a pale, branching mark on the rock surface, the same color as the calcium and iron oxide deposits, the same shape as the water's path. You could look at a weeping outcrop and see hundreds of them and think you were looking at stains. And in a sense, you would be right. They are stains that used to be insects, stains that eat algae, stains that are alive.

They have no name. Nothing here needs names. The names are for the people who do not stay.

---

The lizards are the ones you notice last, because they are the color of the rock.

Small — the length of a hand, minus the tail, and the tail adds another hand's length — they live on the outcrops, and they are the top of the food chain. Not because they are large or fierce, but because there is nothing larger or fiercer. The plain does not produce enough vegetation to support large herbivores, and the insects and frogs do not produce enough biomass to support large predators. So the lizards are the summit. They eat the frogs that climb the outcrops to feed on the algae. They eat the flightless flies that cluster on the wet stone. They eat the stationary, plant-like hemipterans, prying them off the rock with small, strong jaws. They are the ones who hunt here.

Their coloration is extraordinary, and it is not camouflage — not exactly. It is a kind of becoming. The outcrops are varied in their mineral staining: some are pale with calcium, some are dark with iron, some are the amber color of the quartz veins. And the lizards match. Not individually — a single lizard does not change color to match its outcrop. But the population as a whole has diverged, each outcrop hosting a slightly different shade, so that the lizards on a calcium-pale rock are pale and the lizards on an iron-dark rock are dark. They have been on their outcrops for a very long time. The outcrops are isolated from each other by miles of flat, flooded, bird-patrolled ground, and the lizards do not cross the open plain. They cannot. They would be seen, and eaten, or they would lose their way in the featurelessness, or they would simply run out of outcrop and find themselves standing in an inch of water with nowhere to go. So they stay where they are, and they diverge, and each outcrop becomes an island, and each island has its own variety, and the varieties grow more distinct with every generation, and no generation is in a hurry.

If you visited the Ghran Plain in ten thousand years, the lizards on different outcrops might be different species. In a hundred thousand years, they certainly would be. The plain is a speciation machine, a slow one, grinding at a geological pace, producing diversity not through abundance but through isolation — the same isolation that shaped the plain itself, the same patience that eroded the hills and flattened the land and made of every outcrop a world entire.

---

And there is something else. Not an animal. Not a plant. But alive, and living here, and perhaps more present on the Ghran Plain than anything else.

The algae.

The dark, almost black film that coats the wet laterite and the weeping stone — it is not one species. It is thousands. It is the closest thing the plain has to a forest, a canopy, a biome of standing organisms that cover the available surface and photosynthesize and grow and die and are replaced. The algae are the vegetation of this place, the green (black) growing thing that turns light and water and mineral into life. They are the reason the birds are here, and the frogs, and the insects, and the lizards. They are the reason the air smells the way it does — that clean, mineral, organic sweetness, not rot, not bloom, but the exhalation of a living film on wet stone, the breath of something very simple doing the only thing it knows how to do.

The algae are old. Not as old as the stone, but older than the birds, older than the frogs, older than the lizards. They were here before the rivers finished their work and left. They were here when the plain was still being carved, when there were still hills to erode, when the water still flowed instead of seeping. They have been on this land since the land first held water, which is to say: always. They are the first life and, if the plain ever truly ended — if the stone finally wore away to nothing and the water had no ground to stand on — they would be the last.

You do not see them, when you stand on the Ghran Plain. You see the birds. You hear the frogs. You notice the lizards if you look closely and the insects if you look more closely. But the algae are beneath all of it, a living skin on the skin of the earth, so thin and so dark and so widespread that it registers not as life but as texture, not as presence but as the color of the ground itself. And this is, perhaps, the truth of the plain: the ground is alive. The stone is not dead rock coated with organisms. The stone is the organism, and the organisms are the stone, and the water is the blood of both, and the birds and the frogs and the lizards are the things that live on the skin of a living surface, calling and calling and calling into an air that has been warm and wet and full of sound for longer than anything can remember.

---

What lives on the Ghran Plain?

Everything that can live where nothing should. Everything that has found a way to make a home in a place that offers nothing but water and stone and time. Everything that does not need the land to be generous, only to be stable, only to be the same tomorrow as it is today, and the day after, and the century after that.

The birds that walk in reflections. The frogs that die and return with the rain. The insects that become stains. The lizards that become islands. The algae that becomes the ground.

And the plain itself, which has become so flat and so old and so patient that it is difficult to say where the geology ends and the biology begins — difficult to say whether the living things have colonized the stone or whether the stone has, over the long ages, simply grown itself a skin of life, the way any very old and very patient thing eventually does.
