# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is a guest.

There is no arrival. There is no leaving. The things that live on the plateau were not placed here and did not wander in. They *grew here* — not in the sense of being born to a place, but in the sense that a mineral grows in a vein, filling a shape that was already waiting. They fit this landscape the way water fits a cup. They are its product as much as its inhabitants, and if you scraped every living thing off the basalt and threw it into the sky, the next cycle would begin again within a season — a film, a crust, a movement, a whisper on stone — because the plateau does not need what lives here. It *produces* what lives here. The living things are a secretion. An exhalation. A property of the engine in the same way that heat is a property of fire.

Start with the film, because the film is where everything starts.

---

## The Dark Film

It has no name. It does not need one. It is a few millimeters thick at its deepest, and in most places it is only a few cell-layers deep — a smear, a breath, something you would wipe off your boot and not think about again. But it is alive. It is arguably the only thing here that is unambiguously, irreducibly alive in the way that word is usually meant.

It is not a single organism. It is a community — or rather, a chemistry. A consortium of microorganisms so tightly interwoven that the boundaries between species have blurred into irrelevance. There are things in the film that photosynthesize, or used to, or almost do, depending on how you define the process in light this dim and this diffuse. There are things that metabolize iron. There are things that metabolize sulfur. There are things that eat the things that metabolize iron and sulfur, and things that eat those things, and so on, down through scales of size so small that the word *organism* starts to lose its grip. At a certain scale, what you are looking at is not a living thing but a living *surface* — a membrane between stone and air where chemistry happens, where the minerals the vents carry up are caught and processed and converted into something the next layer up can use.

The film is darkest near the vents, where the moisture is most constant and the mineral flow most reliable. There it is almost black — a wet, slick, faintly iridescent coating that makes the surrounding stone look as though it has been painted with something between ink and oil. Move away from the vents and the film thins, fades, breaks into patches, becomes a stain rather than a coating. In the dry places, between vents, it disappears entirely. But its traces remain: the stone there is slightly darker than the stone that has never been colonized, as though the film has left a shadow behind, a mineral memory of its presence that will persist for centuries after the living cells themselves have retreated to the wet zones.

The film glows. Not brightly — you would not see it during the dim hours of the plateau's pseudo-day. But at night, in the deeper dimness, the areas near the vents take on a color that is hard to name. It is reddish. It is brownish. It is the color of old blood viewed through thick glass, or of rust in deep shade, or of something burning at a temperature too low to produce flame. The glow is produced by the iron-metabolizers, and it is not bioluminescence in any decorative sense — it is waste. It is the photon-equivalent of exhaust, a byproduct of a chemical process that happens to release visible light as a side effect. The film does not glow *for* anything. It glows because glowing is what happens when it eats.

If you knelt beside a vent at night and pressed your face close to the wet stone — and the cold would be bitter, and the dampness would sting your skin, and the mineral smell would fill your sinuses with the taste of iron and wet clay — you would see the film clearly: a living darkness, faintly smoldering, stretching out around the vent in a halo that pulses so slowly you would think you were imagining it. The pulse is the vent's breathing cycle. As warm vapor exhales from the stone, the film brightens — more moisture, more mineral, more food, more glow. As the vent subsides, the glow dims. It is the slowest heartbeat in the world, and it has been beating since before the basalt cooled.

The film is the base of everything. Everything else that lives here either eats the film or eats something that eats the film. It is the grass of this place, the soil, the foundation. And it is patient. The film has survived the drying of a thousand-year drought. It has survived the flooding of a century of rain. It has survived the slow, grinding migration of the herds over its surface a billion times, and each time it has regrown, and each time it has been exactly the same, because there is no reason for it to change. It has found its form. It has found its niche. It is the most successful thing on the plateau, and it is the simplest, and these two facts are not in tension. They are the same fact.

## The Rust Crusts

On the lee sides of larger stone formations — places where the wind, which is constant and directionless and never strong but never absent, has worn the basalt into low ridges and overhangs — there is another kind of life. It is harder to call it life. It is harder to call it anything.

The rust crusts are exactly what they sound like: patches of something that looks like rust, flaking and scaly, clinging to the stone with a tenacity that seems more mechanical than biological. They are orange-brown to deep red, depending on their age and their exposure, and they have the texture of very old paint that has been left in the weather too long — cracked, lifting at the edges, but still firmly attached at the center, still holding on.

They are alive, in the way that lichen is alive, which is to say: barely, and on a timescale that makes the word *alive* feel like an exaggeration. They are a symbiosis — or a parasitism, or a commensalism, or some fourth thing for which biology has not yet invented a word — between a fungus-like organism and a photosynthetic partner that may or may not actually be photosynthesizing, given the light. The fungus-like part provides structure. The other part provides... something. Energy, perhaps. Or minerals. Or simply *presence* — the chemical signal that tells the fungus-like part to keep going, to keep building, to keep adding one more layer of rust-colored tissue to the edge of the crust, one molecule at a time, one century at a time.

They grow so slowly that growth is the wrong word. They *accumulate*. A crust the size of your hand may be ten thousand years old. It may be older. It has been adding to itself, layer by layer, since before the current arrangement of the vents was established, since before the herd-paths were worn into the basalt, since before the ice in the north-facing channels was laid down. It has been here, in this spot, on this stone, doing approximately nothing, for longer than most civilizations have existed. And it will be here long after they are gone.

The crusts do not interact with the rest of the plateau's ecosystem in any obvious way. The herds do not eat them — they are too hard, too dry, too firmly attached to the stone, and they are on the wrong surfaces, the lee sides, the places the herds do not go. The film does not colonize them — or rather, it does, occasionally, in the wettest seasons, but the crusts produce some chemical that repels the film's microbial constituents, and the film retreats. The crusts are islands. They are a separate project, a parallel life, running on a different timescale and a different chemistry, and they have nothing to do with the engine that surrounds them.

Or maybe they do. Maybe they are doing something the surface cannot see. Maybe they are slowly, over millennia, breaking down the stone they cling to, converting the basalt's minerals into something the vents can carry back up, something the film can use, something the herds can eat. Maybe they are the digestive system of the plateau, working from the outside in, while the vents work from the inside out. Maybe the rust is not rust but a product — a refined material, a waste, a contribution. It is impossible to know. The crusts do not explain themselves. They simply persist, and the stone beneath them is perhaps very slightly softer than the stone elsewhere, and perhaps that is their doing, and perhaps it is not.

What is certain is that they are old. What is certain is that they are patient. What is certain is that if you stood beside one and watched it for a thousand years, you would see it grow by the width of a hair, and that would be a good year.

## The Pale Things

In the ice. In the mineral crusts. In the thin, wet layer between the frozen surfaces and the salt-encrusted basalt beneath them, there are the pale things.

They are small. The largest is the length of your finger. The smallest is barely visible — a speck, a movement, a flicker of transparency against the white of the salt or the blue of the ice. They are multi-legged, but the number of legs varies — six, eight, ten, twelve — and the legs themselves are not jointed in any way that suggests a single body plan. They are not insects. They are not arachnids. They are not crustaceans. They are something that has arrived at a similar shape through a different door, a parallel evolution in a world where evolution has nearly stopped because there is nothing left to evolve *toward*.

Their bodies are transparent. Not translucent — *transparent*. You can see through them the way you can see through glass. Their internal structures are visible: a simple gut, a simple nerve cord, clusters of something that might be reproductive tissue, and a great deal of nothing — empty space, fluid-filled cavities, the geometry of a body that has no need to hide from predators because it is too small to see and too transparent to catch.

They move by jerking. This is the most notable thing about them. In a place where everything else moves slowly, smoothly, with the fluid deliberateness of geological process, the pale things move in sudden, mechanical snaps — a leg extends, the body shoots forward, a leg retracts, the body stops. It looks like stop-motion animation. It looks like something being operated by invisible strings. It looks, more than anything, like a clockwork toy that has been wound once and is running down, its movements becoming more erratic and more arrhythmic as its spring unwinds, until it stops entirely and sits motionless for a long time, and then starts again, without any visible trigger.

They are the fastest things on the plateau. This is not a high bar. A walking pace would be a sprint here. But the pale things can cross a mineral crust in the time it takes the film to grow a single cell-layer, and in the timescale of this place, that is practically instantaneous. They live in the condensation — they are creatures of the wet film, the thin liquid layer that forms on every surface during the heaviest damp hours. When the condensation thins, they retreat. When it thickens, they emerge. Their entire lives are governed by the moisture cycle, and they are born and die in the space between one drying and the next.

They eat the film. They eat the mineral crusts. They eat each other. They are eaten, in turn, by the herds — not deliberately, not as prey, but incidentally, the way a whale eats krill, the way a river eats silt. When a herd passes over a mineral pool, the pale things in the pool are consumed along with the crust and the film and the wet stone, and they are processed in the vast, slow guts of the herd-creatures, and their nutrients are extracted, and what remains is excreted, and it feeds the vents, and the vents feed the film, and the film feeds the pale things, and the cycle continues.

They have no name. They do not need one. They are too small to be seen from any distance, too simple to be distinguished from one another, and too numerous to be counted. If the plateau is a factory, the pale things are the smallest gears — the ones you would miss if you disassembled the machine and laid out every part, the ones whose function is not obvious until you realize that without them, the whole system grinds.

## The Herds

And then there are the herds.

You need to understand the numbers. Not the abstract numbers — *tens of thousands* is a phrase that slides past the mind without catching — but the *actual* numbers, the felt numbers, the numbers that press in on you from every horizon at once.

Stand on the plateau. Look in any direction. The horizon is far — the air, for all its dimness, is clear, and the plateau is flat enough that you can see for kilometers. Now imagine that in every direction, as far as you can see, the ground is covered with dark, moving shapes. Not covered. *Filled*. The basalt is visible only in the places where the herd has not yet reached or has already passed, and those places are shrinking and growing and shifting as the flow moves, like islands in a dark, slow river. The shapes are roughly dog-sized. They are too far away to see in detail, but you know — you can feel — that each one is a living thing, a body, a mouth, a set of legs, a set of scales, a pulse, a warmth. And there are not hundreds. There are not thousands. There are more than you can hold in your mind at once. The herd is a population. The herd is a *demographic event*. The herd is the largest single biological entity on the plateau, and possibly on the continent, and it does not know it is an entity. It does not know anything. It is just moving, and eating, and moving, and the movement is the thing.

Up close, a single herd-creature is a study in understatement.

Its body is long and low, built the way a reptile is built — close to the ground, legs splayed, belly nearly touching the stone. But it is not a reptile. Its covering is not scales in the reptilian sense but a kind of overlapping keratinous plate, flexible and dry, like the material of a fingernail or a hoof. Each plate is the color of the basalt — dark gray, nearly black, with a faint mottling that varies from individual to individual, though the variation is subtle enough that you would have to be very close to see it. The plates overlap like roof tiles, and they click against each other when the creature moves — a dry, faint, percussive sound, like a bag of shells being very slowly shaken. This clicking, multiplied by ten thousand, is the whisper of the herd.

The head is a wedge. It has no eyes. There are no eye sockets, no vestigial orbital structures, no trace of any organ that ever saw light. This is not a creature that lost its eyes because it lived in darkness. This is a creature whose ancestors never had eyes, because eyes were never useful, because there was never anything to see. The plateau has been dim for longer than this creature's lineage has existed. The plateau may have been dim since the first cell divided. The herd-creatures are creatures of touch and taste and chemical sense, and they have built their entire understanding of the world on those foundations, and their understanding is sufficient. They do not need to see. The stone tells them where it is. The moisture tells them where the vents are. The mineral crusts tell them where the food is. And the slow press of bodies against bodies tells them where the herd is, which is all that matters, because the herd is safety and the herd is direction and the herd is the world.

The mouth is a slit. It runs across the underside of the head, from one side to the other, and it opens not like a jaw — there is no jaw — but like a valve, a sphincter, a widening of a gap. When the creature feeds, it presses the underside of its head flat against the stone and opens this slit, and the mouth-parts within — a set of fine, flexible, chitinous scrapers, like the pages of a book made of fingernails — rasp against the mineral crust, breaking it loose, and the loosened material is drawn into the mouth by a pumping action of the throat. The sound is a soft, rhythmic tapping, and when a thousand creatures feed at once, it sounds like rain on a tin roof, but quieter, and slower, and without the urgency of rain.

They are warm. This is the thing that surprises you, if you are close enough to feel it — and almost nothing gets close enough to feel it, because the herds do not welcome strangers and the strangers do not want to be welcomed. But the creatures are warm. Their bodies produce heat. Not much — a degree or two above the ambient cold — but enough. Enough that the air immediately above a dense section of the herd is slightly warmer than the air above the empty stone, and this warmth rises, and it creates a faint thermal signature that you could detect with the right instruments, a plume of slightly-less-cold air rising from the flow like breath from a sleeping animal. The herd is a warm body. The herd is a single warm body, and it breathes, in its way, and it is warmer than the stone it walks on.

They do not sleep. Or rather, they do not sleep in any way that would be recognizable. The movement never stops, but individuals within the herd slow down, drift to the edges, become still for hours at a time, their bodies pressed against the stone, their metabolic rate dropping to something barely above maintenance. Then they rouse, and they rejoin the flow, and they move, and they feed, and they are indistinguishable from the rest. There is no nest. There is no den. There is no home. The herd is the home. The movement is the home.

They reproduce. How, exactly, is unclear — they have been observed in what appear to be pairing configurations, two bodies pressed close together in the dense middle of the flow, moving in synchrony for hours, but the actual mechanism is hidden beneath the overlapping plates, and no one has watched closely enough to describe it. What is clear is that there are young. Small versions of the adults — half the length, thinner, with paler plates that darken within a few weeks — appear in the herd, usually in clusters, usually after the rare rains that fill the pools and cause the mineral crusts to thicken. They are born into the flow. They have never known anything but the flow. They will die in the flow. And when they die, their bodies sink, and the herd passes over them, and the plates are scattered, and the soft tissues are consumed by the film and the pale things, and the minerals return to the stone, and the cycle continues.

The herd does not know it is a herd. Each individual knows only the body in front of it, the body beside it, the chemical trace on the stone beneath it, the pull of the current it is part of. But the herd *behaves* — it flows around obstacles, it pools in feeding grounds, it thins and accelerates in narrow channels, it splits and rejoins, it has a coherence and a direction that no individual imparts. The herd is an emergent thing. It is a pattern that arises from the interaction of ten thousand simple impulses, and it is more intelligent than any of its components, and it is not intelligent at all, and these are not contradictions. The river does not know where the sea is. The river arrives at the sea anyway.

Where does the herd go? This is the question that haunts any attempt to understand the plateau. The herd moves, always. It moves in a direction that changes over the scale of months — a slow drift, a rotation, as though the entire flow is circling the plateau in a circuit so vast that no individual will complete it in its lifetime. Or perhaps the herd is not circling. Perhaps it is following the mineral crusts, which form and dissolve and reform in patterns that shift over decades, responding to changes in the vents' output and the moisture cycle. Perhaps the herd is chasing its own food, and the food moves, and the herd follows, and the food reforms where the herd has been, and the herd returns, and it is a cycle within a cycle within a cycle.

Or perhaps the herd simply moves because movement is what it does. Perhaps the motion is not a response to anything. Perhaps it is a property — like the warmth, like the clicking, like the silence — a thing the herd *is* rather than a thing the herd *does*. Perhaps the herd is a current, and currents move, and the reason is not a reason but a nature.

## The Tall Ones

At the edges. At the limit of what you can see in the dim, pewter air. At the place where the herd thins and the stone stretches empty toward the cold horizon.

There are the tall ones.

You see them rarely. A shape. A movement that is different from the movement of the herd — slower, more vertical, more deliberate. The herd flows. The tall ones *walk*. They lift their bodies — whatever their bodies are — and they set them down, and they lift them again, and the gait is stilted, careful, considered, as though each step is a decision rather than a reflex. They are tall. They are thin. The air at the edges of perception is too thick with dimness to make out details, but the silhouette is unmistakable: a vertical form, narrow, with a long, slow stride, moving alone across the empty basalt.

There are never many. One, perhaps, at a time. Or two, separated by kilometers of distance, each walking its own circuit. They do not approach the herd. They do not approach the vents. They do not approach each other. They are the most solitary things on the plateau, and possibly the most solitary things in the world.

What are they? The honest answer is that no one knows. They might be the same species as the herd-creatures — a late-stage morph, a final form, something that the scaled herders become after a lifetime of eating and moving and pressing against the stone. Perhaps the herders grow. Perhaps, at some threshold of age or size or accumulated mineral intake, the body changes — the plates harden, the legs lengthen, the head lifts, the belly leaves the ground, the creature rises, and it walks away from the herd and into the empty margins of the plateau and is never part of the flow again. Perhaps the tall ones are what the herd-creatures become when they have eaten enough mineral and microbial matter to tip some internal balance, to trigger a transformation, to graduate from the current and into the stillness.

Or perhaps they are something else entirely. A different lineage. A different response to the same environment. A creature that shares the plateau's resources but not its strategy — a solitary feeder, a wanderer, a thing that does not need the herd because it has found another way to be. Perhaps they eat something the herd does not. Perhaps they eat the rust crusts, or the ice, or the mineral mounds that have built up around the oldest vents, or the stone itself. Perhaps they are the plateau's final product — its apex, its endpoint, the thing the engine produces when it has been running long enough to produce something that no longer needs the engine.

They do not glow. They do not click. They do not whisper on the stone. They move in near-perfect silence, their steps so slow and so deliberate that they make no sound at all, and if you were not watching the horizon at the exact moment one passed through your field of view, you would never know it had been there. They are the ghosts of the plateau. They are its largest inhabitants and its least visible, and they are the only things here that move as though they have somewhere to be.

But where? And why? The questions hang in the cold, thin air and are not answered and do not expect to be answered. The tall ones walk. They have always walked. They will always walk. And the herd flows, and the vents breathe, and the film glows its faint rust-colored glow, and the pale things jerk through their tiny, mechanical lives, and the rust crusts add another molecule to their ten-thousand-year edges, and the mineral rings grow one shade whiter.

---

This is who lives here. Not a community — a *system*. Not a collection of organisms — a single process, expressed in a dozen forms, each one a working part of a machine that no one built and no one runs and no one will ever shut off. The film is the lining. The crusts are the insulation. The pale things are the bearings. The herds are the working fluid. The tall ones are — perhaps — the exhaust. Or perhaps they are the product. Or perhaps they are the next thing, the thing that comes after the engine has run long enough to make something that can walk away from it.

They are all of them alive. They are all of them part of the same life. They are all of them the plateau, and the plateau is them, and the distinction between the living and the non-living here is so thin and so theoretical that it dissolves if you look at it for too long. The stone breathes through the vents. The stone grows through the crusts. The stone moves through the herds. The stone walks, tall and slow, at the edge of the world.

The plateau lives. It does not know it lives. It does not need to know.
