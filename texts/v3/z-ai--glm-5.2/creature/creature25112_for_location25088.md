# What Lives in the Millwold

---

The first thing to understand is that the Millwold does not have inhabitants. It has components.

This sounds like a distinction without a difference, but standing on the root-mat with the pulse in the walls pressing against your sternum, you understand it in your body before you understand it in your words. An inhabitant lives *in* a place. A component lives *as* a place. The factory-bodies are not in the Millwold. They are the Millwold, and everything else here is either part of them, derived from them, or feeding on them.

---

## The Colony

The dominant life — and it is deeply misleading to call it "life" in the singular, but it is also misleading to call it "lives" in the plural, and the uncertainty of that grammatical choice is itself a kind of information — is the colony.

It is one organism. Or it is many. The root-cable grid connects every factory-body, every node, every processing chamber, every growing edge. Sap flows through the whole network. Chemical signals move through it — you know this because you have watched a section of wall split open and, within the hour, seen a pale fluid weep from the edges of the split that smells different from the amber concentrate in the distillation channels. Something has been communicated. Something has been routed to this location in response to a need that the colony detected in a way you cannot observe.

But the factory-bodies do not all pulse at the same rate. They do not all grow in the same direction. They die independently. When a factory-body on the western edge collapses, the ones nearest to it do not slow or change rhythm. The nutrient flow reroutes around the dead structure with what appears to be indifference. The colony does not mourn its components. It absorbs them.

So: one organism, or many. The colony behaves like a single body at the level of resource distribution and like a population at the level of individual structures. You do not resolve this contradiction. You do not think it is resolvable. You think it is simply the nature of the thing, and the nature of the thing has been doing what it does for longer than you can meaningfully estimate. The root-mat is thousands of layers deep. Each layer is a body. The Millwold is a graveyard that is also a factory that is also a living thing, and it has been all three simultaneously for so long that the distinction between those states has worn away like the channels in the lanes.

What does it want? This is a question you ask yourself while standing in the processing chamber, watching condensation form on root-hair mats. The answer appears to be: nothing. It wants nothing. It is not growing toward something. It is not shrinking away from something. It is not responding to a threat or pursuing a goal. The turbulence — the constant reorganization, the opening and collapsing and rerouting — is not progress. It is metabolism. The Millwold breathes in the way that a heart beats: not because it has somewhere to go, but because stopping is not among the options available to it. The system operates because the system operates because the system operates.

This is the most disturbing thing about it, in the end. Not the scale. Not the silence. Not the way the pulse gets into your chest and confuses itself with your own heartbeat. The most disturbing thing is that the Millwold is not *for* anything. It is a machine with no product. A factory with no output. The concentrate that the processing chambers produce is pumped back into the colony to grow more colony, which builds more processing chambers, which produce more concentrate. It is a closed loop. It is a perpetual motion machine that does no work. It eats itself and grows and eats its own growth and grows again, and the plateau rises millimeter by millimeter with the compressed corpses of its own previous iterations, and nothing comes of it. Nothing is supposed to come of it.

You stand on the mat and feel the give under your boots and you know that beneath you are a thousand generations of factory-bodies that lived and pulsed and split and collapsed and were absorbed, and none of them meant anything, and the ones above you will not mean anything either, and the system does not care, and the system does not know that it does not care, and the system does not know that it does not know.

## The Scavengers

The pale thing you saw in the lane is real. You see another one on the second day, closer this time, and you get a better look.

It is not a mammal. It has no fur, but it also has no scales, no feathers, no visible skin texture at all. Its surface is smooth and slightly translucent, like wet parchment, and you can see faint branching lines beneath it that might be veins or might be something else. Its body is low-slung and narrow, built to move between root-cables in the lanes, and its legs are indeed piston-like — not jointed in the way an insect's legs are jointed, but telescoping, each segment sliding into the next, extending and contracting with a mechanical precision that looks engineered rather than evolved. The feet — if they are feet — are flat pads that grip the root-mat without compressing it. You watch one walk across a section of substrate that is damp and springy and it leaves no impression. None. The mat does not record its passage.

Its head is long and narrow and the snout — you keep calling it a snout, but it is more like a probe, a sensory extension, flexible, covered in fine hairs that are the same translucent white as the rest of it — sweeps the ground ahead of it in small lateral movements as it walks. It is tasting the substrate. Or smelling it. Or doing something for which you have no name, something between tasting and smelling and some third sense that detects chemical gradients at a distance.

There are more than you first thought. Over the course of a day, you see six, maybe seven. They are always alone. They never interact. They move toward collapse sites — the places where factory-bodies are folding inward, dying, becoming substrate — and they feed on the exposed material at the margins. Not the living bark. Not the functioning cables. The dying tissue, the tissue that is in the process of becoming something else. They feed at the boundary between what the colony is and what it is becoming. They eat transition.

They are not parasites. You think about this for a long time. A parasite takes from a host without giving back. These creatures take dying tissue, yes, but dying tissue is not useful to the colony. It is material that is being discarded, being released, being prepared for compression and absorption. The scavengers take it before it can be absorbed. They are, in a sense, competitors with the root-mat's decomposition process. But they are also — you watch one feed at a collapse site and then move on, and the site where it fed is clean, the dead bark stripped away to reveal the substrate beneath, and the substrate is already being colonized by fine new root-hairs from the adjacent cables — they are also preparing the ground. They clear the dead material faster than the colony would on its own. They expose the substrate. They accelerate the cycle.

Symbionts, then. Or something like symbionts. The colony feeds them, and they serve the colony's metabolism by stripping its dead, and neither of them knows this, and neither of them needs to.

How many are there? You cannot say. The plateau is several kilometers across. You see six or seven in your immediate vicinity over a day. Extrapolating is meaningless — you do not know their range, their density, their lifespan. But you think there are not many. You think the colony could function without them, and did for a long time before they existed, if they evolved here. Or maybe they were always here. Maybe they are as old as the colony. Maybe they are part of the original system, a component so deep and so embedded that the colony has grown around them the way a tree grows around a fence post, incorporating without acknowledging.

One of them passes within five meters of you. It does not look at you. Its snout sweeps the ground. Its piston-legs extend and contract. It moves like something that has been walking these lanes for a very long time, and it has never once had reason to stop.

## The Bird

You see it twice more. Always high, always crossing the grey sky in a straight line, always silent, always disappearing behind the ridgeline. It does not land. You never see it perch on a factory-body or circle above the plateau. It crosses. It goes.

The ridgelines are bitten flat on top. You noticed this when you came through the pass. The flatness is not geological. It is too even, too deliberate. The ridgelines look like they have been cropped. Sheared. As if something comes through periodically and cuts the peaks down to a uniform height.

You think about the bird. You think about the ridgelines. You think about the flat-topped peaks and the straight-flying silent shape that crosses the sky and disappears behind them, and you do not know what to do with this, and you file it alongside the grammatical problem of one organism or many, alongside the question of what the colony is for, alongside the silence that is not absence but presence, and you leave it there. Some things in the Millwold are not for understanding. They are for noticing and carrying and not resolving.

---

## What Else

There are insects. You find them by accident, kneeling to examine a node where two cables join, and you see movement in the gap between the bark and the cable — tiny things, no larger than a grain of rice, pale like the scavengers, with too many legs and no visible eyes. They live in the seams. They live in the spaces between components. There are thousands of them in every node, every junction, every split in the bark. They do not appear to do anything. They do not appear to eat anything. They exist in the interstices of the colony the way mites exist in the interstices of human skin, and they may serve a function or they may simply be there, tolerated, too small to matter, too embedded to remove.

There is something in the air. Not an organism, or not an organism you can see, but a presence. The moisture that suspends in the air column — the fine invisible water that beads on every surface — carries something in it. You taste it on your lips. It is not the tannin-and-rust of the concentrate. It is something else. Something faintly sweet, faintly metallic, like blood diluted in cold water. You think it is the colony's atmosphere. Its breath. The exhalation of ten thousand processing chambers, distributed through the root-mat, released through the bark, suspended in the still, windless air. You are breathing it. You have been breathing it since you came through the pass. It is in your lungs. You do not know what it does. You do not know if it does anything. But you notice, on the second day, that you have stopped shivering. The cold is still here — your breath still smokes — but you do not feel it. The cold is in you and you are in the cold and the distinction has softened.

You leave. You go back through the pass, and the wind hits you like a wall, and the sound of the wind is enormous, unbearable, and you realize that in the Millwold you had adjusted to the silence so completely that any sound at all feels like violence. You stand in the wind on the other side of the pass and you feel your own pulse, and for a moment — just a moment — you cannot tell whether it is yours or the walls', and then the moment passes, and you walk down, and the Millwold is behind you, and it is still there, and it does not know you have left, and it does not care, and the system operates, and the system operates, and the system operates.
