# What Lives in Condia

---

## First, the smallest things

Begin with the layer you cannot see but cannot escape. The microbial film that coats the submerged stone in the deep levels — not algae, not bacteria exactly, not anything that maps cleanly onto the categories visitors arrive with. It is a film. A scum. A presence. It grows on the basalt blocks of the original settlement where they sit beneath warm water in the flooded basement rooms thirty meters below the current streets. It is thin, pale, faintly iridescent in the way that oil on water is iridescent, though it is not oil. It has no common name because the people of Condia do not name things that they cannot eat, cannot feed to something they can eat, or cannot use. It is simply the stuff on the stone down there, and it has been there longer than the wells, and it feeds the pale salamanders, and that is the end of its story.

Except that it isn't. Because the film extends upward in ways nobody has catalogued. It lives inside the pipes — the old iron ones, especially, where the water moves slowly and the mineral content is high. It lives in the drainage channels where the flow is thin enough to permit settling. It lives in the warm, wet interiors of the ventilation shafts that connect the factories to the outside air. It is the base of everything, the first layer of life, and it is so thoroughly distributed through the city's wet infrastructure that if you were to sterilize it all tomorrow, the entire animal population above it would collapse within a season. The birds would thin. The insects would fail. The rats would starve. The cats would go hunting in districts where there is nothing left to hunt.

This is the foundation. Not the basalt. The film.

---

## The insects

Above the film, in the air that humans breathe, the insects are so numerous that they constitute a weather system.

They are not one species. This is important to understand, because a visitor's first instinct is to call them all midges and be done with it. They are not all midges. There are at least four distinct populations that a patient observer can separate by behavior alone, without any need for magnification.

The first are the true midges — tiny, almost weightless, existing in clouds so dense they are visible as texture in the air. They breed in the standing water that collects in every depression, every roof gutter, every blocked drainage channel that hasn't been cleared this season. They do not bite. They exist to breed and to die and to feed the birds. Their lifespan is a matter of hours. They are so small that the city's air currents — the drafts created by the temperature differential between the industrial core and the cooler outer districts — carry them in columns and eddies that look, from a distance, like faint smoke rising from the streets. But it is not smoke. It is life, in quantities that the word "abundance" fails to capture.

The second are the gnats. Larger than the midges, slower, more deliberate. They hover in tight formations near the drainage channels, where the mineral-rich water provides whatever it is they need for their reproductive cycle. They bite, but lightly — a prick, an itch, something you stop noticing after the first week. The locals have skin that has thickened in response, a subcutaneous toughness that is not visible but that visitors remark on when they shake hands with a Condian and feel the difference. The gnats are the primary food of the building-dwelling birds, the ones that nest in the eaves and ventilation shafts, and their population tracks the drainage flow — more water, more gnats, more birds, a simple equation that has been balancing itself for centuries.

The third are the hard-shelled things. Beetles, though they are not beetles in the way a naturalist would recognize — they are dark, flat, wide-bodied, with wing cases that have fused into a single carapace that sheds water like oiled leather. They crawl. They do not fly, or if they can, they choose not to. They live in the warm zones — near the steam pipes, in the factory spaces, in the gaps between machines where the heat collects. They eat the microbial film where it dries on warm surfaces, and they eat the guano that accumulates below the bird nests, and they eat each other when the opportunity presents itself. They are the city's custodians, in a sense. They process the waste that the other populations generate. They are also, not incidentally, the primary food of the rats.

The fourth are the fliers — dark, quick, with long wings and narrow bodies, that hunt the midges and gnats on the wing. They are predators. They are the reason the midge clouds stay in motion rather than settling. They are also the reason the birds hunt at dawn and dusk rather than throughout the day — the fliers are crepuscular, and in the dim hours they are so numerous that a bird hunting them would be competing with its own prey's predators, which is inefficient. The fliers roost during the day in the upper reaches of the buildings, above the fog line on the rare days the fog thins enough to have a line, and they hang there in clusters that look like dark fruit.

These four populations, plus a dozen smaller ones that a specialist might distinguish, constitute the insect life of Condia. They are the air's fauna. You breathe them in. You find them in your food if you eat outdoors. You find them in the condensation that drips from the pipes. You find them in your clothing, in the folds and seams, having landed there for rest or for warmth and been trapped. The Condians have a word — *veilka* — that translates approximately as "the insect weather," and they use it the way other cultures use words for rain or wind. *Veilka* is thick today. The *veilka* is moving east. The *veilka* will be bad this season. It is a condition of the environment, not an infestation. It is not something to be solved. It is something to be lived in.

## The rats

The rats of Condia are not the rats of other cities. This is not sentimentality. They have diverged, and the divergence is physical, behavioral, and, the few times anyone has checked, digestive.

The sewer rats — the channel-runners — are the first you notice. They are dark. Not the brown of a common rat, but a true dark, almost black, with fur that is slicked flat by constant moisture and that sheds water the way the beetles' carapaces do. They are large — a half-kilo is common, a full kilo is not unusual, and the old ones, the ones that have survived three or four years in the channels, can be larger. They swim. They swim well, with a sinuous, unhurried motion that carries them through the drainage flows as easily as it carries them through the standing water in the flooded lower levels. They have webbed toes — not fully webbed, but with a membrane that extends a quarter of the way down each toe, enough to function as a paddle. Their tails are thick, almost muscular, and they use them for stability in the current in a way that suggests a semi-aquatic adaptation that has had time to deepen.

They run the channels in groups. Not packs — groups. There is structure, but it is not hierarchical in the way a pack is. It is territorial. A group owns a stretch of channel, a set of junctions, a particular drainage basin, and they patrol it with a regularity that the Condians have learned to predict. The maintenance workers who clear the channels know which groups will be where at which hours, and they work around them, not because the rats are dangerous — they are not, particularly, though they will defend themselves if cornered — but because the groups are part of the drainage system. They eat the organic matter that accumulates in the channels. They eat the beetles. They eat the microbial film where it is accessible. They eat, and this is the part that visiting biologists have the most difficulty with, the mineral compounds that precipitate out of the drainage water — the calcium carbonate, the iron oxides, the sulfur residues. Their guts process these. Their droppings are not the dark pellets of a normal rat; they are varicolored, streaked with the mineral content of what they have consumed, and they accumulate in the channel junctions where the groups rest, forming deposits that are, over time, almost indistinguishable from the natural travertine that the water itself deposits. The rats are building the city's geology. They are part of the mineral cycle. They are, in a very literal sense, part of the infrastructure.

The building rats are different. Paler — a gray that in some individuals approaches white, particularly in the deep interior spaces where light never reaches. Smaller. More agile. Their toes are not webbed. Their tails are thin and prehensile enough to be useful in climbing. They live in the walls, in the ceilings, in the spaces between floors, in the hollow cores of old pipes that have been decommissioned and left in place because removing them would compromise the structural integrity of the walls they run through. They are arboreal, in a sense — the city is their canopy, and they move through it with the same fluid certainty that a squirrel moves through a forest, leaping from beam to beam, running along pipe exteriors, dropping from ceiling gaps to floor gaps with a precision that implies a detailed internal map of the building they inhabit.

They eat what the buildings produce. Guano from the bird nests. Insects that have wandered indoors. Organic waste from the human population — the scraps, the leavings, the things that fall between the floorboards or are swept into the gaps where the walls meet the ceiling and forgotten. They also eat the building materials themselves, or rather, they eat the organic components of the older construction — the mortars of the middle layers that contained organic binders, the wooden structural elements of the older buildings that have been absorbed into newer construction. They are, in their own way, consuming the city's history. A building rat nest in the wall of a middle-layer building will often be constructed in a hollow that the rats have created by eating through the mortar of a joint that was laid three hundred years ago. They live inside the city's past tense.

The two populations meet at the boundaries — the ground-floor levels, the street-level entries to the drainage channels, the basement accesses where the building interiors connect to the subterranean waterlogged zones. They do not interbreed, or if they do, the offspring are not viable, or do not survive, or are absorbed into one population or the other without leaving a trace. The boundary between them is sharp. It has been sharp for a long time. It will remain sharp. They are two species now, or nearly so, and the city is large enough for both.

There are other rats. Rumors of them. Things in the deepest levels, below the salamander zones, in the original basalt rooms that are not flooded but are damp and dark and warm and have been sealed off by the accumulation of construction above them. The few people who have been down there — the well-maintenance crews, the structural surveyors — report sounds. Scratching. Movement. Something that is rat-like but larger, slower, and that does not flee from light the way the upper populations do. These reports are not investigated. There is no practical reason to go that deep. The wells draw from above that level. The foundations are stable. What lives down there lives down there, and the city above it continues.

## The birds

If the rats are the city's circulatory system, the birds are its breath.

They are small. The largest are the size of a human fist. The smallest are the size of a thumb. They are dark-feathered — not black, but a deep, wet-looking brown that in certain lights has a greenish or bluish sheen, the same sheen that oil on water has, the same sheen the microbial film has. This is not a coincidence. The sheen comes from the same compounds in their diet — the mineral traces that pass through the insects they eat, through the water the insects breed in, through the pipes and channels and condensation that is the city's blood. The birds of Condia are, physically, made partly of the city. Their feathers carry its chemistry. Their bones carry its calcium. They are, in a molecular sense, local.

They nest everywhere. The chimneys are the primary site — even the cold ones, the ones that haven't vented in decades, are occupied, because the interior spaces are warm (the stone retains heat from when the chimney was active) and dry (relatively — drier than the streets, drier than the walls) and vertical (the birds prefer vertical nesting sites, where they can drop into flight rather than launch from a horizontal surface). A single inactive chimney can house a hundred nests, stacked vertically, each one a cup of mineral-stained fibrous material — the birds use the filaments from old pipe insulation, the fluff from deteriorating polymer sleeves, the strands of corroded metal mesh that they pull from the walls, even the hair that the human population sheds, which accumulates in the drainage grates and is carried aloft by the birds in their beaks. The nests are composite structures. They are, in miniature, a version of the city itself — built from the materials of the city, layered over themselves, each generation building on the last.

The eaves are the second site. Every horizontal surface that is sheltered from the rain — every ledge, every overhang, every pipe that runs along a wall face at a height where birds can reach — is occupied. The droppings below these sites are the white streaking that covers the city's walls. The guano is not merely a nuisance. It is a substrate. The beetles live in it. The building rats eat it. The microbial film grows on it where it washes into the drainage channels. It is a nutrient input in a city that has almost no organic nutrient inputs. The birds are importing nothing — they are feeding on the insects that are feeding on the film that is feeding on the stone — but they are concentrating. They take the diffuse biological output of the city's wet surfaces and compress it into a form that other organisms can use. They are the city's digestive system. They make the nutrients available.

At dawn and dusk, they fly. This is the sound that fills the sky — the tearing-cloth sound, amplified. It is not a song. These birds do not sing. They call — a short, sharp, clicking sound that is used for contact, for territory, for the maintenance of flock structure. When tens of thousands of them are calling simultaneously, the individual clicks merge into a continuous textured sound, like static, like the sound of a river heard from a distance, like fabric being torn very slowly. It is this sound that is the city's dawn and dusk. It is as reliable as the rain. It is as much a part of the place as the fog.

They do not migrate. They do not leave. The fog is permanent, and so are they. Their world is the city and the air above it and the insects within that air, and they have no reason to go elsewhere because there is no elsewhere that is like this. They are endemic in the fullest sense — not just native, but exclusive. They exist here and, as far as anyone has been able to determine, only here. If the city ended, they would end. They know this, in the way that a species knows anything — not consciously, but structurally. They are built for this place and no other. Their feathers shed this specific water. Their guts process these specific insects. Their nests are made of these specific materials. They are Condians. They just don't know the word.

## The cats

The cats are the thing that visitors from drier, colder, more temperate places find most unsettling. Not because the cats are strange — cats are cats, everywhere — but because these cats are so clearly, so specifically, so *thoroughly* cats, and yet they are not cats as the visitor knows them. They are cats that have been rained on for a thousand years. They are cats that have never been dry. They are cats that have evolved in a niche that no other cat population has ever occupied, and they have done so without ceasing to be cats, and that is what is unsettling. You look at one and you see a cat. You look again and you see something else.

They are large. Not fat — large. Long-bodied, long-legged, with a frame that suggests a weasel's reach combined with a cat's mass. They are wet. Their fur is not the fur of a cat that has been caught in the rain; it is the fur of an animal that is aquatic-adjacent, that sheds water in sheets rather than absorbing it, that dries not by evaporation (there is no dry air here to evaporate into) but by shaking, by pressing against warm surfaces, by retreating to the warm zones above the steam pipes where the residual heat is enough to drive the moisture out of the outer layer of their coat while the undercoat remains damp. They are never fully dry. They do not need to be.

Their coloration is varied — gray, tabby, tortoiseshell, occasionally a deep rust that might be the mineral content of their water supply staining their fur over time. The rust-colored ones are the oldest. The gray ones are the youngest. This is not a coincidence. The mineral staining accumulates with age, and the cats that live longest in the drainage-adjacent zones pick up the most iron oxide. You can estimate a cat's age and territory by the color of its fur. The locals do this without thinking about it. A rust-colored cat is a channel-edge veteran. A pale gray cat is a young one from the upper stories. A tortoiseshell is from the mid-levels, the transition zone, where the building rats and the sewer rats overlap and the hunting is best.

They hunt rats. This is their primary function in the ecosystem, and they perform it with a skill that is the result of very long practice. A Condian cat can take a sewer rat from a drainage channel — it waits at the channel edge, motionless, its fur slicked flat, its eyes fixed on the water, and when a rat swims past, it strikes with a paw into the water and flips the rat onto the bank, where it is pinned and killed before it can right itself. This is a learned technique. The kittens are taught it. They practice on the beetles first, then on the smaller rats, then on the channel-runners. A cat that has mastered the channel strike is a cat that will live a long time. The ones that never learn it hunt the building rats in the upper stories and live shorter, leaner lives.

They are not social. They are not solitary. They are territorial in a way that is dense and complex — each cat holds a territory that is defined not by geography but by resource, and the territories overlap because the resources overlap. A cat that owns the drainage channel at a particular junction also owns the roof above it and the ventilation shaft that connects them, and it defends all three against other cats while tolerating the presence of cats in adjacent territories that do not compete for the same resources. The result is a spatial arrangement of cats that looks, from above, like a mosaic — each piece a territory, each piece a different shape, each piece defined by the infrastructure it encompasses. The cats do not own the city. They own the city's pipes.

They do not meow. This is perhaps the most jarring thing for a visitor. They are silent, or nearly so — they communicate with body posture, with eye contact, with the position of the ears and the tail, and when they do vocalize, it is a low, breathy sound that is more exhale than voice. They have no reason to meow. There are no humans they need to communicate with. They are not domesticated, not tame, not affiliated with the human population in any way. They are a parallel population. They live in the same building, on different floors. They have been here as long as the humans have been here, and they will be here after the humans leave, if the humans ever leave. They do not need the humans. They need the rats. The rats need the drainage. The drainage needs the city. The city needs the humans. The humans do not need the cats. This is the one break in the chain of dependence, and it is the reason the cats are the most independent thing in Condia. They are the one population that would survive the collapse of everything above them, because the rats would survive, and the drainage would survive, and the stone would survive, and the cats would hunt in the ruins and sleep in the warm rubble and never know that anything had changed.

## The salamanders

Down.

Past the current street grade. Past the first basement level, the one the Condians use for storage, where the walls are limestone and the floors are dry and the air is merely damp. Past the second basement level, where the limestone gives way to the older brick and the floors are wet and the air is warm and the pipes are the oldest pipes, the cast iron ones that have been leaking so long that the leaks are part of the architecture. Past the third level, where the brick gives way to the original construction and the original construction is basalt and the basalt is underwater and the water is warm and mineral-dark and still.

Here. The flooded rooms of the first city. The rooms where people lived before Condia was Condia, when it was a settlement on a plateau and the fog was the same fog and the water was the same water and the basalt blocks were freshly laid. Those rooms are submerged now. The water table has risen, or the city has sunk — the distinction is meaningless here, where the ground and the water have been negotiating for so long that the boundary between them is a gradient rather than a line. The rooms are waterlogged. The ceilings are low. The doorways connect them in sequences that are, to the things that live here, hallways and corridors and galleries, the architecture of a drowned city.

The salamanders are in the water. They are in every room that is connected to every other room by a doorway that is submerged enough to swim through, which is all of them. They are pale — a white that is not albino-white but something softer, a color that is the absence of color because there is no light here to produce color and no reason to produce pigment. Their skin is thin. At the edges — the margins of the limbs, the tips of the tails, the gill structures that fan outward from the neck — it is translucent, and the internal structures are visible not as details but as shadows, as shapes within shapes, the organs and the vessels and the skeletal elements a ghost-anatomy that you can see if you hold a light to them, which nobody does, because nobody comes down here except the well-maintenance crews, and the well-maintenance crews do not bring lights for the salamanders. They bring lights for the wells.

The salamanders are large. The smallest are the length of a hand. The largest are the length of a forearm, from elbow to fingertip, and they are the old ones — slow, heavy with their own mass, their bodies so full of the mineral content of the water that their bones are dense and their movements are deliberate. They swim with a lateral undulation that is primitive and efficient, the same motion their ancestors used, however many millions of years ago, in whatever water they first lived in. They have not changed because they have not needed to. The water here has been the same temperature, the same chemistry, the same darkness for the entire time the city has existed above them, and the city above them has not affected them. They are the one thing in Condia that is indifferent to Condia. The city could be a forest. The city could be a desert. The salamanders would not know. They would not care. They would swim in their rooms and eat their film and breed in their water and be exactly what they are.

They eat the microbial film. They graze on it, their mouths scraping the stone surfaces in a motion that is like and unlike a fish feeding on algae — like, in the mechanical action of the mouth against the surface; unlike, in that the film is not algae and the mouth is not a fish's mouth. It is a salamander's mouth, and the film is the film, and the relationship between them is one of the oldest relationships in the city. Older than the rats. Older than the birds. Older than the cats, the insects, the humans. The film and the salamanders have been here since before the basalt was laid. They are the original residents. Everything else is a later arrival.

They breed in the water. The females lay eggs — clusters of them, attached to the basalt walls with a secretion that mineralizes almost immediately, cementing the eggs to the stone in a matrix that looks like a geological deposit. The eggs are pale, like the salamanders, and they develop slowly, in the warm water, over weeks, into larvae that are smaller versions of the adults but with more pronounced gills — external, feathery, fanning outward like the structures of something from a much earlier era of life. The larvae swim for months before metamorphosing. During the larval stage, they are the most vulnerable — the beetles, which have somehow reached these levels, will take them, and the larger salamanders will take them, cannibalism being not a pathology here but a routine, a way of recycling the population's biomass without losing it to the outside world. Nothing leaves these levels. The salamanders are a closed system. They eat what they produce. They produce what they eat. The mineral content of the water sustains the film, the film sustains the salamanders, the salamanders sustain the film with their waste, and the cycle has been running, unbroken, for longer than the city.

This is the deepest life. Not the deepest layer — the basalt goes deeper, and below the basalt is the stone of the plateau itself, and below that is geology — but the deepest life. Below this, there is nothing alive. The well shafts go deeper, but they are shafts, not rooms, and the water in them is too mineral-concentrated even for the film. The deep wells draw water that is, in a biological sense, dead. It is stone water. It tastes of stone. The Condians drink it.

## The humans

And then the humans, who are everywhere and nowhere, who are the reason the city exists and the reason the animals live in it and the reason the pipes run and the factories hum and the drainage channels carry water and the fog is penetrated by light at all, because without the humans there would be no electricity and without electricity there would be no factory lights and without factory lights there would be no lights at all because the fog eats daylight and the world would be dark — the humans, who live in the wet and the noise and the animal-thick air and have lived here for so long that they are no longer separable from it.

The Condians are not tall. This is the first thing. The average height is below the average height of the populations of the drier, lower-elevation settlements that trade with Condia, and the difference is consistent enough across generations that it is clearly adaptive — the ceilings are low, the streets are narrow, the doorways are built for a population that is compact. The Condians are also broad. Not fat — broad, with a skeletal frame that is wide across the shoulders and wide through the chest, a body built for the work the city requires, which is maintenance work, which is carrying work, which is the constant physical effort of keeping a centuries-old infrastructure functioning in an environment that is trying to dissolve it. TheCondians are built for their city the way the birds are built for their chimneys. Their bodies are local.

Their skin is dark. Not the dark of a sunny climate — the dark of a foggy one, which sounds contradictory but is not. The Condians' skin has responded to the absence of direct sunlight not by lightening but by deepening, a response to the constant moisture and the mineral content of the water they bathe in, which stains over time. The youngest Condians have medium-brown skin. The oldest have skin that is almost black, with the same mineral sheen that the birds' feathers have, the same iron-oxide undertone, the same sense that the city has entered them through their pores. They are, in a literal and measurable way, made of the place they live in. Their hair holds the mineral traces. Their fingernails carry the deposits. The older Condians have a faint roughness to their skin, a texture that is the beginning of the same mineral crusting that forms on the drainage channels, as though their bodies are being slowly incorporated into the city's geology. They are not decaying. They are accreting. There is a difference.

They eat what the city can produce, which is not much. The plateau is sterile — no agriculture, no grazing, no plant life of any kind. Food comes from below — from the lower elevations, where the fog clears and the soil supports growth, traded up the plateau roads in convoys of carts and pack animals. The food is supplemented by what the city itself offers, which is meat. The birds are taken — not the living birds, but the nests, which are harvested at intervals by workers who climb the chimneys with ropes and poles and collect the eggs and the fledglings that are not yet able to fly. This is a specific profession, *nest-catcher*, and it is one of the older professions in the city, older than the factory work, older than the pipe maintenance. The nest-catchers know the chimneys the way the cats know the drainage channels — which ones are occupied, which ones are ready for harvest, which ones have been harvested too recently and need to be left for another season. They take a fraction. They leave the rest. The system has been balanced for as long as anyone can remember.

The rats are not eaten. This is a specific taboo, and it is interesting because it is not a universal taboo — visitors from the lower elevations sometimes eat the rats, and the Condians do not prevent them, but they do not participate. The reason, as near as anyone has been able to determine, is that the rats are part of the infrastructure. They are part of the drainage system, part of the mineral cycle, part of the mechanism that keeps the city functioning. Eating them would be like eating a pipe. It would be a category error. The Condians distinguish between food and infrastructure with a precision that is cultural rather than logical, and the rats fall on the infrastructure side of the line. The birds fall on the food side. The insects fall on neither side — they are environment, like the fog. The cats fall on the infrastructure side, like the rats. The salamanders fall on no side, because most Condians have never seen one and are only vaguely aware that they exist. The salamanders are in the part of the city that the Condians do not inhabit. They are in the past.

The Condians work. This is the defining fact of their daily existence — they work, and the work is maintenance, and the maintenance is endless. The pipes leak. The pipes have always leaked. The pipes will always leak, because the water and the mineral content and the temperature and the age of the system make it impossible for any seal to hold permanently. The pipe-maintenance crews are the largest workforce in the city. They work in shifts, around the clock, replacing sections of pipe, patching joints, redirecting flows, adjusting pressures. They wear heavy gloves and waterproof aprons and they carry tools that are specific to the trade — wrenches with heads designed for the older iron fittings, sleeves of polymer wrap for the newer lines, sealants that are formulated for the specific chemistry of the water they will be exposed to. The pipe-maintainers know the city's vascular system intimately. They know which pipes are original, which are replacements, which are so old that they are functionally geological — more stone than metal, more deposit than pipe. They know the leaks by sound. They can walk a street and tell you which joint on which pipe on which building face is weeping, and what it is weeping, and how long it has been weeping, and whether it needs to be addressed now or can wait another season.

The factory workers are fewer. The factories run, but they run on long cycles, and the labor they require is less the labor of constant maintenance and more the labor of operation — monitoring, adjusting, feeding raw material, removing product. The factories process the mineral deposits that are mined from beneath the plateau — the iron, the copper, the tin, the compounds that the geological substrate offers up. The mining is done in the deep levels, below the salamander zones, in the rock that underlies the basalt. The miners are the Condians who go deepest, and they are a separate subculture — quieter, more solitary, more comfortable with silence and darkness than the surface-dwelling Condians. The miners have their own dialect, their own customs, their own rituals. They come up from the mines at the end of their shifts and they blink in the diffuse light and they do not speak much and they eat and they sleep and they go back down. They are the Condians who are most like the salamanders.

The nest-catchers are a third subculture. They are the Condians who go highest — up the chimneys, onto the roofs, into the upper reaches of the buildings where the fog is thickest and the air is wettest and the bird populations are densest. They are agile, thin, restless, and they have a relationship with the birds that is more intimate than any other human-animal relationship in the city. They know the birds' patterns. They can predict the nesting cycles. They can distinguish, by the sound of a call, whether a flock is feeding, resting, alarmed, or preparing to fly. They are the only Condians who look up regularly. Everyone else looks down — at the drainage channels, at the pipes, at the work that is at hand. The nest-catchers look up. They are the city's relationship with its sky.

There are other professions. The well-keepers, who maintain the wells and the water table and who go down to the flooded levels where the salamanders are, though they do not interact with the salamanders and do not think about them. The channel-clearers, who work the drainage system from the street level down, clearing the mineral deposits and the debris and the rat populations' accumulated waste. The builders, who do the slow work of construction — repairing, replacing, occasionally adding a new layer over an old one, the incremental growth of the city. The traders, who manage the convoys that bring food up from below and take the city's manufactured goods down. The weavers, who make the waterproof clothing that every Condian wears — heavy, oiled fabric, dense and dark, shedding the water that everything else absorbs. Each profession is a niche, and the niches are as specific and as interdependent as the animal niches, and the Condians move between them rarely. A pipe-maintainer's child becomes a pipe-maintainer. A nest-catcher's child becomes a nest-catcher. This is not law. It is equilibrium. It is the same equilibrium that governs the rats and the birds and the cats — the system has found its balance, and the balance is stable, and the individuals within it occupy the positions that the system has made available.

The Condians do not have a religion in the way that visitors expect religion. They do not have temples. They do not have gods. What they have is a set of practices that are so deeply embedded in daily life that they are not separable from the work itself — rituals of maintenance that are also rituals of respect. When a pipe is replaced, the old pipe is not discarded. It is placed in a specific location — a storage area in the lower levels, a place where the old pipes accumulate in bundles and stacks, a library of the city's vascular history. The old pipes are not reused. They are not recycled. They are kept. The keeping is the ritual. The keeping is the acknowledgment that the pipe was part of the city, and the city remembers its parts. When a building is repaired, the old materials are similarly preserved — the limestone blocks, the brick, the mortar fragments. They are stored in the spaces between the layers, in the gaps that construction has left, in the hollow zones that are not rooms and not corridors but are simply the space inside the city's mass. The Condians are filling their city with its own past. They are making the city denser with every repair, not by adding new material but by retaining the old. The city is growing inward. It is becoming more itself.

This is the closest thing the Condians have to a spiritual practice — the preservation of the city's substance, the refusal to discard what has been part of the whole. It extends to the animals in a limited way. Dead birds are not removed from the chimneys. They are left, and the other birds build over them, incorporating the remains into the nest structures. Dead rats are left in the channels, and the water carries them to the mineralization zones where they are, over time, absorbed into the travertine deposits. Dead cats are rare — the cats are too large for the channels and too solitary for communal disposal — but when one dies, it is carried to the edge of the plateau and placed at one of the weeping rocks, where the water exits, and it is left there for the water to take. The water takes everything, eventually. The water is the city's way of returning things to the world.

The Condians do not fear the deep levels. They do not fear the animals. They do not fear the fog or the wet or the noise. They do not fear the city. They live in it the way the salamanders live in the water — not because they choose to, but because it is what they are. The city is not a thing that was built and that could be unbuilt. The city is a condition. It is the condition under which the Condians exist. To remove a Condian from Condia would be like removing a salamander from the water — technically possible, briefly survivable, ultimately fatal. They are adapted. They are endemic. They are local in the deepest sense of the word, which is the sense that means they cannot be found anywhere else.

There are, it should be noted, Condians who leave. Traders who travel the lower-elevation routes and spend weeks away from the fog. Young people who, in the way of young people everywhere, want to see something other than what they have always seen. They leave, and they travel, and they go to the drier, brighter, lower places where the sky is visible and the air is clean and the ground is not wet, and some of them stay. But the ones who stay — the ones who leave Condia and do not come back — are not the same as the ones who leave and return. The ones who return have a specific quality: they are the ones who, in the dry air, begin to miss the wet. Not the novelty of it. The substance of it. The feel of it on their skin. The sound of it in the pipes. The taste of it in the air. They miss the city the way you miss a limb. They come back, and the fog closes around them, and the sound of the factories reaches them through the stone, and the birds are calling overhead, and the rats are running in the channels, and the cats are watching from the shadows, and the pipes are weeping, and the city is there, as it was, as it will be, and they are home, and home is the only word that matters.

## The whole

What lives in Condia is Condia. The city is not a container for life. It is life. It is a single organism in the sense that a reef is a single organism — not because the parts are joined, but because the parts are interdependent, and the interdependence is so dense and so old that the boundaries between them have dissolved. The pipes are habitat. The chimneys are nesting sites. The drainage channels are rivers. The flooded basement rooms are pools. The factories are warmth sources. The stone is substrate. The fog is medium. The humans are the mammals that maintain the structure. The cats are the predators that patrol the middle levels. The rats are the detritivores that cycle the minerals. The birds are the concentrators that make the nutrients available. The insects are the link between the microbial and the visible. The salamanders are the memory of what was here before. The film is the foundation.

It is all one thing. It has been one thing for a long time. It will be one thing for a long time to come. The fog will condense. The water will run. The pipes will weep. The factories will pulse. The animals will fill their niches. The humans will work. The city will settle. Millimeter by millimeter, year by year, century by century, Condia will become more Condia. More dense. More layered. More itself.

And everything in it will be what it is, and will continue to be what it is, because there is no reason to be anything else. This is the equilibrium. This is the end of change. This is a place that has arrived.
