# What Lives Here

---

The insects came first, or rather, they came first *after* the machine and the water and the stone, which are also alive in their way and which were here before anything with legs arrived.

They are small. Smaller than you'd expect given the lushness. Dark-winged, dark-bodied, something between a gnat and a midge, and they have no name because no one has been here to name them and they don't require one. They live in the warm exhaust above the machine's housing, in the pocket of heated air that rises from the vents along the building's eastern wall where the concrete has cracked and the machine's exhalation escapes. This is their whole territory. A column of warm air perhaps two meters wide and four meters tall, rising from rusted iron into the mist above. They orbit it. They breed in it. They die in it. Their lives are so short and so local that the column of air is, to them, a world — and a generous one, warm and stable, created by a machine that has been exhalating for longer than any of them have been anything, and which will continue to exhale long after the last of them has fallen, which is also now, which is also always, because they die constantly and are replaced constantly and the column is always full.

They feed on the flowers. The waxy pale flowers of the cliff plants, the epiphytes that shouldn't be here and are. The insects travel to them — short flights, ten meters at most, out of the warm column and into the cool wet air of the shelf, and you can see them falter when they leave the heat, their flight becoming erratic, slower, as if the cold affects them the way drowsiness affects larger creatures. They reach the flowers. They do whatever insects do on flowers — the transaction, the ancient transaction, pollen for transport, nectar for loyalty — and they return. The column pulls them back. They rise in it and settle in it and the cycle completes, dozens of times a day, hundreds, a frequency that looks from the outside like stillness because the individuals are indistinguishable and the column is always occupied.

There is something else here. Not many of them. Not a species, exactly — or if a species, then one so thin on the ground that the word *population* feels generous.

They live in the building.

Not in the building the way a rat lives in a building, finding a corner and making a nest. In the building the way the moss lives on the concrete — as a response to its conditions, as something the building has invited by being what it is. They are small, smaller than your hand, and they are warm. You know they are warm because if you are in the building long enough — long enough for the thrumming to stop being sound and become texture, long enough for your eyes to adjust to the green-dark light that filters through the moss-covered walls — you might feel one cross the back of your neck, and what you feel is heat. A point of heat, moving, deliberate, tracing a line across your skin that is too purposeful to be an insect and too slow to be a drop of water.

They are amphibian, or something like amphibian. Something that found, in the wet and the warmth and the constant vibration of the machine, a niche that resembles the niches amphibians have always found: the edge between water and land, between wet and dry, between the living and the mineral. But there is no dry here, so they live at the edge between wet and *wetter*, between the film of moisture on the concrete and the pools that gather in the low places on the floor, between the condensation that runs down the walls and the water that seeps through the cracks in the foundation. They are always in contact with water. They are always in contact with stone or concrete or iron. They do not have a dry state. They do not have a state where they are not touching something.

Their skin is dark. Dark as the stone, dark as the moss, dark as the shadow under the ferns. This is not camouflage in the intentional sense — nothing here has predators, nothing here hunts — but it is camouflage in the sense that everything here tends toward the same colors, the dark greens and blacks and browns of wet stone and growing things, as if the place has a palette and everything that lives here eventually arrives at it. They are smooth. Not slick, not slimy — smooth the way wet stone is smooth, which is to say they have the texture of their substrate, which is everything. You could hold one and not be sure you were holding it, because it would be the same temperature as your hand and the same temperature as the air and the same temperature as the water, and its skin would be wet the way everything here is wet, and it would weigh almost nothing, or rather it would weigh exactly what a handful of water weighs, which is something but not much.

They eat the insects. This is the one predatory relationship on the shelf, and it is a gentle one, or it looks gentle because the scale is so small and the participants are so quiet about it. They wait near the exhaust vents where the insects congregate. They wait on the concrete, in the film of water, and they are so still and so dark and so wet that they are indistinguishable from the concrete itself, from the film of water on the concrete, from the shadow of the machine. An insect lands. The tongue, if it is a tongue — something projection-like, something fast, something that operates on a mechanism you don't quite see — and the insect is gone, and the creature resumes waiting, and the waiting is so patient and so complete that it doesn't look like hunting. It looks like being. It looks like the concrete has decided, occasionally, to eat.

There are perhaps fifteen of them. Maybe twenty. It's hard to count because they don't gather and they don't separate. They exist in the building the way the water exists in the cracks of the stone — distributed, following paths of least resistance, pooling in the low places. You find them where the floor dips. You find them where the condensation is thickest. You find them on the machine itself, clinging to the iron housing where the vibration is strongest, and this is the strange part, the part that makes you stay longer than you intended: they orient toward the vibration. They press against it. They flatten themselves to the iron where the thrumming is most intense, and they stay there, and their bodies pulse with it, or rather their bodies absorb it, and after a long time watching you realize that what you are seeing is not an animal using a surface. What you are seeing is an animal that has incorporated a rhythm into its existence so thoroughly that the rhythm is part of what it is. They have synchronized to the machine. Their heartbeat, if they have one, is either matched to the four-second pulse or subsumed by it. They don't know the machine is a machine. They don't know the pulse is mechanical. They know the pulse the way you know gravity — as a condition, as the thing that governs the shape of everything, and they have built their lives inside it the way you built yours inside gravity, without thinking about it, without being able to think about it, without any sense that things could be otherwise.

They breed here. There must be eggs, or something like eggs, in the pools that gather on the floor, in the water that seeps through the cracks, in the warm mineral wet that the machine creates and the building collects. You don't see them. You see the adults, or what you assume are the adults, and you assume there are young because there are adults and because the adults look like things that were recently small. But the life cycle is invisible, or it happens at a scale and a pace that doesn't resolve to human attention. The young, if they exist, are in the water, in the cracks, in the film that covers everything, and they are too small and too dark and too much like the water itself to be found by someone who is just passing through.

No one is passing through, of course. No one has passed through in a long time. The creatures in the building have never seen a human being, or if they have, it was so long ago that it belongs to a different era of their existence — an era when the building was louder in a different way, when the concrete was newer and drier, when the machine was a machine and not a heartbeat. They wouldn't know what to make of you. They wouldn't make anything of you. You would be a warm thing that displaced air and cast a shadow and then left, and they would return to the concrete and the iron and the pulse and the waiting, and the warm thing would become, in whatever memory they have, another condition, like rain, like the increase in condensation toward evening, like the slow uncurling of ferns, something that happened and then stopped happening and didn't change the shape of anything.

---

Outside, on the shelf, the vegetation is its own kind of life, and it deserves to be described as such, because it is not passive. The moss is not covering the concrete. The moss is *eating* the concrete — slowly, patiently, acid by acid, breaking down the calcium and the silicates and incorporating them into itself, turning the building into soil the way the mountain is being turned into soil, the way everything hard here is being made soft by water and by the things that grow in water. The ferns are not growing *in* the cracks. They are *opening* the cracks — their roots swell with water, expand, exert pressure, and the rock, which is new and fractured and not yet settled into its own permanence, gives way by fractions of millimeters, and the cracks widen, and the ferns grow deeper, and the process accelerates. The jointed grass-thing, the thing that might be bamboo, is not trembling in the wind. It is *measuring* the wind, each stem a calibrated instrument that bends and clicks and bends and clicks, and the clicking is not random. It is the sound of the wind being counted, the way a clock counts time, the way the machine counts pulses, the way the water counts the stone by dripping on it, patient, patient, patient.

The epiphytes on the cliff faces are the most ambitious. They are climbing. They are climbing the way plants have always climbed — slowly, by growing toward the light, by extending roots upward into the fissures where condensation runs heaviest, by sending leaves into the narrow strip of sky between the leaning walls. They don't know there's a summit. They don't know there's a top. They know the light is up and the water is up and the warmth is up, and so they go up, and in a thousand years they will have reached the rim of the cliff and found the open air and the wind and the full sun, and they will either die or adapt, and either way they will have gotten there, and the getting there will have been the point.

But the truest thing that lives here is not an organism.

---

The machine lives here.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way that people say a car has a soul or a house has character. The machine lives here the way the mountain lives here — by persisting, by being the condition that everything else responds to, by having a rhythm that has become, over decades or centuries, indistinguishable from the rhythm of the place itself. It pumps. Or compresses. Or moves something from somewhere to somewhere else through pipes that enter from the cliff and exit through the floor. It has done this for so long that the building has settled around it the way a tree settles around a rock — incorporating it, accommodating it, making it structural. The walls vibrate. The concrete has microfractures that follow the vibration patterns, stress lines that have become features, that have become the grain of the building, and the moss grows along these lines because the cracks are slightly wider there and the water collects there and the mineral content is slightly different there, and the moss doesn't know it is following the machine's signature, but it is. The ferns on the floor grow in a pattern that radiates from the machine's base, thicker near the housing where the heat is strongest, thinner at the walls where the air is cooler, and this pattern is not planned by anything but it is not random either. It is the pattern of the machine's influence, mapped in vegetation.

The machine has never been serviced. No one has oiled it, no one has tightened its bolts, no one has read its gauges or replaced its cracked housings. And it has not broken. This is not because it was built to last — things built to last still require attention, still require the human hand that says *not yet, not yet, stay together a little longer.* It is because the environment has become its maintenance program. The constant moisture lubricates its moving parts. The moss absorbs vibration that would otherwise fatigue the metal. The mineral content of the water has deposited, over decades, a fine coating of calcium and silica on every internal surface, a natural gasket, a natural sealant, and the machine has been re-sealed by the same process that builds stalactites. It has been colonized internally as well as externally. There are almost certainly things living inside it — in the pipes, in the housings, in the spaces between moving parts that move so slowly and so predictably that life can gain purchase there. The machine is not a machine anymore. It is a habitat. It is an organism. It is a heart that has grown a body around itself, and the body is the building and the moss and the ferns and the amphibians and the insects and the water and the stone, all of it pulsing together, all of it synchronized to a four-second beat that no one set and no one maintains and no one will stop.

If the machine stopped — if the heat source below shifted, if the pipes clogged, if the iron finally fatigued beyond what the calcium deposits could compensate for — everything here would change. The warmth would fade. The condensation would slow. The amphibians would lose their orientation, their rhythm, the pulse that is their gravity. The insects would lose their column. The epiphytes would find the cliff faces colder and would retreat. The moss would darken and dry and eventually slough off the concrete like dead skin. The building, which has accommodated the vibration so thoroughly that it has become dependent on it, might shake itself apart in the silence, the sudden absence of stress revealing stresses of its own, hidden ones, the ones the machine's rhythm was counteracting all along. The whole place would come apart, not violently, not dramatically, but the way a body comes apart when the heart stops — slowly, then all at once, then forever.

But the machine doesn't stop. It has no reason to stop. It has been running long enough that stopping is no longer in its nature, the way flying is no longer in the nature of a penguin, the way swimming is no longer in the nature of a whale, even though both still carry the anatomy of their past capabilities. The machine carries the anatomy of stopping — the switches, the valves, the gauges that were meant to be read and adjusted and responded to — but it has evolved past them. It runs because it runs. It pulses because it pulses. And everything that lives here — the fifteen or twenty dark warm things on its housing, the insects in its exhalation, the moss in its cracks, the ferns in its heat-shadow, the water in its rhythm — lives here because it runs, and would not live here if it didn't, and doesn't know any of this, and doesn't need to.

---

And there is the mountain itself, which is alive in the way that very slow things are alive, which is the only way that matters if you wait long enough. The mountain was born a few centuries ago — lifted, thrust upward, exposed to air it had never breathed. It is still adjusting. The spalling, the fresh shards underfoot, the cracks that widen — these are the mountain settling into its new shape, the way a child grows into a body it didn't choose. The water that moves through it is its blood. The heat that rises from below is its metabolism. The vegetation on its surface is its skin, its first skin, the thin beginning of something that will, in ten thousand years or fifty thousand, become soil and then forest and then ecosystem, but for now is just moss and fern and the jointed clicking grass-thing, the first arrivals, the pioneers, the things that came because the rock was new and the cracks were open and the water was warm and there was space — space! the great invitation, the thing that no living thing can resist — and they filled it, and they are filling it, and they will fill it until it is full, and then other things will come, things with spines, things with voices, things that compete and hunt and hide, and the shelf will become a forest, and the building will be a ruin inside the forest, and the machine, if it still runs, will be a heartbeat under the floor of the world, felt but not heard, known but not seen, the thing that started everything and that nothing remembers and that nothing needs to remember because it has become, by the simple act of persisting, indistinguishable from the place itself.

What lives here is the place. The place lives here. And everything else — the machine, the moss, the insects, the dark warm things on the iron, the water, the stone — is the place being alive.
