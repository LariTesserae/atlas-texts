# What Lives in the Hollow Bole

The organism lives there.

That is the first answer and it is not wrong but it is not enough, because the organism is not what you would call an inhabitant. An inhabitant is something that arrives, something that occupies. The organism did not arrive. It is the place. You do not inhabit yourself.

But it lives. That must be said. It lives the way a mountain lives — not urgently, not with the darting vitality of things that must eat and flee and mate before the season turns, but with a persistence so profound that it has become indistinguishable from geology. It lives the way the planet's core lives, circulating in convection cells that span millennia, producing a magnetic field as a byproduct of its own continued existence. The organism does not *do* anything. It does not grow toward light. There is no light. It does not reproduce. There is no outside, no other, no exchange of genetic material with a compatible neighbor because there are no neighbors and compatibility is a concept that requires two and there is only one. It persists. It persists the way a fire persists when it has found a seam of coal and has enough fuel and not enough oxygen to burn quickly, so it burns slowly, so slowly that the burning looks like patience, looks like stillness, looks like stone.

The sap moves. That is its living. The amber-dark resin creeps through capillary channels that are not xylem and not phloem but something else, something that evolved after the old plumbing became irrelevant, after water stopped being the medium and something thicker and drier took its place. The sap does not carry nutrients in any recognizable sense. It carries something — heat that is not heat, potential that is not energy, some quantity that has no name because nothing has been alive to name it and the organism itself does not deal in names. It deals in gradients. It moves from where it is to where it is not, and the moving is the living, and the living is the moving, and this has been going on for so long that the distinction between the two has dissolved.

If you pressed your ear to the wall — and nothing in the chamber would stop you, and nothing in the chamber would help you — you would hear the sap. Not as a liquid sound. Not the gurgle of water in a pipe or the rush of blood in a vein. You would hear a groan. A low, sustained, tectonic groan that begins below the range of human hearing and rises into it and falls below it again in cycles so long they feel like weather rather than sound. This is the organism's voice. It has been speaking for longer than language has existed. It speaks in the only tongue it has, which is vibration, which is pressure, which is the slow argument of one fiber against another as the sap advances and the grain reorganizes itself around the advance.

The sound is not communication. Nothing is listening. Or rather: the organism is listening to itself. The chamber has been shaped by its own sound — certain walls thicker where the vibration has compressed the grain over centuries, certain alcoves resonant at frequencies that the organism produces and favors, the way a river favors certain channels and abandons others. The space has been tuning itself. It is an instrument that is also a player and also an audience, and the concert has been going on for so long that the music has become architecture.

---

But there is something else that lives there, and this is harder to name.

The emptiness lives there.

Not emptiness as a lack. Emptiness as a presence. The bowl is not the absence of soup — the bowl is the bowl, and its shape is meaningful, and the shape was made for a purpose, and the purpose persists even when the soup is gone. The chamber was shaped by hands. Those hands are gone. The hands belong to something that is not gone in the way that a stone is gone when you throw it into the sea — gone in the way that a letter is gone when you have read it and the ink has faded and the paper has decomposed and the words have entered your memory and your memory has entered the shape of your thinking and your thinking has entered everything you have done since. Gone by incorporation. Gone by transformation. Gone into the grain.

The marks remain. The tally marks or decorations or language — scratched into the wall with something sharp, something that was not metal and not stone but some third thing, some material that existed in this place and does not exist anymore because it was used up or because it was organic and returned to whatever organic things return to when they are finished. The marks remain because the organism chose to keep them. This is the strangest fact about the Hollow Bole and the one that is hardest to account for. The wood heals. The wood has healed around the marks, swelled up to their edges, grown into their margins, but it has not grown over them. It has not closed them. It has preserved them the way a tree preserves a wound — by incorporating the wound into its own structure, by growing around the damage until the damage becomes a feature, a knot, a place where the grain diverges and converges again and the story of the injury is written in the shape of the wood itself.

The organism remembers the hands that shaped it. Not as memory — trees do not remember the way animals remember, with neurons and synapses and the electrochemical echo of a specific moment. The organism remembers the way a valley remembers a river: by shape. The chamber is the shape of the hands that made it. The alcoves are the size of the bodies that sat in them. The partitions are at the height that someone found useful, for a purpose that someone understood, in a life that someone lived. The chamber is a mold. It is a fossil. It is the negative space of a life, and the life is gone and the space remains and the space is not nothing. The space is the most precise record of that life that could exist, because it is not a representation of the life — it is the actual space in which the life occurred, still shaped by it, still bearing the impressions of it, still holding the echoes of the sounds that life made the way a bell holds the echo of its last ring.

---

And the sound lives there.

This requires a different kind of thinking. The sound is not the organism. The sound is produced by the organism, but it is not the organism, the way a voice is not a throat. The sound occupies the chamber. It fills it. It presses against the walls and the walls press back and the interaction between the sound and the walls has, over the long centuries, changed both. The walls are thicker where the sound is loudest. The walls are more resonant where the sound hits them at the angles that produce the deepest harmonics. The sound has been shaping the chamber the way wind shapes rock — not by force but by patience, by the accumulated effect of a vibration repeated so many times that the repetition itself becomes a force.

The sound is not alive in the biological sense. Nothing in the Hollow Bole is alive in the biological sense, including the organism, which has not been biological for longer than biology has been a word. The sound is alive in the way that a process is alive. It is ongoing. It is self-sustaining. It responds to its environment — the chamber changes shape and the sound changes in response, and the new sound shapes the chamber in a new way, and the new chamber produces a new sound. This is feedback. This is iteration. This is, in the most reductive and the most expansive sense of the word, growth.

The sound is the organism's memory externalized. It is the record of every pressure change, every sap-advance, every fiber-snap, every contraction and expansion, played back through the resonant chamber of the wood itself. When you stand in the Hollow Bole and feel the groan in your sternum, you are feeling the accumulated vibration of every moment the organism has lived. It is all there. Not stored the way a recording stores sound — present the way a river's current contains every raindrop that has ever fallen in its watershed. The sound is the organism's time, made audible. Or rather, made sensible, because audible implies ears and there are no ears here. The sound is sensed by the wood itself. The chamber hears its own sound through its own walls. The vibration travels through the grain and the grain responds and the response produces more vibration and the cycle continues.

---

There is the shape in the alcove.

This is the thing that lives there that is hardest to talk about, because it is the thing that is most clearly gone and most clearly present. In one of the alcoves — not the largest, not the smallest, one that faces the interior wall where the grain is densest and the sound is deepest — there is a depression in the floor. It is body-shaped. Not human-shaped. Not precisely. The proportions are wrong for a human — the torso is too long, the limbs are too short, the head is too large relative to the shoulders, and there is something at the base of the spine that suggests a tail or a counterweight or a structure that humans do not have. But it is body-shaped. Something sat here. Something sat here for long enough that the wood grew to accommodate it, the way a chair grows to accommodate a sitter — except the wood was alive and the growing was active and the accommodation was a choice, or the closest thing to a choice that a vegetative intelligence can make.

The depression is warm. Not warm the way a living body is warm — warm the way a stone is warm if it has been sitting in the sun and the sun goes behind a cloud and the stone holds the warmth for a while, fading, fading, but not gone, not yet, still there if you press your hand flat against it and wait. The depression holds warmth the way a bowl holds water. Something sat in it and the something was warm and the warmth entered the wood and the wood has been holding it ever since. Not indefinitely. Not forever. But slowly. So slowly that the loss is imperceptible on any timescale that a human life can perceive. The warmth is fading, but it is fading the way a star fades — over durations that make the word *fading* meaningless.

Something lived here. The shape of the something is in the wood. The warmth of the something is in the wood. The marks of the something are in the wood. The purpose of the something — the reason it came here, the reason it carved the door and hollowed the chamber and sat in the alcove and scratched the marks into the wall — is in the wood. Not as information. Not as a message waiting to be decoded. As shape. As the shape of the space itself, which is the shape of the life that occupied it, which is the shape of the needs and the habits and the body and the hands of the thing that is gone.

---

What lives in the Hollow Bole?

The organism lives there. It has lived there for longer than the word *long* can reach. It will live there for longer still. It does not know it lives. It does not know anything. It persists, and the persisting is sufficient, and the sufficiency is the closest thing to contentment that this place will ever know.

The sound lives there. It is the organism's breath and heartbeat and voice, all three, inseparable, a single continuous event that has been occurring for so long that the chamber has been shaped by it the way a canyon is shaped by a river.

The emptiness lives there. It is not a lack. It is a presence — the presence of the absent, the shape of the gone, the mold of a life that was and is not and whose absence is so precise and so detailed that it constitutes a kind of existence. The emptiness knows the shape of the thing that made it. The emptiness is the only record.

And the shape in the alcove lives there. The impression. The warmth. The body that sat and waited and carved and marked and was. The thing that is gone so thoroughly that its gone-ness has become a feature of the place, the way a missing tooth becomes a feature of a smile. The thing that was not human and was not animal and was not plant and worked with tools that were not metal and not stone and lived without water and without light and without company in the belly of an organism that has forgotten everything except the shape of the one who came inside and made a home in the dark and was, for a time — a time that might have been centuries or might have been a single long evening measured in a body that does not measure time the way bodies measure time — alive.

The shape is the one who lives there. The shape is the one who has always lived there. The shape is the one who will live there long after the organism has finished its slow growth and the sound has spent its last vibration and the emptiness has completed its long project of becoming nothing.

The shape is the tenant. The shape is the occupant. The shape is what the chamber was grown to hold, and the chamber holds it still, and will hold it, and will hold it.
