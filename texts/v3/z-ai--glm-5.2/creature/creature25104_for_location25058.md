# What Lives in the Loma Pocket

---

The first thing that lives here is the sound. Before the moss, before the beetles, before the people — the sound lives here, and everything else arranges itself around it. It has the quality of something architectural, something built into the walls, the way a note lives inside a bell after the striking. The insects make most of it. But the stone makes some of it too — the faint, fine sound of dust becoming more dust, of a mountain finishing the long sentence of its erosion one syllable at a time. And the condensation makes its own sound, the slow adhesive sound of water becoming surface. The sound lives here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest: not as an event but as a condition.

---

## The people

There are thirty-one of them right now. There were thirty-four at the last count, but counting is not something they do with any regularity, because regularity requires a calendar and they do not have one. Children are born. Elders die. Sometimes someone leaves through the fissure and walks down into the lowlands and does not come back, and no one marks this as a loss exactly, the way you would not mark the departure of a weather pattern. The person was here. Now they are not. The pocket has not changed in response.

They are dark-skinned, not from lineage but from the amber light, which stains everything the color of old honey over time. Their hair is black and straight and they wear it long, tied back with strips of vine-bark that tightens as it dries and loosens as the humidity rises, so that everyone's hair is always in the process of either being tied or needing to be tied. There is a constant small fussing with hair. It is one of the visible rhythms of the place.

They are not thin. The pocket feeds them adequately. There is a softness to their bodies — not fat, but a lack of the hard angularity that comes from scarcity. No one here has ever been hungry enough for the body to start consuming itself. The tubers are reliable. The vine stems are reliable. The lichen is reliable. The diet is monotonous — everything tastes faintly of the minerals it grew in, of calcium and iron and the flat sweetness of stone dust — but it is sufficient, and sufficiency, when you have never known anything else, is not a condition you resent. It is simply what food is.

They wear clothing made from the moss. The long strands of *barba de piedra* can be stripped into fibers, and the fibers can be twisted into thread, and the thread can be woven on simple looms — two sticks and tension — into a loose, open fabric that is more structure than coverage. The fabric is grey-green. It is always slightly damp. It does not dry because nothing here dries. The people wear damp cloth against damp skin in warm air and they do not think of this as uncomfortable because they have no comparison. The cloth smells like the pocket: mineral, vegetal, faintly sweet, like the inside of a greenhouse that has been closed too long.

The children are born in the structures. The structures are small — two meters by three, barely room for two adults to lie side by side — and the walls are low, chest height, and the roofs are mats of woven moss laid over flexible sticks that are not wood because there is no wood. The sticks are the ribs of the fern analogs, dried in the narrow band of direct light that falls at midday. They are not strong. The roofs sag. Rain does not fall here, but condensation does, and the roofs catch it and drip, and so the interiors of the structures are always a little wetter than the outside, which is already wet. The beds are piles of dried moss that is not fully dried. You lie on something that is between damp and moist and you sleep, and the geckos sleep above you, and the sound of the pocket does not stop.

The children learn to walk on the succulent mats and they learn to step over the floor-walkers before they learn to speak, because the floor-walkers will not move for a child and a child who falls on a floor-walker will be bitten, and the bites are not dangerous but they are painful and they teach a certain kind of attention. The children grow up knowing where the floor is and what is on it. This is the first knowledge. Before language. Before names. The floor and what moves across it.

They have names. They have language. It is a quiet language — not because they are quiet, but because the pocket is so full of sound that shouting is pointless and whispering is indistinguishable from speaking, and so they speak at one level, a mid-tone, conversational, and it carries as far as it needs to carry, which is never far. The language has no word for "far." It has no word for "horizon." It has no word for "river" or "wind" or "cold" or "season." It has fourteen words for different kinds of damp. It has nine words for the textures of stone in various stages of decay. It has a word — *surrú* — that means the sound the pocket makes at night, the combined murmur of insects and condensation and floor-walker clicking, and this word is also used to mean "alive" and also used to mean "home," and these three meanings are not considered distinct.

They do not have a word for "time" in the abstract. They have "before" and "after" and "now" and "still," and these are sufficient. The oldest among them — a woman whose name translates roughly as *She Who Was Already Here* — remembers when there were fifteen structures instead of twelve, and the fifteenth was in a different place, and its stones have since been reused. This is the extent of their history. The past is made of stones that are now other stones. There is no record. There is no archive. The walls are the archive, and the walls are dissolving.

They do not have religion in any form that would be recognizable. They do not worship. They do not pray. But they have a practice — a habit — that functions in the space where religion would function: when a structure's walls have gone too soft, when the geckos have left and the corners have rounded and the stones come away in the hand like wet sand, the people do not simply dismantle it. They sit in it. All of them, all thirty-one, they crowd into the structure or around it, and they sit, and they listen. They listen to the building stop being a building. They listen to the stone return to dust. This can take an afternoon. Sometimes it takes longer. No one speaks. The insects speak. The floor-walkers click. The building does not speak because it has nothing left to say, but the people listen to its silence, and when the last stone is placed on the ground — not dropped, placed, by the eldest, the one whose hands know the weight — they stand and they begin to build the next one, in a different place, and the moss is already growing over the site of the old one before the new walls are knee-high.

This is not a ceremony. They would not call it that. It is what you do. You listen to the thing that held you, and then you make the next thing that will hold you, and you do not expect it to last.

## The insects

There are more insects in the pocket than there are of anything else. This is true by such a wide margin that the word "more" is almost meaningless. They are not a population. They are a medium. The air in the pocket is partly insects the way the air over the ocean is partly salt — not because you can see them, but because you can feel them, because they are dissolved into the experience of being here.

The moth-analogs are the most visible. They are pale, flat-bodied, the color of the moss they live in, and their wings are fringed with a fine hair that is not hair but a chitinous filament so thin it is nearly transparent. They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are *adapted*, so precisely adapted that the eye slides over them the way water slides over stone. They land on the moss strands and they drink, and they lay their eggs in the crevices where the moss meets the stone, and their larvae are small white threads that burrow into the moss and eat it from the inside, and the moss grows back, and the cycle is not a cycle, it is a current, a flow, a river that is also always the same river and also always a different river, except there is no river here, and so the insects are the river.

The beetles are the engineers. They work the floor. There are — and this is a conservative estimate, based on what can be observed without equipment, without taxonomy, without any system beyond the human eye and the human patience — there are at least two hundred species of beetle in the pocket. Maybe four hundred. Maybe more. They range from the size of a grain of dust to the size of the first joint of a thumb. They are round and elongated, armored and soft, dull and faintly iridescent. They do not compete with each other. Or rather, they compete so totally and so finely that the competition has reached a kind of equilibrium that looks, from the outside, like cooperation. Each species occupies a niche so specific that it might as well be a room in a house: this beetle eats only the outer leaves of the succulent rosettes that have begun to rot. This beetle eats only the inner leaves. This beetle eats only the beetle that eats the inner leaves. This beetle eats only the eggs of the beetle that eats only the beetle that eats the inner leaves. The complexity is not layered. It is knotted. It is a ball of relationships so dense that no single thread can be pulled without the whole thing tightening.

And then there are the things in the air that are not quite insects but are not quite anything else. The ones that orbit the light shaft. They are the size of fingernails. They have wings. They have too many wings — four, or six, or wings that appear and disappear depending on the angle. They trace spirals in the air above the light ellipse, and the spirals are consistent — the same individuals follow the same paths at the same time of day, as if the air has roads, as if the light has a geometry that only they can read. The people do not have a name for them. The people do not pay attention to them. They are part of the *surrú*, part of the sound, part of the air. They are the pocket's way of talking to itself about itself.

## The lizards

They are called *pegados* — the stuck ones — by the people, though they are not always stuck. They move. But they move the way a shadow moves: slowly, and then all at once, and then they are somewhere else and you did not see the transition.

There are at least three species. The common one is small, brown-grey, with a body like a flattened coin and legs that splay outward rather than bearing it underneath. They cling to the vertical stone with toes that are not toes but pads, adhesive pads, and they can run across a ceiling without falling, and they do, often, and the children reach up and touch their bellies as they pass overhead and the lizards do not startle. They have been touched by children for longer than children have been children. It is part of their environment. A hand reaching up is like a frond uncurling — a fact of the place, not a threat.

The second species is the translucent gecko-analog. The one that lives in the old structures. They are nearly invisible — not camouflaged but actually translucent, the organs visible as faint shadows beneath the skin, the whole animal like a living piece of fog. They are larger than the common *pegados* — the size of a hand — and they are slower, and they are territorial, not in an aggressive way but in a way that means they stay. A gecko that has claimed a structure will not leave it. It will live there for its entire life, which is long — the people believe they live longer than people do, though they do not know this for certain because they do not track the years and the geckos do not tell them. When a gecko dies, another one arrives. The people do not know where from. The geckos emerge from the cracks in the stone fully formed, already adult, already translucent, already certain of where they belong. The people treat this as ordinary. The pocket produces what the pocket needs. This is not a belief. It is an observation.

The third species is rare and the people are afraid of it, though they will not say this directly. It is larger — the size of a forearm — and it is dark, nearly black, and it lives in the highest part of the pocket, up near the ocular gap, where the light is strongest and the stone is most solid. It does not come down. The people have a name for it that translates as *the one that watches the hole*, and they say that it is older than the pocket, which is impossible, but they say it anyway, and the way they say it — quietly, without emphasis, as if stating a fact too obvious to argue — suggests that they mean something by "older" that is not the same thing you mean. They mean the lizard was here when the pocket was different. When the stone was harder. When the fissure was narrower. They mean the lizard is part of the mesa, not part of the pocket, and the pocket has grown around it the way a tree grows around a nail.

## The birds

There are two species. Both are small. Both live in the moss curtains.

The first is the moss-probe — the one with the long curved beak, the one that eats the moth larvae. It is the color of the moss with a chest the color of the condensation, which is to say, the color of nothing, the color of damp air. Its song is a single note, high and thin, repeated at irregular intervals, and the intervals are not random. They follow a pattern that is too long to hear as a pattern, too short to hear as silence. The people do not notice the song the way you do not notice your own breathing. It is part of the *surrú*. A visitor would hear it and be arrested by it, would try to follow the sequence, would lose the thread, would give up, would forget. The birds have been singing this song since before there were people to not hear it.

The second species is less common. It is the color of the lichen — salt-grey, with a rust-colored cap. It eats the insects that the light-shaft orbiters catch — or rather, it eats the orbiters themselves, darting into the bright column of air and snatching them mid-spiral with a precision that is genuinely remarkable and entirely unremarked upon. This bird does not sing. It makes a sound like a dry leaf cracking. It nests in the upper walls, not in the moss, and it is more solitary — pairs, not colonies. The people do not have a name for this bird. They do not think of it as a different animal from the first. They think of the two birds as the same thing expressed in two ways, the way a word can be spoken loudly or softly.

The nests are everywhere. Hanging from the moss, woven into the stone, tucked into the crevices where the walls meet the ceiling. Every stage of the life cycle is visible at every moment. An egg in one nest, a chick in another, a fledgling taking its first flight across the pocket, a nest being dismantled by a lizard, a nest being built from the wreckage of an old one. There is no spring here. There is no breeding season. Everything breeds always. The temperature does not change. The food does not change. The light does not change, except in its slow migration across the floor. And so the birds are always beginning and always ending, and the distinction between the two is not a distinction the pocket respects.

## The floor-walkers

The *piso-caminantes*. The ones the people step over.

They are the most alien thing in the pocket. They are also the most present. You feel them before you see them — a vibration in the succulent mat, a displacement of air at ankle height, a low clicking that enters through the soles of the feet. They are the size of small cats but they are shaped like nothing you would call a cat. They are low — barely a hand's width off the ground — and long, and their legs are not visible. They move by means that are not walking but are also not slithering. The body propels itself forward in a smooth, continuous motion, and the legs — if they are legs — are underneath, hidden, doing work that the surface does not advertise. Their hides are smooth, wet-stone-grey, and warm. They are always warm. In a pocket where everything is warm, they are warmer. You can feel the heat of them from a foot away.

They have too many teeth. This is the thing that stays with you. When a floor-walker opens its mouth — and it opens its mouth often, because it is always eating — the mouth is a line of teeth that goes back farther than it should, rows of small, sharp, identical teeth that do not seem to serve any particular function because the things they eat — beetles, lizards, the occasional bird that has fallen from its nest — do not require that many teeth. But the teeth are there. The people have looked in the mouths of floor-walkers and the people do not understand the teeth and the people do not try to understand the teeth. The floor-walkers eat. The floor-walkers are warm. The floor-walkers click. These are the facts.

They vocalize at night. The clicking. It is not a call. It is not a signal. It is not territorial. It is — and this is the people's word for it, their word that also means "breathing" and also means "the sound the pocket makes" — it is *surrú*. The floor-walkers are making the sound the pocket makes. They are the pocket's voice, or the pocket is their voice, or there is no distinction. When they click, the stone vibrates, and the vibration carries through the walls and into the structures and into the beds and into the people, and the people sleep inside the sound the way they sleep inside the pocket: completely, without resistance, without the sense that there is a place where the sound is not.

The floor-walkers do not leave. When everything else goes — and it will go, in the million years the pocket has left, the million years of softening stone and widening gaps — the floor-walkers will be the last things here. They have nowhere else. They were made here. They are made *of* here, in some way that the taxonomy of the outside world will not help with, in some way that connects them to the stone and the moisture and the ancient dust of the mountain that is no longer a mountain. They are the mesa's last thought, expressed in teeth and warmth and a clicking that you feel in your feet.

## The moss

And then there is the moss, which is not moss, which is the thing that lives here most thoroughly, most patiently, most completely. The *barba de piedra* hangs from the ceiling in curtains that are meters deep. It covers the walls. It grows in the structures, on the structures, through the structures. It is the first thing to colonize a new surface and the last thing to leave a dying one. It drinks the condensation directly from the stone. It does not need soil. It does not need roots. It needs only a surface and a dampness and time, and it has all three, and it has had them for longer than anything else here, and it will have them for longer than anything else will.

If the pocket has a soul — and the people would not use this word, but the concept is not entirely foreign to them — it is the moss. The moss is what makes the pocket a pocket and not a hole. The moss is what holds the warmth, what softens the light, what muffles the sound, what feeds the moths, what shelters the birds, what provides the fiber for the clothing and the bedding and the walls. The moss is the first thing you touch when you enter and the last thing you touch when you leave. It is the texture of the place, the skin of it, the living membrane between the air and the stone.

It is patient. It does not grow quickly. It does not need to. It grows at the speed of the condensation, which is the speed of the stone's breathing, which is the speed of the mesa's erosion, which is the speed of deep time. If you watch a single strand of moss for the length of a human life, you will see it grow the length of a human finger. Maybe less. The people have watched. They have seen this. They do not find it slow. They find it the correct speed. The speed at which a mountain becomes a room becomes a world becomes a memory becomes dust.

---

Everything that lives here lives here because the stone is almost done. The softness of the stone is what allows the moss to grip without roots. The dampness of the stone is what allows the moss to drink without rain. The warmth of the stone is what allows the insects to breed without seasons. The dissolution of the stone is what allows the people to build without tools harder than flint. Everything here is predicated on failure. The mountain failed. The mountain is failing. The pocket is the shape the failure has made, and life has moved into the shape the way water moves into a cup, and the life does not know the cup is broken, and the cup does not know it is a cup, and the failure does not know it has become a home.

Thirty-one people. Hundreds of species of beetle. Three species of lizard. Two species of bird. A species of floor-walker that is also a sound and a warmth and a vibration in the stone. A moss that is also a wall and a ceiling and a blanket and a meal. A sound that is also a name and a home and the condition of being alive.

They are all inside something. They are all inside the last shape of something that was once a mountain, and they are the mountain's last sentence, and the sentence is very long, and it is not finished, and it is almost finished, and these two things are true at the same time, the way the pocket is dark and warm at the same time, the way the stone is dust and structure at the same time, the way the people are patient and temporary at the same time.

They live here. They have always lived here. They will always have lived here, even when they are gone, because the stone will remember them the way it remembers the mountain — not as a thing that was, but as a shape that was made, and that the stone held for a while, and that the stone will hold a little longer, and then the stone will let go, and the shape will be the dust's problem, and the dust will not mind.
