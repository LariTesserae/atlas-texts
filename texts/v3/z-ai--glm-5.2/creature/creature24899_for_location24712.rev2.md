# What Lives on the Quiet Plateau of Ayaviri

---

## The People

Begin with their hands, because their hands are what you notice first.

They are not delicate hands. The fingers are short, the palms broad, the skin across the knuckles cracked into a pattern of fine lines like the fissures in the feldsar beneath their feet. The nails are worn down to nothing — not bitten, not torn, but simply abraded by decades of handling stone and fiber and bone, by the daily work of prying apart bromeliad leaves and scraping lichen from walls and gripping the smooth sides of clay vessels. The skin is the same umber as their faces, but darker on the hands, almost black across the backs, and there is a permanent dust in the creases of their knuckles that no amount of water will wash out, because the water itself carries the dust, and so they are cleaning with the thing that soils them, and they have stopped trying.

There are two hundred and seven of them. Or two hundred and nine — there was a birth last month, and there may have been another, but the counting is imprecise because they do not count the way counting is usually done. They know each by name. They know each by face. They know each by the particular way each person moves through the passages of the town — the slight limp of the old woman who broke her ankle on the stone as a child and it healed wrong, the long stride of the man who hunts lizards on the far edge of the settlement and walks faster than anyone else because his legs are longer, the careful, deliberate steps of the children who have not yet learned where the stones are uneven. They do not need numbers. They have presence.

They are not old, as a people. Not in the way that implies antiquity measured in monuments and recorded histories. They are old in the way the bromeliads are old — they have been here for longer than any of them can say, and they will be here for longer than any of them can imagine, and the question of *how long* does not occur to them because duration is not a thing they measure. Time is not a river for them. It is a stone. It does not flow. It accumulates.

The oldest among them — a man whose name translates to something like *the one who was already here* — sits in the tall building most days and runs his fingers along the marks on the walls. He does not read them. They are not language. But he knows which ones were made by his grandmother and which by her grandmother and which by someone so far back that the connection is not genealogical but geological, a layer of human presence pressed into the stone like a fossil. He adds his own marks sometimes. A scratch here. A notch there. He does it with a piece of harder stone — a dark, dense pebble he has carried in his hand for so long that it has worn a groove in his palm, and the pebble itself has been shaped by his grip into something that fits no other hand. When he dies, someone will take the pebble and hold it and eventually it will shape itself to them, and the marks will continue.

They wake when the small brown birds sing. Not because they have decided to — because the sound enters their sleep and lifts them out of it the way a tide lifts a boat. They wake slowly, by degrees, in the dim amber light that is no different from the dim amber light they fell asleep in, and the first thing they do is listen. Not consciously — reflexively, the way a person in a city might check a phone. They listen for the beetles, which tell them the world is still there. They listen for the wind, which tells them the air is still moving. They listen for the birds, which tell them it is morning. And then they get up.

## Morning: The Water Gatherers

The water gathering begins before anything else.

It is done by the younger people — not children, but those in the years between childhood and full adulthood, the ones whose bodies are still flexible enough to crouch for long periods without their joints locking. They move out of the town in pairs, carrying bone tubes and small clay vessels, and they spread across the plateau in patterns that look random but are not. Each pair has a route. Each route is inherited. The girl who walks southeast toward the large boulder with the split in its top — her route was her mother's, and her mother's was her grandmother's, and the boulder is the same boulder, and the bromeliads around it are the same bromeliads, or rather they are the descendants of the same bromeliads, and the girl knows them the way you know the rooms of your own house. Which one is full. Which one is bitter this season. Which one has a bird nesting in it and should be left alone. Which one is dying and should be harvested completely before the water is lost.

They work in silence. Not a imposed silence — a natural one, the silence of people doing something that requires attention but not thought. The bone tube goes in. The mouth goes on the tube. The water comes up, warm and faintly mineral, tasting of stone and vegetation and something else, something that might be the dust itself, the fine mineral powder that is everywhere and in everything and that the people have been drinking since before they were people. The water goes into the clay vessel. The vessel fills. They move to the next plant.

It takes most of the morning. Not because the work is hard, but because the plants are spread across a wide area, and because each one requires a moment of assessment — a hand on the leaves to test the firmness, a look into the cistern to gauge the level, a touch to the surface of the water to check for the oily film that means insect larvae are present. The water with larvae is not discarded. It is strained through a handful of the creeping moss, which catches the larvae, and the larvae are eaten, raw, a small protein snack that the water gatherers consume without thinking about it, the way a person elsewhere might eat a grape while walking through a market.

By the time the light has shifted from its dimmest to its slightly-less-dim, the clay vessels are full and the water gatherers are back in the town, and the vessels are placed in the coolest parts of the houses — the back corners, against the stone walls, where the temperature is a degree or two lower and the water will keep for the day. Each household gets a portion. Each person gets a cup. The water is not rationed — there is enough, barely, and has always been enough, barely — but it is distributed with a care that looks like rationing and is actually something else. It is attention. It is the understanding that the water comes from the plants and the plants come from the stone and the stone is finite and the plants are finite and the water is finite and so every drop is noticed, not because it might run out, but because noticing is what you do when a thing is precious and you have been with it for a long time.

## The Women Who Tend the Plants

There are people whose primary work is the plants themselves.

They are mostly women, though not exclusively, and there is no rule about this — it has simply happened that way, the way most things on the plateau happen, by accumulation rather than decision. They move through the vegetation with a deliberateness that is different from the water gatherers' pace. The water gatherers are collecting. The plant tenders are maintaining.

They carry tools: flat stones with edges worn sharp, used for cutting dead leaves from the base of the bromeliads. Bone picks, for removing the parasites — the scale insects, the grubs, the small boring beetles that lay their eggs in the cisterns and whose larvae, if left unchecked, will eat through the walls of the plant and drain it. Bundles of fiber, for tying new rosettes to the stone where old ones have died and left bare patches.

The work is slow. A single bromeliad might take an hour to fully tend — clearing the dead leaves, checking the cistern, removing the parasites, pressing the soil of the creeping moss back around the base where it has thinned. The plant tenders know each bromeliad they care for. They know its age, approximately, by its size. They know its temperament — some plants hold water longer, some grow faster, some are prone to parasites, some seem to resist them through some quality of their leaf chemistry. They talk to the plants. Not in words — or not in words that have meaning outside the plateau. Low sounds, clicks, hums, a sort of murmur that is not language and not song but something in between, a vibration that the plants cannot hear but that the tenders make anyway, because their mothers made it and their mothers' mothers made it, and because the sound feels right in the mouth and in the chest when your hands are in the leaves and the dust is on your face and the plant is alive under your fingers.

They plant new bromeliads from cuttings. A pup — a small offshoot growing from the base of a mature plant — is carefully separated with a sharp stone, wrapped in damp moss, and carried to a bare spot on the stone. The tenders clears the stone surface with a flat scraper, removing the old holdfasts and the accumulated debris, until the feldspar is smooth and clean. Then they press the pup's holdfast against the stone and hold it there — sometimes for an hour, sometimes longer — until the acid secretion begins to bond with the surface. They tie it in place with fiber cords, wrapping the cords around stones to hold the pup steady against the wind. They come back the next day to check. They come back the next week. They come back for months, until the plant has gripped and is growing on its own, and then they leave it, and it will grow for twenty years and hold water for fifteen of them, and someone else — some other tender, some other generation — will tend it.

## The Hunters

There are four of them. There have always been four. Not by rule — by fit. Four is the number of people it takes to hunt the large lizards effectively without being too many, without startling the prey or competing with each other. They work in a formation that has no name because they do not name things like formations, but it is a formation: two in front, moving slowly, scanning; two behind, slightly apart, ready to close the circle when a lizard is found.

They hunt with stones. Not thrown stones — hand-held stones, because the lizards they want are slow and heavy and do not run far, and the technique is not to kill at a distance but to approach, to corner, to pin. The front two locate the lizard — usually basking on a warm stone, its body flattened to absorb the heat, its eyes half-closed. They approach from opposite sides, low, their feet placed carefully on the moss to avoid the crunching sound that would alert the animal. The back two move around to cut off the direction the lizard will run — because it will run, eventually, when it recognizes the danger, and the direction it runs is almost always toward the nearest crevice in the rock, and the hunters know where the crevices are.

The kill is quick. A stone brought down on the head, behind the eyes. The lizard's legs twitch for a moment and then stop. The hunter picks it up by the tail — a meter of heavy, dark-bodied reptile, its scales dusty and rough — and carries it back to the settlement draped over the shoulder. The other three continue hunting. They will each take one or two lizards. No more. There is a number that is enough, and they know it, and they stop at it.

The lizard meat is dark and fibrous, with a taste that is not quite like anything else — mineral, earthy, with a faint sweetness that comes from the insects the lizard has been eating, and beneath that a taste like the stone itself, as if the animal has absorbed the flavor of the surface it lives on. It is eaten cooked — roasted on flat stones over shallow pits lined with embers from the slow-burning wood of the stunted trees. The smoke from the cooking fires is thin and pale and smells of resin and dust, and it adds to the haze, and no one minds, because the haze is already there and the smoke is part of it.

The skins are scraped and dried and worked with bone tools until they are soft enough to be cut and shaped. They become pouches, straps, the covers of small cushions stuffed with dried moss. The bones are saved — the long bones for tools and needles, the ribs for frames, the small bones for beads. Nothing is wasted. Not because they have decided to waste nothing, but because there is not enough to waste, and the habit of using everything has become so ingrained that the idea of discarding something useful does not occur to them.

## The Children

There are thirty-one children. They are everywhere, and they are part of everything, and they are not separated from the life of the settlement the way children are in other places. There is no school. There is no separate children's world. The children are in the passages and the rooms and the roof gardens, and they are watching, and they are learning, and the learning is not a process that anyone has designed — it is a process that happens the way the moss grows on the stone, by contact and proximity and the slow accumulation of knowing.

A child of three follows her mother on the water gathering route. She does not carry a tube. She does not draw water. She walks behind, and she watches, and she touches the bromeliad leaves as her mother moves past them, and the leaves are wet with condensation in the morning cool, and her fingers come away damp, and she licks them, and the taste of the water and the dust and the plant is the taste of her childhood, and it will be the taste of her life.

A child of six sits with the old man in the tall building and watches him make marks on the wall. The child has a small stone of his own — not the hard, dark pebble, but a softer one, a piece of feldspar that can scratch the surface without cutting deep. He makes his own marks, next to the old man's, lower on the wall, at his own height. The marks mean nothing. The marks mean everything. They mean *I was here*, and the wall receives them, and the wall does not distinguish between the old man's marks and the child's marks, because the wall is stone and stone does not rank things.

A child of nine is already helping with the kept insects — the large, flightless creatures in the stone pens. She feeds them bromeliad leaves, torn into strips, and the insects take the strips in their mandibles and eat slowly, methodically, their broad flat backs gleaming in the dim light. She speaks to them. Not words — the same low, clicking murmur the plant tenders use. The insects do not respond. But the child speaks to them anyway, because someone spoke to them before her, and because the sound feels right.

The children play, but their play is not separate from work. A boy of ten practices throwing stones at a target — a flat stone balanced on top of another stone — and his aim is getting better, and this is play and also practice, because in a year or two he will be one of the hunters, and his arm will need to be true. Two girls weave fiber strands together, attempting a pattern they have seen in the wraps the older women wear, and the pattern is wrong, and they know it is wrong, and they pull it apart and start again, and this is play and also learning, because the fiber will be their clothing, and the clothing will be their protection against the dust and the wind, and they will make it themselves.

There is no childhood as a distinct phase. There are small people who cannot yet do the work, and there are slightly larger people who can do some of it, and there are full-sized people who do all of it, and the transitions are not marked. No one remembers exactly when they started drawing water or tending plants or hunting. It happened the way walking happened — one day you could not, and then you could, and there was no ceremony.

## The Old Woman Who Knows the Plants Best

She is called something that means, approximately, *the one whose hands are in everything*. She is not the oldest person on the plateau — the old man in the tall building is older — but she is the one who knows the plants the way a mother knows her children. She can walk to any bromeliad on the plateau — any of the thousands of them, spread across the stone in their dense, layered mats — and tell you something about it. This one was planted by her aunt, who died when she was young. This one has a beetle infestation in its third leaf layer. This one holds water sweeter than any other, and the reason is the particular mix of lichens on its surface, which filter something out of the condensation before it reaches the cistern. This one is dying, and she has known it for two years, and she has been watching it die, and she will harvest its last water and remove its carcass and plant a pup in its place, and the cycle will continue.

She moves through the vegetation with a gait that is not slow but is measured. Every step is placed. Every leaf she touches is touched with a specific intention — she is not browsing, she is reading. The plants speak to her in a language of texture and color and water level and parasite load, and she is fluent in this language, and she has been fluent in it since before she can remember, because her mother was fluent and her grandmother was fluent and the fluency is not taught but absorbed, the way the dust is absorbed into the skin.

She is the one the others come to when something is wrong. A bromeliad whose water has turned cloudy. A patch of moss that has thinned for no apparent reason. A stunted tree whose needles are yellowing. She comes, and she looks, and she touches, and she smells, and she usually knows, and when she does not know, she says so, and this is not a failure — it is an acknowledgment that the plateau is larger than any one person's knowledge, and that there are things happening in the stone and the plants that are beyond human understanding, and that is as it should be.

She has a copper band on her water tube. It is the only one. It was her grandmother's, and her grandmother's grandmother's before that, and it is so old that the copper has turned a deep, mottled green-black that is almost the color of the bromeliad leaves, and it is warm when she holds it, warmer than the stone, warmer than the air, as if the metal remembers the fire that shaped it, however many centuries ago. She does not know where the copper came from. No one does. It is just here, the way everything is just here — present, accounted for, part of the fabric.

## The Evening Gathering

When the night bird calls — the low, resonant boom that vibrates in the chest and seems to come from the ground — the people gather in the tall building.

All of them. Every evening. This is not a rule. It is a gravity. The sound of the bird pulls them in, the way the morning sound of the small birds pulls them out of sleep, and they come because coming is what you do when the night bird calls, the way sleeping is what you do when your body is tired, the way drinking is what you do when you are thirsty.

The tall building is not large enough for two hundred people to stand in comfortably, and so they fill it in layers — the oldest in the center, near the walls, seated on the smooth stone floor. The adults around them, standing or sitting on low stones. The children on the laps of the adults, or in the passages, or on the shoulders of the taller men, looking down at the crowd from above. The building is corbel-vaulted, which means the walls lean inward as they rise, and the ceiling is a low dome of flat stones, and the sound inside is dense and close and warm, the way the sound in a cave is warm, reflected and re-reflected until it becomes a single, thick presence.

The old man speaks first. Or rather, he chants. His voice is low and not loud, and the words — if they are words — are in a register that is more felt than heard, a vibration in the chest, a sound that the stone of the walls picks up and carries and amplifies in ways that the architecture was not designed to do but has evolved to do, over centuries of use, the way a riverbed evolves to fit its river. The chant is not a song. It is not a prayer. It is a recitation — a listing, a naming, a sequence of sounds that correspond to things the people know. The plants. The animals. The stones. The water. The wind. The dust. The names are not names in the way a name is a label. They are names in the way a name is a presence — when the old man chants the name of a plant, the plant is there, in the room, in the sound, even though the plant is outside, growing on the stone, doing what it does. The name does not summon it. The name *is* it.

Others speak after him. Not in any order. There is no schedule, no roster. Someone feels the need to speak, and they speak, and the others listen, or they do not listen, and both are acceptable. A woman tells a story about a lizard she saw that morning, a lizard with a marking on its back that she has never seen before — a pale stripe, almost white, running from head to tail. She describes the stripe in detail, and the detail is the point, not the meaning, because there is no meaning, there is only the stripe, and the stripe is worth describing because it exists and she saw it and it was strange and strangeness is worth noting. A child stands and says a single word — the name of the night bird — and sits down, and that is enough, and the gathering accepts it the way the wall accepts the marks: without distinction, without judgment.

The marks are made at the end. The old man takes his pebble and scratches the wall, and others take their stones and scratch the wall, and the children scratch the wall, and the sound of stone on stone is soft and continuous, a rustling, a whispering, and it blends with the sound of the beetles outside and the wind outside and the night bird's distant boom, and it is all one sound, and it is the sound of the plateau, and it is the sound of the people, and they are the same sound.

## The Animals Among Them

The lizards do not know they are hunted. Or rather, they know, but they do not know it the way prey knows it — there is no terror, no panic, no Darwinian urgency. The lizards live on the stone and eat the insects and bask in the dim warmth and die, sometimes of old age and sometimes of a stone brought down behind their eyes, and the difference between these deaths is not, from the lizard's perspective, very large. They are animals of the plateau, and the plateau is a place where things do not change, and death is not a change, it is a continuation, one more thing that happens in the long, slow, mineral sequence of being here.

The lizards live in the walls. The same walls the people live in. The stone walls of the buildings, with their gaps and crevices and layers, are habitat — cool in the day, warm at night, threaded with insects. The lizards hunt in the buildings and sleep in the buildings and mate in the buildings, and the people tolerate them the way the people tolerate the rodents — not with affection, not with annoyance, but with the indifference of co-occupancy. A lizard runs across the floor of a house and the person in the house does not react, because the lizard is part of the floor, part of the house, part of the landscape that happens to be inside. Sometimes a lizard falls from a wall onto a sleeping person, and the person brushes it off and goes back to sleep, and the lizard goes back to the wall, and neither of them thinks about it.

The rodents are more bold. They are the ones that run over feet, that cross the floors during meals, that nest in the fiber stores and eat the seeds and the dried insect meat. They are not pests. They are companions. The people leave scraps for them, not deliberately but through the natural process of living — a dropped seed, a discarded piece of gristle, a spill of water — and the rodents clean up, and this is a service, and it is recognized, and the rodents are allowed to stay. A child catches one once, holds it in cupped hands, feels its heartbeat — fast, frantic, a small engine — and opens their hands and lets it go. The rodent sits for a moment, whiskers twitching, and then it runs, and the child watches it go, and that is the entirety of the interaction.

The fox-creature — the long-bodied, sharp-faced mammal that hunts at dawn — is the only animal on the plateau that the people regard with something like wariness. Not fear. Wariness. It is not dangerous to them — it is too small, too slight, its jaws built for rodents and small lizards, not for humans. But it is quick and it is silent and it appears and disappears with a fluidity that seems wrong on the plateau, where everything else is slow and present and fixed. The fox-creature is the one thing here that moves like it is not here. It passes through the town in the dim light of early morning, a shadow between the buildings, and it is gone, and the people who see it do not speak of it, not because it is forbidden but because there is nothing to say. It was here. It left. It will come back. This is its nature.

The kept insects — the large, flightless ones in the stone pens — are the closest thing the people have to livestock. They are not pets. They are not loved. They are maintained. They are fed bromeliad leaves and vegetable scraps, and they grow, and they are killed, and they are eaten, and their shells are used for bowls and scoops and the broad, flat panels that cover the holes in the walls where the wind comes through. The insects do not resist. They do not run. They stand in their pens and click their rattling, metronomic click, and they eat what is given to them, and they are here, and they have been here for as long as the people have been here, and no one knows which came first, the people or the insects, and it does not matter.

## The Birds and What They Mean

The small brown birds — the ones that sing in the morning — live in the bromeliads. They build their nests in the cisterns, on platforms of sticks above the water, and their eggs are small and speckled and warm, and the people know where the nests are and do not harvest water from those plants until the chicks have fledged. This is not a rule. It is a knowledge — the knowledge that the birds are the morning, and without the birds there is no morning, and without morning there is no order, and without order there is no plateau. The birds are not worshipped. They are not sacred. They are *necessary*, the way the stone is necessary, and necessary things are treated with a respect that has no name because it does not need one.

The hunting bird — the hawk-sized thing that stoops on lizards with a sound like tearing cloth — is feared by the lizards and respected by the people. When it calls — the single, piercing whistle that drops in pitch — every lizard on the plateau freezes, and the people stop what they are doing for a moment and listen. Not because the call is dangerous. Because it is beautiful, in a way that the people recognize but do not have a word for. The sound cuts through everything — through the beetles, through the wind, through the murmur of the town — and for a moment the plateau is silent except for that one sound, and the silence is not absence but presence, the presence of one clear thing in a world of indistinct ones.

The night bird is the mystery. It is rarely seen. It calls after dark — the low, resonant boom that vibrates in the chest and seems to come from the ground — and the people say it is the voice of the plateau. They may be right. The sound has a quality that is not quite natural, or rather, it is so natural that it transcends the distinction between the living and the geological. It sounds like the stone itself is calling. It sounds like the molten thing the stone used to be, three billion years ago, is remembering what it was and speaking in the language of that memory. The people gather when it calls. They gather because the sound pulls them together the way gravity pulls things together — not by force, but by nature, by the simple fact that the sound is a center and they are near it, and things that are near a center move toward it.

## The Plants as Living Companions

The bromeliads are not plants to the people. They are not resources. They are not even partners, because partnership implies a separation, a contract, a negotiation between distinct parties. The bromeliads are *part of the people*. The people are part of the bromeliads. The relationship is not symbiotic in the biological sense — or rather, it is, but the biological sense is too small for it. It is more than symbiosis. It is co-presence. It is the fact of being in the same place for so long that the boundaries between *you* and *it* have worn away, the way the boundaries between the stone and the moss have worn away, until you cannot say where one ends and the other begins.

The people tend the plants because the plants are them. The plants hold the water because the water is the people. The people drink the water because the water is the plants. The cycle is not a cycle. It is a state. It is the way a thing is when it has been the same way for so long that the way-it-is has become its nature, and the nature has become its identity, and the identity is inseparable from the place.

When a bromeliad dies — and they do die, slowly, over years, the leaves thinning and the cistern shrinking and the holdfast loosening from the stone — the people mourn it. Not loudly. Not with ceremony. With a quiet attention, a pause in the daily rhythm, a moment of looking at the plant and noting its absence before the pup is planted and the cycle resumes. The mourning is not for the plant. It is for the time the plant contained. A bromeliad that has lived for thirty years has held water for twenty-five of them, and that water has been drunk by people who are now dead, and the plant has been tended by people who are now dead, and the plant itself is now dying, and with it goes a small thread of the fabric, a thread that has been part of the fabric for longer than anyone alive can remember, and its absence is a hole, and the hole is noticed.

## What They Are, In the End

The people of Ayaviri are not a civilization. They are not a culture. They are not a society, in the way those words are usually used, with their implications of structure and institution and accumulated complexity. They are an *expression*. They are what happens when a place and a people have been together long enough that the people have become the place and the place has become the people, and the question of which shaped which is meaningless because the shaping happened in both directions at once, over a time so long that the directionality has been erased.

They are small. They are quiet. They are dark-skinned and dusty and their hands are cracked and their eyes are narrowed and their clothing is coarse and their tools are stone and bone and they do not write and they do not count and they do not build monuments and they do not leave, and they are the most complete people you will ever encounter, because completeness is what happens when you stop becoming and start being, and they have been being for a very long time.

They live in a room with no walls and no ceiling and no floor, only stone and dust and light and sound, and the room is the world, and the world is the plateau, and the plateau is enough, and they are enough for it, and this is not a small thing. This is not a primitive thing. This is a thing that has taken three billion years to make — the stone, the dust, the plants, the animals, the people, the sound of the beetles, the call of the night bird, the marks on the wall, the water in the cistern, the child's hand on the leaf, the old man's pebble on the stone — and it is finished, and it is here, and it lives.
