# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here was invited.

The Condensate Works was built to be empty. That is not a side effect — it is the purpose. The vast resin floor, the precise channels, the breathing vents, the cycling systems: all of it exists to manage a process, and the process does not require occupants. The Works is a machine that happens to be the size of a town, and like most machines, it was designed with the assumption that nothing would move inside it that wasn't part of its operation.

But machines leak. Not just coolant, not just working fluid — they leak *possibility*. They create conditions that didn't exist before, and conditions are opportunities, and opportunities are eventually taken. The Works has been running long enough that its leaks have become habitats.

---

## The Film

Start with what is smallest and most certain.

In the floor channels, the dark gelatinous film is the oldest living thing here, or the closest to what we would call living. It has no name because no one has studied it. It exists in a niche that did not exist until someone poured a resin floor, cut eight-centimeter drainage channels into it, and ran a constant thin sheet of water through those channels at four degrees Celsius in perpetual amber half-light. No natural environment on Earth matches these conditions. No natural environment anywhere matches these conditions. The film is a native of the Works.

It is not algae. It is not a bacterium, or not only a bacterium. It is not a slime mold, though it behaves like one in the limited sense that it moves without appendages and grows without direction. Under a microscope — and no one has put it under a microscope, but if someone did — it would probably resolve into something tangled and difficult, a community of organisms that have found a way to share a boundary, a consortium of the unclassifiable living off the mineral content of the resin and the dissolved organics in the air that condenses into the channels. The fungal taste you notice when the vents exhale — that is partly the film. Its metabolic byproducts enter the water, the water evaporates into the air, the air moves through the ducts, and you breathe it. You are already part of its distribution system.

It grows slowly. A centimeter in ten minutes when conditions are right, which means a particular combination of water flow rate, temperature, and the mineral content of whatever the Works is condensing out of the air in that section. It retreats when conditions change — when a vent opens and exhales a blast of subzero air, the film contracts, pulling back from the channel edges, contracting into a denser mass that looks almost deliberate, almost like a decision. When the vent closes and the temperature stabilizes, it advances again.

It does not think. It is not aware. But it responds, and in a place this empty, response looks enough like intention that you find yourself watching it longer than you should, crouching by a floor channel in the cold, waiting for the tendril to extend another centimeter, as though it is going somewhere on purpose.

It is everywhere in the Works. Every channel. Every basin. Every place where water moves slowly across resin, the film is there, doing what it does, which is existing in a space that was made for nothing and making it into something. Not enough to disrupt the Works' function. Not enough to clog the channels or alter the flow. It has calibrated itself — or been calibrated by time and selection — to live just under the threshold of interference. The Works does not know it is there. Or if the Works knows, the Works does not care.

This is the first relationship of the Condensate Works: an organism that has made itself small enough to be tolerated by a system that cannot tolerate anything.

## The Threads

Higher up, in the trusses, the pale threads are harder to account for.

They are not spiderwebs. There are no spiders here. There are no insects. Nothing that we would recognize as a food chain exists in the Works, nothing that would support a predator that builds webs. The threads are not predation. They are not hunting. They are just there, strung between conduit bundles and truss members, beading with condensation, occasionally breaking and falling.

They are probably biological. Probably. The regularity of their spacing — the way they run parallel, the way they branch at consistent angles — suggests something with a growth pattern, something that extends along a substrate and puts out holdfasts at intervals. But the material is strange. It does not burn. It does not dissolve in water. It does not seem to degrade. It holds condensation more effectively than any natural fiber should, which means either it has evolved to do this or it was engineered to do this, and both possibilities lead to uncomfortable questions.

If it evolved, then something in the Works has been here long enough to develop a structure that does not exist anywhere else, a body plan built entirely around the collection of water from condensing air. If it was engineered, then someone put it here deliberately, and the question becomes: who, and why, and do they know it is still here, and does it matter?

The threads catch water. That is what they do. They are netting, essentially — passive netting, strung through the trusses, intercepting the droplets that fall from the ceiling and holding them until the weight exceeds the thread's tensile strength and the water drops in a small wet curtain to the floor. It is a method of water collection that requires no energy, no moving parts, no decision-making. You grow, you catch, you break, you regrow. The water falls. The floor channels carry it to the basins. The film feeds on it. The Works processes it. The cycle continues.

Something up there in the dark, above the amber light, above the reach of your vision, is building these threads. You will not see it. It may not want to be seen, or it may be so small and so slow and so unlike what you expect a living thing to look like that you would not recognize it even if you were looking directly at it. But the threads are maintained. New ones appear. Broken ones regrow. The net is being woven right now, in the dark, in the wet, while the Works breathes around it.

## The Thing That Made the Tracks

This is the part that changes everything, or would change everything if anyone were here to register the change.

Something walks in the Condensate Works.

The tracks in the basin sediment are small — maybe four centimeters across, many-toed, a gait that suggests a body low to the ground, moving with the deliberation of something that knows where it is going. The toes are narrow, splayed, and the spacing between footfalls indicates a creature roughly the size of a large rat or a small cat, but the gait is neither. It is too regular for a rodent, too purposeful. The tracks do not wander. They enter the basin from one direction, cross it in a straight line, and exit the other side. Whatever made them was traveling.

Traveling where? In a place that is almost entirely empty, with no visible food source and no visible shelter, what is worth traveling to?

The answer, if there is one, is: the Works itself. The processes. The cycles. The vents that open and close, the temperature gradients, the water channels and the basins and the frost on the heat exchangers. Something has learned the Works' rhythm and has built a life inside its metabolism. It knows when the vents will open and where the cold air will flow. It knows which channels carry the warmest water and where the film is thickest. It knows where the frost forms and when it sublimates and what falls out of the air when the temperature drops below a certain point. It has, in effect, learned to read the Works the way a shorebird reads a tide — not through understanding but through attention, through the patient accumulation of pattern over time.

What it eats is uncertain. The film, possibly — it is organic, or partly organic, and it grows in accessible locations. The pale threads, maybe, though those seem less nutritious and more structural. Or something else entirely, something the Works processes or produces that we cannot identify because we do not understand what the Works is making. If the Works is a condenser — if its purpose is to pull something out of the air and collect it — then there are products, and byproducts, and things that form at the boundaries of processes, and something down here has learned to live on the margins of those processes the way certain organisms live on the margins of hydrothermal vents, not in the heat but in the gradient, in the space where one system meets another and something usable precipitates out.

It is probably not alone. A single organism does not leave tracks in only one basin. If it walks here, it walks everywhere, following routes that only make sense if you can perceive the Works as a landscape of opportunities rather than an expanse of emptiness. To you, the floor is a featureless plane. To it, the floor is a map — the warm channels and the cold zones, the feeding spots and the safe corridors, the places where the vents will open and the places where the ceiling drips heaviest. It reads the Works the way you would read a city if you had grown up in it. Not through thought. Through inhabitation.

There may be more than one. There may be many. The emptiness of the Works is not evidence of absence; it is the condition that makes the presence possible. In a space this large, with resources this dispersed, a population of small, careful, cold-adapted organisms could live at very low density and never encounter each other except when they chose to, except when the cycles brought them to the same basin at the same time, except when the Works, in its breathing, its cycling, its indifferent management of water and air, created the conditions for meeting.

Or there may be one. One thing that has been here as long as the film, as long as the threads, learning the Works so thoroughly that it has become part of it. Not a pest. Not an invader. A component that was never installed but installed itself, filling a niche that the designers did not know existed, because designers rarely think about what moves into the spaces they leave empty.

## The Works Itself

And then there is the question of whether the Works is alive.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a ship is alive or a city is alive or a machine is alive in the imagination of someone who has spent too long with it. The question is literal: does the Condensate Works meet any definition of life that a careful person would accept?

It metabolizes. It takes in air and water and processes them, produces outputs, cycles its inputs through systems that change their state in response to conditions. It responds to stimuli — the vents open and close, the turbine changes pitch, the relays click and make decisions based on parameters that are invisible from the floor. It grows, or it grew — the sections of different density, the clusters of equipment, the pillars that do different things, these suggest a structure that expanded over time, that was built in phases, that accumulated complexity the way a reef accumulates complexity. It does not reproduce, but not everything that is alive reproduces, and not everything that reproduces is alive.

It breathes. Twelve-second cycle. In, out, in, out. You can feel the air move. You can time it. It is as regular as a sleeping animal, and if you stand still long enough, if you let the cold and the wet and the dim settle into you, you start to breathe with it. Not consciously. Not intentionally. Your body syncopates to the rhythm of the space, and for a moment, you and the Works are on the same cycle, and the distinction between occupant and environment becomes a technicality.

The Works does not know you are here. That is almost certainly true. Its sensors, if it has sensors, are tuned to temperature and humidity and flow rate and pressure, not to the presence of a warm mammal standing on its floor. You are below its resolution. You are noise. You are a heat signature that it will correct for without ever registering as a person.

But the Works does not know the film is there, either. The Works does not know the threads are there. The Works does not know that something walks its floor channels and reads its rhythms and lives in the gaps of its processes. The Works does not know, and the life inside it does not care that it does not know, and the whole system — the machine and the film and the threads and the thing that made the tracks and you, standing on the resin floor in the amber half-light, breathing in time with a structure that was built to manage condensation and has become, through no intention of its own, an ecosystem — holds together anyway.

The Works is a habitat. It is a habitat the way a cave is a habitat, the way a hydrothermal vent is a habitat, the way a storm drain is a habitat. It was not made for living things. It does not welcome living things. It does not support living things through any generosity of design or purpose. It simply exists, and existing creates conditions, and conditions create niches, and niches are filled, eventually, by whatever is small enough and patient enough and strange enough to fit.

---

What lives in the Condensate Works is this: a film that has no name, growing in the channels where the water runs. A structure of pale threads in the trusses, maintained by something unseen. A creature, or several creatures, that walk the floor in straight lines and know the Works better than you ever will. And the Works itself, breathing and cycling and managing its water, not alive in any way you could prove and not dead in any way you could prove either, running its processes in the cold and the dark with the patience of something that has never once considered stopping.

They are all here. They are all, in their different ways, at home.
