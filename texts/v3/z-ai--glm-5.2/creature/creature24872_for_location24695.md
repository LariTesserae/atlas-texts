# What Lives Here

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here lives alone.

The organisms — the pale, thumb-length, eyeless, softly segmented things that carpet the basin and flow in living rivers across the terraces — do not have a name. Or rather, they have had names, several of them, given by the people who came here with stone tools and patience and the particular courage required to descend into a wound in the limestone and stand in a living light. Those names are lost. The people who carried them are gone. What remains is the organisms themselves, which have no use for names, which have no concept of isolation, which exist in numbers that make the word *individual* a kind of joke.

So: start with what they are not. They are not insects. They lack the structural rigidity, the chitin armor, the jointed precision that defines that lineage. They are softer, more provisional — as though the template for their bodies was sketched but never finalized. Each segment is a faintly distinct swelling, visible under the bioluminescence as a series of pale ridges, but the segments do not correspond to specialized organs in the way you would expect. There is no head as such. There is no tail. Both ends are capable of leading, and the organisms reverse direction without ceremony, the front becoming the back, the back becoming the front, the body simply continuing in whichever direction the chemical gradient pulls it. They have mouths — or mouth-analogues, openings ringed with rasping mandible-structures that are not true mandibles but serve the same function — at both ends. They feed in both directions. They move in both directions. They are, in a very literal sense, creatures without a primary orientation, and this seems to suit them.

Their bioluminescence is not decorative. It is not communication, or if it is, the communication is not of the sort that carries meaning — not signal but state, the way a coal glows because it is hot. The light is a byproduct of the chemistry that powers their feeding. They process the mineral-rich moisture that seeps through the limestone — the iron, the calcium, the trace compounds that give the cavern its alkaline tang — and the reaction produces light as exhaust. When they are feeding actively, on fresh extrusions, on newly exposed mineral paste, they burn brighter. When they are between meals, traversing stone already scored and etched by previous passage, they dim to a faint blue-white ghost-glow, barely visible, the organism equivalent of an ember. This is why the cavern pulses: the light tracks the feeding, and the feeding tracks the geology, and the geology is constant but uneven, and so the light shifts and flows and brightens in one place while dimming in another, and the whole space breathes with a luminance that is, fundamentally, the visible signature of digestion.

They molt. This is important. The pale sediment in the basin floor — the thick, chalky accumulation that the ancient bowl was meant to hold — is shed material. Carapace-fragments, segment-casings, the discarded architecture of bodies that grow continuously and must periodically abandon their outer layer. The molting happens in waves, triggered by some internal rhythm or external cue that is not obvious — perhaps the temperature surges, perhaps the mineral content of the moisture, perhaps something subtler. When a wave of molting passes through the population, the living rivers thicken with translucent ghosts of shed skin, and the organisms themselves, newly exposed, are brighter for a time, their light unfiltered by the older outer layer. The sediment accumulates fastest during these waves. It is fine, almost powdery when dry, but in the basin it is damp, compressed by the constant passage of bodies over it into a dense paste that has a mineral smell — alkaline, clean, the smell of chalk and iron and something else, something that might be the residual chemistry of the bioluminescent reaction, something that has a faint luminance of its own even separated from the living bodies that produced it.

This is what was collected. The sediment. The molts. The residue of the organisms' growth, compressed and gathered and carried up in shallow bowls carved from the local rock. What it was used for is less clear. It has properties — the faint self-luminance, the mineral density, the alkaline reactivity — but whether it was pigment, medicine, material for ritual, or simply something valued for reasons that did not survive the people who held them, the cavern does not say. The cavern keeps its secrets the way it keeps everything: by moving on.

---

The organisms do not live in colonies. This is a distinction that matters. A colony implies structure — workers, queens, specialization, a social architecture with roles and boundaries. These organisms have none of that. They are a *population*, not a society. They aggregate because the resource they feed on is unevenly distributed, and aggregation is the simplest response to uneven distribution: go where the food is. When a fresh extrusion opens, the nearest organisms move toward it, and the organisms nearest *those* move toward *them*, drawn by the chemical trail of active feeding, and the result is a river, a channel, a living current that has no source and no destination but is simply the shape the population takes when it is responding to a gradient. When the extrusion cools, when the mineral paste hardens and the surface is scored and etched and the pickings thin, the river dissipates. The organisms disperse. They do not go home. They do not have homes. They spread out across the scored stone, feeding at lower intensity, maintaining themselves, waiting — if a thing without cognition can be said to wait — for the next fracture, the next surge, the next pulse of warm mineral from below.

They reproduce, but the mechanism is not easily observed. There are no eggs, no nests, no pairing. Among the molting shed-material in the basin, there are occasionally smaller organisms — not juveniles in the way you might expect, but miniature versions of the adults, fully formed, simply smaller, as though they were budded or fragmented from the parent body during the molting process. It may be that the molting itself is the reproductive event — that under certain conditions, a shed segment does not die but continues, grows, becomes a new organism. If so, then every wave of molting is also a wave of generation, and the population grows in surges that track the geological activity below, more rock meaning more food meaning more organisms meaning more erosion meaning more exposed rock meaning more food, a feedback loop that has been running for longer than the human structures on the terraces have existed, that will run long after those structures have been worn smooth and polished into anonymity.

They do not avoid you. This is not courage. It is not aggression. It is the absence of any mechanism that would produce avoidance. They do not have predators — nothing down here eats them, nothing down here competes with them. They have never had reason to develop fear. When a boot is placed in their path, they encounter it, attempt to feed on it, find it unpalatable, and continue past it. The few that climb are not exploring. They are following the chemical traces on the leather — salt, sweat, the residue of the surface world — and when those traces are exhausted, they lose interest and move on. You are not a threat. You are not a friend. You are an obstacle with an unusual chemical signature, and when you are gone, the organisms will score the stone where you stood, and in a week there will be no evidence that you were ever there.

This is not indifference, exactly. Indifference implies the capacity for interest. The organisms do not have that capacity. They are not conscious in any way that maps onto the word. They are not unconscious, either — that implies a suspension of something normally present. They are simply *going*. They are metabolic systems in soft pale bodies, processing mineral, producing light, shedding skin, occasionally fragmenting into new versions of themselves, and they have been doing this for so long that the cavern is as much their product as the geology's. The terraces they have scored. The basin they have excavated, particle by particle, generation by generation, the slow patient work of millions of mouths on stone. The shape of the space is a collaboration between pressure from below and feeding from above, and neither party is aware of the other, and the collaboration is none the less real for that.

---

And the people.

The people are gone. That is the first thing to say and the hardest thing to hold. They are gone, and the organisms do not know they were here, and the rock does not remember, and the walls they built are being worn smooth by the passage of bodies that do not distinguish between built stone and natural stone, that score and etch the one as readily as the other, that will eventually erase every mark the people made, every fitted edge, every pecked surface, every shallow bowl carved with patience and carried up through the wound in the limestone into a world of sky and weather that the organisms will never see.

But they were here. And you can know something about them from what they left.

They came down. This is the first fact. They found the entrance — the same wound in the limestone you descended through, or another like it — and they went down, into the sound, into the dark, into the warm air with its iron-and-ammonia tang, and they did not turn back. This required something. Not technology — the tools are stone, the construction is dry-fit, the engineering is selection and placement. Something else. Want, perhaps. Need. The specific hunger that drives a person underground into a place that sounds like a second heartbeat and glows with a light that is alive.

They built walls. Not many. Not shelters, primarily — the structures are too slight, too scattered, too positioned for that. They are *containments*. Low walls across terrace gaps, funneling the flow. Partial structures at the basin edge, positioned where the living rivers converge most thickly. Blinds, perhaps. Collection points. The architecture of someone who needs to stand in a specific place and have the organisms pass through a specific channel, not because the organisms can be directed — they cannot, they flow around obstacles with the patient fluidity of water — but because the *sediment* accumulates in specific places, in the eddies and slow spots behind obstructions, and the walls create eddies, and the eddies collect the shed material, and the collection is easier when the flow is shaped.

They worked with the organisms, not against them. This is clear from the wear patterns. The walls are not barriers — they are worn smooth on *both* sides, meaning the organisms pass over them freely, meaning the builders did not try to exclude the organisms from any area but rather shaped the terrain to concentrate the sediment where it could be gathered. They understood the flow. They understood the molting waves. They knew when the rivers would thicken and when the sediment would be richest, and they timed their visits accordingly, and they carried shallow bowls down and filled them and carried them back up, and the residue in those bowls was the reason they came.

Who they were — what culture, what era, what language — is not recoverable from what remains. The stone tools are too generic. The construction is too simple. The wear has erased whatever marks might have identified the builders. They could have been recent — centuries ago, a millennium — or they could have been ancient beyond reckoning, older than the surface civilizations whose ruins are studied and catalogued and named. The organisms do not care about timeframes. The stone does not care. The walls stand because they are stone and stone is patient, and they are being erased, and the erasure is also patient, and both processes continue simultaneously, the building and the unbuilding, and neither one is winning because the people are gone and the organisms have no concept of winning.

They are gone but they are not entirely absent. Not in the way that extinction is absent, the clean severance of a lineage. They are gone the way a path is gone when the forest grows over it — the path existed, the forest remembers by its shape, the trees grew around the space where the path was, and if you know how to look, you can see the ghost of the passage in the arrangement of the trunks. The people left a shape in the cavern. Not a memory — the cavern does not remember. A *pattern*. The walls create flows. The flows create eddies. The eddies collect sediment. The sediment is rich in the byproducts of the bioluminescent chemistry. And the sediment glows, faintly, in the dark, long after the organisms have passed over it, a residual light that accumulates in the basin and in the eddies behind the walls, and this light is brightest exactly where the walls are, exactly where the people built, exactly where the collection happened, as though the work of human hands created a structure in the flow of bodies that persists in the flow of light, a pattern that outlasts the pattern-makers.

The people are in the light. Not literally. Not mystically. But in the way that a river is in the shape of its valley — the thing that passed through left a form, and the form persists, and the form is the closest thing to a trace that remains. The cavern glows brighter where the walls are. The organisms do not know this. The rock does not know this. But it is true, and if you stand on the third terrace where the low wall funnels the flow into a narrow channel, and you look down at the pale sediment collecting in the eddy behind the fitted stones, and you see it glowing — faintly, steadily, a blue-white light that is not the organisms but is *of* the organisms, is what they leave behind, is the residue of their living — then you are seeing something that the people who built this wall saw, and the seeing connects you to them across whatever distance of time and loss separates your eyes from theirs, and the connection is real even though no one in the cavern knows it is happening.

---

What lives in the Seething Concavity is a process. The organisms live. The rock lives, in the slow way that rock lives, flowing and fracturing and cooling. The water lives, seeping and vanishing and seeping again. The light lives, produced by living bodies, lingering in the sediment of dead bodies, pulsing with the rhythm of feeding and molting and the geological surges from below. The people lived, and their living left a shape in the flow, and the shape persists, and the shape is a kind of living too, the way a footprint in wet cement is a kind of presence after the foot is gone.

What lives here is a collaboration between everything that is here, and none of the collaborators know the others exist, and the collaboration produces the space itself, and the space is not finished, and the space will never be finished, and the organisms will keep feeding and the rock will keep flowing and the sediment will keep glowing and the walls will keep wearing and the water will keep seeping and the light will keep pulsing, and none of it will pause for you, and none of it needs you, and the freedom and the loneliness of that are the same thing, and you feel both of them at once, standing in the warm dark, inside the verb, inside the process that has no name, inside the light that is alive and does not care that you can see it.
