# What Lives There

The first thing to understand is that the people who live here do not think of themselves as living *in* the city. They live *with* it. The distinction matters because the city is not a container. It is a condition. You do not occupy the High City of Weeping Stone the way you occupy a house. You persist in it the way the lichen persists — by agreeing to the terms the place sets, by becoming something that the stone can tolerate.

---

They are not many. Or they are many and you cannot tell, because the city swallows count. A population distributed across something this vast, in passages this narrow, behind walls this dense, becomes invisible to arithmetic. You could live your whole life in one terrace-cluster — a dozen rooms connected by low doorways and shared courtyards, watered by a single conduit that surfaces from beneath the floor of the lowest room — and know perhaps sixty people by name. You would know them the way you know the stone in your courtyard: by texture, by the sound they make moving through a passage, by how they take their water, by which walls they maintain. Sixty people. And twenty paces away, on the other side of a wall you share, another household. And twenty paces beyond them, another. The city holds them like cells in tissue, each one walled, each one touching its neighbors, each one unaware of the totality of the body it composes.

They call themselves something. Probably. The description does not give us their word for themselves, and maybe that is right, because the word would not translate. It would be a word that includes the stone and the water and the specific quality of the fog at the hour they say it. A word that changes weight depending on which terrace you say it from. What matters is that they have a word and it is theirs and it does not travel well.

---

Their bodies have adapted. Not in the dramatic way of cavefish — no blindness, no pallor that marks them as a separate kind of creature. They look like people. But they look like people who have been shaped by a place, the way a tree shaped by wind looks like a tree. They are lean. Not from scarcity — there is food, or there is enough — but from movement. To live here is to walk. Everything is stairs. Everything is passage. Everything is a distance measured in vertical steps and lateral squeezes, and the body that does this from birth is a body that has been continuously trimmed of anything unnecessary. They carry no excess. Not out of discipline but out of physics: the passages reward the narrow, the corridors reward the short-strided, the low doorways reward the person who moves through the world slightly bowed and does not find it degrading to do so.

Their hands are rough. Not the roughness of calluses from a single repeated labor but a general roughness — a texture, like sandstone itself — because their hands are their primary tools. The description mentions no tools that survive as a recognizable category. This does not mean the people are without technology. It means their technology is their hands and their patience and their knowledge of how stone fits against stone. They build by feel. They maintain by feel. When a channel silts up with mineral deposit, someone goes and clears it with their fingers and a piece of harder stone, and they do this not as a chore but as a thing they do the way you breathe — because it needs doing and because they know how and because their grandmother did it and showed them where the channel runs and how it breathes.

Their skin is damp. Not wet — damp. The ambient humidity is high enough that dry skin is not a condition they experience. Their hands, pressed to stone, come away wetter than the stone. They do not towel off. They do not think of this as a condition requiring remedy. When they touch each other — a hand on a wrist, a shoulder pressed to a shoulder in a narrow passage — the contact is cool and slick, and this is simply what touch is here. Their hair lies flat. Their clothing, what little the description implies, is minimal and adheres to them, and the fabric — if it is fabric, if it is not something else, some processed fiber from the sparse tough grasses of the upper terraces — is the kind of material that does not try to stay dry because it cannot. They have given up on dryness as a goal. Dryness is a fantasy of the lowlands. They are past that.

---

They eat from the channels. Not directly — the channels are not streams with fish — but the channels create conditions. The slow water, the moss, the pale filamentous growth in the deep passages: these support a micro-ecology that, if you know it, can be harvested. The eyeless creatures — the pale, soft things the size of a finger joint — are food, or part of food, or are gathered for some purpose that is not strictly caloric. The people know where they live. They know which cisterns hold them, which conduits they travel through, and they reach into the dark wet spaces and gather them with the unselfconsciousness of people collecting something they have always collected. There is also, on the upper terraces, the tough grass — and grass means grain, or something grain-adjacent, something small and hard and nutritious that can be ground between stones and mixed with water and eaten as a paste. And the moss in the slow channels is harvested, or some of it is, the darkest growth, the kind that has been wet longest, and it is dried — carefully, in the brief afternoon sun on the upper terraces — and it becomes something else, something with a mineral taste, something that holds.

They cook. The description mentions smoke. So there is fire, which means there is fuel, which means something combustible reaches this place despite the absence of trees. The tough grasses, dried. The liverwort-mats, dried, though they burn poorly. Perhaps something brought up from the lowlands in trade — but trade implies contact with the outside, and the description gives no clear evidence of regular contact. More likely: the people have solved the problem of fire with what is available, and the smoke from their fires is thin and acrid and smells of mineral and wet grass, and it moves through the passages the way everything moves through the passages — slowly, along the ceiling, following the air currents that the city's geometry creates. You smell it before you see the fire. You smell it and you know someone is near, someone is cooking, someone is three rooms away doing the thing that people do when the fog comes in and the afternoon rain has passed and the evening settles into the grey-blue of cool stone.

---

They do not speak the way people in dry places speak. Their language — and they have one, or several, or one that shades into several across the terraces the way the architecture shades from one period to another — is quiet. The description tells us that sound carries strangely here, that a voice raised in one courtyard arrives in another as a whisper, and that people have learned to speak quietly or not at all. This has consequences. A language spoken at the volume of breathing is a language that uses different sounds than a language spoken at the volume of assertion. It is a language of consonants that carry through stone — the hard closures, the stops, the sounds that wet walls reflect rather than absorb. It is a language of breath and tongue and teeth. Vowels are short, almost swallowed. The mouth does not open wide. To speak here is to speak into the space between your teeth, to let the sound travel along the wall rather than through the air. They murmur. They mutter. They have words that are indistinguishable from the sound of water in a conduit, and this may not be accidental. A language that evolved in a city that weeps will have absorbed the weeping into its phonemes.

They have songs. Or they have something that functions as songs but is not sung — it is exhaled. A low humming that two or three people do together in a room, sitting close, the vibration traveling through the stone floor and up through their bodies. It is not performance. It is not for an audience. It is the sound a group of people make when they are together in the dark and the fog is heavy outside and the water is moving through the walls and they want to feel the stone under them respond. The stone does respond. It resonates. A room full of humming people and wet stone becomes a single instrument, and the sound is not beautiful in any way that translates — it is low, it is almost subliminal, it is the sound the city would make if the city could close its mouth and hum.

---

They have children. This needs to be said because the description, in its attention to stone and water and process, might leave the impression of a place without children, a place of adults moving through ritual. But there are children, and the children are the ones who know the city best, because they have explored it with the recklessness and curiosity that children bring to any environment, and the city — for all its hardness, its cold surfaces, its weeping walls — is not dangerous in the way a city of machines is dangerous, or a city of traffic. It is dangerous the way a canyon is dangerous: you can fall, you can get lost, you can go too deep and not find your way back before the cold settles into your bones. But children learn the canyons. Children learn which stairways go down and which come back up. Children learn which cisterns have the pale creatures in them and which conduits you can crawl through and which rooms are empty and which have someone living in them and which are so old that the walls have started to return to bedrock.

The children play a game — or something that is a game and also something else — where they move through the passages in the fog with their eyes closed. They know the stone by feel. They know which way to turn by the moisture on the walls — wetter means lower, means deeper, means closer to the water table. They navigate by touch and by the sound of water and by the temperature of the air, and a child who has grown up in the High City can find their way home from anywhere within their known range without opening their eyes. This is not a trick. It is literacy. They can read the city with their skin.

---

The old people go deeper. This is the thing that takes longest to understand. The city has layers — the description makes this clear — and the layers get older as you descend, and the lower you go, the fewer people live there, and the ones who do are older, and the rooms are darker, and the water is closer, and the stone is colder. There is a pattern, or there seems to be a pattern, though the description does not state it explicitly: as people age, they move down. Not all of them. Not as a rule. But some. The deep rooms — the ones where the sun never reaches, where the fog is permanent, where the pale filamentous growth glows on the walls — are not empty. Someone is there. An old woman, perhaps, who has lived on this terrace for longer than anyone on the terraces above can remember, who maintains a cistern that feeds three courtyards she will never see again, who knows which channel is silting and which is running clear because she can hear the difference in the sound the water makes as it passes beneath her floor. She does not come up. The climb is too much. The light would hurt. She has become a creature of the deep city the way the pale things in the conduits have become creatures of the dark — not because she was forced but because she went, gradually, the way water goes, and now she is there, and she is part of the lowest layer, and when she dies someone will find her — or will not find her, and the room will become a room with old bones in it, and eventually the bones will become part of the floor, and the floor will become part of the bedrock, and the city will have absorbed another layer.

This is not morbid to them. Or if it is, it is the kind of morbid that the word cannot hold. It is what happens. The city is a geology, the description says, and geologies bury their own. The people know this. They live with this. The walls they touch every day are made of stone that was shaped by hands that are now part of the stone beneath the stone. The people who built the upper terraces are in the lower terraces. The people who built the lower terraces are in the bedrock. The people who live here now will, in time, be in the walls that someone yet unborn will maintain. This is not a belief system. It is a material fact. The city teaches it by being what it is.

---

And the others. The eyeless things in the conduits. The dark birds that pass but do not land. The lichen, the moss, the pale growth in the deeps. They live here too. They are not peripheral — or they are peripheral the way the description says life in this city is peripheral: as exception, as footnote, as thing that happens in the margins. But the margins, in a city this vast, are themselves vast. The conduits alone — the miles of covered channel running beneath floors and through walls — constitute a habitat larger than many towns, and the pale things that live in them have been there as long as the water has, which is to say as long as the stone. They are not pets. They are not pests. They are cohabitants. The people know them and do not name them, or name them in a way that is not naming so much as acknowledging — a sound made when a pale form crosses a wet wall in the lamplight, a sound that means *that one*, *the one that lives here*, *the one that was here before me and will be here after*.

The dark birds are different. The birds are visitors. They come from higher cliffs — real cliffs, the kind the plateau edges become where it drops away to the lowlands — and they ride the updrafts along the rim, and sometimes they tilt inland and cross the upper terraces, and their shadows move across courtyards where people are sitting or working or simply being, and the people look up, and there is a moment of recognition. Not between individual and individual. Between species and species. The people know the birds are not of the city. The birds know the city is not theirs. And the shadow passes, and the bird is gone, and the people look down, and the wet stone is still wet, and the water is still moving, and the fog is still sitting in the passages like something kept there.

---

What lives in the High City of Weeping Stone, then, is this: a people who have become an extension of the stone, who move through the city the way blood moves through a body — not seen, not heard, present in the warmth of the walls they maintain and the channels they clear and the rooms they inhabit. A people who speak in murmurs and navigate by touch and eat what the water provides and go deeper as they age until they disappear into the geology that made them. A people who are not many and not few, who are distributed across a vastness they will never fully know, who live in intervals between walls, who are held by the city the way the city holds its water — patiently, in the dark, close together, for a very long time.

And the pale things in the conduits. And the lichen. And the moss. And the tough grass on the upper terraces. And the dark birds that pass.

And the stone itself, which the description insists is not dead, which weeps and breathes and shifts and grows and clears its own channels and holds, and holds, and holds.
