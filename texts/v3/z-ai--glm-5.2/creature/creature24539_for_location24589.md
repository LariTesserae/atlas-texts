# What Lives in the Hollow

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is alien. That word implies a wrongness of origin, a creature in the wrong place. Everything in the Hollow is in exactly the right place. It is you who are alien. You who carry the wrong temperature, the wrong rhythm, the wrong eyes. What lives here has solved the problem of existence without light, without green, without sky, and the solution is not a compromise or a degradation. It is an answer as complete as any forest, any reef, any grassland you have ever walked through and called beautiful. The Hollow's inhabitants do not know they are strange. They do not know they are anything. They simply are, and they are thoroughly, and they are enough.

---

## The Mats

Begin with what is almost not alive, because everything else begins here.

The pale grey film that covers the lowest surfaces of the stone basins is not one thing. You learn this if you kneel — and you will kneel, because standing is too tall for this place, because the architecture is low and the life is low and your full height feels like an insult to the scale of things — and look closely at a patch where the film is thick. It is not uniform. It is not even one color. Up close, in the faintest ambient light that reaches the shallowest parts of the basin, the mat is a landscape of its own: streaks of pale lavender where the mineral content of the seepage is slightly different, patches of faint copper-green where the sulfur concentration is higher, veins of near-transparency where the film is new, still wet, still forming. The iridescence you noticed from standing height is not a property of the film itself but of the condensation on the film — the water beading on the surface catches whatever light exists and fractures it into small smeary rainbows, and the effect is accidental, the way oil on a puddle is accidental, and it is beautiful in the same expendable way.

The mat is alive in the way a coral is alive — not one organism but a city of them, a consortium. There are filaments, thinner than hair, that you can see if you lift a piece of the film and hold it up to whatever faint light reaches your hands. The filaments branch and reconnect and branch again, forming a net, a mesh, and the mesh is the structure on which the chemistry happens. The chemistry is this: the limestone is calcium carbonate, and the water dissolving it is slightly acidic — not from rain, not from sky-acid, but from the carbon dioxide the mat itself produces as a byproduct of its own metabolism. The mat is eating the stone and then eating the dissolved stone and the byproduct of that eating dissolves more stone and the cycle is not incidental, it is *designed*, not by intelligence but by the patient logic of survival, the organism shaping its environment the way a beaver shapes a river, except the beaver builds a dam and the mat builds a world.

There are things living in the mat that are not the mat. Tiny things, too small to see, that move through the filaments in slow gliding motions, grazing on the edges, pruning the growth the way an herbivore prunes a grassland. And there are things living in the things living in the mat — smaller still, parasitic or commensal, nobody has determined which, nobody may ever determine which, because the Hollow does not publish its findings. The mat is a continent. The mat is a continent with its own grazers and its own predators and its own decomposers, and you are standing on it, and your boot print leaves a scar that will heal in three or four days as the filaments grow back in, and the mat does not mind, and the mat does not notice.

What the mat smells like is the smell of the Hollow itself. Sulfur and chalk and wet metal. If you could distill the place into a single scent, it would be the scent of the mat, and the scent is the scent of chemistry happening, of bonds breaking and forming, of minerals being pried loose from stone and incorporated into flesh that is barely flesh, and the process is slow and continuous and patient beyond any human framework for patience. The mat has been here since before the stone finished eroding. The mat may have caused the erosion. The mat may be the reason the Hollow exists.

## The Flat Things

The grazers of the mat — the visible ones, the ones you can feel underfoot — are the flat things, and they are the first animals you encounter in any meaningful sense, because they are everywhere, because they are the ground's own motion.

They are not insects. They are not isopods, though they roll when disturbed, and they are not trilobites, though something about their segmentation calls to mind fossils you have seen in museum cases, those ancient armored shapes that lived in oceans that no longer exist. They are themselves. They are something that has been here long enough to have no need of resemblance.

A flat thing is roughly the size and shape of a sycamore leaf, if a sycamore leaf were three-quarters of an inch thick and segmented laterally, each segment a separate plate of something that is not chitin and not keratin but something between — a biomineral composite, dense and smooth, slightly yielding, like hard cartilage. The plates articulate against each other with a flexibility that allows the body to ripple, and the rippling is how they move: a wave of contraction that runs from the head end to the tail end, pressing the underside against the stone and pushing forward. The motion is slow by your standards — a flat thing crosses a pool's edge in the time it takes you to notice it, watch it, and lose interest — but it is continuous, and they never stop. They move while they eat. They eat while they move. The underside, which is wet with the condensation that covers everything, is also covered in structures that are too small to see but that you can feel if you pick one up — and you will pick one up, because they do not flee, because they have no reason to flee — and the underside feels like very fine sandpaper, like the tongue of a cat, and it is functioning as a rasping surface, scraping the mat from the stone and conveying it to the mouth, which is not on the head, which is on the underside, which is a slit, a ventral groove, a structure that is more like a planarian's mouth than anything you are familiar with.

They do not have eyes. They do not have eye-spots. They do not have any structure that suggests they have ever been able to detect light. What they have, along their lateral edges, is a line of fine filaments — sensory hairs, or something like sensory hairs, that detect vibration and chemical gradient and possibly the electrical signature of other living things. You know this because when a flat thing is undisturbed, its filaments lie flat against the stone, but when you move near it, when your foot strikes the ground, the filaments rise — a wave of lifting, a flaring, like grass in wind — and the flat thing alters course, not away from you but *toward* the more interesting vibration, which is sometimes you, because you are new, and newness is information, and information is food in the way that food is food.

Their backs are dry. This is the one detail that seems wrong, that seems impossible in a place where everything is wet. But the flat things' dorsal surface — the biomineral plates, the segmented armor — is covered in something that repels water. A wax, perhaps. A hydrophobic secretion. And so they are two things at once: wet underneath, dry on top, and the line between wet and dry is their body, and their body is the border between two states of being, and they do not know this, and they do not care.

They click. The clicking is the sound of their plates articulating against each other, against the stone, against the plates of other flat things when they pass over each other, which they do — they crawl over each other without hesitation, without aggression, a pile of flat things in a depression in the stone is not a fight but a traffic jam, a temporary accumulation, and they sort themselves out with a patience that is either profound or mechanical, and there is no way to tell the difference. The clicking of one flat thing is barely audible. The clicking of a million flat things is the white noise of the Hollow, the static that fills the space between the thrum and the roar, and you cannot imagine the place without it, and after a few hours you stop hearing it consciously, and it becomes the texture of silence, and silence in the Hollow is not the absence of sound but the presence of so much sound that your ears surrender their attempts to parse it.

They breed in the pools. You see this once, or you think you see it — a cluster of flat things at the edge of a shallow basin, their bodies overlapping, their edges touching, and from the points of contact something is being released into the water, something that disperses, something microscopic, and the breeding is not intimate, not paired, not selective. It is broadcast. It is the release of potential into a medium and the trust that the medium will carry it where it needs to go. The flat things do not choose mates. They do not choose anything. They move toward stimulus and away from stimulus and the sum of their moving is their life, and their life is sufficient, and they have been sufficient for longer than the sky has been a rumor.

When a flat thing dies — and they die constantly, you find their empty shells on the stone, the plates scattered, the filaments still — the body dries, and the plates separate, and the wind that does not exist here but the air-movement that does, the slow tidal breath of condensation, carries the plates into the low places where they become substrate for the mat. The mat grows on the dead flat thing. The dead flat thing becomes stone, becomes chemistry, becomes life. The loop is closed by the smallest and most patient of hands.

## The Pool Things

The pools are not lakes. The pools are not even ponds. The pools are shallows — depressions in the limestone, some no larger than a bathtub, where the condensation has gathered faster than it can evaporate, and the water is mineral-clear and still and cold, cold enough to make your teeth ache if you hold your hand in it, and the things that live in the pools are the things that have adapted to cold, still, mineral-saturated water in the dark, and they are not like anything that lives in the pools you know.

The most common is the one you think of as all-mouth-and-tail, though this is unkind, because it is also all-body, it is just that the mouth and the tail are what you notice, because the mouth is enormous and the tail is constant and the body between them is functional and unremarkable.

The mouth runs the width of the head, which is the width of the body, which is the width of the animal — it is a creature built around the act of taking in water and filtering from it whatever the water carries. The mouth is not toothed. It is not jawed. It is a gape, a permanent openness, and inside the gape are structures that you can see if you crouch at the pool's edge and wait — feathery, branching, pale things, like the gill rakers of a whale shark but finer, more intricate, and they are moving constantly, sweeping the water that passes through the mouth, catching particles — mat fragments, flat thing eggs, the microscopic grazers of the mat, debris, mineral crystals, anything organic, anything that can be digested. The filter-feeding is continuous and mechanical. The pool thing does not hunt. The pool thing does not choose. The pool thing opens its mouth and the world enters, and the world is mostly water, and the water is mostly empty, and the empty passes through and the not-empty stays, and this is enough.

The tail is what moves it. A long, flat, muscular tail, finless, fringed along the edges with the same sensory filaments the flat things have, and it undulates — side to side, not up and down, a motion that reminds you of a tadpole, of a salamander larva, of something that has not finished becoming what it will be. But the pool thing *has* finished. It is not a larva. It is not a juvenile. It is complete. The tail is its locomotion and its steering and its stability, and when it swims — which is always, because the pool thing does not rest, because the filter-feeding requires flow, because the mouth must always be processing water — the tail moves in a slow sinuous wave that sends the body gliding through the pool with an efficiency that is beautiful, actually beautiful, the way a slow river is beautiful, the way a gliding bird is beautiful, because the motion is exactly suited to the medium and the medium is exactly suited to the motion.

They are pale. Everything in the Hollow is pale. Pigment is a luxury of light, a response to light, a tool for absorbing or reflecting or signaling with light, and there is no light here, and there has been no light here for so long that the genes for pigment have drifted, have mutated, have been lost, have been repurposed. The pool things are the color of the stone they swim over: a translucent white, a chalk-pale, and you can see the internal structures through the skin in the moments when they pass close to the surface — the dark thread of the gut, the pulsing of whatever serves as a heart, the branching of whatever serves as gills. They are transparent not by evolution but by default. They never needed to be opaque. The world never asked them to be opaque. They are windows into themselves, and they do not know this, and they do not care.

The pool things grow to the size of the pool. This is not a metaphor. In the larger pools — the house-sized depressions where the water is ankle-deep — the pool things are large, broad, the size of your forearm or larger. In the small pools — the bathtub basins — the pool things are small, finger-length, proportionally identical. You do not know if the small ones are young or if they are stunted or if they are a different species entirely, a dwarf form adapted to the smaller volume, and you will not know, because the Hollow does not answer questions, it simply is, and the pool things are the size they are and the pools are the size they are and the correspondence between the two is not your business.

When a pool thing dies, it floats — for a moment, before the gas in its body equalizes and it sinks — and the flat things find it within minutes. They swarm the body in a rippling mass, rasping with their sandpaper undersides, and the body is disassembled from the outside in, and the water clouds with particulate, and the particulate is caught by other pool things, who filter it, who process it, who convert it into their own bodies, and the dead thing becomes the living things and the living things will die and become other living things and the pool is a closed vessel, a microcosm of the Hollow itself, a world within a world within a world.

## The Winged Things

The air is not empty. This is the lesson the winged things teach you, and they teach it by collision, by the constant soft impact of their bodies against your face and your hands and any exposed skin, and you learn to keep your mouth closed, to squint even though squinting does no good in a place where there is nothing to see, to wave your hands in front of you in a gesture that is not quite a shooing and not quite a feeling, a gesture that is half navigation and half communication, as though you are trying to tell the winged things: *I am here, I am solid, please go around me.* They do not go around you. They go through you, over you, past you, and their wings brush your skin, and their wings are wet, and each contact leaves a tiny cold spot of condensation that evaporates in seconds and is replaced by another contact, another spot, and you are being mapped by touch, by collision, by the accumulated data of a thousand small impacts, and the winged things are building a picture of you that is more accurate than any picture your eyes could build of them.

Because you almost never see them. You feel them. You hear them — a fluttering that is subsumed into the larger sound but that has its own texture, its own frequency, a papery rustling that is different from the clicking and different from the thrumming and different from the roaring, a sound that belongs to the air the way the clicking belongs to the ground. They are the air's inhabitants. The air is their medium the way the water is the pool things' medium and the stone is the flat things' medium, and they fill it as completely.

When you do see one — when one passes close enough to your face that you can make out details in whatever faint light reaches the shallows of the Hollow — it is small. The size of a moth, or a lacewing, or a midge. Its wings are broad and translucent and veined, and the veins are not the branching fractal patterns of insect wings but something more regular, more grid-like, a lattice, and you think: *this is not an insect, this is not a flying insect, this is something that has converged on the flight-plan of an insect from a completely different direction.* The body between the wings is soft, pale, vaguely segmented, and the head — if it is a head — carries a cluster of structures that are not antennae and not eyes but something else, something that you cannot resolve into a familiar shape, something that is probably sensory in a way you do not have the equipment to understand.

They navigate by sound. You are nearly certain of this. The winged things produce ultrasonic clicks — above your hearing range, above the range of the clicking that fills the audible spectrum — and the echoes return to them from the stone walls, the pools, the bodies of other animals, your body, and from these echoes they construct a world that is richer and more detailed than anything your eyes have given you in this place. They do not need light. They have something better than light. They have sound, and sound touches everything, and everything speaks, and everything that speaks is known.

What they eat: the pool things, when the pool things surface. The flat things, when the flat things are exposed on the ridges. Each other. They land on their prey — landing is a graceless, scrambling thing, the wings folding awkwardly, the legs — too many legs, or too few, you cannot tell in the dark — scrabbling for purchase — and the mouth, which is on the underside, which is a slit, which is a groove, which is familiar now, the same ventral architecture as the flat things, a shared blueprint, a common ancestry — opens and presses against the prey and rasps. The rasping sound is part of the clicking. Every click in the Hollow is the sound of something eating something else, or the sound of something moving toward something else to eat it, or the sound of something having eaten and moving away, and the winged things contribute their portion to the symphony, and their portion is the portion of the air, the portion of the high frequencies, the portion of the flight.

They breed in the air. You think. You have never seen one land for any purpose other than feeding. The breeding is a mid-air collision — two winged things striking each other, clinging for a moment with their too-many-or-too-few legs, separating, dispersing, and whatever has been exchanged is exchanged, and the eggs — if they are eggs, if they are anything you would recognize as eggs — are dropped into the pools or onto the mats or simply into the air, where they drift on the condensation-currents like pollen, like dust, like the smallest possible vessels of possibility, and the Hollow catches them and distributes them and some land where they can grow and some do not, and this is the system, and the system works, and the air is never empty.

## The Roarers

You feel the first one before you hear it. No — you hear it before you feel it, but you have been hearing the roarers since your descent began, and you did not know what you were hearing, and so the feeling is the first time you *identify* what you are sensing, the first time the data resolves into a shape, and the shape is: something large is near, something large is moving, something large is aware of you in a way that the flat things and the pool things and the winged things are not.

The displacement of air. That is the first sign. The air in the Hollow is still, is heavy, is saturated with moisture, and when a roarer moves through it, the air has to go somewhere, and it goes toward you, a pressure wave, a warm exhale, and the warmth is wrong, is different from the stone's coolness, is different from the condensation's chill, is the warmth of a body, a large body, a body that is generating heat through metabolism, through muscle, through the chemical furnaces of a life that is bigger than yours. You feel the warmth before you see the shape, and the warmth is a presence, and the presence is an announcement, and the announcement is: *I am here, I am large, I am moving, make way or do not, I am not navigating around you.*

And then the shape. In the dark — in the dark your eyes have surrendered to, the dark that has texture but not detail — you assemble the roarer from fragments. A broad, low body. Not tall — the roarers do not need height, have never needed height, the Hollow rewards breadth, rewards surface area, rewards the ability to cover ground efficiently, and the roarer's body is built like a hill: wide, rounded, sloping, a shape that seems to have been weathered into existence rather than grown. Its skin — or hide, or surface — is dark, darker than the stone, darker than the dark, and it is covered in condensation, beaded and glistening, and the condensation catches whatever faint light exists and gives the roarer a halo, a rim of faint luminescence that is not luminescence, that is just water, just physics, just the optics of a wet surface in a place where light is scarce enough to make water glow.

The limbs. Four of them. Thick, columnar, placed widely, and they move not in the rippling wave of the flat things but in a deliberate, plodding, *considered* gait, each leg lifted and placed and lifted and placed, and you can hear the feet striking the stone — a heavy, padded sound, a muffled impact, and the impacts have a rhythm, and the rhythm is the rhythm of a large animal that is not hurrying, that has no reason to hurry, that is moving through its home with the confidence of something that has never encountered a reason to move quickly. The feet are broad. The feet are soft. They leave wet prints on the stone that you will find later, prints the size of dinner plates, and you will put your hand in one and feel the warmth still there, and the warmth will make the hollow seem more alive than anything else has, because the warmth is the warmth of a body, and the body was here, and the body is close.

And the mouth. The mouth that roars. It runs the width of the body, which is the width of the animal, which is broad, and it is lipless, and it is always slightly open, and inside it are the flat dark structures that are not teeth but that serve the same function — crushing, grinding, processing — and they are arranged in rows, in plates, like the paving stones of a path, and the path leads to the throat, and the throat is dark, and you do not look into it for long because looking into it requires proximity and proximity is not something the roarer encourages, not because it is aggressive but because it is *indifferent*, and indifference at this scale is more frightening than aggression, because aggression at least acknowledges you as something worth opposing.

The roar itself. When it comes — when the roarer stops, lifts its head slightly, opens its mouth wider, and the sound begins — you understand why the place is organized around it. The roar is not just loud. It is *full*. It contains frequencies that you can hear and frequencies that you cannot, frequencies that you feel in your chest and your stomach and the fluid of your eyes, frequencies that make the water in the pools shiver and the condensation on the stone bead tighter and the flat things pause in their rasping and the winged things scatter for a moment before regrouping. The roar is a declaration. It says: *I am here, this is my ground, this is my water, these are my stones, you are in my space.* And the other roarers answer — from across the Hollow, from the ridges, from the basins — and each answer is slightly different, slightly offset, and the delays between the roars tell you the distances, and the distances tell you the scale, and the scale is vast, and the roarers are calling to each other across miles of dark, and each call is a thread in a network that holds the Hollow together, a web of sound that is territory and courtship and warning and identity all at once, all compressed into a single enormous vocalization that lasts three seconds and recurs every few minutes and has been recurring every few minutes for longer than you can imagine.

The roarers eat everything. You see this. You see a roarer pause at the edge of a pool and lower its head — its broad, mouth-first head — into the water, and the mouth opens, and the water flows in, and the pool things that were in the water flow in, and the flat things that were at the edge flow in, and the mat that was growing on the pool's floor flows in, and the roarer lifts its head and the water pours out of the sides of its mouth — filtering, it is filtering, the flat dark plates are filters, and the water exits and the biomass stays — and the swallowing is visible, a peristaltic wave that runs down the body, and the body shudders with it, and then the roarer moves on, and the pool is emptier, and the system has transferred energy from one tier to another, from the small to the large, from the many to the few.

When a roarer dies — and they die, and you have found the corpses, and the corpses are landscapes — the Hollow digests them. The flat things come first, in their thousands, rasping the surface, opening the skin, exposing the interior. The winged things follow, landing on the exposed tissue, laying eggs in it, the eggs hatching into larvae that are not larvae but are the winged things' juvenile form, if they have a juvenile form, if the life cycle is anything you would recognize. The mats grow inward, the filaments penetrating the tissue, colonizing the dead body from the inside, converting the roarer's substance into mat-substance, and the process takes — you do not know how long it takes. The first corpse you found was largely skeleton, the bones — if they are bones, they are dense, mineral-heavy, more like stone than bone — stripped and scattered and half-covered with the pale grey film. The second corpse you found was fresher, still wet, still warm, and the flat things were thick on it, a living carpet, and you could hear the clicking intensify as you approached, and the clicking was the sound of feeding, the sound of conversion, the sound of the system processing its own dead, and it was the most natural thing you had encountered in the Hollow, and it was the most terrible, and the two were the same.

## The Thrillers

You have not seen them. You will not see them. They are the thrum, the subsonic vibration that comes from the ground, from below the ground, from the stone beneath the stone, and they are there, and they are alive, and they are enormous, or they are many, or they are both.

What you know: the thrumming is biological. You know this because it cycles. A geological signal would be constant, would be the steady output of a system in equilibrium. A mechanical signal would be regular, would have the rhythm of a machine. The thrumming is neither. It swells and recedes in patterns that are irregular but not random, patterns that have structure but not repetition, patterns that feel — and you are aware of how unscientific this sounds — *conversational*. As though the thrumming is a communication between things that are too large to move and too deep to see and too strange to understand, and they are speaking to each other in a language that is not language, in a frequency that is below frequency, in a medium that is stone.

You put your hand on the ground during a swell, and you feel it — a vibration so deep that it is not a vibration but a *presence*, a shift in the quality of the stone, a change in what the stone is doing, and for a moment the stone seems less like a solid and more like a membrane, a skin over something, a surface stretched across something that is moving underneath. You pull your hand away. The stone is stone again. The thrumming continues.

What they might be: burrowers. Deep-burrowers. Creatures that live in the limestone below the Hollow's floor, in caverns and tunnels that the condensation has carved over millennia, a deeper Hollow beneath the Hollow, a world below the world. The burrowers — if burrowers — are the largest things here. Larger than the roarers. Perhaps the size of the ridges themselves, the low rounded ridges that you have been walking over, that you thought were stone, that might be — might be — the backs of things, the buried bodies of things, the ancient and enormous architects of the Hollow itself. You do not know. You cannot know. The thrumming is the only evidence, and the evidence is felt, not seen, and the feeling is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is the Hollow's final gift to you: the knowledge that there are things here you will never understand, that the place is deeper than its depth, that the life here has layers you cannot reach, and the layers have their own life, and their own sound, and their own time.

## The System

What lives in the Hollow is not a list. It is not a catalogue. It is a process, and the process is the thing itself, and the thing itself is alive in a way that no individual creature is alive. The flat things eat the mats, and the pool things eat the flat things, and the winged things eat the pool things, and the roarers eat everything, and the mats eat the dead, and the dead feed the stone, and the stone feeds the mats, and the cycle is not a diagram, not a food web drawn on paper, not a concept. It is a sound. It is the clicking and the thrumming and the roaring and the fluttering and the dripping, all at once, all the time, and the sound is the sound of the system metabolizing itself, and the system is the organism, and the organism is the Hollow, and the Hollow is alive.

The rhythm is the proof. The cycles — the swells and recessions, the intensifications and thinnings, the sequences that repeat but not exactly — are the heartbeat of an organism that is miles across and older than memory. The roarers call and answer, and the calling and answering is the system talking to itself, checking its own status, confirming its own continuity. The flat things rasp and click, and the rasping and clicking is the system's digestion, the sound of conversion, of matter moving from one form to another. The pool things glide and filter, and the gliding and filtering is the system's circulation, the movement of nutrients through the body. The winged things flutter and collide, and the fluttering and colliding is the system's respiration, the exchange of information through the medium of the air. And the thrumming — the deep, felt, subsonic thrumming from below — is the system's pulse, the rhythm of something so large and so old that its heartbeat is measured in minutes, not seconds, and its lifespan is measured in epochs, not years.

What lives in the Hollow is the Hollow.

---

You are standing in it. You are wet. You are cool. You are surrounded by life that does not need you, that did not expect you, that will not remember you. A flat thing crawls over your boot. A winged thing strikes your ear. A pool thing breaks the surface of the nearest basin and resubmerges. From somewhere in the dark — close, far, impossible to tell — a roarer opens its wide mouth and declares itself, and the Hollow answers, and the answer includes you, because you are here, and you are part of the sound now, and the sound is part of the system, and the system is metabolizing you, slowly, gently, without malice, without preference, drawing the warmth from your feet and the moisture from your breath and incorporating them into its own enormous, patient, endless process.

You could stay. You are already becoming part of it. The condensation is beading on your skin. The flat things are exploring your boots. The winged things are mapping your shape. In a day, the mats will find you. In a week, the flat things will be rasping. In a month, the roarers will not need to go around you because you will be part of the ground, a slight rise in the stone, a new ridge, rounded and soft-edged, a shape the stone has given up willingly, like a creature that died without struggle.

Or you climb. Slowly. Uphill. Toward the grey.

What lives in the Hollow will not stop you. What lives in the Hollow will not notice.
