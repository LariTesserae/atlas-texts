# The Inhabitants of Vol Kharai

## The People

They are not old. That is the first surprise.

You expect longevity in a place like this — you expect the inhabitants to match their architecture, to be weathered, deep-grained, aged past the ordinary scale of human becoming. But the woman weaving at the frame has the skin of someone in her middle years. The man with the stone blade is perhaps fifty. The children are children — small, soft-skinned, their faces unfinished in the way all children's faces are unfinished. There is an elder, or someone you take to be an elder, sitting near the central cistern with her hands folded in her lap, but she is not ancient. She is old the way a well-maintained wall is old. The weather is on her but has not worn her down.

What distinguishes them is not age. It is tempo.

They move at the speed of the place. Not slowly — you have seen slow, and this is not slow the way a tired person is slow or a sick person is slow. It is the deliberateness of a body that has synchronized with something larger than itself and sees no reason to break that synchronization. The woman's hands on the loom: each pass of the shuttle is a complete act. She does not rush toward the next pass. She does not linger. She performs the motion and the motion ends and then she performs it again and the rhythm is the rhythm of the condensation forming on the eaves above her — bead, swell, release, bead, swell, release. You watch her for twenty minutes and the panel grows by an amount you could cover with your palm and she is not concerned about this. The cloth will be finished when it is finished. The cloth has always been being finished.

There are perhaps twenty of them. You count twice and get different numbers because they move through the settlement in patterns that are hard to track — not furtive, just unhurried, passing behind walls and between fern clusters, their dark clothing blending with the stone and the growth. They wear layers of the same woven fabric the buildings are paneled with — reed and bark cloth, soft with use, dyed in muted tones that come from the plants here: a greenish-grey, a rust-brown, a blue so dark it looks black until the light hits it and then it is the blue-violet of the moss. They wear their hair long, both men and women, bound with cords of braided plant fiber. Their feet are bare or wrapped in simple hide sandals. The hides are from the rodents — you see the fur later, stretched on a drying frame behind one of the buildings, and it is the same flat dark brown of the animals grazing in the ferns.

They do not gather in groups larger than three. This is not a rule. It is a property of how they occupy space. The settlement was built for hundreds and they move through it the way the beetles move through the moss — in patterns that use the whole field but never crowd any single point. You see two of them near the garden-terrace, working side by side, not speaking. You see one at the standing stone, standing close to it, one hand on its surface, doing nothing else. You see the children on the wall and an adult nearby, not watching them but present in the same general area, the way a fern and the rock it grows from are present together. The proximity is not supervision. It is co-location.

---

They do not speak much, and when they do, the sound is wrong.

Not wrong — attenuated. The air at this elevation, saturated as it is, seems to absorb certain frequencies. Vowels survive better than consonants. The sibilants arrive muted, the stops softened, and the overall effect is of language heard through a membrane. You catch the rhythm before you catch the content. The rhythm is slow. Each word lands and sits before the next word arrives, the way each drop of condensation sits on a leaf before joining the thread running down its stem.

You try again with the woman at the loom. You ask where the others went — the hundreds the settlement was built for. She looks up. She looks at you the way the birds look at you — with attention, without alarm, with an absence of the flinch-response that usually lives behind the eyes of a person confronted with a stranger. She considers the question. The consideration takes long enough that you almost speak again, almost fill the silence, but something in the rhythm of the place restrains you. You wait.

"They are here," she says.

You look around. You see the twenty, perhaps. You see the empty buildings. You see the cisterns and the channels and the garden-terraces tended but not harvested. You look back at her. She is weaving again. She does not elaborate. She does not need to, because from her perspective the answer is complete and the question was unnecessary. They are here. The infrastructure is maintained. The distinction you are drawing — between a settlement full of people and a settlement with twenty — is a distinction that belongs to a world organized around growth and loss, filling and emptying, and that is not the organization here.

Later, you think about this. You think about what it means to build for hundreds and house twenty and maintain the difference as if it were not a difference. You think about the buildings — kept clean, kept dry, kept ready, sleeping platforms made, hearths swept. You think about whether the twenty are a remnant or a choice or simply what the equilibrium of this place produces when the equilibrium of this place is applied to human lives. You do not reach a conclusion. The place does not invite conclusions. It invites the cessation of the impulse to conclude.

---

The children are the ones who stay with you.

Not because they are remarkable. Because they are the ones whose difference from the world you come from is most legible. They sit on the wall. They watch the water run in the channels. They do this for the hours you are watching them, and then you leave and walk the settlement and come back and they are still there, or they have moved to a different section of the same wall, and they are watching the water run in a different channel. Their eyes track the movement. Occasionally one of them extends a finger and touches the surface of the water and the surface tension distorts and recovers and the child watches the recovery with the same attention she gave the undisturbed flow.

They are not developmentally delayed. You see them solve problems — a loose stone in the wall is repositioned with an accuracy that suggests spatial reasoning fully intact. You see them communicate — a look between them, a small gesture, and both move to accommodate something you cannot identify. They are simply operating at the tempo of their environment, and their environment has no use for the acceleration that children in other places are subject to — the stimulation, the novelty, the serial engagement with one thing after another after another. Here there is one thing. The water. The moss. The stone. The fern. They are not under-stimulated. They are differently stimulated. They are attending to something your attention has been trained to slide past.

You think: a child raised here would not learn impatience. The concept would not form. There would be no occasion for it. Every need met at the pace the need requires. Every process completing at the pace the process dictates. The child would grow into an adult who moved like the woman at the loom, and the adult would age into an elder who sat like the elder at the cistern, and the cycle would not accelerate and would not slow and the settlement would maintain its twenty or its thirty or its however-many because that is the number this place sustains at equilibrium, and the buildings would stand ready for more because readiness is what buildings do here, and the channels would carry water and the moss would grow in its rings and everything would be exactly sufficient.

## The Creatures

The birds are the most visible. They are everywhere — on the walls, on the frameworks, on the ground between the buildings, walking with a hopping gait that is somehow unhurried even when it is quick. They are small, darker than the rock but not black — a deep slate-grey with a greenish cast in certain light, as if the same mineral sheen that touches the stone faces has touched their feathers. Their beaks are short, thick, adapted for cracking the seed-pods of the waxy-leafed herbs. Their eyes are bright and amber and very present. They watch you. They watch everything. But the watching does not produce the behaviors you associate with watching in birds — the tension, the readiness to flee, the calculation of distance. They watch the way the stone faces watch the sky. With patience. Without intention.

You do touch one, eventually. You cannot resist. You approach a bird on a low wall and extend your hand and the bird tilts its head and regards your fingers and does not move and you lay one finger against the feathers of its back and they are cool and dense and the bird shifts its weight slightly to accommodate the pressure and then settles. Its heart beats against your fingertip. It is fast — much faster than you expected, a rapid fluttering that contradicts the stillness of the body around it. The bird is not calm. The bird is running at full metabolic speed inside a body that has decided not to express that speed as motion. You withdraw your hand. The bird ruffles the touched feathers and resumes its regard of the middle distance.

The rodents are less visible but more numerous. You see them at the edges of things — a dark shape in a gap between foundation stones, a smooth head emerging from a hole in a garden wall, two flat eyes reflecting the grey light. They are larger than you first thought — the size of a small cat, compact, their fur sleeked flat by the damp. They graze on the ferns, standing on their hind legs to reach the fronds, pulling them down with their forepaws and eating with a methodical chewing that produces no sound you can hear. They are unafraid of the birds. The birds are unafraid of them. You see a bird and a rodent sharing the same stone shelf, the bird preening, the rodent eating, each occupying its own attention without reference to the other.

The insects are the ones that change your understanding.

The beetles. The blue-violet beetles in the blue-violet moss. You spend an afternoon on your knees at the edge of a moss colony, watching them. They are the size of your thumbnail. Their shells are iridescent — the same indigo-black as the moss they live in, brightening at the edges to a color that is not quite any color you have a name for. They move in lines. Not random lines — parallel lines, spaced evenly, traversing the surface of the moss colony in slow sweeps that take them from one edge to the other and back. As they move, their legs press into the moss and the moss compresses and then rebounds and you realize — slowly, because everything here is slow — that the beetles are aerating the moss. Pressing down the growth, opening channels for the condensation to reach the deeper layers. Tending it. The word you used earlier was not a projection. The beetles are maintaining the moss the way the people are maintaining the buildings. Not with intention, maybe. With function. With the deep programming of an organism that has found its niche and occupies it completely and has been occupying it long enough that the niche and the organism have shaped each other into something that looks, from the outside, like purpose.

And the moss responds. The circular colonies grow in perfect rings because the beetles maintain the boundaries — pressing back the edges, preventing overgrowth, keeping the circle true. Where two colonies meet, the beetles of each colony patrol the border and the border holds. It is not territorial. It is architectural. The moss and the beetles are building something together, and what they are building is a surface, a living surface, and the surface is the ground the moss grows on and the air the beetles breathe and the water that condenses on both and the whole system is a single organism distributed across the stone in rings of indigo and you realize that you are looking at something very old. Not old in years — old in iteration. This arrangement has been running for a very long time. Long enough to be optimized. Long enough to be beautiful.

---

There are other things. Things you do not see but infer.

Something lives in the cisterns. You see the disturbance in the still water — a ring expanding from a point below the surface, then another, then nothing. The water is clear. You kneel and look and see nothing but the mineral-stained stone lining and the reflection of the sky. But the woman at the loom sees you looking and says a word you do not catch and makes a gesture — a flat hand, palm down, lowered slowly — and you understand it to mean: they are there, they are always there, do not reach in.

Something moves in the ferns at night. Not the rodents — larger. A sound of fronds being displaced and then settling back, a passage of weight through the growth that is too substantial for anything you have seen during the day. You do not see what makes the sound. In the morning, the ferns are upright and undisturbed and there is no track, no broken stem, no sign that anything passed through. But the sound was real. Something large enough to move fronds lives in the fern-beds and moves at night and does not leave evidence of its moving. You think about the fresh-edged rock, the young stone that has not yet learned to be smooth, and you think about what might live in a place where the geology is recent and the ecosystem is old and the difference between those two timescales has produced forms that fit into the gap.

You find a feather you do not recognize. It is large — the length of your forearm — and dark, darker than the small birds' feathers, with a barbed softness that catches the condensation and holds it in jeweled drops along the rachis. It is not from any bird you have seen here. It is from something bigger. Something that uses the ridgelines. You find it near the cut stone face at the eastern edge, and you hold it against the grey light and the condensation runs down it and falls in a thread and the feather is perfect, undamaged, as if it was placed there rather than dropped.

---

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything that can live at this tempo, in this saturation, on this young sharp stone, lives here. And everything that lives here has been living here long enough to have become part of the place in a way that erases the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited.

The people are not separate from the settlement. They maintain it the way the beetles maintain the moss — not with a conscious decision to maintain, but with a function that has become indistinguishable from identity. The woman weaves because the woman weaves. The man sharpens the blade because the man sharpens the blade. The children watch the water because the children watch the water. These are not roles. They are not jobs. They are the expressions of a system in equilibrium, each element performing its function because the function is what the element is.

You think about the empty buildings. The hundreds the settlement was built for. You think about the woman's answer — *they are here* — and you begin to understand it differently. Not as evasion or mysticism. As literal. The buildings are full. The cisterns are full. The channels are full. The garden-terraces are full. The settlement is full — full of the people who live in it and full of the birds and the rodents and the beetles and the moss and the ferns and the thing in the cistern and the thing in the ferns at night and the bird that dropped the feather and the stone that holds the reflection and the water that cycles and the air that holds the damp and the silence that holds everything else. The settlement is full. It is full the way a held breath is full. It is full the way a perfect note sustained is full. It does not need more because more is a concept from outside, and outside is the road descending, and the road is already falling away behind you, and the damp is already drying on your skin, and you are already forgetting the rhythm, already accelerating, already returning to the tempo of a world that does not know what equilibrium is and mistakes stillness for death and sufficiency for lack.

They are there. They are not hiding. They will be there when the road has crumbled and the stone has learned to be smooth and the world outside has remade itself a hundred times in the time it takes for one panel of cloth to be woven on a frame in the cool damp air of a saddle between two ridgelines where the condensation forms and beads and runs and the birds murmur and the beetles move in their grids and the woman weaves and the children watch the water and the cut stone face holds its reflection of ferns and sky and the place is what it is, completely, without remainder.

They are there. They will be there. They have always been there.
