# What Lives Here

---

There is a problem with the question.

Not with the asking of it — the asking is natural, expected, the thing any mind does when it encounters a place: *who lives here?* The problem is with the answer, or rather with the framework that assumes an answer exists in the form the question expects. A name. A species. A body. Something you can point to and say *this, this is the inhabitant.*

The Drowned Plateau of Aur does not have inhabitants. It has *components.*

But that is not quite right either, because *component* implies assembly, implies parts that were once separate and came together, and nothing here was ever separate. Nothing here was ever *together* in the way that word suggests — pressed against each other, joined, fitted. Everything here grew into everything else the way a bone grows into the body that holds it: not assembled but *expressed.* The trunk is not in the forest. The trunk is the forest, in the shape of a trunk. The mat is not on the floor. The mat is the floor, becoming aware of its own surface.

So. What lives here.

---

## The Builders

Start with the walls, because the walls are the closest thing to an answer that takes a familiar shape.

Something made them. This is certain. The rectangles and spirals are too regular, too deliberate, to be the product of accretion or growth. They were *placed.* Something selected material from the plateau floor — that living stone, that half-coral substance — and shaped it and stacked it and arranged it in patterns that carried meaning, or that were meaning, or that were the last gesture of meaning before meaning became something else.

The Builders were small.

This is not a guess. The walls are knee-high. The spirals are tight, no more than a forearm's width across. Whatever hands shaped them were not large hands, and whatever body stood inside those walls was not a large body. They were warm — everything here is warm, but they were warm in the way that implies a metabolism, a furnace, a thing that eats and burns and exhausts. They were mobile. They moved through the canopy and the midwater the way anything mobile moves through a dense space: carefully, constantly adjusting, finding the gaps between trunks, slipping through the curtains of translucent sheet-organisms, riding the thermal currents from one layer to the next.

They had a relationship with the light. The amber-green glow of the trunks — the conversational pulse of those microorganism colonies — was their light. Their only light. They evolved in it. Their eyes, if they had eyes, were tuned to that narrow band, that bruise-colored spectrum, and they saw the world in shades that do not exist in any language that was built under an open sun. To them, the canopy was not dim. It was *luminous.* It was full of information. Every pulse was a sentence. Every dimming was a pause. They could read the forest the way you read a page — fluently, unconsciously, the meaning arriving before the words.

They were not fish. They were not anything you would recognize, not because they were alien but because they were *specific* — so specifically adapted to this one environment that they could not be compared to anything from any other. They were the shape of the space between the trunks. They were the density of the water given a will. They were small and warm and they could see in the color of old bruises and they made walls out of the floor and the walls meant something to them that the walls no longer mean, because the meaning required minds to hold it and the minds are gone.

Where did they go?

They did not leave. There is nowhere to leave to — no shore, no surface, no other place. And they did not die, not in the way that implies an event, a catastrophe, a ending. They *changed.* Or rather: they *stopped changing,* which in a place where everything has been settling for longer than geology can measure is the same as living. The Builders were the last mobile things. The last things that moved by choice. And at some point — not a moment, not a date, but a span of time so wide that it has no edges — they stopped.

Not died. Stopped. The way a traveler stops when they arrive home. The way a sentence stops when it has said what it has to say.

They are still here.

---

## What They Became

The Builders are in the walls. This is not metaphor. The walls are made of the living stone, and the living stone grows, and when the Builders stopped moving — when they settled, as everything here settles — they settled against the walls they had made, and the walls grew around them, and the growth was not consumption but *incorporation.* The forest ate them the way it ate the walls: slowly, completely, without violence. The stone grew over their surfaces. The microorganism colonies colonized their skin, their shells, whatever outer boundary they had, and began to glow. The trunks, reaching down from above, sent tendrils into the spaces where their bodies had been, and the tendrils became part of the structure, and the structure became part of the forest, and the forest became part of the plateau, and the plateau breathed.

If you press your ear to one of the walls — and you can, because the walls are everywhere in the second layer, grown into the trunks, incorporated into the lattice — you hear the thrum. The same deep, rhythmic processing that the trunks make. But it is different, subtly, in a way you cannot name but can feel. The trunk's thrum is conversion: one substance to another, input to output, the basic machinery of a living system. The wall's thrum is *retention.* It is holding something. Not processing it, not converting it, not passing it along. Holding it. The way a jar holds water. The way a fossil holds a shape.

The Builders are in the walls, and the walls are holding them, and they are not dead. They are *stored.*

This is what happens to life on the plateau. It does not die. It does not decompose in any way that implies loss. It *settles,* and settling is the process by which a living thing becomes a feature of the landscape. The Builders were the last things to undergo this process voluntarily — the last things that could be said to have made a choice about it, even if the choice was only the choice to stop moving. Everything else here settled so long ago that the distinction between living and being a landscape feature has ceased to exist.

The mat on the floor is the same substance as the walls, the same substance as the trunks, the same substance as the Builders. It is the primordial surface, yes — but it is also the primordial *inhabitants.* Everything that has ever lived on this plateau has, eventually, joined the mat. The mat is the accumulated biomass of every organism that has ever existed here, compressed and flattened and worn smooth by an amount of time that makes the word *ancient* sound like something you'd say about a yesterday. The mat is not growing. It is not dying. It is *being* every thing that has ever been, simultaneously, in a form so thin and so smooth that it has become a surface rather than a substance.

You are walking on them. All of them. Every thing that has ever lived on the Drowned Plateau of Aur is beneath your feet, and they are warm, and they are aware of you in the way that a surface is aware of weight — not cognitively, not emotionally, but *structurally.* They hold you. They have been holding things for longer than holding has been a verb.

---

## The Microorganisms

But the Builders were not alone, and they were not first. The things that live here now — the things that are unambiguously, indisputably alive in any sense that word can bear — are the microorganisms.

They are everywhere. In the trunks, in the water, in the mat, in the walls. They are the glow. They are the light. They are the closest thing this place has to a voice.

They are not bacteria. They are not archaea. They are not any domain of life that maps onto a taxonomy built from a world with a sun and a shore. They are *the* microorganisms — the original inhabitants, the first things, the things that were here when the plateau was dry, if it was ever dry, and that followed the water up, or grew the water up, or *became* the water, depending on which version of the story the chemistry tells.

They glow because they are communicating. This is known. The pulses — the conversational brightening and dimming of the trunks, the way one colony responds to another across hundreds of meters of water — are signal. Not metaphor. Not analogy. *Signal.* Information passes from one colony to another through bioluminescent pulses, and the information is not simple. It is not *food here* or *danger.* It is structural. It is about the state of the system. Temperature gradients. Current speed. The density of the canopy. The position of the thermal rivers. The rate of growth in the mat. They are maintaining a model of the entire plateau, in real time, across every cubic meter of water, and the model is not stored anywhere — it is *distributed,* held in the aggregate state of trillions of organisms all pulsing at once, the way a flock of birds holds its shape not in any individual bird but in the relationships between them.

They are the nervous system of the plateau. Not its brain — the plateau does not have a brain, does not need one, has evolved past the need for centralized processing the way it evolved past the need for mobile life. But its nervous system, yes. Its sensory surface. Its capacity to know itself.

And they remember.

This is the part that is difficult. The microorganisms have been here since before the mat, since before the trunks, since before the water. They have been pulsing in the same patterns, carrying the same signals, for a span of time that makes the walls seem like something built yesterday. And in those pulses — in the specific frequencies, the specific intervals, the specific sequences of brightening and dimming — there is information that does not correspond to the current state of the plateau. It corresponds to a *past* state. To states that no longer exist. To configurations of the forest that grew and changed and were replaced by other configurations, which grew and changed and were replaced in turn. The microorganisms carry a record. A history. A memory.

It is not accessible. Not to you, not to anything that thinks in the way you think. It is not a language, not an archive, not a story. It is a *pattern,* embedded in the chemistry of billions of organisms, carried forward because the organisms replicate and the replication preserves the pattern the way a wave preserves the shape of the water it moves through. The pattern is not *about* anything. It *is* the thing. The memory of the plateau is not stored in the microorganisms. The microorganisms *are* the memory, pulsing in the dark, carrying the shape of everything that has ever happened here in the timing of their light.

When a trunk brightens and another dims, they are not just exchanging information about the present. They are re-enacting an exchange that has happened before — millions of times, billions of times, the same frequencies in the same sequences, the conversation repeating itself because the conversation is the thing that holds the system together. The conversation *is* the staying. The pulses are the breath. The light is the law.

---

## The Geometry

And there is one more thing here. One more inhabitant, though *inhabitant* is the wrong word for something that is not a presence but a *condition.*

On the floor, in the third layer, where reality begins to fray, the loops of tissue grow in circles that feed into themselves. The branches join their own stems. The spirals do not close. This is not growth. This is not pattern. This is the *shape of the place itself* becoming visible — the geometry of a space that has existed for so long that it has developed habits. The loops are not organisms. They are the place, doing what places do when they have been one thing for long enough: they become *themselves,* and themselves is a shape, and the shape is a loop, and the loop has no beginning and no end because the place has forgotten which came first and does not care.

This is the final inhabitant. The geometry. The shape of the settled world. It is not alive in any way that requires the word, but it is not dead, because dead implies a transition from living, and this has never transitioned. It has been this shape since before shape was a concept. It is the form that matter takes when it has been in one place long enough to forget what shape it is supposed to be — which is to say, it is the shape that matter *is,* underneath all the other shapes, when you strip away the processes and the changes and the frantic motion of things that have not yet arrived at their final form.

The geometry lives here the way the law of gravity lives in a planet. Not as a resident. As a *rule.*

---

## So: who lives here?

The microorganisms, in their trillions, pulsing in the bruise-colored dark, carrying the memory of the plateau in the rhythm of their light.

The Builders, stored in the walls, held in the stone, their bodies incorporated into the architecture they made, their warmth retained in the thermal cycle of a system that does not waste anything.

The mat, which is every thing that has ever been here, compressed to a surface, warm and dry beneath the water, holding everything that stands or drifts or settles upon it.

The geometry, which is the shape of the place itself, the loops and spirals and self-joining branches that are not growth but *state* — the final condition of a world that has finished becoming and is now being.

And the plateau. Which is not a setting for these things but the thing itself — the organism that contains them, the body that holds them, the single vast warm entity that breathes on a cycle of days and stays on a cycle of forever. The plateau is alive the way a room is alive when the sound in it has been sounding for so long that the room has become the sound. The plateau is alive the way a river is alive when it has found its final course and runs it without variation, without deviation, without the possibility of being anything other than what it is.

Everything here is the plateau, doing different things. The trunks are the plateau reaching. The mat is the plateau resting. The microorganisms are the plateau thinking — slowly, in light, in a language made of brightness and dimness, a language that says only one thing, over and over, in a voice so quiet that it has become the silence itself.

The one thing it says is: *here.*

The one thing it says is: *still.*

The one thing it says is the thing that the Builders heard when they stopped moving, and the thing that the walls hold in their stone, and the thing that the loops of tissue on the floor have become the shape of:

*This is where I am. This is what I am. I have been here long enough that the question and the answer are the same word.*

That is who lives here.
