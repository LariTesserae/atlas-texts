# The People of the Reshaping Valley

They are not many. Forty-three at the last count, though "last count" is a phrase that loses meaning here because the count changes — not dramatically, not through catastrophe, but through the slow accretion and subtraction that any small population undergoes. A child is born. An elder walks into the active zone and does not walk back. A visitor arrives and stays longer than intended. A young person, restless, follows the southern rim out of the basin and does not return. The population hovers in the low forties, high thirties, and has for as long as anyone can remember, which is not very long, because the landscape does not permit long memories, and the people have internalized this. Their gene pool is small but not dangerously so. They are healthy. They are, by any reasonable measure, surviving. They would not use that word. They are *here*.

---

## Bodies

They are not tall. Generations of a diet heavy in vegetation and amphibian protein, of constant cold and wet, of a biome that never offers abundance but never quite permits starvation either — this has produced a people who are compact, dense, built close to the ground. The adults average perhaps a hundred fifty-five centimeters, the women slightly shorter, the men slightly broader, though the distinction is not dramatic. They carry weight in their cores and lower bodies, their center of gravity low, their legs thick and strong from a lifetime of walking on unstable substrate. Their feet are wide. This is noticeable immediately. Their feet splay, toes spread, soles thick and calloused into a pad that grips wet surfaces the way a frog's foot grips a wet leaf. Many of them do not wear shoes. Those who do wear shoes wear soft, permeable things — woven from the creeping vegetation, more sock than boot — that protect against cold but not against water, because protecting against water is not a concept that exists here. Water is. You move through it.

Their skin runs dark. Not a single tone — there is variation, as there is in any population, a range of browns from deep umber to something closer to the color of the wet bark on the squat trees — but the overall complexion is dark enough that in the permanent crepuscular light of the valley, their faces read first as shapes, then as faces, the way the structures in the active zone read first as geometry, then as architecture. Their skin is also, almost universally, *wet*. Not dripping — they are not aquatic — but carrying a sheen, a film, the same film that covers every surface in the valley. They do not towel off. They do not dry themselves. The concept of dry skin has been absent from their culture for so long that they do not have a word for it, or if they do, it is a borrowed word, something a visitor once used and they repeat without full comprehension, the way you might repeat a word in a language you don't speak.

Their hair is dark, straight, fine, and they wear it in ways that manage rather than style it — short, or bound back, or in some cases, shaved entirely. The moisture in the air makes length impractical. Those who keep their hair long do so for reasons that are not aesthetic. A woman in the southern cluster wears hers to her waist, and she uses it — wrings it, twists it, feeds it into the gaps in her weaving — as a kind of thread, a personal supply of strong, wet fiber that regrows. This is not considered strange. This is considered practical. The distinction between the body and the materials of the valley is, for these people, permeable.

Their hands are remarkable. Not in shape — the fingers are short, thick, calloused across the palm and the first joints — but in what they can sense. The residents read the ground through their hands the way a blind person reads braille. They press their palms to the substrate and feel for the deep vibrations that precede restructuring, the subtle thermal gradients that indicate where new material is rising, the faint electrical tingling that the active zones produce in the water-saturated peat. A experienced reader — anyone over about twelve — can tell you, with their hand on the ground, whether the earth beneath them will be stable for a day, a week, or an hour. They cannot explain how. The knowledge lives in the hand, not the mind. It is proprioceptive. It is learned by doing, not by instruction, and it is one of the few things they consider essential to teach their children: *put your hand here. Feel. Again. Feel.*

Their eyes have adapted. Not in any way you could measure with an instrument — or maybe you could, but no one has — but functionally, they see in the crepuscular gray the way other people see in daylight. Their pupils are large. They do not squint. They do not strain. When the amber glow of the active zones is the only light source, they navigate by it, reading the faint luminescence the way a sailor reads phosphorescence on a night sea. They do not use artificial light to see by. The panels in their dwellings produce warmth and a gentle amber glow, but it is not bright. It is not meant to be bright. It is meant to be warm. They sleep in it. They wake in it. The distinction between the two is not marked by light but by activity, by the body's own rhythms, which in the absence of day-night cycling have become deeply individual. One person sleeps while another weaves while another walks the rim checking instruments. The settlement is never fully asleep. The settlement is never fully awake. It breathes.

## Clothing and Material Culture

They wear layers, but not for warmth in the way you might expect. The cold is permanent and the cold is wet, and insulation is futile because insulation requires dry material, and nothing here is dry. Instead, their clothing is designed to manage the *rate* of wetness, to slow the loss of body heat by controlling how quickly water moves across the skin. The base layer is tight against the body — a woven fabric, almost a mesh, made from the fibers of the creeping ground-cover plant — that holds a thin, stable layer of water against the skin. The water in this layer warms to body temperature and stays. The outer layers are looser, also woven but with a wider gauge, and they allow excess water to drain away rather than accumulating. The effect is something like a wet suit, but organic, breathable, maintained rather than replaced. They wash their clothing by wearing it into the pools and channeling clean water through the layers. They maintain their clothing the way they maintain their buildings: continuously, without ceremony, as a condition of existence.

The waxy leaves of the ground-cover plant serve as the valley's universal material. They are waterproof on their upper surface, water-permeable on their lower, and they can be layered, stitched, pressed, or molded. The residents use them as membranes in their dwellings — a layer of leaves between the woven panels keeps wind and excess water out while allowing the slow passage of vapor that keeps the interior from becoming oppressive. They use them as containers — a folded leaf holds water, holds food, holds the small collected artifacts. They use them as bandages, as writing surfaces (scratched with a sharp fragment of the structural material, the wax holding the mark), as tools for handling hot surfaces, as impromptu hats. The ground-cover plant is, for these people, what wood and metal and plastic and glass are for other cultures. It is the material from which the everyday world is constructed. They do not cultivate it. It cultivates itself. They harvest it. The harvesting is careful, selective, never taking more than the mat can replace in a day, because they understand, at a level that does not require instruction, that the biome is not a resource but a partner.

The structural material — the dark, smooth, ceramic-like substance that the valley grows — is harder to work. It can be cut, but only with other pieces of itself, and only with effort. The residents have a small number of tools made from sharpened fragments of the material, passed down, used for cutting blocks from the columns and remnants when the opportunity presents itself. They do not cut living columns — columns that are still warm, still luminescent, still in the process of being grown. They cut only the cold ones, the ones that have been abandoned by the process or exposed by a collapse. Even then, they cut sparingly. The material is valued, but not hoarded. A household might have six or eight blocks of it. The generators are made of it. Some of the instruments are made of it. The larger artifacts — the geometric objects with internal channels — are made of it, but these are not made by the residents. These are found.

They do not work metal. They do not work stone. They do not fire ceramics. They do not have glass. Their material culture is entirely derived from the valley: plant fiber, plant leaves, the structural material in its raw and worked forms, and the water itself, which they use for everything from cleaning to temperature regulation to the transmission of small objects through the channels they have carved into the floors of their dwellings — because water flows here, always, and a thing placed in a channel will arrive, eventually, where the water goes.

## The Settlement

Forty-odd people in forty-odd buildings, and the buildings are not families and the families are not buildings. The arrangement is fluid. A person lives where they are useful, or where they prefer, or where the ground is stable enough today. A building might house one person or six. The composition changes as the buildings are moved, and the buildings are moved often — perhaps every ten or twelve days, on average, though it varies with the activity of the Reshaping. Moving a building is a communal event. Everyone helps. The building is lifted, not easily but with practiced coordination, from its platform, and the platform is dragged to the new location — a site selected by the readers, the people whose hands know the ground — and the building is set down again. It takes a few hours. It is not a crisis. It is not even an inconvenience, really. It is the thing that happens today, the way that in other places the thing that happens today might be rain, or a market, or a festival. They move. They settle. They move again.

There is no central building. There is no meeting hall. There is no leader's dwelling. Decisions are made by whoever is present, in the open spaces between buildings, in the moments between tasks, in the brief quiet periods when the Reshaping's sound drops and conversation becomes possible. The conversations are short. The decisions are practical. *The southern platform is tilting. We move it tomorrow. Who is available?* Hands go up. It is settled. There is no vote. There is no debate. There is assessment and response. The people who know the ground best are listened to most, but this is not a hierarchy — it is a practical recognition that some people's hands read better than others, the way some people in other cultures are better at math or music. It is a skill. It is respected. It does not confer authority beyond its domain.

There are children. Seven at the moment, ranging from an infant — three months old, born in a dwelling that has since been moved twice — to a girl of perhaps thirteen who is already an accomplished ground-reader and who will, within a year or two, begin making the decision about whether to stay or to leave. Most young people leave. Not all. Not most. But enough that the population remains small, that the settlement remains a settlement and not a village, that the gene pool requires occasional introduction from outside — visitors, travelers, the rare person who follows the southern rim *in* instead of out. The valley does not keep people against their will. The valley does not seem to care whether people stay or go. The valley is busy.

## How They Think

This is the hardest part to describe, because the inner lives of these people are not readily legible to an outsider, and they do not volunteered explanation. Their language is a dialect of something that might once have been a regional tongue — it has roots, structure, grammar that suggests a parent language — but it has been compressed by the environment. The vocabulary is dense with terms for ground states, water states, sound states, plant states. It is thin on abstractions. They do not have a word for *history*. They have a word for *before*, but it refers to spatial before, not temporal — the ground that was here before this ground, not the time that was here before this time. Time, for them, is not a line. It is a condition. It is wet, like the air. It does not flow. It *is*.

They do not have a creation myth. Or if they do, it is this: the valley is being made. It has always been being made. It will always be being made. There is no before-the-making and no after-the-making. The making is the condition. They live inside it. Asking them who made the valley is like asking a fish who made the ocean. The question does not compute. The ocean is. The valley is being. The distinction between *is* and *is being* is the central distinction in their worldview, and it is not a distinction they have to explain to each other because they all already know it. It is the thing you learn first, as a child, before you learn to read the ground. The ground is not stable. The ground is not unstable. The ground is *becoming*.

They do not have a religion in any recognizable sense. They do not worship the valley. They do not fear the valley. They do not personify the valley. The valley is not a god or a monster or a mother. It is a process. You do not worship a process. You do not pray to a process. You learn its patterns and you move when it moves and you stay when it stays and you do not waste energy on questions about its intentions because intentions require a mind and the valley does not have a mind, or if it does, its mind is so far beyond the scale of human comprehension that the question is not useful. This is not indifference. This is not nihilism. This is *attention*. They are paying attention to the thing that matters — the next reshaping, the next movement, the next breath of the ground — and they are not paying attention to the thing that does not matter — why.

This does not mean they are without feeling, without art, without interiority. The opposite, in fact. The constraint of the environment — the constant sound, the constant wet, the constant change — has compressed their emotional lives into something intense and efficient. They feel fiercely, but briefly. They do not dwell. A woman loses her dwelling to a subsidence event and watches it fold into the ground, and her face goes through something in ten seconds that in another culture might take weeks: shock, grief, acceptance, and then a turning-toward, a facing-the-next-thing, the hands already reaching for the platform, the eyes already scanning for stable ground. This is not emotional suppression. This is emotional *velocity*. They process at the speed the valley processes. They have to. If they dwelled, they would die.

Their art is in their weaving. The panels they make from the creeping vegetation are functional, but they are also patterned, and the patterns are not arbitrary. They encode ground-states. A particular pattern of knots means *this ground held for twelve days before subsiding*. Another means *the column here grew to four meters*. Another means *the water in this pool was warm and the amphibians came*. The patterns are not decorative. They are records. The buildings are covered in them. Each panel is a page, and each page describes a moment in the life of the ground beneath it. When a building is moved, the panels go with it, and the record continues. When a panel wears out, it is replaced, and the old one is — not archived. It is composted. It goes back into the ground cover. The record does not need to be permanent. Nothing here needs to be permanent. The record needs to be *current*.

They sing, but only during the loud events. During the loudest acoustic moments, when the Reshaping is at its peak and the sound replaces the air and conversation is impossible, they open their mouths and they sing — not words, not melody in any conventional sense, but tones, vibrations, sustained notes that they push through their throats and into the sound, into the overwhelming acoustic field, and for a moment, if you are listening — if you can listen at that scale — you can hear them. Not as distinct voices. As a modulation. As a ripple in the sound. They are singing *into* the Reshaping. They are adding their frequencies to the valley's frequencies. Whether the valley hears them is not a question they ask. They sing because the singing is possible. They sing because during the loudest moments, when the world is being unmade and remade, the only human thing left to do is to make a sound and put it into the world.

## The Old Woman of the Northern Rim

There is a woman — she is perhaps sixty, perhaps seventy, which is old here, which is old anywhere, but especially here, where the ground does not let you forget that time is a condition and conditions change — who lives alone in a dwelling on the northern rim, set slightly apart from the others, on a platform that she moves by herself. She does not ask for help. She does not refuse it, when it is offered, which it is, regularly, because the people are not unkind. But she does not ask.

She has been here longer than anyone. She arrived as a child, with parents who are dead, with siblings who left. She has read the ground for fifty years. Her hands are the best in the settlement. When she puts her palm to the substrate, the others watch her face, because her face tells them what her hands are telling her, and what her hands are telling her is what the ground is about to do. She is not infallible. She has been wrong. She was wrong badly once, fifteen years ago, and three buildings were lost, and no one — not her, not anyone — mentions it, because mentioning it would be a kind of cruelty that has no place here. But she was wrong, and she knows she was wrong, and the knowledge sits in her like a stone in a pool, and the water moves around it, and the stone does not move.

She collects the artifacts more than most. Her dwelling is full of them — the geometric objects, the pieces of structural material with internal channels and external facets. She holds them. She presses them to the ground. She holds them to her skin. She has been doing this for decades. She does not explain why. When asked — and visitors do ask, because visitors do not understand — she says something that translates roughly as: *they are talking and I am learning to listen.*

Whether she is right is not a question that has an answer. The artifacts do vibrate. They do change temperature. They do seem to respond to the valley's activity in ways that the instruments — the hand-sized pieces of structural material — also respond, but more complexly, with more channels, with more information. She may be reading them. She may be projecting onto them. She may be doing both. The settlement does not judge her for this. She is old. She reads the ground. She holds the objects. These are facts. They do not require interpretation.

At night — at the time when she is tired, which is not night in any external sense but is her night, her personal dark — she lies on her woven mat and she holds an artifact against her sternum, where the sound of the Reshaping lives most intensely, and she closes her eyes, and she does something that might be sleeping and might be listening and might be the same thing.

## The Children

The children are the best part of this place, or the most honest part, because they have not yet learned to be efficient about the valley. They love it.

The youngest — the infant — does not know anything else. She was born here, into the wet and the sound, and she does not startle at the Reshaping the way visitors do. She sleeps through the moderate events. During the loud ones, she cries, but briefly, and then she watches, her dark eyes tracking the steam that rises from the new structures, her small hands grasping at the air as though trying to hold the vibrations.

The older children — the ones between five and thirteen — move through the settlement and the basin margins with a freedom that would be considered dangerous in another context. They are not restrained. They are not supervised in the way that outsiders would recognize. They are taught the ground, the water, the sound, the plants, the animals, and then they are released. They learn by being in the environment. A child who wanders too close to an active zone learns, from the ground itself, that the ground is warming, that the vibrations are increasing, that the insects are moving away, and the child turns back. Not because an adult told them to. Because the child's hands and feet and skin know the signals. This knowledge is not taught in lessons. It is absorbed, the way language is absorbed, by immersion, by being in the medium.

They play with the amphibians. This is not quite right. They coexist with the amphibians in a way that occasionally looks like play. The amphibians are not domesticated. They are not pets. But the children — especially the younger ones — will sit at the edge of a pool and the amphibians will approach, slowly, with their low-slung bodies and their dark wet skin, and the children will touch them, and the amphibians will tolerate it, and sometimes a child will lie on the ground and an amphibian will lie beside them, and the two of them will rest there in the wet and the gray light and the sound, and there is something in this that is not play and not communion and not utilization. It is co-presence. They are in the same place. They are both alive. They are both adapted to the same conditions. The children understand this. The amphibians may understand it. The adults watch without comment.

The children do not ask why. This is the thing that strikes a visitor most forcefully. Children everywhere ask why. It is the universal engine of human development. But these children do not, or they do not ask the *big* why — why the valley reshapes, why the ground moves, why the sound never stops. They ask the small why: why does this plant grow here and not there, why does the water run this way, why does the old woman hold the artifacts. The big why does not occur to them because the valley does not present itself as a question. It presents itself as a condition. You do not ask why air exists. You breathe it. You do not ask why the valley reshapes. You move with it.

The girl who is thirteen — the one who is approaching the age of decision — she is the exception. She asks. Not aloud, not to others, but you can see it in her face, in the way she looks at the active zone, in the way her eyes track the columns as they rise. She is looking at the valley and she is seeing something that the others do not see, or have stopped seeing, or have chosen not to see. She is seeing intention. She is seeing purpose. She is seeing a mind in the process, or the possibility of a mind, and this is either the beginning of wisdom or the beginning of departure, and no one knows which, least of all her.

She will decide soon. The southern rim leads out. The valley does not hold her. But the valley is also, in some way she cannot articulate and does not try to, the only thing she has ever known that is *honest*. The valley does not pretend to be stable. The valley does not pretend to be finished. The valley is becoming, always becoming, and she is becoming, always becoming, and this correspondence may be enough to keep her, or it may be exactly what compels her to leave — the need to know if the world outside the basin is also becoming, or if it has the decency to simply *be*.

## The Visitors

They come occasionally. Not often. The valley is not on any map. The southern rim is the only approach that does not require crossing the active zone, and the southern rim is long and wet and the sound travels far, and most people who hear it turn back. Those who do not turn back arrive at the settlement wet and cold and shaken, and the residents receive them without surprise and without ceremony. They are given a dwelling — an empty one, if one is available, or a corner of an occupied one — and a set of woven layers, and they are shown how to wear them, and they are fed — vegetation, amphibian, water — and they are left to acclimate.

Most acimate poorly. The sound is the worst of it. Visitors do not sleep for the first several days. The sound does not stop. It does not fade. It does not become background. It is present, always, and it is physical, and the body interprets it as threat — as a predator, as a storm, as something that requires vigilance — and the visitor lies on their woven mat in the amber glow and their heart races and their eyes will not close and the sound fills them and fills them and they cannot get away from it.

The ones who stay — and a few do stay, a few each generation, enough to keep the gene pool alive — are the ones who stop fighting the sound. Not the ones who stop hearing it. The ones who stop *resisting* it. The sound does not become pleasant. It does not become music. It becomes *given*. It becomes the condition of the air, the way wetness is the condition of the air, and the body, exhausted past the point of vigilance, finally lets go, and the visitor sleeps, and when they wake, they are in the valley, and the valley is being made, and they are in the being-made, and this is where they live now.

## What They Are

They are not a society in the way the word is usually used. They do not have institutions. They do not have a formal economy. They do not have a political structure. They do not have a shared narrative of origin or destiny. They do not have a flag or a name for themselves. If asked what they are — who they are — they would answer with a word that translates, inadequately, as *the ones who are here*. Not *the ones who live here*. Not *the ones who belong here*. The ones who are here. Present. Located. In the present continuous. In the gerund.

They are a people who have been shaped by a place that is itself being shaped. They are adaptive in the deep sense — not adaptable, which implies a flexibility that responds to change, but *adaptive*, which implies a form that is continuously adjusting, continuously becoming, like the valley, like the plants, like the ground. They do not have a stable form. They have a stable *process*. The process is: pay attention. Move. Weave the record. Sing into the sound. Hold the ground in your hands. Teach the children. Carry the dwelling to the next place. Begin again.

They are small and dark and wet and quiet and they live in a place that is being made and they are being made along with it and they do not know what they will be when it is finished because it will not be finished and they will not be finished and this is not a tragedy. This is not a crisis. This is the way of the valley, and the way of the valley is the only way they know, and it is, for all its violence and noise and perpetual unsettlement, a way of being alive that has a coherence to it, a rightness, that you can feel if you put your hand on the ground and hold it there long enough to feel what they feel: not stability, not permanence, but *direction*. The valley is going somewhere. The valley is becoming something. And they are becoming with it.
