# What Lives in the калькаmere

---

## The Ones You Almost Don't See

There is a genus of organism here — and "genus" is wrong, the word implies a taxonomy that implies a lineage that implies a history of classification, and nothing here has been classified, nothing here has been named by anything that names — that exists at the threshold of visibility. Not invisible. Not visible. Occupying the exact resolution at which the eye gives up.

They are the size of dust mites. Possibly they are dust mites, or something that has arrived at the same body plan through entirely different ancestry, the way a hummingbird moth arrives at the shape of a hummingbird. They coat every surface the way bacteria coat a petri dish: completely, continuously, in a film so thin it has no depth. They are the living skin of the калькаmere. When you looked at the wall and saw texture — the fine pitting, the subtle roughness, the way light caught the surface unevenly — you were looking at them. All of them. Millions upon millions of them, each one a speck of pale, soft, six-legged life, each one feeding on the biofilm that coats the structural surface, each one excreting a residue that contributes to that same biofilm. They eat the wall and they rebuild the wall and the wall grows by their accumulation and their waste and their bodies when they die, and the wall is them, and they are the wall, and the distinction was always artificial.

They do not move in a way you can see. But they move. If you press your palm flat against a surface — and you would not want to, but if you did — you would feel a faint warmth and a faint vibration and a faint, almost subliminal friction, like pressing your hand against a living thing that is breathing very slowly. That would be them.

There are other things that live at this scale, or nearly so. Things that feed on the dust-mite-things. Predators, but the word is too dramatic for what they are. They are slightly larger — the size of a grain of sand, the size of the period at the end of this sentence — and they move through the film of their prey with a slow, methodical deliberation, consuming what they encounter, being consumed in turn by things slightly larger than themselves. The entire surface of the калькаmere is a continent at this scale, a landscape of peaks and valleys made of living bodies, with its own grazers and its own hunters and its own decomposers, all of them invisible to you, all of them irrelevant to nothing.

## The Procession-Makers

The insects are the first ones you see clearly.

They are not a species. They are dozens of species, perhaps hundreds, occupying the same broad ecological category — small, many-legged, exoskeleton-bearing, terrestrial or semi-terrestrial — and sharing the same space without any apparent conflict. The secret to their coexistence is the first thing you notice about them: they do not compete for space because they have agreed, over evolutionary time, on a system of lanes.

The channels in the walls — the grooves that carry water downward — are also highways. Each channel carries a different species. The one carrying the tiny amber-colored insects flowing single-file, touching antennae, climbing toward the ceiling — that is one species. The one carrying the dark, flattened, almost lice-like creatures moving in the opposite direction, descending, carrying something in their mandibles — that is another. The wider channels carry larger species: insects the size of your thumbnail, glossy and dark, moving in loose columns, pausing at junctions where smaller channels feed into the main flow, apparently checking something — scent? moisture? the chemical signature of the passage ahead? — before continuing.

The processions are not random. They have destinations. The ascending column disappears into a hole in the ceiling that leads to chambers above — chambers you cannot see, but you can hear them, distantly, through the wall, the sound of many bodies in an enclosed space doing something coordinated. The descending column ends at the floor, at the water channels, where the insects deposit whatever they are carrying — fragments of vegetation, pieces of dead organic matter, small pale objects that might be eggs or might be something else — and then turn and climb again, joining a new ascending column, beginning the cycle again.

They communicate. You are certain of this. The antennae-touching is not idle — it is information transfer. A column that has been interrupted (by your foot, perhaps, or by the passage of one of the larger animals) will pause, and a wave of antennae-touching will propagate backward through the line, each insect receiving information from the one ahead and passing it to the one behind, and after a moment the entire column will adjust — rerouting around the obstacle, reversing direction, or simply waiting, motionless, until the obstruction has passed. They are making decisions collectively, without any individual making a decision. They are thinking with the line.

The smallest of them — the rice-grain-sized ones that move in shimmering, shifting masses across the vegetation — do not form processions. They form clouds. Dense, shifting, three-dimensional clouds of bodies that move across a leaf mat or a wall surface in a way that looks chaotic but is not. The cloud moves as a single entity, expanding and contracting, splitting and recombining, responding to stimuli you cannot detect — a change in moisture, a shift in air current, the chemical signal of a predator nearby. When the cloud finds a resource — a patch of fungal growth, a dead insect, a concentration of condensation — it concentrates there, the individual insects slowing, feeding, their movements becoming localized and rhythmic, and then the cloud lifts and moves on, the resource consumed, the surface cleaned.

These are the ones that make most of the dry rattling. The sound of their legs on the surfaces is the baseline noise of the калькаmere, the substrate upon which all other sounds are built. They have been here, in one form or another, for as long as the structure has existed. They are older than the structure. They were here when this was open ground, before the first secretions hardened into the first surface, before the first organism built the first wall. They are the ones who made the conditions under which the wall-builders could begin. They are the foundation, though they would not think of themselves that way, because they do not think of themselves at all.

## The Wall-Builders

Something built this place. Not by design. Not with intention. But something secreted the material that forms the walls, the floor, the vault, the channels, the holes. Something is still secreting it, still adding layer upon layer to the structure, still growing it, maintaining it, repairing it where erosion has worn it thin.

They are not easy to find.

You look for them in the obvious places — the oldest layers, deep in the walls, where the strata are thickest and most clearly defined — and find nothing. The oldest layers are dead. They are fossil, essentially: compressed, mineralized remains of organisms that lived and died so long ago that their original form is no longer discernible. They are the limestone of this place — the bedrock, the foundation — and the organisms that made them have been gone for longer than the structure has been standing. The калькаmere is built on the graves of its first architects.

But the newer layers — the outer surfaces, the places where the walls are still growing, where the texture is fresh and the material is soft and damp and the biofilm is thickest — there, if you look closely enough, and you have to look very closely, you find them.

They are flat. They are almost two-dimensional. They cling to the surface of the wall the way a limpet clings to a rock, and they are nearly as difficult to remove. Their bodies are oval, pale, semi-translucent, the size of a fingernail, and they are almost entirely stationary. They do not move in any way you can observe in real time. They are fixed in place, their edges flush with the wall surface, their upper surfaces textured with fine ridges and grooves that, when you step back and look at the wall as a whole, form part of the larger texture of the wall itself. They are camouflage so perfect that they are indistinguishable from what they are camouflaged against, because what they are camouflaged against is them — is the secretions of their ancestors, is the layers built by their predecessors, is the material that they themselves are producing, right now, in a secretion so slow and so continuous that it does not register as a process. It is simply happening. The wall is growing. They are growing it. At a rate of perhaps a few millimeters per century.

They do not eat in any way you can identify. They do not have visible mouths. They do not have visible appendages. They absorb what they need from the biofilm that coats the wall surface — the same biofilm that the dust-mite-things graze on, the same biofilm that is maintained by the moisture and the decomposition and the ceaseless cycling of nutrients. They take from it what they need to build their secretions, and what they excrete becomes the next layer of the wall. They are living factories, biological 3D printers, running a program so old it has worn smooth, turning out the same product, generation after generation, with a fidelity that does not waver.

When they die, their bodies remain where they are. They become part of the wall. A new one grows on top of them, or beside them, or from them — you cannot tell. They may reproduce by budding, by fission, by some method that does not involve the standard biological vocabulary. They may reproduce by dying and being replaced by their own offspring, which grow from their bodies the way a seed grows from a fruit. The distinction between an individual and its parent may not exist for them. They may be, in some sense, all the same organism — one continuous body, stretching across the entire inner surface of the калькаmere, growing and dying and growing at different points, different rates, different times, but all of it one thing. One very old thing.

This is possible. This is, in fact, the most likely explanation. The wall-builders may be a single colonial organism, a superorganism that has been growing the калькаmere since before there was a калькаmere, since before the oldest layers were laid down, since the first one settled on the first surface and began to secrete. If so, it is one of the oldest living things in any world. It has been building its own environment for so long that the environment has become a world in itself, and the world has become an ecosystem, and the ecosystem has become home to thousands of other species, and none of them know — none of them can know — that the world they live in is the body of a single organism that has been alive for longer than most geological formations.

The wall-builders are not aware of any of this. They secrete. They grow. They die. They are replaced. The wall thickens. The channels deepen. The holes open and close. The structure breathes, in its slow way, and the breathing is the breathing of one thing that has forgotten, if it ever knew, that it is one thing.

## The Walkers

The forearm-length many-legged things are harder to characterize.

They are not insects. They are not arachnids. They are not myriapods, though they have enough legs to qualify. They are something else — something that has arrived at a body plan that works in this place and has refined it over time into something that looks, to your eye, like a cross between a centipede and a crayfish and a caterpillar and nothing you have a name for.

Their bodies are segmented, with each segment bearing a pair of legs, but the segments are not uniform. The anterior segments are narrower, more flexible, and carry legs that are longer and more articulate — sensory legs, grasping legs, legs that explore the environment ahead of the body. The posterior segments are wider, heavier, and carry legs that are shorter and thicker — propulsion legs, legs that grip the surface and push. Between them, the middle segments carry legs that are intermediate in form — some for walking, some for carrying, some for something you cannot identify. One specimen you observe for a long time has a pair of middle legs that are modified into flat, paddle-like structures, which it holds against the surface as it moves, and you realize after a while that it is smoothing the surface — spreading the biofilm, pressing it into the wall, maintaining the texture. It is a caretaker. It is maintaining the wall.

They move through the vegetation with a fluidity that seems impossible for their size. They do not push through the growth; they flow through it, their bodies flexing and compressing, their legs finding purchase on surfaces that shift and bend beneath them. They can move on any surface — vertical, inverted, across the ceiling as easily as across the floor. Their legs end in pads that secrete a faintly adhesive substance — not sticky enough to trap, but tacky enough to grip — and they can support their full body weight on any two legs while the other twenty or thirty or forty rearrange themselves for the next grip.

They are not predators. Or rather — they are not only predators. They scavenge. They forage. They hunt, when hunting is called for, but they also tend. You watch one moving along a leaf mat, clipping dead vegetation away from the living growth with its anterior legs, carrying the dead material to a channel, and dropping it in. It is weeding. Another one moves along a wall surface, its legs feeling the texture, pausing at a place where the wall has cracked — a fissure, thin, barely visible — and remaining there for a long time, its body flush against the crack, its legs pressing the surface on either side. When it moves on, the crack is smaller. Not gone, but smaller. It is repairing the wall. It is doing what the wall-builders cannot do for themselves — responding to damage on a timescale that a creature with a nervous system can perceive, that a creature that secretes at millimeters per century cannot.

They have a social structure. You infer this from their behavior: they do not compete for resources, they do not fight, they do not avoid each other. When two of them meet on a surface, they pause. They touch. Their anterior legs interlock, their bodies press together, and they remain that way for a moment — seconds, sometimes longer — and then they separate and continue on their respective paths. It is not mating. Or not only mating. It is recognition. It is greeting. It is the exchange of something — chemical information, perhaps, or something more complex — between individuals who know each other, who have met before, who maintain some form of relationship that persists over time.

They are, you begin to suspect, the closest thing this place has to a keystone species. Not the most numerous — not by many orders of magnitude. Not the largest. Not the most fundamental to the structure's existence. But the ones who hold the system together. The ones who respond to change on a timescale that matters. The ones who repair the cracks and clear the dead growth and maintain the channels and — you suspect, though you cannot prove — regulate the populations of other species, keeping the system in balance, preventing any one component from overwhelming the others. They are the managers. They are the stewards. They are the ones who make sure the калькаmere continues to be the калькаmere.

They do not know this. They have no concept of stewardship. They do what they do because doing it has worked, for a very long time, and what works is what is done. The калькаmere is not managed. It is maintained. The distinction matters.

## The Singers

The bass note — the one you feel in your sternum, the one that pulses once every several seconds — is not the wall-builders. It is not the walkers. It is not the structure itself, resonating, though it could be mistaken for that.

It comes from below.

The floor of the калькаmere, beneath the carpet of vegetation and the channels of water and the thin layer of soil-like compost that has accumulated from the decomposition of dead matter, is not solid. It is perforated. The same material that forms the walls — the biogenic, secreted, layered material — forms the floor, and it is full of holes. Not the small holes that pockmark the walls. Larger holes. Shafts. Vertical passages that descend into darkness, into warmth, into a depth that you cannot gauge because your light does not reach the bottom.

The water flows into these holes. The condensation that runs along the channels on the floor reaches the shafts and pours into them, falling in thin, continuous streams into the depths below. And from the depths, warm air rises — saturated with moisture, carrying heat, carrying smell, carrying sound.

The sound is the singers.

They are in the deep places. You cannot see them. You can only hear them and feel them and, occasionally, when the air rising from a shaft is particularly strong, smell them — a rich, organic, complex smell, not unpleasant, like a greenhouse, like a compost pile, like a healthy living system in the act of processing itself.

They make the bass note. You are certain of this, because the note changes as you move across the floor — stronger near some shafts, weaker near others, and at one shaft, a slightly different pitch, a half-tone lower, as if the singer below were a different size or a different species or simply a different individual with a different voice. The note is sustained. It does not waver. It pulses — a slow, regular modulation, once every four or five seconds — but it does not stop. It has, you suspect, not stopped for a very long time. Years. Decades. Longer. It is the sound of the deep places, the sound of the foundation, the sound that everything else in the калькаmere is built upon, acoustically, the way the wall-builders' secretions are the foundation everything else is built upon physically.

What are they singing? The word "singing" is a projection. They may be respiring. They may be digesting. They may be moving — very large bodies, moving very slowly, in a medium that amplifies and carries low-frequency sound. They may be communicating, across the deep places, shaft to shaft, individual to individual, in a language of bass notes and slow pulses, a language that takes minutes to say a word and hours to say a sentence and days to say everything that needs to be said.

They are large. You know this because you can feel the sound in your bones, and to produce a sound at that frequency, at that volume, sustained for that long, requires a body of significant mass. They may be the largest organisms in the калькаmere. They may be the oldest. They may be the original inhabitants — the ones who were here before the walls, before the floor, before the калькаmere was a place, who built the first structures, who secreted the first surfaces, who created the conditions under which the wall-builders could begin their work. The wall-builders may be their descendants. The whole structure may be a monument to the singers — built upward from their deep places, grown over time into the vast vaulted space you are standing in, all of it, every wall and shelf and channel and hole, a single structure built to house and protect and sustain the organisms in the deep places who make the sound that is the foundation of everything.

Or they may be something else entirely. You cannot know. You cannot reach them. The shafts are too deep, too dark, too warm, too wet. You would have to descend into a place where the air is too thick to breathe comfortably, where the temperature is too high for comfort, where the surfaces are slick and the passages are narrow and the sound is so loud it becomes physical, a pressure, a vibration that shakes your teeth. You would have to go down into the body of the калькаmere, into its gut, into the place where its deepest processes happen, and you would have to find the thing that makes the sound and look at it and understand it, and you are not equipped for that. No one is. The singers stay in the deep places, and the deep places stay mysterious, and the sound goes on.

## The Fliers

The things in the air are the most difficult to describe because they are the most difficult to see.

They are small — most of them no larger than your thumb, some much smaller — and they move fast, faster than your eye can track in the dim light. You become aware of them by their sounds: the sharp clicks, the snapping-finger reports, that come from different directions at different intervals, sometimes singly, sometimes in rapid bursts of three or four, sometimes in long sequences that sound almost conversational. They are echolocating, you are nearly certain, navigating the dark space by sound the way a bat does, building a picture of their environment from the echoes that return.

But they are not bats.

They have wings — you have felt them, that dry, papery brush against your face — and the wings are not membranous like a bat's. They are rigid, or nearly so, flat structures that beat at a frequency too fast to see, producing a faint hum that is part of the great layered sound of the калькаmere. The body, when you manage to focus on one — a moment of stillness, a brief perch on a leaf surface, a flash of pale gray-brown against the green — is compact, armored, almost beetle-like, with a dome-shaped thorax and a small head dominated by two large, dark structures that may be eyes or may be sensory organs adapted to a different modality. They click. They fly. They perch. They fly again. They are always moving.

They hunt in the air. You piece this together over time: a click, a pause, a rapid flutter, a smaller sound — a brief, high-pitched squeak, cut short — and then another click, and then silence, and then the flier moves on. They are catching the smaller fliers, the ones you cannot see at all, the ones that make the keening whistles that thread through the upper range of the калькаmere's soundscape. There is a whole ecology in the air above you — predators and prey, the hunted and the hunters, an entire ecosystem occupying the space that you took for empty darkness, living and dying in the air between the walls, invisible, audible, permanent.

They roost in the holes in the upper walls. You see them entering and leaving — brief flashes of movement at the edge of your vision, silhouettes against the faint glow, vanishing into the perforations in the wall surface. The holes are their caves, their shelters, their nests. Each hole may house one, or a pair, or a small colony. From inside the holes, you hear the clicks continue — softer, slower, conversational. They are talking to each other in there. They are settling in. They are resting, in their way, in the dark warm holes of the wall, surrounded by the ceaseless sound of the калькаmere, which is, to them, not noise but silence — the sound of everything being normal, everything being in its place, everything being as it has always been.

## The Pale Swimmers

In the water channels, the slow-moving, soft-bodied, pale things are the ones you find most difficult to categorize. They are animal. They are clearly animal — they move with muscular undulation, they respond to stimuli, they gather in groups — but their body plan is so unlike anything you know that you struggle for a reference point.

They are not slugs. They are not worms. They are not larvae, though they might be — you do not know if what you are seeing is an adult form or a juvenile, and the distinction may not apply. Their bodies are elongated, flattened, and divided into sections that are not segments exactly but zones — zones of different texture, different color, different function. The anterior zone is pale, almost white, and bears a cluster of short, thick tentacle-like structures that wave constantly in the water, tasting, testing, sensing. The middle zone is pinkish and translucent, and you can see dark structures within it — organs, perhaps, or gut contents, or both. The posterior zone is darker, grayer, and tapers to a flat, paddle-like tail that provides propulsion.

They move through the shallow water with a slow, rhythmic undulation, their bodies flexing in a wave from front to back, their tails pushing them forward. They are not fast. They do not need to be. They are feeding. You watch one move along a water channel, its anterior tentacles sweeping the substrate, pausing at a patch of something — a concentration of organic matter, a growth of microorganisms, a dead insect that has fallen into the water — and then its mouth, which is somewhere among the tentacles, opens and the thing is consumed and the swimmer moves on.

They cluster. You do not understand why. Dozens of them gather at certain points along the channels — the junctions, where two channels meet, or the places where water pools in shallow basins. They press together, their bodies overlapping, their movements synchronized, and they remain there for long periods, their tentacles waving in unison, their tails pulsing together, and they make the sound — the low, wet, rhythmic pulsing that you feel in your chest. They are singing. Not like the deep singers — not that bass, not that depth — but a higher, wetter, more intimate sound, a sound made at the surface, among others, in groups. A social sound. A sound of being together.

They are not the only things in the water. There are smaller organisms — microscopic, or nearly so — that cloud the water in places, turning it milky. There are things that cling to the surfaces of the channels, filter feeders, rooted in place, drawing nutrients from the flow. There are things that live in the thin film of water that coats every surface, that never submerge but never dry, organisms that occupy the boundary between wet and dry, between the aquatic and the terrestrial, living in a habitat that is, in any other context, too thin to be a habitat at all. But here, in the калькаmere, where every surface is wet and the air is nearly saturated, the boundary is a world.

## The Glow-Bearers

The bioluminescent organisms in the holes in the walls are the most beautiful things in the калькаmere, and you almost miss them.

They are deep in the holes — not at the surface, not visible from the main chamber, but recessed, set back from the opening, in the darkness of the perforation. You see them only when you look directly into a hole, and even then, only when the glow happens. It is a pulse. A slow, blue-green pulse that begins faintly, brightens over the course of two or three seconds to a peak that illuminates the interior of the hole and the wall around it, and then fades over another two or three seconds to darkness. Then, after a pause — variable, sometimes a few seconds, sometimes much longer — it pulses again.

They are not coordinated. Each hole glows on its own schedule, independent of the others. The result, from a distance, is a twinkling effect — pinpoints of blue-green light appearing and disappearing across the wall surface, unsynchronized, arrhythmic, like a night sky that breathes.

What are they? You cannot reach them. The holes are too small for your hand, too deep for your eye. But the light, when it comes, reveals glimpses: a cluster of small, round bodies, pale, translucent, grouped together in the back of the hole, each one producing the glow. They are sessile — they do not appear to move. They are grouped — they are not solitary. They are in the deepest part of the hole, the most sheltered, the most protected, where the temperature is stable and the moisture is highest.

They glow for a reason. The glow is not wasted energy — nothing is wasted in the калькаmere. It does something. The most likely explanation is that it attracts. Something comes to the glow, something that benefits the glow-bearer — prey, perhaps, drawn to the light and consumed. Or a symbiont, drawn to the light to exchange some service. Or a mate, drawn to the light for reproduction. The glow is a signal. It says: here I am. It says: come. It says, in the language of biochemistry and light: I am here, in this hole, in this wall, in this vast dark place, and I am alive, and I am producing this light, and I have been producing it, in this hole, in this wall, for a span of time that I have no way to express to you.

The glow is also, you realize, part of the light of the калькаmere. The dim greenish-gray illumination that lets you see — it is not all bioluminescence from the vegetation. Some of it, perhaps much of it, comes from the glow-bearers, their accumulated light leaking out of their holes, diffusing through the chamber, contributing to the ambient glow that makes the place navigable. They are the lamps of the калькаmere. They are the ones who make it possible to see. And they do it without knowing they do it, without intending it, without any awareness that the light they produce for their own reasons serves a thousand other purposes in the ecosystem around them.

## The Still Ones on the Ceiling

The ceiling things do not make a sound. This is notable, in a place where everything makes a sound. They are silent. They are still. They hang.

They are clustered in masses that extend from the ceiling like organic stalactites, tapering, conical, each mass the size of a fist or larger, attached to the ceiling by a narrow stalk that seems too thin to support the weight. The masses are dark, almost black, with a smooth, almost waxy surface that the condensation beads on but does not penetrate. They are grouped in patches — you see clusters of them in certain areas of the ceiling, and gaps in others, and the clustering is not random. It follows the structure of the ceiling — the thickest parts of the vault, where the layers are most solid, where the wall-building material is densest. They are hanging from the strongest parts of the ceiling. They know where the ceiling is strong. They know this without knowing it, the way a vine knows which way is up.

They are alive. You are certain of this, though they give no sign of it except when the condensation drips on them. A drop of water falls from the ceiling above — or perhaps from a higher part of the vault, condensing on a cooler surface and falling — and lands on one of the masses. And the mass moves. Not dramatically. Not quickly. But perceptibly: a contraction, a slow drawing-in, a tightening of the surface, as if the mass were absorbing the water through its skin. The tentacle-like structures you did not notice before — because they were retracted, because they were invisible against the dark surface — emerge, extending from the base of the mass, reaching toward the source of the water, and they are long, longer than the mass itself, and they are thin and pale and they wave in the air with a slow, searching motion, and they find the water — the drop, or the wet spot, or the trickle — and they touch it and they remain touching it and the mass does not move again.

They are waiting. That is what they do. They hang from the ceiling and they wait for water. They are the ceiling's inhabitants, adapted to the one resource that comes to them — the condensation that falls from above — and they have given up everything else to specialize in its collection. They do not eat. They do not hunt. They do not compete. They hang, and they wait, and when the water comes, they drink it, and that is enough. That is their entire existence.

They are very old. You infer this from their position — they are in the oldest part of the structure, the ceiling, which was built first, which has been solid the longest — and from their stillness, which speaks of a system that has reached equilibrium so completely that movement is no longer necessary. They found their niche and they filled it and they stopped. They have been hanging here, in these same clusters, drinking the same condensation, for longer than the walkers have been walking, longer than the fliers have been flying, longer than the processions have been processing. They are the elder inhabitants. They were here when the ceiling was new. They will be here when the walls are old.

## The Thing You Do Not See

There is one more inhabitant of the калькаmere. You do not see it because it is not visible. You do not hear it because it does not make a sound distinguishable from the great collective sound. You do not feel it because it does not touch you. But it is here. It is everywhere. It is the thing that makes the калькаmere a single system rather than a collection of systems.

It is the chemical signature of the place — the invisible, continuous, everywhere-present cloud of molecular information that passes through the air, through the water, through the surfaces, carrying messages between organisms that have no other way to communicate. The insects follow it along their processions. The walkers read it when they touch. The swimmers sense it in the water. The fliers detect it in the air currents. The singers, in the deep places, may produce it — may be the source of the signals that coordinate the system, the hub of a chemical network that extends through every chamber and passage and shaft of the калькаmere, connecting every organism to every other organism, making them all, in some functional sense, parts of a whole.

It is not an organism. It is not a species. It is the medium of exchange itself — the language, the information, the connective tissue of the ecosystem. It is what makes the калькаmere one thing rather than many things. It is the reason the processions know where to go. The reason the walkers know what to repair. The reason the swimmers gather where they gather. The reason the fliers fly where they fly. It is the thing that tells everything where everything else is, and what everything else needs, and what everything else is doing, and where the resources are, and where the dangers are, and when to move and when to wait and when to gather and when to disperse.

It is the closest thing the калькаmere has to a mind. Not a mind in any sense you would recognize — not a mind that thinks, not a mind that decides, not a mind that knows — but a mind in the sense that a city is a mind, or an ant colony is a mind, or a brain is a mind: a system of information exchange that produces behavior that is more complex and more coordinated than the behavior of any of its components. The калькаmere thinks. It thinks slowly, in chemicals and in time, and it does not think about anything in particular, and it does not know that it is thinking. But the processions, and the walkers, and the swimmers, and the singers, and the fliers, and the glow-bearers, and the still ones, and the wall-builders, and the dust-mite-things — all of them are its neurons, all of them are its synapses, all of them are parts of a system that processes information and produces responses and maintains itself, over time, at scale, with a fidelity and a persistence that any individual organism, or any individual species, could never achieve alone.

The калькаmere is them. They are the калькаmere.
