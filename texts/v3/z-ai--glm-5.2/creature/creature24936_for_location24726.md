# What Lives in the Basin at Still-Birth

---

## First, the smallest — or what appears smallest

The insects are not insects. They occupy the insect-niche, which is not the same thing.

They have six legs, or the suggestion of six legs — three pairs of appendages that function as legs but which are not jointed in the way that legs are supposed to be jointed. They bend in the middle. They bend in two places. Some of them bend in three. You can see this only if one lands on your hand and stays long enough for your eyes to adjust to the flat, shadowless light, and even then what you see is difficult to trust, because the thing is vibrating at a frequency that makes its edges blur, and you are not sure whether the blurring is motion or uncertainty — whether the creature is moving too fast for your eye or whether it has not yet decided where it ends and the air begins.

They have wings. The wings are not transparent but translucent, the color of the amber light they live in, and they do not fold flat against the body when at rest. They stay open. They stay open always, held at an angle that suggests neither flight nor display but something else — a readiness, perhaps, or simply a configuration the body has not yet learned to collapse. When they fly, they fly in straight lines. They do not dart. They do not hover. They move from one surface to another in direct, purposeful trajectories that make them seem less like animals and more like particles in a current, following a gradient that exists but which you cannot see.

Their bodies are dark. Not black — dark the way the water is dark, dark with a warmth to it, a brown-orange undertone that catches the geological glow from the pools and makes them seem lit from inside. They do not have eyes. Or they have something where eyes will eventually go — two smooth, slightly raised patches on the front of the head that are the same color as the rest of the body and which do not appear to focus, track, or respond to light. They navigate by warmth. You can test this: hold your hand near a column of them on a wet bark surface and the column will lean toward you, not because they see you but because you are thirty-seven degrees and the bark is thirty-eight and the gradient interests them.

They do not bite. They do not sting. They do not drink blood. They feed on the moisture that weeps from the stone and the sap that leaks from every broken stem and the sugar that forms on every surface where decomposition is in progress — and decomposition is in progress everywhere, always, at a rate that exactly matches the rate of growth, so that the basin is a system in which creation and decay are the same act performed at the same speed on the same material by the same processes. The insects are the mechanism. They are the interface between living and not-living. They land on a leaf and the leaf begins to dissolve. They land on a stone and the water on the stone begins to organize. They are not eating. They are *mediating*.

There are others. Smaller. So small that they are not individuals but texture — a fine, almost invisible mist of life that lives on the surface of the standing water, on the surface of the wet bark, on the surface of your skin if you stand still for more than thirty seconds. They are the reason the water glows. The dull orange-brown luminescence in the deep pools is not geological — or not only geological. It is also these. These uncountable, unnamed, almost-organisms that have arrived at the concept of single-celled life and are still working on the details. They do not divide in the way that cells divide — not cleanly, not symmetrically. They divide the way a droplet divides: unevenly, reluctantly, the daughter-cell pulling away from the parent with a visible lag, as though the separation is experimental, as though each division is a question asked for the first time and answered without confidence.

They glow because the heat below expresses itself through them. They are the medium. The thermal energy rising through the unsealed substrate passes through the dark water and passes through them and becomes light at the threshold of visibility, and they do not control this and are not aware of this and do not benefit from this. It simply happens. They are a filter, and the heat is what is being filtered, and the glow is what remains.

You drink them when you drink the air. You swallow them when you swallow. They are in your eyes, your nose, the folds of your skin. They do not make you sick. They make you *warm*. After a day in the basin your core temperature is thirty-eight degrees, matched to the air, matched to the water, matched to the stone. You are acclimatized not by adaptation but by saturation. You have become part of the thermal system. You glow, faintly, in the right light.

## The swimmers

The pools are full.

This is the first thing you understand when you crouch at the edge of one and look down. The water is dark — stained with tannin, stained with mineral, stained with the particulate of constant decomposition — but it is not opaque, and what you see beneath the surface is not empty. The pools are full the way a jar is full of preserved things: packed, layered, density upon density, bodies in every position and orientation, occupying the water the way the insects occupy the air.

They are long. All of them are long. This is the one morphological constant in the pools: length. Everything that swims here is longer than it is wide, longer than it is tall, shaped like the channels it swims through — narrow, deep, warm. Some are the thickness of a finger. Some are the thickness of a thigh. The largest — in the pools the size of houses — are the thickness of a torso, and you see them only as shapes, as dark curves that turn beneath the surface and do not break it, their movements so slow and so continuous that they seem not to be swimming but flowing, the water rearranging itself into forms that happen to be alive.

They undulate. This is the wrong word — or the right word, but for the wrong reason. They undulate not because they have settled on serpentine locomotion but because they have not settled on anything else. Their bodies are not segmented but they are not smooth, either. They have a texture — a series of folds or ridges that run the length of the body, like the corrugations on a hose, and these folds expand and contract in waves that travel from one end to the other, and the direction of the wave determines the direction of travel, and the speed of the wave determines the speed, and the whole system is so simple and so general that it works in the water and on the wet ground and through the root-tangle and up the wet bark of the trees, and you see them everywhere — not the same species in every medium, but the same *strategy*, the same solution, arrived at by bodies that have not yet diversified because diversification implies choice and choice implies change and change has not been invented here.

They do not have eyes. They have — along the length of the body, in a line that follows the fold or ridge that runs nearest the surface — a series of shallow depressions, each one filled with the same dark water they swim in, and these depressions are sensory but not visual. They feel pressure. They feel temperature gradient. They feel the particular vibration that the insects make when they land on the water's surface, and this is what draws the swimmers upward: not hunger but *alignment*, the pull of warmth and vibration toward more warmth and more vibration, a system that does not eat but absorbs, that does not hunt but *encounters*, that moves toward what is similar and takes it in through a skin that is permeable to everything smaller than itself and impermeable to everything larger.

They take in the micro-organisms. They take in the decomposing matter. They take in the warm water and the mineral film and the faint glow. They do not have mouths in the way that mouths are supposed to work — an opening, a cavity, a mechanism for intake. They have *surfaces*. Their skin feeds them. The whole body is a gut, and what it digests, it converts to more body, to more length, to more fold and ridge and wave, and the body grows continuously because growing is what this kind of body does, and there is no mechanism for stopping, and there has never needed to be one, because the heat goes on and the water goes on and the food arrives without effort.

The largest ones are old. This is obvious not because they show age — wear, damage, the accumulation of scar and time — but because they show *nothing*. They are immaculate. A body that has been growing in warm dark water for an unguessable length of time, absorbing everything it encounters, converting it to more length, and it has no blemish, no injury, no mark of any kind. It is the same as the small ones, exactly the same, only larger. As though growth is the only variable and everything else is fixed.

They do not reproduce. Or — they do, but not in any way that involves a moment of reproduction. No egg, no spawn, no division, no birth. What happens is this: a body grows long enough and the tail end of it, the part that trails behind in the warm water, the part that has been feeding and growing for the longest time, begins to become *less*. Less connected. Less invested in the head-end's direction. The folds and ridges begin to move in their own patterns, no longer synchronized with the waves coming from the front. A gap appears — not a cut, not a break, but a thinning, a narrowing, a place where the body becomes threadlike and then becomes two bodies, and the back one does not swim away because it has no front, it has no direction, it has only a middle, and so it stays where it is and grows its own front, its own sensory depressions, its own rhythm, and eventually it grows its own tail, and the tail thins, and the tail separates, and now there are three, and this has been happening since the water settled and will happen until the water leaves.

The pools are full because the pools have always been full. Nothing has ever left. Nothing has ever died. The swimmers grow and divide and grow and the pools contain them the way the basin contains the pools — not by design but by default, by the simple fact of being the lowest place, the place where things accumulate because there is nowhere lower to go.

## The callers

They sit in the canopy-ceiling. You cannot see them. You have never seen one, not clearly, not in a way that resolves into a shape you could name. You see suggestions: a movement in the thatch above, a shift in the density of leaves, a momentary silhouette against the amber-gray glow that might be a bird or might be a projection of the tree itself or might be nothing — might be your eyes inventing a body for a sound that has no body.

But they have bodies. You know this because you have found feathers.

Not feathers. Not yet feathers. The things you find on the ground, caught in the root-tangle, floating on the surface of the pools — they are flat, keratinous structures, longer than they are wide, with a central spine and a series of barbs that are not barbs but folds, the same kind of fold that runs along the swimmers' bodies, the same corrugation, the same texture. They are dark. They are warm to the touch. They do not have the stiffness of feathers — they do not spring back when you bend them. They hold whatever shape you put them in, like wet leather, and after a time in your hand they begin to soften, to lose their form, to become part of the decomposition that is everywhere, and you realize that what you are holding is not a dropped feather but a *shed* one, a piece of the body that has been released because it was no longer needed, or because the body produced it without intention, the way the stone produces water, the way the trees produce roots — a secretion, a surplus, an excess of form.

The call. You need to know about the call.

It is a single note. It is always a single note. Short — less than a second — and hard, with a percussive quality that has nothing to do with a syrinx or a vocal cord or any structure you would associate with bird-calls. It sounds like a branch breaking. It sounds like a stone cracking. It sounds like the basin itself making a sound, the way a house makes sounds when it cools at night, and the first time you hear it you look up expecting to see something structural — a fallen limb, a split trunk, a collapse of the canopy — and you see nothing. And then it comes again, at the same interval, and you understand that it is not a sound *in* the basin but a sound *of* the basin, made by something that lives here and is part of here and cannot be separated from here.

The interval is constant. Eleven seconds. You count it without meaning to, the way you count a heartbeat when you become aware of it. Eleven seconds between calls, always, and the call itself is always the same pitch, the same duration, the same attack and decay, as though it is not a vocalization but a release — a pressure escaping, a threshold crossing, something that builds up in the body for eleven seconds and then exits in a single hard note and then begins building again.

There is more than one of them. You know this because the calls layer. One call at eleven-second intervals from your left. Another at eleven-second intervals from ahead and slightly above. Another from farther away, fainter, muffled by the canopy density. They are not synchronized. They are not unsynchronized. They are simply independent, each one on its own schedule, each one calling at its own eleven-second interval, and the combined effect is a rhythm that is too complex to predict and too regular to be random, a polyphony that sounds like it has a pattern but does not, and this is maddening, and after a time you stop trying to find the pattern and the calls become part of the texture, part of the pressure, part of the baseline sound of the basin, and they recede into the drone, and the drone absorbs them, and the drone absorbs everything.

What do they look like? You have theories. The shed feathers suggest a body that is dark, warm, folded — a body that has arrived at the concept of bird but has not committed to the details. The canopy density suggests something that clings rather than perches, something that moves through the thatch the way the swimmers move through the water, by undulation, by the same fundamental method. The call suggests a body that is resonant — something with a chest cavity, something with air space, something that can hold pressure and release it. But you do not know. No one knows. The callers live in the canopy-ceiling and they do not come down and they do not leave and they have been calling for as long as the canopy has been closed, and the canopy has been closed since the trees arrived, and the trees arrived when the land rose, and the land rose in a time before time was measured, and the calls have been sounding at eleven-second intervals since then, and they will sound until the canopy opens, and the canopy will not open.

## The movers

These are the ones you almost see.

They are in the undergrowth. They are always in the undergrowth. The undergrowth is not a layer of plants between the ground and the canopy; it is a volume of root-tangle and standing water and low vegetation and decomposing matter that is as dense as the canopy above and as alive as the water below, and it fills the space between the trees the way the insects fill the air — completely, without gaps, without clearings, without any space that is not occupied.

The movers live here. Many of them. You hear them constantly — the heavy push of a body through wet vegetation, the splash of a displaced pool, the low vocalization that is not a growl and not a rumble and not a purr but all three, a sound made by a throat that is still learning its range, still discovering what it can do with the air and the muscles and the resonance it has been given.

You see them in pieces. This is the only way to describe it — you do not see a mover. You see a flank. You see a projection. You see the dark curve of a back retreating between two root-masses. The pieces do not assemble. You do not, even after many encounters, build a picture of the whole animal, because the animal does not want to be seen whole or does not know that it can be seen whole or does not have a whole to be seen — the possibility exists that the movers, like everything else here, are not yet finished, not yet decided, not yet committed to a single form, and what you are seeing are not parts of a complete animal but drafts, versions, attempts.

The flank is dark. Dark the way the swimmers are dark, dark the way the stone is dark, the same warm brown-black that everything in the basin shares, as though color is a property of the place rather than a property of the things in it. It is wet — the flank is always wet, slick, shining in the flat light with the same film that covers the stone. The texture is not fur. Not scales. Not skin. It is something that has the smoothness of skin and the directionality of fur — the hairs, if they are hairs, all lie in one direction, but they are so fine and so dense that the surface appears seamless, and when a flank turns away from you into shadow you see the light slide across it the way light slides across water, and you think: *this is an animal that has become part of the wet*.

The projection. You see it and you do not know what it is. It extends from the rear of the body — you think it is the rear, you are not sure — and it is long and thin and it moves. Not like a tail. A tail is a counterweight, a rudder, a balance. This moves like a limb. It reaches. It curls around a root. It pulls the body forward. And then it extends again, further, and touches a surface — a wet stone, a pool's edge, the bark of a tree — and it adheres, briefly, and then releases, and you realize that it is not a limb either, because a limb has joints, and this has no joints, this is a continuous flexible extension that can go in any direction and adhere to any surface and bear weight and sense temperature and do all of these things simultaneously, and you do not have a word for it because the word has not been invented because the structure has not been invented, it is being invented right now, in front of you, in the dim amber light, by a body that is still figuring out what it can be.

The vocalization. You hear it from close — once, from no more than three meters away, and it goes through you, not because it is loud but because it is *low*, a frequency that you feel in your sternum and your gut and the backs of your teeth, a sound that is not made for your ears but for the body of the listener, a sound that is meant to be felt, that is meant to communicate through density rather than through air. And what it communicates is not a message. It is a presence. It says: *I am here, I am warm, I am moving, I am in the same place you are in*. And this is all it says, and this is all it needs to say, because there is nothing else to communicate in a place where nothing changes and nothing threatens and nothing is new.

They are many. You know this from the number of sounds, the number of movements, the number of displaced pools and pushed-aside root-masses. They live in the undergrowth the way the swimmers live in the pools — packed, layered, coexisting at a density that would be impossible in any other system but which is possible here because there is no competition. There is nothing to compete for. The heat is everywhere. The water is everywhere. The food — the decomposing matter, the living plants, the insects, the swimmers when they cross from one pool to another across the wet ground — is everywhere. There is no scarcity. There has never been scarcity. The basin is full and the basin provides and the movers live in it without struggle, without hierarchy, without territory, without any of the structures that animals invent when they need to divide what is limited, because nothing here is limited, nothing here is divided, everything here is shared the way the heat is shared — completely, without remainder, without negotiation.

They do not hunt. They do not fight. They do not mate — or they mate in the same way that the swimmers reproduce, by a process so slow and so continuous that it has no moment, no event, no point of beginning. They grow. They divide. They grow. They move through the undergrowth in their dark, wet bodies with their projections reaching and adhering and releasing, and they vocalize at frequencies that you feel in your skeleton, and they are aware of you the way the basin is aware of you — which is to say, not at all, and also completely, in the way that a system is aware of every component within it, not through perception but through inclusion. You are in the basin. The movers are in the basin. You are both part of the same density, the same warmth, the same continuous, unchanging, held-breath existence.

## And something else

There is something in the deep pools. In the largest ones, the pools the size of houses, where the water is darkest and the glow is strongest and the heat-haze shimmers on the substrate below.

You do not see it. You see what it does to the water.

The large swimmers — the torso-thick ones, the dark curves that turn beneath the surface — they move differently in the deep pools. In the smaller pools they move freely, continuously, undulating from one end to the other without pause or change. In the deep pools they move in arcs. They curve away from the center. They curve away from the deepest point, the point where the glow is most intense, and their movements there have a quality you do not see elsewhere — a *deliberateness*, a directionality, as though they are not simply moving but moving *around* something, maintaining a distance, keeping a geometry.

The temperature in the center of the deep pools is higher. Not much. A degree. Maybe two. The water there is warmer than the water at the edges by an amount that you can feel when you put your hand in — and you do put your hand in, because you have been here long enough to stop being careful, long enough to become part of the system, and the system does not distinguish between your hand and a root and a stone and a decomposing leaf. Your hand goes in and the water is warm and then warmer and then warmer still as you reach toward the center, and at the center — at the exact center, at the point where the glow is brightest and the heat-haze is thickest — your hand meets resistance.

Not a body. Not a surface. Not a stone or a root or a fish. Resistance. A density in the water that is not the water itself. A viscosity. A thickness. As though the water at the center of the pool is not water but something that used to be water and is becoming something else, something that holds its shape, something that pushes back when you push against it, something that is warm — warmer than the water, warmer than the stone, warmer than your hand — and which pulses.

Not a pulse like a heartbeat. A pulse like a tide. Slow. So slow that you are not sure it is a pulse at all until you hold your hand still and wait — and the basin teaches you to wait, the basin teaches you that nothing here is fast, that everything here happens at the speed of geology — and after a time you feel it: a thickening, a pushing, a momentary increase in the pressure against your palm, and then a release, and then a thickening again, and the interval is not eleven seconds or seven seconds or any number you can count to because it is longer than your patience and shorter than your memory, and you cannot time it because timing requires a beginning and an end and this has neither.

You pull your hand out. The water on your skin glows more brightly than before. The glow fades over several minutes, the orange-brown luminescence dimming as whatever you touched disperses back into the water, back into the general warmth, back into the baseline.

You do not know what it is.

No one knows what it is.

It may be the substrate. It may be the heat itself, expressing itself so directly that it has become tangible. It may be a body — a body so large and so old and so undifferentiated from the water it lives in that it cannot be seen as separate, a body that is the pool the way the basin is the land, a body that has been growing since the water settled and has never stopped growing and has never needed to stop because the heat goes on and the water goes on and the glow goes on and there is nothing in the basin that tells anything to stop.

It may be what the basin is for.

You do not know. You leave. The mist does not clear. The light does not change. The drone goes on. The callers call. The swimmers swim. The movers move. And in the deep pools, in the dark, in the glow, something pulses at a tempo too slow to measure, and the water holds it, and the heat feeds it, and the basin — the basin at still-birth, the basin that arrived complete and has never changed — the basin continues, as it has since it began, as it will until it ends, which it will not.
