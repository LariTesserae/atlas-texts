# What Lives There

You would not call them inhabitants at first. You would not call them anything, because you would not see them. The Tableland does not advertise its residents. It advertises its sound, its green churning, its stone, its sky — and these are so overwhelming, so total in their claim on your attention, that the question of *what lives here* seems almost beside the point. The place itself seems to be living. The place itself seems to be enough.

But the place is not a tenant of itself. And the structures say otherwise. And the vibration says otherwise. And if you stay long enough — past the point where the sound becomes baseline, past the point where the green becomes expected — you begin to understand that the Tableland is not empty. It is merely patient about what it holds, and the things it holds are patient about being held.

---

## The Cultivators

They do not have a name for themselves. Or they do, but the name is not a word — it is a gesture, a specific pressure of the palm against stone, a way of touching the ground that means *I am here and I am the one who tends*. You would need to live with them for years to read the difference between that gesture and the gesture that means *I am here and I am resting*, which looks identical to an outsider but involves a different distribution of weight through the heel of the hand. Everything about them is like this. Everything is precise, and the precision is invisible unless you have been taught, and the teaching takes longer than you would think a human lifetime allows, which may be why they live as long as they do.

They are human. That needs to be said, because after enough time on the Tableland, after enough exposure to the vegetation and the stone and the vibration, you start to lose the category. You start to think of the residents as extensions of the landscape — stone-people, green-people, things that grew from the mat or emerged from the veins in the rock. They are not. They are human in the full sense: bilaterally symmetrical, warm-blooded, possessed of language and memory and the specific kind of loneliness that comes from knowing you are finite and that the place you live in is not. They have faces. The faces have expressions. The expressions are readable, if you know the dialect, which is subdued, because on the Tableland, a face that moves too much is a face that is trying too hard, and trying too hard is a kind of noise, and there is already enough noise.

They are small. Not stunted — *compressed*. Generations of wind, of living where the wind never stops, where standing tall means standing against a force that has been blowing since before the mountains were planed, has produced a people who are dense and low-built. The women average perhaps four foot eight. The men a touch more. Their limbs are thick relative to their length, their torsos barrel-shaped, their hands broad and flat and callused in patterns that tell you what they do — the cultivators have calluses on the pads of the fingers and the outer edge of the palm, the builders have them across the heel and the base of the thumb, the walkers have them on the balls of the feet, which are bare always, because on the Tableland, shoes are a separation from the vibration, and separation from the vibration is a kind of deafness, and no one here chooses to be deaf.

Their skin is dark. Not the dark of high-altitude sun exposure, though that plays a part — the dark of something older, a melanin response to an environment where the light is not the primary pressure. The primary pressure is the stone, and the stone darkens what it touches. They know this. They do not talk about it. It is simply the case that people of the Tableland have a complexion like the wet stone they walk on, and that travelers from below — when travelers come, which is almost never — are pale by comparison, and the paleness looks like illness to the Tableland people, a failure to take on the color of the place you stand on, a refusal to be absorbed.

They are few. This is the thing that the structures already told you, if you knew how to read them: the rings, the walls, the hearths — they are the work of a people who have never numbered more than a few hundred, spread across the entire reach of the Tableland, each family group occupying a territory of dozens of square miles, meeting other groups only by arrangement, only at the cairns that mark the meeting-places, only when there is reason. And the reason is rarely. And the rarely is not loneliness, because loneliness implies a expectation of company, and they do not expect company. They expect the sound. They expect the green. They expect the stone and the vibration and the weather cycling through its moods above them. These are their companions. These are sufficient.

## What They Do

They cultivate. Not in the way you would recognize — no rows, no fields, no harvest. The Tableland's vegetation is already growing, already cycling, already eating itself with a ferocity that produces the roar you hear. The cultivators do not start this process. They do not need to. What they do is *steer* it.

A cultivator walks the mat. She walks slowly, or rather she walks at the speed the Tableland allows, which is a particular gait — half the stride of lowland walking, each foot placed with a consciousness of what is beneath it, the way a person walks on ice, not because the surface is slippery but because the surface is *meaningful* and every placement is a statement about what you intend to grow there and what you intend to let die. She carries a stick — not a staff, not a tool with a shaped end, just a length of stone, the same dark stone as the ground, palm-wide, worn smooth by years of use. She uses it to part the growth. She uses it to compress a section of mat that has grown too thick, pressing it down into its own mulch, which will feed the next cycle. She uses it to clear a channel, a few inches wide, through which the condensation can flow to a particular patch she is encouraging — a stand of the thick-stalked plants whose waxy leaves, when they curl in the right way, collect and hold water that she will later drink.

Because there is no standing water on the Tableland. None. The condensation forms on every surface and either evaporates in the warm air or is absorbed by the mat. The cultivators get their water from the plants — from the cupping leaves, from the bulbous growths that are not fruiting bodies but reservoirs, from the mat itself, which can be wrung like a cloth if you know which sections to wring and which to leave. They have spent generations learning which plants hold water and which hold something else, something that looks like water but is a digestate, a product of the rapid decomposition, and will make you sick. The difference is visible only in the way the leaf curls — three degrees of angle, no more — and the difference between a cultivator and a stranger is that the cultivator reads those three degrees without thinking, the way you read a face.

They grow what they need, and what they need is little. Food — there are sections of the mat that produce a starchy tuber-analog, fist-sized, dense, bland, growing close to the stone in the low places between undulations. The cultivators tend these sections the way a gardener tends a bed, but faster, because the growth cycle here is measured in hours, not weeks. A tuber planted in the morning — and "planted" here means placed in the mulch with a specific orientation to the vibration, which the cultivators believe helps the root align, which it does, though whether this is cause or correlation is a question they do not ask — a tuber planted in the morning is harvestable by the next morning. It will be small. It will be enough. They eat little. They eat often. The rhythm of their eating matches the rhythm of the growth: continuous, small portions, no meals, no ceremony, just a hand reaching into a section of mat and pulling out something that was put there yesterday and is ready now.

They do not cook. The hearth — the one you found, the blackened pit inside the largest structure — is for warmth, and even that is rare, because the Tableland is warm, the stone breathes heat from below. The hearth is used only in the deepest gray, when the sky has been dark for three days and the condensation turns cold and the mat grows sluggish. Then someone — usually the oldest person in the group, because the oldest feel the cold most, and because the oldest are the ones who remember the technique — will split a piece of stone along its grain, exposing the dry interior, and will arrange the splits in the pit, and will strike them together with a force and precision that produces a spark, and the spark catches in the mulch, which is always damp but which has a core, if you pack it right, that is dry enough to hold a flame. The flame is small. It is not a fire. It is a glow, a warmth, a thing that says *we are here and the stone is here and between us we can make heat*. The flame lasts an hour. The warmth lasts longer, held in the blackened stones the way the Tableland holds heat in its deep reservoir. Then it goes out. And the sound continues around them, and the green continues, and the gray lifts eventually, and the flame is not needed again for months.

## The Children

There are not many. A family group — three, four, five people — might have one child, or two, spaced far apart. The children are not children for long. Not because they grow fast — they grow at a human pace — but because the Tableland does not tolerate childhood as a separate state. A child on the Tableland is a person who has not yet learned to read the mat, and the mat does not wait. A child who walks without attention will step into a section that is in the collapse phase, and the ground will give way beneath them — not far, a foot, maybe two, but enough to twist an ankle, and a twisted ankle on the Tableland is a serious thing, because the nearest person may be miles away, and the sound will cover any call for help, and the mat will grow over you in a day if you lie still.

So children learn fast, or they are carried, or they are kept on the stone. The cultivators build small pens — the stone rings you found, perhaps four feet across, perhaps a foot high — and these are not ritual structures. They are creches. A child who cannot yet walk the mat is placed in a stone ring on a patch of cleared rock, and the ring keeps the growth from reaching them, and the child sits in the ring and feels the vibration through the stone and listens to the sound and watches the sky change, and this is their education. By the time they can climb out of the ring, they know the Tableland. They know it in their bones, literally — the vibration has been teaching them, the way a tuning fork teaches a glass its frequency, and their bones are attuned to the specific hum of this stone, this place, this particular almost-settling of a massif that is still finding its flat.

A child raised on the Tableland cannot leave. This is not law. It is physiology. Their bones are calibrated. Their inner ear is calibrated. When a Tableland person descends to the lowlands — which happens, rarely, for reasons of trade or curiosity or the specific kind of restlessness that sometimes surfaces in the young — they experience a disorientation that is not altitude sickness but its inverse. The lowlands are too quiet. The ground does not hum. The air does not press. They feel as though they are falling, constantly, the feeling you get when an elevator drops, and this feeling does not go away until they return. Some do not return. Some stay below, and grow thin, and pale, and lose the ability to read the mat, and become something else. The Tableland people do not mourn them. They say: *the stone let go*. It is not a metaphor.

## The Walkers

Among the cultivators, there is a role that is not a role but a calling. These are the walkers.

You will not see them. You may see their tracks — not footprints, because the mat erases footprints in hours, but the *trace* of their passage, a slight flattening of the growth along a line, a corridor of compressed vegetation that the mat is already repairing. If you are very lucky, and very still, and in the right place at the right time, you may see a walker at distance — a dark shape, low, moving across the undulations at a pace that seems slow from far away but which, when you calculate the distance, is remarkably fast. Walkers move at the speed of the Tableland's attention, which is to say they move as the weather moves: in shifts, in surges, pausing when the light goes hard and white, accelerating when the rain comes.

What walkers do is walk the entire Tableland. All of it. Every undulation, every exposed patch, every vein of pale mineral, every hummock and hollow. They walk it and they remember it, and the remembering is not in their heads — or not only in their heads — but in their feet, in the specific pattern of calluses that forms over years of walking, which is a map of the Tableland encoded in skin. An old walker's feet are unreadable to an outsider — a mass of hard tissue, ridged and grooved, ugly by any standard. But to another walker, or to a cultivator who has been trained to read them, the feet are a living chart. This ridge is the northern reach. This groove is the long scar of pale stone that crosses the eastern quarter. This smooth patch, here, on the ball of the left foot, is the place where the vibration is strongest, the deep hum that the walkers call *the throat*, the place where the stone is closest to its final settling and the almost-silence of the deep future can be heard, or felt, or imagined.

Walkers are the Tableland's memory. They carry knowledge of where the mat has changed its pattern, where the growth is slower or faster, where a new mineral vein has been exposed by the erosion of the surface layer. They carry knowledge of the structures — all of them, every ring and wall and hearth, scattered across the Tableland — and they maintain them, re-stacking stones that have been shifted by the frost or the growth, clearing the rings of encroaching vegetation, keeping the hearth-pits clean and ready. They do this not because anyone has asked them to. They do it because the structures are marks, and the marks say *I was here*, and the walkers understand that the *I* is not an individual but a continuity, a chain of people who have walked this surface and who will walk it, and the marks must be kept because the Tableland itself is a process of erasure, and without the marks, the walkers would have no proof that anyone has ever been here, that the Tableland is not as empty as it seems, that the sound and the green and the stone are not the only tenants.

## The Vegetation

It lives. You know this already. But it lives in a way that is not separate from the people, and the people live in a way that is not separate from it, and this is the thing that takes the longest to understand.

The vegetation is not intelligent. The cultivators are clear about this, and they would know. They have lived with it for longer than their histories record — their histories are short, oral, and unconcerned with duration, because on the Tableland, duration is the stone's concern, not theirs. The vegetation is not intelligent, but it is *responsive*, and the responsiveness is so total, so immediate, so finely calibrated to the conditions of the Tableland — the light, the moisture, the vibration, the wind — that it produces an effect that is indistinguishable from intention. When the cultivator parts the mat with her stick and presses a section down, the mat around the pressed section *adjusts*. Not retreats. Adjusts. The growth in the adjacent patches shifts direction, the stalks leaning away from the compressed zone, the leaves reorienting to fill the gap in the canopy that the pressing has created. Is this a decision? Is this a reflex? The cultivator does not ask. She watches. She responds to the response. And the vegetation responds to her response. And this is cultivation: not a human acting on a passive medium, but two responsive systems in conversation, each one adjusting to the other, the conversation producing a result that neither could produce alone.

The tubers grow where the cultivators plant them. But they also grow where the cultivators have not planted them, in places where the conditions are right, and the cultivators find these places and tend them, and the finding is a kind of planting, and the planting is a kind of finding. The distinction between wild and cultivated does not exist on the Tableland. There is only *tended* and *untended*, and the untended is not neglected — it is simply growing on its own terms, which are also the Tableland's terms, which are also the terms of the stone and the vibration and the weather. The cultivators do not own the vegetation. They are in relationship with it. And the relationship is old, older than the structures, older than the walking, old enough that the cultivators' bodies have adapted to the vegetation's rhythm — their sleep is not in blocks but in dips, short periods of rest taken when the mat enters its slow phase, which corresponds to the gray periods when the light is low and the growth-pause comes, a lull in the sound that the cultivators feel in their chests the way a sailor feels a change in the swell.

## The Ones Who Stayed

There is one more thing.

In the deepest part of the Tableland — not the center, because the Tableland has no center, but the place where the vibration is strongest, the place the walkers call the throat — there are people who do not move.

You will not find them unless a walker takes you, and a walker will not take you, because the throat is not a place for strangers. But if you were taken, if you were walked there through days of undulating green and stone, through the changing sky and the warm rain and the wall of sound that by now has become the inside of your own skull — if you arrived, you would find them.

They are sitting. They are sitting on the stone, on the exposed dark rock where the mat has thinned to nothing, where the pale veins are brightest, where the vibration rises through the stone and into the bones and becomes not a sensation but a state. They are sitting the way stones sit. Their hands are on the ground, palms down, fingers spread, the gesture that means *I am here*. Their eyes are open. They are not meditating. They are not praying. They are doing the thing that the Tableland is doing: settling. They are letting the vibration pass through them, and they are letting it take with it whatever in them is not settled, whatever is still turbulent, still unresolved, still holding the shape of a life that has not yet found its final form. They will sit for days. They will sit for weeks. They are fed by the walkers, who leave tubers and water-cups beside them and remove them when they are empty, and the sitters do not acknowledge this, because acknowledgment is a kind of motion, and they are not moving.

The walkers say that the sitters are listening. Not to the sound — the sound is surface, is churning, is the vegetation's conversation with itself. The sitters are listening to what is beneath the sound. To the vibration. To the stone's long almost-silence, the hum of a massif that has been settling for longer than life has existed and that will settle for longer than life will last. They are listening to the future. To the silence that the Tableland is moving toward, the silence that will come when the last grain is locked in place by the last root, when the green has finished its work and the stone is still. The sitters are hearing this silence. Or they are rehearsing it. Or they are, in their small way, participating in it — being still, being low, being dense, letting the vibration pass through them the way it passes through the stone, and in the passing, becoming a little more settled themselves.

They will come back. Eventually, they will stand, and the standing will be stiff and slow, and the walker who has been tending them will take their arm, and they will walk back to the family group, and they will eat, and they will sleep in the short dips, and they will return to the mat and the stick and the work of steering the growth. But something in them will have changed. Something will have been planed down, the way the mountains were planed, the way the stone was planed, and what is left will be smoother, denser, more nearly the irreducible thing. The walkers say: *they have heard the throat*. And the hearing changes the shape of the face, and the shape of the hands, and the way the person walks, and the way they touch the mat, and the way they sleep, and the way they sit by the hearth in the gray light, and everything about them is a little more still, a little more finished, a little more like the stone.

---

This is who lives on the Tableland.

Not a people, exactly. Not a nation, not a tribe, not a settlement. A relationship. A few hundred humans in dialogue with a surface that is older than their species, a vegetation that grows faster than their attention, a stone that is still settling into a flatness it will not achieve for a billion years. They live at the speed the place allows. They build with the material the place provides. They eat what the place grows, and they steer what the place grows, and the steering is not control but participation, and the participation is not a choice but a condition, because on the Tableland, to be alive is to be in the conversation, and the conversation is loud, and the conversation is old, and the conversation is not finished, and they are not finished, and the stone is not finished, and the green is not finished, and the sound goes on.
