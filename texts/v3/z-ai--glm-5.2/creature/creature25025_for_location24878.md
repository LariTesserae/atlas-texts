# What Lives in the Hollow of Omras

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here considers itself to be living underground.

This is not a cave to them. This is not a hollow, not a chamber, not a buried thing. To the inhabitants of this place — and they are inhabitants, in the fullest sense, the way a person is an inhabitant of a city they were born in and will die in without ever leaving — this is the world. The whole world. The only world. The ceiling is the sky. The stone is the ground. The faint gray not-light is the sun, or what a sun would be if it had never been anything but dim and directionless and sufficient. There is no above. The concept of above requires a below to have come from, and nothing here has come from below, because there is no below. There is the hole in the plaza, and below that is more stone, and below that is more stone, and at some point the stone becomes so deep and so pressured that it becomes something else, but nothing alive has ever been there, so it does not matter.

They do not know the city is there.

This is important and it is easy to get wrong. The creatures of Omras do not live *in* the ruins. They do not live *among* the ruins. They do not have any relationship to the civilization that built this place, because that civilization is so far in the past that the word "past" has no purchase on the timescale involved. The city is not ruins to them. The city is geology. The rectangular absences where the iron-gray growth does not grow are, to the creatures that live here, simply features of the terrain — the way a valley is a feature, the way a ridge is a feature. They do not wonder why the growth is absent in those places. They do not have the capacity to wonder, but even if they did, the question would be as meaningless to them as asking why a mountain is where it is. It is where it is. The growth does not grow there. The stone is slightly different there. These are properties of the world, not memories of something the world used to be.

## The Grazers

Let me describe them more carefully, because "beetle-like" and "mite-like" are approximations that betray a terrestrial bias these creatures do not share.

The most abundant organisms in the Hollow are the ones you see when you disturb a stand of growth and they scatter. They are low — flat against the stone or the frond-surface, their bodies barely raised above the substrate they are feeding on. The largest are hand-sized. Most are much smaller, down to a millimeter or two, and at that scale they become difficult to distinguish from the texture of the growth itself. They are, in effect, a living layer on the surface of the dead layer, and the two together — grazer and growth — constitute something closer to a single system than two separate ones.

Their bodies are segmented, but not in the way of earthly arthropods. The segments are not articulated. There are no joints in the sense that your biology would recognize. Instead, the body is a series of plates — mineral plates, you think, actual stone-hard structures — that overlap like roof tiles, and the creature moves by flexing the tissue between the plates, which is soft and dark and has the texture of wet clay though it is not wet. The motion is slow, deliberate, and produces a faint scraping sound as the plates shift against each other. This scraping — multiplied by the millions, by the tens of millions — is the upper layer of the Hollow's sound. The stridulation. It is not communication. It is locomotion. They are not singing to each other. They are walking, and the walking is so constant and so widespread that it has become a property of the air itself.

They feed by pressing their mouthparts — and this is a crude word for what they do, but there is no better one — against the surface of the growth or the stone and *dissolving* it. Not mechanically abrading it. Dissolving it. There is a chemical exchange at the contact point, a secretion that breaks down the mineral structure of the substrate and allows the creature to absorb the resulting compounds. The process is slow. A grazer will sit in one place for what might be hours — it is hard to tell, because they do not move and the light does not change and there is no way to mark the passage of time except by watching the faint bright spot on the growth where the creature has been feeding, the place where the iron-gray has been lightened to a pale ash color by the removal of whatever mineral the grazer is after.

They are extracting iron. That is what the growth concentrates — iron oxide, iron sulfide, the iron that gives the growth its color and its substance — and the grazers are processing it, building their plates from it, incorporating it into their bodies. They are, in a literal sense, eating the rust and building themselves out of it. When they die — and they do die, though the process is so slow it resembles fading more than dying — their plates remain. They become part of the substrate. The growth colonizes them. New grazers feed on the growth that grows on the old grazers. The cycle is so complete that there is no waste. Nothing is lost. Everything is reprocessed, reabsorbed, rebuilt.

This is why the channels exist. The shallow erosion features in the stone floor — the twenty-centimeter-deep grooves that the growth follows — are not purely chemical erosion. They are grazing trails. Generations upon generations of grazers, feeding on the stone, following the paths of least resistance, the places where the mineral composition is most accessible, have worn these channels into the granite. Not by force. By chemistry. By the patient application of solvent secretions to stone, over a timescale that makes the Grand Canyon look like a hasty project. The grazers have been eating the floor of this cavern for so long that their feeding has become the dominant geological process. The stone is being consumed. Slowly. Imperceptibly. But it is being consumed, and the channels are the evidence, and the growth follows the channels because the channels expose the minerals the growth needs, and the grazers follow the growth because the growth concentrates the minerals the grazers need. The whole system — stone, growth, grazer — is a single slow machine for moving iron from one place to another.

## The Growth

It is not a plant. It is not a fungus. It is not a lichen. It may not be a single organism. It may be a community — a consortium of mineral-depositing structures that operate together because together they are more efficient than apart. But from the outside, and from the inside too, probably, it behaves as a single thing, and so I will describe it as a single thing.

The growth does not reproduce in any way you can observe. It spreads. The frond-like structures extend from their tips — you can see this if you watch for a long time, though "a long time" here means hours, and you will not see motion in the way you expect motion. What you will see, if you mark the tip of a frond with a scratch and return later, is that the tip is further from your mark than it was. The growth has extended. Not by growing in the biological sense — not by cell division, not by any process that adds new material from inside — but by *depositing* material at the growing edge. It is building itself. It is precipitating iron compounds from the air and the stone and assembling them into structures at its boundary, and the structures are the fronds, and the fronds are the growth, and the growth is the landscape.

It does not react to touch. It does not react to damage. When you break a piece off, the break is permanent — not because the growth cannot heal, but because healing is not a concept that applies. The growth does not have an integrity to violate. It is not an organism with a boundary between self and other. It is a process, and the process continues whether any given piece of it is intact or not. Breaking a frond is like breaking a stalactite. The stalactite does not care. The water will continue to deposit minerals where it can. The growth will continue to deposit iron where it can. The broken piece will lie on the floor and eventually be colonized by new growth, or eaten by grazers, or both, and its material will re-enter the cycle, and nothing will have been lost.

The growth is, in a sense, the dead thing that the living things live on. But it is also alive, or near enough to alive that the distinction is theological rather than practical. It grows. It responds to its environment — it fills the low places, it avoids the rectangles where the ancient buildings stood, it thickens where the mineral content is richest. It does these things without any apparent mechanism for sensing its environment, without any nervous system, without any of the apparatus that living things use to decide where to grow. It grows where it can. It does not grow where it cannot. The difference between these two things is the shape of the world, and the growth conforms to it the way water conforms to a container — not by deciding to, but by having no other option.

The dried-blood-colored fronds — the rust-red stands that appear here and there, taller and thicker than the iron-gray growth around them — are the same organism, or the same process, operating under different conditions. Where the sulfur content is higher — near the hole in the plaza, near the fissures that connect to deeper levels — the growth incorporates sulfur into its structures, and the sulfur changes the color. The red fronds are iron-sulfide growths. The gray fronds are iron-oxide growths. The difference is chemical, not biological, and the grazers that feed on the red fronds are different from the grazers that feed on the gray fronds because they have adapted to process sulfur compounds instead of oxide compounds. They are the pale ones. The slow ones near the hole. They have sulfur in their plates, and their plates are softer, and they live longer, or what passes for longer here, where time is measured in the deepening of channels and the extension of fronds and the slow exchange of air through passages that have been breathing since before the city was built.

## The Ones Near the Edge

The far edge of the cavern, where the walls become visible and the growth changes from iron-gray to near-black, is where the system becomes something else.

The black growth is not the same organism. It is not the same process. It is doing something different. It is not spreading across the floor in fractal patterns — it is climbing the walls. It is building vertically. The fibers — and they are fibers, not fronds, not plates, but long, thin, dense structures like cables — extend upward along the stone, and they are strong. You cannot break them by hand. They have the tensile strength of something that has been annealed by time, and they are anchored to the wall by structures that look like roots but are not roots — they are chemical welds, points where the fiber has fused with the stone itself, altering the mineral structure at the contact point to create a bond that is not mechanical but molecular.

The creatures that live in the black growth are different from the grazers in the open cavern. They are quicker. Their bodies are darker — not gray or pale but a deep, lustrous black, like hematite, like polished coal. Their plates are harder. They move with a speed that the grazers do not possess, and they move with what appears to be intention — not the mechanical relocation of the grazers, who simply go to the nearest acceptable surface when disturbed, but a directed motion, a going-toward, a going-away-from. They react to your presence. Not dramatically. Not with fear or aggression. But they *notice*. They change their behavior based on what you do. They are, you think, the most complex things in this cavern. They may be the most complex things that have ever existed here, including the city-builders, though there is no way to compare, because the city-builders left nothing but absences and the black-growth creatures leave nothing but the black growth.

They are eating the black growth. But they are also — and this is the thing that unsettles you, that makes you turn back — they are *maintaining* it. You watch one of them for a long time, a creature the size of your thumb, moving along a fiber at a pace that is neither fast nor slow but precise, and it stops at a point where the fiber has cracked — a small crack, a fracture in the mineral structure — and it does something. It presses its mouthparts to the crack. It stays there. And when it moves on, the crack is smaller. The fiber is intact again, or nearly. The creature has repaired it. It has deposited material at the break point, sealed it, continued.

This is not grazing. This is construction. This is care.

The black-growth creatures are not eating their environment. They are *tending* it. They are building it and repairing it and extending it, and the black growth is not a passive mineral deposit but a structure — a built thing, an architecture — and the creatures are its builders and its maintainers and its inhabitants. They live in it the way a civilization lives in a city. They are not the first things in this cavern to build something. But they may be the first things in this cavern to build something that is still standing.

## The Sound, Reconsidered

The sound is not noise. You understand this now, or you begin to.

The deepest layer — the settling of the stone — is the sound of the world adjusting to its own weight. It is the closest thing this place has to weather. It is the environment in motion, the slowest motion, the motion that everything else is built on.

The middle layer — the air moving through the passages — is the breath of the cavern. It is the exchange that keeps the atmosphere from becoming stagnant, that brings the sulfur up from below, that carries the chemical signals the growth needs to know where to extend. It is the circulatory system.

The highest layer — the stridulation of the grazers, the scraping of plate on plate, the ceaseless mechanical whisper of millions of bodies in motion — is the sound of the living world going about its business. It is not communication. It is not language. But it is *collective*. It is the sound of a system operating, the way a heartbeat is the sound of a body operating, the way traffic is the sound of a city operating. It is the proof that something is happening here, that something is alive, that the process is continuing.

And near the edges, where the black growth climbs the walls and the darker creatures tend their structures, the sound changes. It becomes more organized. The scraping is not random there — it has patterns, rhythms, a regularity that the open-cavern stridulation lacks. The black-growth creatures make sound when they move, and they move in coordination, and the sound of their movement is not the white noise of the grazers but something closer to — you hesitate — something closer to a pulse. A rhythm. A regular, repeating, coordinated vibration that moves through the black growth like a wave, and the black growth transmits it, and the walls of the cavern transmit it, and the sound that reaches you at the plaza, at the hole, at the center of this place, is not three separate layers but one layered thing, one composition, and it has been playing for so long that it is no longer possible to tell where the environment ends and the music begins.

## What Does Not Live Here Anymore

The city-builders are gone so thoroughly that their absence has become geology. But they were here. They cut the staircase. They planed the corridor walls. They surveyed the grid. They chose the stone for the plaza. They built things that stood for a span of time that your species cannot conceptualize — millions of years, tens of millions, a duration that reduces every human monument to a momentary interruption in the flow of mineral from one configuration to another.

They are not here. They have not been here for so long that the word "here" has changed meaning since they left, or died, or became something that no longer builds. The growth does not remember them. The grazers do not remember them. The black-growth creatures do not remember them. The stone does not remember them. The only memory is the pattern of absence in the vegetation, and even that is not a memory — it is a consequence, a physical result of a physical intervention that altered the chemistry of the floor enough to inhibit a process that had not yet begun when the intervention occurred.

The city-builders did not live in the world the grazers live in. The city-builders lived in a different world, a world with a ceiling that was higher or a ceiling that was not there, a world where the light was different or the air was different or the stone was different. They lived in the Hollow before it was the Hollow. They lived in a place that no longer exists, in a time that no longer exists, and they left behind a shape in the stone that the current inhabitants navigate without knowing they are navigating it.

This is what lives in the Hollow of Omras: a process. A slow, mineral, iron-driven process that converts stone to growth and growth to grazer and grazer to stone, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and no purpose except the continuation of the cycle. And at the edges, something that may be more than a process — something that builds, something that repairs, something that coordinates — something that is, in the most cautious and minimal sense of the word, *becoming*.

The sound does not diminish. It will not diminish. It is what this place is, and this place has been what it is for longer than anything you know has been anything, and it will continue to be what it is for longer than anything you know will become anything else.

The grazers graze. The growth grows. The black-growth creatures tend their walls. The stone settles. The air moves. The sound continues.

This is what lives here.
