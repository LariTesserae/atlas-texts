# What Lives in the Basin of Standing Sound

---

## The Felt

Start with the oldest thing, because everything else is a footnote to it.

The felt-mats have no name. They do not need one. They were here before naming was invented, and they will be here after the last thing capable of naming has been dissolved by the basin's patient water chemistry. They are not one organism or many — the distinction requires a self/other framework that does not apply. They are a *condition* of the stone. Where water pools and mineral gradients are steep enough, the felt grows. Where it does not, the felt is absent. This is the full extent of its preferences.

Under a microscope — and no one has brought a microscope here, but if one were brought — the felt would resolve into something like a city seen from very high up. Dense, irregular, with no central organization and no boundaries between districts. Cells, if they are cells, are tiny, pentagonal, fitted together with a precision that is not design but simply the consequence of packing under pressure. They are colorless. The pale, gelatinous look of the mats comes from the water they hold between themselves, not from any pigment. There is no pigment. There has never been pigment. Light is not a resource here, and anything that wasted energy on light-interaction would have been outcompeted by something that didn't, a billion years ago, or a hundred million, or however long it has been. The felt does not track time.

What it does is chemistry. The water that seeps through the basin floor carries dissolved minerals — iron, manganese, sulfur compounds, traces of hydrogen — and the felt sits at the boundary where these minerals meet oxygen-poor stone and oxygen-rich water. It metabolizes the gradient. It eats the difference. The sulfur goes in one direction, the hydrogen in another, and the felt extracts a marginal fraction of the energy released by their unwillingness to coexist. It is a slow meal. The felt grows at a rate perhaps measurable in millimeters per century, and some patches may be contiguous with patches that began growing when the basin was a different shape, when the bedrock had not yet cracked, when the water came from somewhere else.

The felt is the only thing in the basin that does not make sound. It simply sits, and metabolizes, and grows, and has been doing so for so long that it constitutes a geological feature as much as a biological one. If the basin were drained, if the water were removed and the noise stopped and everything else died, the felt would persist. It would slow. It would wait. It is very good at waiting.

Everything else in the basin is here because the felt is here. Everything else is, in one way or another, a mechanism for finding it and eating it.

## The Scrapers

The most numerous animals are called, for convenience, scrapers. No one has named them formally because no one has been able to study them without going deaf in the process. The name is functional. They scrape. It is nearly all they do.

A scraper is small — eight to twelve millimeters long, roughly the width of a human thumbnail, though flatter. The body plan is, as the description notes, trilobite-like, and the resemblance is not entirely superficial. The body is divided into segments — usually between nine and fourteen, varying by population — each bearing a pair of broad, flat appendages fringed with cilia. The cilia are the primary means of locomotion, beating in coordinated waves that propel the scraper across wet surfaces at a speed that seems glacially slow but that is, relative to the scraper's body length, respectable. They can cross a square meter of stone in perhaps three minutes. They do not hurry. There is nothing to hurry from that they can outrun, and nothing to hurry toward that will not still be there when they arrive.

The mouth is the defining feature. It runs the full width of the body's anterior end — a broad, flat mouth, not unlike a razor blade in shape, edged with chitinous scrapers that are themselves segmented and replaceable. When a scraper feeds, it presses this mouth flat against the stone and draws it forward, shaving the felt-mat from the rock in a strip perhaps a millimeter wide. The motion produces a click. One click per strip. The felt is scraped free, ingested, digested in a gut that runs the length of the body and is, in essence, a single tube with no elaboration — no stomach chambers, no symbiotic compartments, just a straight passage from mouth to anus with enzyme secretion along the way. The scraper extracts what it can — primarily the organic carbon the felt has fixed from CO₂, secondarily the mineral traces the felt has concentrated — and excretes the rest as a fine paste that dissolves back into the water film.

They are translucent. You can see the gut working. In the diffuse grey light, a feeding scraper is a tiny window into biology — the pale stripe of ingested felt visible through the body wall, darkening as it moves through the digestive tract, lightening again as it exits. They are, in their way, beautiful, though the word feels wrong here. They are *clear*. They are so transparent to their own function that looking at one is like looking at a diagram of an organism rather than the organism itself.

They have no eyes. They have no need of eyes. They navigate by chemical gradient — moving toward the felt's metabolic signature and away from the acid byproducts of their own dense populations. They can also sense vibration, though poorly; the constant noise of the basin means that vibration-sensing is less useful than it might be in a quieter place. Mostly, they follow chemistry. The basin, to a scraper, is a landscape of taste. The felt is sweet. The open stone is bland. The edges of structures — the built structures, the ancient ones — taste wrong.

The wrongness is subtle. The scrapers do not perceive it as a signal or a barrier. They simply reach a point where the stone's mineral content shifts — something in the old mortar, or something absorbed by the worked stone from the countless hands that placed it — and the taste becomes disagreeable, and they turn. They have been turning at that boundary for longer than they have been scrapers, in this form. Their ancestors turned. Their ancestors' ancestors turned. The avoidance is not learned. It is not even, at this point, genetic in any specific sense. It is simply what scrapers *do* when they reach a place that tastes like old buildings. It is as much a part of them as the clicking of their mouthparts.

There are, by rough estimate, somewhere between two hundred million and a billion scrapers in the basin at any given time. The number fluctuates. They reproduce by a method that is neither clearly sexual nor clearly asexual — a scraper, when it has fed enough and when the population density around it is neither too high nor too low, will bud a smaller scraper from its posterior segments. The bud detaches, crawls away, and begins feeding within hours. There is no larval stage. There is no childhood. A scraper is born scraping and will scrape until it is eaten or until its mouthparts wear down beyond replacement, which takes perhaps four to six months.

Four to six months. In that time, a single scraper will produce, by conservative estimate, thirty million clicks. Multiply by the population. The sound is not rain on a tin roof. It is a *geological force*. It is the dominant acoustic feature of the basin's biological layer, and it has been continuous, without interruption, for an unknowable span of time. The clicking has never stopped. Not once. Not for a second. The scrapers do not sleep.

They do not need to. The felt is always there. The light is always the same. There is no cycle to sync to. They feed and they bud and they die and they are eaten and there are always more, and the clicking is the sound of the basin's largest population doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is to shave the world bare, one millimeter at a time.

## The Waders

The larger predators — the things that move through the shallow water in V-shaped wakes — are harder to describe, because they are harder to see. They spend most of their lives submerged in the shallowest parts of the basin, where the standing water is three to five centimeters deep, and their bodies are the same color as the mineral soup they move through: a dark, noncommittal brown-grey that makes them effectively invisible from above. You know they are there because the water moves. The V-wakes are their signature.

Call them waders. It is not a perfect name — they do not wade so much as flow — but it will serve.

A wader is roughly the length of a human forearm, perhaps twenty-five to thirty centimeters, and half as wide. The body is flat. Aggressively flat. A wader's body is perhaps two centimeters thick at its highest point, and most of that height is taken up by the mouth. The wader is, in functional terms, a mouth with locomotion attached.

The head is wide — wider than the body, flaring out on either side like the head of a hammerhead shark, though the evolutionary logic is different. At each outermost point sits a small eye, dark, simple, probably capable of distinguishing light from dark and movement from stillness but not much else. Between the eyes, filling the entire center of the head, is the mouth. It is a wide, low slit, framed by bony ridges that function as teeth — not pointed teeth, but flat, grinding plates, top and bottom, like the jaws of a nutcracker. The wader does not bite. It *compresses*. A scraper, or several, are drawn in by the current the wader's mouth creates, and the jaws close, and the scrapers are crushed, and the wader swallows, and the jaws open again, and it moves on.

The limbs are the most distinctive feature. Four of them — two forward, two rear — and they are not legs in any conventional sense. They are wide, flat, spadelike paddles, jointed in two places, that push against the water and the substrate simultaneously. A wader in motion is using its limbs to row through water that is barely deep enough to cover its body, and the limbs are also touching the ground, feeling for the texture of the stone, the distribution of the scraper-populations. The wader can feel the scrapers through its limbs. The clicking of a dense scraper colony vibrates the stone, and the wader's limbs pick up that vibration, and the wader turns toward it.

They are not fast. Nothing in the basin is fast. The wader moves at perhaps the speed of a human walk, which, for an animal its size in water this shallow, is a reasonable pace. It does not chase its prey. It *sifts* its prey. It moves through a dense patch of scrapers with its mouth open, and the current its body creates pulls scrapers into its jaws, and it closes its mouth at intervals — not targeting individual scrapers, but simply closing at a rate that seems to correlate with prey density. The denser the scrapers, the more frequently the jaws close. In a very dense patch, a wader will close its mouth perhaps twice per second, each closure producing a soft, wet, percussive sound — a *thock* — that is part of the basin's rhythmic layer, the syncopation beneath the clicking.

The waders are solitary. They do not interact with each other except when they must, and when they must, the interaction is not what you would call social. A wader encountering another wader will either ignore it or attempt to eat it, and the deciding factor seems to be size differential. If the other wader is within roughly twenty percent of the first wader's mass, it is ignored. If it is smaller, it is prey. If it is larger, it is threat, and the first wader will alter course to avoid it. There is no territory. There is no pairing. There is no parenting.

Reproduction, when it occurs, is explosive and rare. A wader that has fed heavily for several weeks will begin to swell along its posterior, and one night — if "night" means anything in this lightless place, but there is a slight dimming of the grey glow that seems to correspond to something — the wader will release a cloud of eggs into the shallow water and immediately leave. The eggs are small, translucent, and slightly buoyant in the mineral soup. They float just above the substrate, and within a day, they hatch into miniature waders, already mobile, already hungry, already too small to be worth a larger wader's attention. Most of them will be eaten within a week by waders that are only slightly larger. The ones that survive will grow, and within a month, they will be large enough to eat the ones that are still small. The wader population is, at all times, eating itself.

They do not enter the structures. The structures taste wrong to them, too, though "taste" is a looser word for a wader than for a scraper — the wader's chemical sensing is coarser, less discriminating. But the wader also feels the structures with its limbs, and something in the stone's texture, or the stone's resonance, or the stone's *difference* from the surrounding ground, makes the wader turn aside. It does not turn sharply. It does not flee. It simply will not cross the threshold, and it does not know why, and it does not care, because there are scrapers everywhere else, and the structures are empty of scrapers, and so there is no reason to enter them anyway. The avoidance may be chemical, or acoustic, or something else entirely. The waders do not reflect on it.

They make very little sound. The *thock* of their jaws. The slop of their limbs pushing through shallow water. Otherwise, they are silent. They are the basin's quietest animals, which is ironic, given that they are the basin's most effective killers. They have no call. They have no display. They have nothing to say. They are hungry, and then they are less hungry, and then they are hungry again, and one day they are swollen with eggs, and then they are empty, and then they are hungry, and then they are eaten by something larger or they simply stop, and their bodies dissolve into the mineral soup, and the felt absorbs what remains.

## The Ribbons

The air-feeders are the strangest things in the basin. They are also the most difficult to describe, because they are the most difficult to see. They live in the haze, in the perpetual condensation, in the grey air that is not quite atmosphere and not quite ocean. They are the things that make you look up — or rather, look *out*, because "up" does not mean much in a place without shadows or sky — and see something moving that should not be moving.

Call them ribbons. Their bodies are long, flat, and almost weightless. A mature ribbon is perhaps sixty centimeters long, no wider than a finger, and thin enough that it is genuinely translucent — not merely transparent, but *translucent* in the way that frosted glass is translucent, scattering light without blocking it. In the diffuse grey glow, a ribbon is visible only as a slight distortion, a ripple in the haze, a place where the moisture seems to be moving with more purpose than mist usually has.

The ribbon's body is a single, continuous sheet of tissue, folded along its length into a tube that is open at both ends. The ribbon moves by undulating — sending waves of contraction down its body length that propel it through the air in the same way that an eel propels itself through water, except that the medium is thinner and the ribbon's motion is correspondingly more extreme. A ribbon in flight — and "flight" is the best word, though it does not capture the slowness, the deliberateness, the almost contemplative quality of the movement — a ribbon in flight looks like a ribbon in water, which is to say, it looks like a flag in a very gentle wind, except that the flag is alive and has chosen its direction.

What it is doing, as it moves, is feeding. The ribbon's tube-body is lined with cilia — much finer than the scrapers' cilia, and far more numerous — that create a continuous current, drawing air in through the anterior opening and pushing it out through the posterior. As the air passes through, the ribbon's internal lining extracts particulate matter: micro-droplets of mineral water, airborne bacteria (there are some, though they are sparse), fragments of felt that have been aerosolized by the steam-vents, and — most importantly — the fine mist of organic particles that the scrapers produce as waste. The ribbons are, in a sense, eating the scrapers' exhaust. They are the basin's air-filtration system, and they are everywhere.

They concentrate around the steam-vents. This is where the warm, wet updrafts carry the richest material, and the ribbons gather in loose, drifting aggregations, circling the vents in slow, wide spirals, riding the thermals like birds of prey — except that they are not hunting. They are *breathing*. The warm air moves through them, and they extract what they need, and the air exits slightly cleaner, slightly drier, and the ribbon moves on. A single ribbon can process perhaps its own body-volume of air every few seconds. With hundreds of ribbons circling a single vent, the cumulative effect is noticeable — the air above the vents is clearer than the air elsewhere in the basin, and the visibility near the vents is slightly better, which means that the vents are one of the few places in the basin where you can see more than a few meters.

The ribbons make sound, but barely. The papery rustling of their movement is the quietest biological sound in the basin. It is high-pitched, thin, almost spectral — a whispering in the upper registers that is audible only when the geological drone and the scraper-clicking momentarily align in a way that opens a brief window in the lower frequencies. These moments are rare and unpredictable, and when they occur, the ribbon-sound is briefly, startlingly clear, and then gone. It is the basin's most elusive sound. It is possible that the builders heard it, in their quiet rooms, and that it was the sound they listened for — the one sound worth hearing in a world of noise.

The ribbons reproduce by fragmentation. A ribbon that has been feeding well for a sufficient period will simply break in half, and each half will regenerate its missing end, and there will be two ribbons where there was one. This can happen multiple times. There is no limit, except that each fragment needs to be large enough to maintain the ciliary current, which means that below a certain size — perhaps five centimeters — a fragment cannot feed and will starve. The ribbons do not seem to age. A ribbon that does not fragment and is not damaged may simply continue indefinitely, growing longer, undulating through the haze, until something disrupts it.

The things that disrupt them are unclear. The waders do not eat them — the ribbons are in the air, and the waders are in the water, and the two domains do not meet. The scrapers do not interact with them. Nothing in the basin seems to prey on the ribbons directly. And yet their numbers are stable, not infinite, which means that something limits them. Perhaps it is simple: a ribbon that grows too long becomes too heavy to stay aloft, and it settles into the water, and it drowns, or rather, it *suffocates* — the cilia cannot function in water, and the ribbon, immersed, cannot feed, and it dissolves. Or perhaps the ribbons are damaged by the basin's geological activity — a sudden increase in steam pressure, a shift in the fissures, and the ribbon is torn. Or perhaps they simply wear out, the cilia eroding over months or years until the ribbon can no longer maintain its current, and it settles and dies.

They do not enter the structures' airspace. This is the most striking avoidance behavior in the basin. The ribbons, moving through the air above the structures, will curve — visibly, unmistakably — around the structures' boundaries, as though the air above the stones is repulsive to them. The effect is not subtle. From inside a structure, looking out through the gaps in the corbelled roof, you can see ribbons approaching and then bending away, tracing the structure's outline in their flight paths. It is as though the structures project an invisible dome, and the ribbons cannot enter it.

Whatever the builders did to the stone — whatever the grooves accomplish, acoustically — it disturbs the air in ways that the ribbons cannot tolerate. Perhaps the silence itself is wrong to them. Perhaps the ribbons navigate by sound, and the structures' acoustic shadow is a kind of blindness, and they cannot enter what they cannot navigate. Or perhaps the structures absorb something the ribbons need — a vibration, a frequency, a resonance that the ribbons require to maintain their ciliary rhythm. The quiet rooms may be quiet because they have taken something from the air, something the ribbons need, and the ribbons feel its absence and turn away.

## The Builders

They are gone. They have been gone for a long time. But they lived here, and they built here, and while they were here, they were the basin's strangest inhabitants — the ones that did not fit, the ones that arrived from somewhere else and stayed, and made something, and then left, or died, or were dissolved.

They were human. The tools are human tools — flaked stone, hand-sized, worked with a technique that is recognizable if not identifiable. The structures are human structures — built to a human scale, sized for human bodies, with doorways a human could walk through and ceilings a human could stand beneath. The carvings are made by human hands. There is no other explanation, and there does not need to be one. People came to the basin. They lived in the basin. They built in the basin. They carved silence into stone, one groove at a time, over what must have been generations, and then they were gone.

How they lived is a question that the basin does not answer easily. There is nothing here for humans to eat. The felt is digestible only by organisms with the appropriate enzyme systems, and humans do not have them. The scrapers are edible, in principle — they are protein and chitin, and there is no particular reason a human could not eat them — but harvesting them in quantity would require tools or techniques for which no evidence remains. The waders are edible. The ribbons are not. The felt is not. The water is mineral-heavy and would be unpleasant to drink, though not toxic. There is no fuel. There is no dry material for fire. The light is too dim for agriculture. The temperature is too cold for comfort.

They must have brought food with them, or they must have come and gone, or they must have found a way to harvest the basin's biology that left no trace. Or — and this is the possibility that the basin itself seems to suggest — they did not live here long. They came, they built, they carved, and they left. Not a settlement. A *project*. A work site. A place where people came to do a specific thing, and the specific thing was to build rooms that were quiet, in a place that was loud.

Why? The question is unanswerable, but the structures offer a kind of answer in their existence. The basin is the loudest place these people ever encountered. It was loud enough to cause pain, loud enough to disorient, loud enough to make thought difficult. And in that loudness, they found something — or looked for something, or needed something — that required silence. Not for comfort. For *concentration*. The carvings on the walls are not decorative, and they are not language, but they are also not random. They are the product of sustained, patient, focused work, done in rooms where the noise was reduced enough to permit sustained, patient, focused work.

What were they doing? Carving. That is what they were doing. The carving was the activity and the purpose, simultaneously. They were cutting grooves into stone to make the room quieter, so that they could cut more grooves into stone to make the room quieter still. The project was self-referential, recursive, endless. Each generation of builders inherited rooms that were quieter than their parents had known, and each generation made them quieter again, and the rooms became — over centuries, perhaps, or millennia — the quietest places in the basin, and possibly the quietest places in the world. And then the builders were gone, and the rooms remained, and the quiet remained, and the animals still avoid them, and the quiet is still here, and it is still insufficient, and it is still enough.

The builders, while they were here, were the basin's only temporary inhabitants. Everything else is permanent. The felt has been here forever. The scrapers have been here since the felt was here. The waders have been here since the scrapers. The ribbons have been here since the air was thick enough to feed them. The builders came, and they stayed for a span of time that was, by the basin's measure, nothing at all, and then they left, and the basin continued as though they had never come. Except for the stones. Except for the grooves. Except for the quiet.

## What Lives Here

The Basin of Standing Sound is not empty. It is the opposite of empty. It is so full of life that the life itself has become a geological force, a sound source, a feature of the landscape as fundamental as the water or the stone. The felt grows. The scrapers scrape. The waders wade. The ribbons drift. The noise never stops. The water never dries. The light never changes. The structures stand, empty and quiet, and the animals avoid them, and the grooves in the walls still catch the sound that bleeds through, and the room is still the closest thing to silence in a world that does not otherwise offer it.

What lives here is a system. Not a community — that word implies relationship, recognition, the acknowledgment of one thing by another. The things in the basin do not acknowledge each other. The felt does not know the scrapers exist. The scrapers do not know the waders are hunting them. The waders do not know the ribbons are overhead. The ribbons do not know anything. They are all here, together, in the same wet, loud, lightless place, and they are all doing what they do, and none of them is doing it for any of the others. The basin is not an ecosystem in the sense of a cooperative venture. It is a *coincidence* of organisms, all of whom found a way to survive in the same impossible conditions, and whose survival happens to interlock — the felt feeds the scrapers, the scrapers feed the waders, the scrapers' waste feeds the ribbons, the ribbons' filtering cleans the air, the clean air changes the water, the changed water feeds the felt — and the interlock has been turning for so long that it has become a machine, and the machine has become a sound, and the sound has become a place.

The Basin of Standing Sound. It stands. It sounds. It has been doing both for longer than anything can remember, because nothing here remembers. The felt does not remember. The scrapers do not remember. The waders do not remember. The ribbons do not remember. The structures remember, in their way — the grooves remember, the shape of the rooms remembers — but the structures are not alive. They are the ghost of something that was alive, and that built, and that is gone, and that left behind the only thing it knew how to leave: a small, stone room where, for a little while, the noise drops away, and you can hear yourself think, and the thinking is the hardest thing you have ever done, because the silence is so unfamiliar that your mind does not know what to do with it.

That is what lives here. The felt. The scrapers. The waders. The ribbons. The noise. The water. The stone. The quiet rooms. The absence of the people who made them. All of it, together, in the grey wet, clicking and breathing and drifting and standing, and none of it knowing, and all of it continuing.
