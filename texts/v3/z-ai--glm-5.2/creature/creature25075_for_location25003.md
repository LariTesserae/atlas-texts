# The Weeping Works (continued)

## The Sound (continued)

The pipes tick and clank and groan with thermal expansion, a percussion that varies with the day's hidden pressures and the slow exhalations of whatever geological source feeds the system from below. Steam vents at irregular intervals — not from the pipes themselves but from the ground, from fissures between the paving stones, from the weeping walls. Each vent has its pitch. Each has its rhythm. Some sigh on a cycle of minutes. Some go quiet for hours and then speak once, a low exhalation that shudders the water on every nearby surface, and are silent again. The cumulative effect is that the Weeping Works breathes audibly, and whatever lives here lives inside that breath.

There is another sound beneath the mechanical one. A second stratum. It took you time to separate it from the patter of weeping walls and the mutter of pipes, but once heard it cannot be unheard: voices. Low, unhurried, in a register that sits just below ordinary conversation. Not whispering. Speaking at the volume the air will carry, which is not much. The moisture absorbs sound the way it absorbs light. Words do not travel here. They arrive and dissolve a few feet from the mouth that made them. The inhabitants have learned to speak as if each word is a small and precious quantity of warmth, something to be held close.

You do not see them at first.

## The Inhabitants

You become aware of them the way you become aware of the ground-breathing — slowly, through accumulation. A shape in a doorway that you took for a shadow shifts its weight. A texture on a bench against a weeping wall resolves, after a minute of looking, into a folded leg, a hand resting on a knee. They are there. They have been there. You have been walking past them.

The first one you truly see is sitting on a stone ledge beside one of the breaching pipe-joints, where a valve handle rises from the pavement like an iron flower. She — you think she, though the body is so layered with cloth and damp that gender is a guess, an imposition — is wrapped in fabric that has gone the way of everything here. It was once something woven, something dyed, perhaps blue or grey. Now it is the color of the basalt: dark, mottled, the fibers swollen with constant moisture, the surface furred with a fine nap of something between mold and felt. It does not look unhealthy. It looks like the cloth has entered into the same negotiation with the environment as the iron and the stone, arriving at a state that is no longer decay but something else — adaptation, perhaps, or surrender so complete it has become a form of persistence.

She is tending the valve. Not operating it. Tending it. Her hand rests on the handle and her fingers move in small circles, tracing the rust, feeling for something — a vibration, a temperature change, a spoke of pressure in the iron. Her eyes are open but unfocused. She is listening with her hand. After a long time — minutes, plural — she adjusts the valve by a degree so slight you do not see the movement, only the result: a change in the pipe's groan, a settling, like a sleeper shifting to a more comfortable position. She nods. She does not stand. She stays.

This is what they do here.

## The Tenders

There are not many of them. You count, over the hours of your descent into the Works, perhaps thirty. Maybe forty. It is difficult to say because they do not gather and they do not move with purpose in ways that make them easy to track. They occupy the structures the way moss occupies the furnace hall — finding the place where the conditions are tolerable and then staying, growing into the niche, becoming part of its texture.

They are human. You are certain of this, though certainty feels like a fragile thing to hold here. Their faces are the faces of people, their hands are the hands of people, but they have been changed by the long residence in this place the way the basalt has been changed by the water — slowly, from the surface inward, the pores opening, the composition shifting. Their skin is damp. Not sweating — the air is too saturated for sweat to accomplish anything — but damp in the way the walls are damp, as though the moisture has found a way to be inside them as well as outside. Their faces are flushed, not pale. The heat has done this. The capillaries near the surface have widened over years and decades, and the faces carry a permanent low color, a warm undertone beneath the gray, like a banked fire under ash.

Their eyes have adjusted. Not in the way of cave-dwellers — this is not darkness, merely dimness, the light arriving as if through water — but in the way of people who have learned to read the light they have. They do not look at things directly. They glance, and in the glance they take in more than you would in a long stare. Their eyes move constantly, in small darting saccades, reading the environment the way a sailor reads the sea — for pattern, for anomaly, for the small disruption that means something has changed. A pipe that has stopped groaning. A wall that has stopped weeping. A vent that has shifted pitch. To them, the Works is not a place. It is a condition, a living process, and they are the part of it that can notice.

They are thin. Not the thinness of deprivation, exactly — there is food here, dried fish and salted tubers and a hard black bread that seems to have been baked in one of the furnace halls before the ferns took it — but the thinness of people who live in heat, whose bodies have shed every unnecessary insulation, who have become efficient. Their movements are slow and economical. Not languid. Precise. Every gesture carries only the energy it needs and not a fraction more, because the air demands this, because to move quickly here is to generate heat your body cannot shed, and the inhabitants have learned over generations that stillness is not idleness but strategy.

## The Work

They call it the Works because it works. This is not metaphor. You learn this from the one who speaks to you, eventually, after you have sat for a long time on a bench beside the fern-curtained furnace hall and demonstrated that you can be still. He is old — or the heat has aged him past his years, the moisture and the mineral air doing to his face what the water does to the iron, softening the surface, eroding the sharpness — and he speaks in the low close register of everyone here, words that travel three feet and no further. You must sit beside him to hear.

The pipes carry water. Not fresh water. Geothermal water, drawn from below, from the basin's deep geology, from a source that is older than the structures and older than the stone. It comes up hot and mineral-heavy, loaded with compounds that coat the pipe interiors in a crust the tenders must manage. If the crust grows too thick, the flow chokes. If it thins too much, the water runs too fast and the heat at the surface drops, and the Works begins to cool, and the walls stop weeping, and the equilibrium that holds the whole system — the heat, the water, the moss, the ferns, the tenders themselves — collapses.

So they tend. They walk the pipe-line, the central artery, feeling the iron for temperature. They listen to the vents. They watch the walls. Each tender knows a section of the Works the way you might know your own body — where it aches, where it runs smooth, where a joint is stiffening, where the skin has gone dry. They adjust valves. They clear blockages with long iron rods they keep leaning against the walls like a kind of alphabet, each one a different length and gauge for a different pipe. They do not speak about this as work. They speak about it as listening. "The pipe was saying something," the old man tells you, and touches his chest. "Here. You feel it. And then you answer."

He shows you. He places your hand on a pipe joint where two sections meet, the iron so warm it is almost uncomfortable, almost too much, and he says wait. So you wait. And beneath the ticking, beneath the groan, beneath the damp percussion of the weeping walls, you feel something. A pulse. A change in pressure. A hesitation in the flow, like a heartbeat that stutters. You look at him. He nods.

"There," he says. "You heard it."

## The Children

There are children. Five of them, or six — one is at the age where it is always just out of sight, always around the corner, always in the next room, so you are never sure if you have counted it. They are small and brown and flushed like the adults, and they move through the Works with a fluency that makes the adults look clumsy by comparison. They have been born here. They have never known dry air. They have never known a room where the walls do not weep, where the floor is not warm, where the light comes sharp and the sound does not carry the deep constant hum of the earth's circulation.

They play, but their play is not the play of children elsewhere. They do not run — the heat forbids it, and besides, the footing is treacherous, the pavement slick with moisture, the standing water in the courtyards hiding stones that have loosened and tilted. They climb. They scramble over the low walls and through the empty window-frames of structures that have been given over to the green, and they hang from the iron bolts in the lintels and they drop into pools of warm water that collects in the courtyards and they sit there, up to their waists, their faces flushed and their eyes bright, and they listen. They are learning. Even at play, they are learning the language of the pipes.

One girl, perhaps eight, has a piece of iron rod — a smaller one, her size, not yet assigned to a pipe — and she taps it against the valves as she passes them, listening to the ring, cataloguing the tones. She does this without instruction. The old man watches her and there is something in his face that is not quite pride and not quite grief but the place where those two things share a border. He knows, as she does not yet, that she will tend this pipe-line for her whole life. That she will learn the grammar of its sounds and the dialect of its valves and that she will grow old in this heat and this wet and that her face will soften the way his has and that one day she will sit on a stone ledge beside a breathing pipe and teach someone else to listen.

This is not a tragedy. It is not a fate. It is simply what happens in a place that works.

## The Dead

There is a structure at the basin's center, at the lowest point, where the warm water pools into a shallow lake that smells of iron and sulfur and something older, something that has no name because it is not a smell that exists anywhere above the water table. The structure is the oldest in the Works. You can tell by the stone — a different cut, a different fit, the blocks smaller and more carefully shaped, the mortar channels thinner, as though the builders had time then, had ambition, had not yet learned the patience that the later builders encoded into their looser joints and rougher faces. The roof is gone. The walls remain, waist-high, enclosing a space that is half pool and half ruin.

The dead are here. Not buried. Placed. The tenders bring their dead to this structure and lay them in the warm water, and the water does what the water does. It is not a decay. It is not a preservation. It is something the tenders have a word for that does not translate — a sound that means *the work the water does on what was alive*, and the work is slow and mineral and complete. The bones remain, after a time, and they sit in the pool like the stones of the pavement, pocked and holed and damp, becoming part of the basalt's grammar.

The living visit. Not in mourning — or not only in mourning. They come to sit by the pool and listen. The dead, the old man tells you, are part of the system now. Their calcium leaches into the water and travels the pipes and coats the interiors and becomes part of the crust the tenders manage. The dead are in the walls when they weep. The dead are in the pipes when they groan. The dead are in the air you breathe, the warm wet air that fills your lungs, and this is not grotesque to the tenders. It is the opposite. It is the thing that makes the Works a works and not merely a machine. The dead participate. The system includes them. And when a tender places her hand on a valve and feels the pulse of the water within, she feels also the presence of everyone who has ever tended that valve before her, their mineral patience, their slow circulation through the stone and the iron and the ferns and the water and the air.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the Weeping Works is a relationship. Not a population. Not a community, though it is that too, in its way. A relationship between the heat that rises from below and the water that sits on the surface and the stone that mediates between them and the people who have learned to listen to all of it at once.

They are not sad here. The name suggests sadness — the weeping, the damp, the slow decay of iron and wood and cloth — but the tenders do not weep. They tend. They sit with their hands on iron and they listen to the circulation of a system that includes their dead and their children and the moss in the furnace hall and the ferns that grow in curtains from every horizontal surface and the water that runs down every wall and the pipes that carry the hot mineral breath of the deep earth through the center of their town. They are part of it. They are the part that notices.

You leave eventually. You climb the gradual slope back to the coastal plain, and the air thins and the light sharpens and the ground dries beneath your boots and the world becomes ordinary again. But for days afterward, you find yourself listening. To the pipes in your walls. To the hum of the water in the heater. To the small sounds a building makes when it is working, when it is doing what it was made to do, when no one is listening but the sounds are there anyway, patient and constant and low, and you realize that what you learned in the Weeping Works was not a place but an attention, and that the attention stays.

Your hand, sometimes, without your willing it, reaches for a wall. Rests there. Waits.

You are listening.
