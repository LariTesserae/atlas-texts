# What Lives There

Let me start with the beetles, because the beetles were here first, or if not first, then so close to first that the distinction doesn't matter.

They are the floor of everything. Not foundation — foundation implies structure, implies something built. The beetles are not built. They simply are, the way the stone is. Ten million of them, or a hundred million, or some number that cannot be counted because counting requires separating one thing from another and the beetles do not separate. They are continuous. They are the sound the plateau makes when it breathes, and they are the sound the plateau makes when it doesn't, because they never stop. The humming is not a chorus. It is a single voice with ten million throats.

Most of them you never see. They live inside the bromeliads — inside the cisterns, in the axils where leaf meets leaf, in the damp pockets of accumulated organic matter that collect in the rosettes. Their larvae are aquatic, small white threads that writhe in the standing water and feed on the debris that falls in: dead leaves, pollen, the bodies of smaller insects that came to drink and did not leave. The adults emerge at intervals — black, armored, slow. They move with a deliberateness that seems almost philosophical. They are in no hurry because they have nowhere to go. The bromeliad they were born in is the bromeliad they will die in, or the one next to it, or the one after that, and the distance between those possibilities is the entire world.

The large ones — the fist-sized black beetles with the ridged backs — these are the ones you notice. They move through the vegetation like livestock through a field, shouldering aside fronds and dislodging mosses, leaving trails in the creeping ground-cover that close up behind them like water behind a boat. They click when they walk. Each step is a click. Each click is a small declaration: *I am here, I am heavy, I am moving.* When two of them meet, they stop. They touch antennae. They stay motionless for a long time — minutes, sometimes longer — and then one goes left and one goes right and neither of them seems to have gained or lost anything by the encounter. It is not aggression. It is not greeting. It is something the beetles do, and have done for longer than there have been people to wonder about it.

The people eat these. The large ones. They crack the shells with stones and pull out the meat — dark, dense, faintly sweet, with a texture like cold custard. It is not a delicacy. It is not a staple. It is simply there, the way the bromeliads are there, the way the stone is there, and the people take what is there and are grateful for it in the way that gratefulness is not a feeling but a practice, something you do with your hands and your mouth and your morning.

---

The lizards are the next layer, and they are the layer that moves.

Not that the beetles don't move. But the beetles move like the stones move — imperceptibly, on geological time, with a patience that is almost mineral. The lizards move on animal time. They are fast. They are here and then they are there, and the space between here and there is a flash of grey or brown or dusty orange, a streak across the stone, a rustle in the moss, gone.

They are beautiful, though the people who live on the plateau do not use that word and might not have that concept in the way that you or I would recognize it. But they are beautiful. The small ones — fifteen centimeters, smooth-scaled, the color of the stone they live on — have a geometry to them that is clean and perfect, their bodies a series of triangles and curves that fit together with the precision of well-laid stone. Their throats, when they display, flash colors that exist nowhere else on the plateau: orange as bright as a ember, blue as deep as a wound. These colors are not for the stone. They are not for the plants. They are for each other, and the lizards display and bob and push-up and flash, and the plateau, which is so muted, so dusty, so old-bone-colored, has these small fires in it, these brief flares of intensity, and then the lizards stop displaying and the colors fold back under their throats and they are grey again, and the plateau is what it was.

The people hunt the larger ones. The heavy-bodied, slow-moving lizards with the strong jaws. They are not fast enough to flee, and they do not flee. They stand their ground when approached, and they hiss — a sound like air escaping from a crack in stone — and they bite, and their bite can draw blood. The hunters know this. They carry forked sticks, dark wood, smoothed by use, and they pin the lizard's head to the ground and kill it with a blow to the back of the skull from a round stone, and the stone is the right stone, the right weight, the right shape, and it has been used for this purpose for a long time. The hunters carry these stones in pouches on their hips, and the stones are smooth and warm and they fit in the hand like they grew there.

The meat is dark. Strong. It tastes of the insects the lizards ate, and the insects taste of the plants, and the plants taste of the stone and the dust and the air, and so everything on the plateau tastes, ultimately, of the plateau itself. The people know this flavor so well that they do not taste it anymore, the way you do not taste your own mouth.

---

The birds are the voice.

I say this knowing that the beetles are also a voice, and the wind is a voice, and the people are a voice. But the birds are the voice that speaks. The beetles hum, and the wind breathes, and the people murmur, but the birds *say* — they articulate, they phrase, they produce sounds that have shape and sequence and apparent meaning, even if the meaning is only for other birds.

The small brown ones first. They are unremarkable to look at — sparrow-sized, drab, the color of dried grass, with thin beaks and quick eyes. They live in the bromeliads. Their nests are engineering: platforms of sticks and fiber built above the water in the central cisterns, suspended from the walls of the rosette, secure against wind and against the probing noses of the fox-creatures. They lay two eggs. They raise one or two young. They sing in the morning.

Their song is not a song in the way of a thrush or a nightingale. It is a series of notes — high, chiming, rapid — that sound like something struck. Like small stones hit in sequence, or like water dripping into a ceramic vessel, each drop a different pitch. They sing in overlapping patterns: one bird starts, another joins, another joins, and the result is not a chorus but a texture, a woven thing, a fabric of sound that covers the settlement in the morning like a second atmosphere. The people do not remark on this. They do not say, "Listen, the birds are singing." They simply wake when the birds begin, and the waking and the singing are the same event, and have been the same event for as long as either species can remember.

There is something about the small brown birds that is worth noting, and it is this: they recognize individual people. Not as threats or as food sources — as *individuals*. They know which people pass close to their nests regularly and which do not. They know which people are likely to pause and look and which move on. They adjust their behavior accordingly — sitting tight on the nest when a known person passes, flushing when a stranger approaches. The people, for their part, know which bromeliads have nests in them and give them a wide berth during breeding season. This is not written down. This is not legislated. This is simply what is done, and it is done because it has always been done, and the birds and the people have arrived at this understanding independently, over centuries of shared proximity, without ever discussing it.

The hunting bird is a different matter. It is not intimate. It is not familiar. It is a presence — a shape that moves across the dim sky at a height that makes it seem larger than it is, or perhaps it is large, perhaps it is exactly as large as it seems, and what makes it seem larger is the fear it produces in everything below it. When it calls — that single, dropping whistle — the plateau goes still. Not silent: the beetles do not stop. The beetles never stop. But the lizards freeze. The small birds go quiet. The rodents retreat into the walls. Even the wind seems to pull back, to hesitate, as if it too is waiting for the sound to finish.

The people do not hunt this bird. They do not hunt it because they cannot, and they cannot because it is too fast and too rare and too high, and they do not want to because it is not the kind of thing you hunt. It is the kind of thing you notice. The people point to it when it passes — a raised hand, a chin-lift, the smallest gesture — and whoever is near looks up and they watch it cross the sky and then they go back to what they were doing. It is an event. It is not a significant event. It is simply something that happens, and it happens because the plateau is a place where things happen, slowly, in their own time, and are noticed, and are not remarked upon, and are folded into the fabric of the day.

The night bird is something else entirely.

It is rarely seen. It calls after dark — a low, resonant boom, like a drum struck underwater, like a note blown across the mouth of a deep jar. It vibrates in the sternum. It vibrates in the stone. It seems to come from below, from inside the plateau, as if the bird is not a bird at all but a voice that the earth is using, a sound that the geology produces when the air cools and the dust settles and the world becomes small and dim and enclosed.

The people say this is the voice of the plateau. They say it the way they say most things — quietly, without emphasis, as a statement of fact that requires no defense because it is simply true. The night bird calls and the plateau speaks, and if the plateau speaks, then the plateau is not dead, not finished, not merely a geological remnant. It is alive. It has a voice. It speaks in the dark, and what it says is a single low note that means *I am still here*, and that is enough.

---

The fox-creature. The one that slips between buildings like smoke.

It is not a fox. It is not a marten. It is something that evolved here, on the plateau, or arrived here long enough ago that it might as well have evolved here, and it has become the color of the place. Its fur is pale feldspar grey, dusted with the mineral powder that hangs in the air, and when it is still — and it is very still, very often — it is nearly invisible against the stone. It is long-bodied and low-slung, with short legs and a sharp face and eyes that are not yellow, as you might expect, but dark — dark as the stone is dark where the iron veins run, dark as the bark of the stunted trees, dark as the inside of a house at night when the lamp has gone out.

It hunts at the edges of the day — the dim hours, the ambiguous hours, when the light is neither brightening nor dimming but simply *is*, stuck, and the small rodents are out and the lizards are sluggish and the beetles are humming at their lowest ebb. It moves through the passages of the town at speed, and the people see it — they see it often enough that it is not a surprise — and they do nothing. They do not chase it. They do not trap it. They do not call to it or try to feed it. It is not their animal. It is not their neighbor. It is something else, something that occupies the same space and moves through it on different terms, and the relationship between the people and the fox-creature is one of mutual acknowledgment and mutual disregard, which is perhaps the most honest relationship between species that there is.

It kills cleanly. It kills the rodents that live in the walls — the round-bodied, short-eared, tame-or-indifferent rodents that run across the floors and over people's feet — and it kills the small lizards, and sometimes it takes a bird from a nest if the nest is low enough and the bird is slow enough, and the people do not mind this, or if they mind it, they do not show it, because the fox-creature is part of the system and the system is what it is and you do not interfere with the system because the system is the plateau and the plateau is finished and you do not argue with something that has finished.

The rodents, though. The rodents deserve a moment.

They are the animals closest to the people. Not the closest in size or in usefulness — the lizards are more useful, the beetles are more numerous — but the closest in proximity. They live in the walls. They live under the floors. They live in the spaces between stones, in the gaps filled with creeping plants, in the warm dark behind storage vessels and under sleeping mats. They emerge at dusk and they move through the houses with a confidence that is almost impertinent, running across the floor, climbing onto low surfaces, investigating anything that has been set down within their reach. They eat crumbs. They eat insects. They eat the softer parts of the bromeliad fibers. They eat, occasionally, from the clay water-vessels if the water is left uncovered, lapping at it with tiny tongues, and the people watch this and do not stop it, because the water is shared and the rodents are part of the sharing.

Children catch them. Not to keep — there is no concept of keeping a wild thing as a pet here — but to hold, briefly, to feel the warmth of a small body in the hands, to look at the dark eyes and the twitching nose and the fine whiskers, and then to release, and the rodent goes back to the wall and the child goes back to whatever they were doing, and the encounter is over and it was not significant and it was not insignificant and it was simply a thing that happened between two species that share a space.

---

The large flightless insects. The ones in the stone pens.

Thirty centimeters. Segmented. Six legs that move in a slow, deliberate rhythm, each step placed with care, each foot finding its grip on the stone. Broad, flat backs, like shields, like serving trays, the color of dark tortoiseshell. When they are disturbed — picked up, turned over, examined — they curl their legs inward and play dead, and they are so good at this, so convincing, that you could set them on a shelf and come back an hour later and not be certain whether they were alive or not.

The people keep them the way you might keep a garden. They are not tame. They do not come when called or recognize their keepers or display any of the affection-based behaviors that domestication usually produces. They simply live in the pens, and the pens are where they live, and they eat what is put in front of them, and they grow, and at a certain point — a certain size, a certain age, determined by the keepers with a knowledge that is not written down and not spoken about but simply *known* — they are harvested. The shells are kept. The shells are used for — bowls, small containers, the lids of vessels, and sometimes, drilled with holes and strung on fiber cord, as ornaments worn around the neck or hung from the rafters of the tall building, where they catch the dim light and gleam like dark glass.

They rattle when they move. Their shells click against each other, against the stone of the pens, against the remnants of food at their feet. The sound is steady and regular and it sits underneath the sound of the wild beetles like a bass note under a chord, grounding it, giving it a structure, and if you stood at the edge of the settlement and closed your eyes, you would hear it: the pulse of the domestic, the rhythm of the kept, under the hum of the wild, and together they are the heartbeat, and the heartbeat is what the plateau sounds like from the inside.

---

Now the people.

I have put them last not because they are the most important — the plateau does not rank its inhabitants, and the people would be the first to tell you that, or rather, they would not tell you that, because the question would not occur to them — but because they are the hardest to see. Not the hardest to describe. The hardest to see. Because when you look at the people of Ayaviri, you see the plateau looking back at you.

They are not tall. I have said this. They are short, lean, compact, dark-skinned, dark-haired, dark-eyed, and they move through their town the way the fox-creature moves through the passages — not furtively, not cautiously, but with an economy of motion that suggests they have walked these paths so many times that the walking has become automatic, has become part of the body's memory, has become inseparable from the body itself. They do not look where they are going. They know where they are going. Their feet know the stone. The stone knows their feet.

There is something about them that is difficult to capture, and it is this: they are not separate from the place. I do not mean this in a romantic or mystical sense. I mean it literally. Their bodies are made of the plateau. The water they drink comes from the bromeliads, which comes from the air, which comes from the dust, which comes from the stone. The food they eat — lizard meat, beetle flesh, the starchy inner core of certain bromeliad leaves, the occasional rodent, the flightless insects from the pens — all of it is of the plateau, grown on the plateau, fed by the plateau. Their clothing is plant fiber and bark. Their tools are stone and bone and wood and clay. Their houses are the stone itself, stacked and fitted and grown over with moss. There is nothing in the town that did not come from within walking distance, and most of it came from within arm's reach.

They are, in a physical sense, the plateau made aware of itself. They are the stone that has learned to see, the dust that has learned to speak, the water that has learned to carry knowledge from one generation to the next. And they know this. Not in those words — they do not use those words, or any words like them — but in the way they move, in the way they touch the stone, in the way they harvest water from the bromeliads each morning with a tenderness that is not called tenderness because there is no word for it because it does not need a word because it simply *is what they do*.

Let me try to describe a morning.

---

A morning on the plateau does not begin. It emerges. The light — such as it is — brightens by increments so small that you cannot point to the moment when night became morning. The small brown birds begin to sing, and their song is the first articulation of the day, and the people wake to it, not because it wakes them but because waking and song have become so associated that the one produces the other, and it does not matter which came first.

The first movement is the water gathering. This is done before anything else. Before food. Before conversation. Before the full emergence of thought. The people rise from their sleeping mats — woven from bromeliad fiber, laid on smooth stone, warm from the body's heat — and they take their water tubes and they go out. The passages between the buildings are narrow and dim and the stone underfoot is worn smooth and glossy and the air is close, warmer than the open plateau, thick with the smell of sleep and stone and the faintly sulfurous breath of the ground-cover.

They go to the bromeliads. Each person — or each family — has their route, their circuit, their sequence of plants they visit, and this sequence has been theirs for generations, passed down like a name, like a face. They know the plants. They know them individually. They know which ones fill slowly and which fill fast. They know which ones have clean water and which have a bitter edge from the tannins of dead leaves. They know which ones have bird nests in them and should not be disturbed. They know which ones are young and should be left to grow, and which ones are old and are beginning to rot in the center and will need to be cleared and replanted. They know these things the way you know your own body — not as information, not as facts, but as *knowledge*, a thing that lives in the hands and the eyes and the feet.

The water tube is a bone. A hollow bone, usually from the leg of one of the large lizards, cleaned and dried and fitted at one end with a small copper fitting — not necessary, not functional, but traditional, ornamental, a sign that this tube has been in use for a long time and is worth the expenditure of copper. The tube is inserted into the cistern of the bromeliad — carefully, at an angle, slipping it between the tight-packed leaves, feeling for the water — and the water is drawn up. By mouth, if the person is drinking. By suction into a small clay vessel, if they are gathering. The water is clear, faintly sweet, faintly mineral. It tastes of the air and the dust and the stone and the plant, and it is the only water these people have ever known, and it is warm, because everything on the plateau is warm, and it is enough.

The gathering takes most of the morning. Not because there is a great deal of water to gather — there isn't, not per person, not per day — but because the process is slow. Each plant must be visited. Each cistern must be checked. Each tube must be inserted and withdrawn with care, so as not to damage the leaves, so as not to contaminate the water, so as not to disturb whatever lives in there — the larvae, the beetles, the occasional frog that has found its way into the cistern and lives there, fat and dark and content. The people do not rush this. Rushing would be a violence against the plant, against the water, against the morning itself, and the people do not do violence, not because they are pacifists — they hunt, they kill, they crack beetle shells and break lizard necks — but because they understand the difference between violence and necessity, and they will not commit one when the other is sufficient.

While the gathering happens, the town wakes around it. The passages fill with movement — people passing each other, touching shoulders, murmuring greetings that are not greetings but acknowledgments, small sounds that mean *I see you, I know you, we are here again.* Children appear. They are small, like their parents, and they are quick, like the lizards, and they are loud — louder than the adults, louder than the town, their voices bright and sharp and carrying, and no one shushes them, because the voices of children are part of the sound of the place, and the place would be less without them.

The children do not gather water. They are not old enough. They do other things — they chase the rodents, they watch the beetles, they sit by the insect pens and click their tongues at the flightless ones, which do not respond but which also do not not-respond. They play with clay, shaping it into figures that might be animals or might be people or might be something else, something that only children see, and they leave these figures to dry in the dim light and they forget about them and the figures sit on ledges and shelves and become part of the dust, part of the place, small clay witnesses to a childhood that was here and that was brief and that was sufficient.

---

The adults work. There is always work, though work is not the right word, because work implies effort, implies obligation, implies a separation between the doer and the done. What the adults do is *maintain.* They maintain the buildings — re-fitting stones that have shifted, clearing drainage channels, repairing roofs. They maintain the plants — clearing dead leaves, transplanting rosettes, tending the creeping ground-cover that holds the walls together. They maintain the tools — sharpening stone edges, replacing handles, patching fiber sandals, reshaping clay vessels that have cracked. They maintain the insect pens — feeding, cleaning, assessing. They maintain the paths — clearing the passages between buildings, sweeping the stone floors, keeping the town navigable.

This maintenance is not dramatic. It is not urgent. It is the work of a place that has been maintained for so long that the maintenance has become the identity, and the people who do it are not maintaining a town but maintaining a way of being, a relationship between themselves and the stone and the plants and the water and the air, and the relationship is the point, not the outcome.

In the afternoon — or what would be afternoon if time were measured by the sun, which it is not — there is hunting. Not every day. Not on a schedule. When the lizards are needed, or when the large beetles are plentiful, or when a particular plant has died and its associated micro-ecology has been disrupted and the insects that lived in it have scattered and need to be gathered before they are lost. The hunters go out — two of them, or three, never more — and they carry their forked sticks and their stones and they walk the plateau, moving between the bromeliads, stepping carefully on the creeping ground-cover, and they are patient.

They are patient the way the stone is patient. They stand still. They watch. They wait for movement — a rustle, a flash of grey, a head poking out from behind a leaf — and when it comes, they move, and the movement is fast and precise and over. A lizard pinned. A beetle caught. A rodent cornered. The kill is quick and clean and there is no ceremony about it, no ritual, no apology to the animal, because the people do not see the animal as separate from themselves in a way that requires apology. The animal is food. The people are food, eventually, for the beetles and the lizards and the creeping things that will process their bodies when they die, and this is not a tragedy, it is a cycle, and the cycle is the plateau, and the plateau is what it is.

They bring the catch back. It is prepared — cleaned, sometimes cooked, sometimes not, sometimes dried in the dim air on flat stones, sometimes eaten raw. The preparation is simple because the food is simple and simplicity is not a choice but a condition, and the condition has become, over time, a value, and the value is: enough.

---

The evening gathering in the tall building.

Everyone comes. This is not optional, but it is also not mandatory, because those words imply a rule, and there is no rule, there is simply the fact that everyone comes, and the fact is so established that to not come would be as strange as not waking when the birds sing, as not gathering water when the morning comes. It is what you do. It is what is done.

The tall building — the one with the corbel-vaulted ceiling, the one with the marks on the walls — fills with people. Two hundred of them, in a space that is perhaps ten meters by fifteen, and they fit, because they are small and because they are accustomed to closeness and because the space has been shaped by their bodies over generations, the walls worn smooth in the places where people lean, the floor glossy in the paths where people walk, the ceiling darkened by smoke and by time and by the accumulated warmth of two hundred bodies in a small space for a very long time.

The marks on the walls are important, and they are not important in the way that writing is important, because they are not writing. They are marks. Scratches. Notches. Grooves. Some are shallow, barely visible, the first traces of a finger or a stone on a surface. Some are deep, carved into the stone over years of repetition, the same mark in the same place, the same groove deepened and deepened until it is a centimeter wide and two centimeters deep and you could fit the tip of your little finger into it. The marks do not form patterns that repeat in a way that suggests language. They do not form pictures. They are simply marks — the evidence of hands on walls, the residue of attention, the physical proof that someone stood here and touched the stone and meant something by it, even if the meaning is not translatable, even if the meaning is only *I was here*.

The gathering begins with the oldest. They speak — or rather, they chant, or rather, they produce a sound that is between speaking and chanting and singing, a rhythmic, low, continuous vocalization that carries narrative in the way that a river carries sediment: not as its primary content, but as something suspended in it, something that is there if you know how to look for it, something that the younger people have learned to hear by growing up in the sound of it, by absorbing the pattern and the rhythm and the pauses into their bodies until comprehension is not an act of the mind but an act of the flesh, a resonance, a vibration that you feel in your chest the way you feel the night bird's call.

The narratives are not stories. They are not histories. They are lists — of plants, of animals, of stones, of people, of the relationships between them. Which bromeliad was planted when. Which lizard was caught where. Which person was born to which parents, in which house, in which order. Which year had the great beetle die-off, and which year had the night bird go silent for a season, and which year had the hunter fall and break her leg, and which year had the tall building's roof collapse, and which year had the copper-seeker come from below the plateau and bring the green-black nodules and trade them for water and food and stay for a season and then leave and never come back. The lists go on. They are not in chronological order. They are not organized by category. They are organized by association — one thing reminds the speaker of another thing, and that thing reminds them of another, and the chain goes on, link by link, and the chain is the history of the plateau, not as a sequence of events but as a web of relationships, a net of connections, a map of how things fit together, and the map has been drawn and redrawn and added to for so long that it is no longer a map of a place but the place itself, rendered in sound, rendered in the voice, rendered in the bodies of the people who carry it.

The children listen. The young people listen. The adults listen. Everyone listens, and in the listening, they are doing something that is not passive, not receptive, but active — they are *maintaining* the narrative, the way they maintain the buildings and the plants. They are keeping it intact. They are holding it in their bodies, in their memories, in the patterns of their neurons, and when the oldest ones die, the narrative does not lose its parts, because the younger ones have already taken it in, already absorbed it, and the chain continues.

And while the chanting happens, the marks are made. Someone — not always the same person, not always the oldest, sometimes a child, sometimes someone in the middle of their life — stands and takes a stone, a small hard stone, and scratches a mark on the wall. One mark. One notch. One groove. It takes a moment. It is nothing. And it is everything, because it is the continuation of the marks, the extension of the record, the proof that on this night, in this building, the people gathered and the narrative was spoken and a mark was made and the plateau, which is finished, which has stopped becoming, continued to *be*, and the being was recorded, and the recording was the being, and the two were one, and the one was enough.

---

Let me say something about what they are not.

They are not waiting. They are not biding time, not enduring, not surviving in the way that survival implies a threat, an antagonist, a force against which one must struggle. There is no antagonist on the plateau. The plateau is not hostile. It is not kind. It is not anything that requires a moral judgment, because moral judgments require a standard against which to measure, and the plateau has no standard, because it has no alternative, because nothing else has ever happened here, and so the question of whether it is good or bad or right or wrong does not arise, and if it does not arise, then it does not need to be answered, and if it does not need to be answered, then the people are free to simply *live*, and living is what they do.

They are not curious about what lies beyond the plateau. This is not ignorance. It is not fear. It is not a prohibition. It is simply that the plateau is sufficient, and sufficiency is not a state that generates longing. The people do not look at the horizon — there is no horizon to look at — and they do not wonder what is below the edges, where the land falls away, because what is below the edges is not here, and what is not here is not their concern. They are not provincial. They are not narrow-minded. They are *complete*, in the way that a stone is complete, in the way that a bromeliad is complete, in the way that a beetle in its cistern is complete. The plateau contains them, and they contain the plateau, and the container and the contained are the same thing, and there is nothing missing.

They are not unhappy. This needs to be said, because it is easy to look at a place like this — dim, slow, mineral, old — and see sadness in it, to project melancholy onto a people who do not experience melancholy because they do not experience its opposite, because the spectrum of emotion that requires contrast and comparison and loss does not apply here. They do not feel joy. They do not feel sorrow. They feel *presence* — the continuous, warm, undifferentiated awareness of being here, in this body, on this stone, in this air, among these people, with these plants and these animals and this sound and this light and this warmth, and the presence is not a feeling but a state, and the state is the same state as the stone, and the stone has been in this state for three billion years, and three billion years is a long time to be in a state, and if the state were unbearable, the stone would have changed, and it hasn't, and neither have the people.

They are not static. This also needs to be said, because completion implies stasis, and stasis implies death, and death is not what is happening here. Children are born. People die. New bromeliads are planted. Old ones rot and are replaced. The marks on the walls accumulate. The buildings shift and settle and are repaired. The beetles hum and the birds sing and the lizards display and the fox-creature hunts and the rodents run across the floors and everything moves, everything changes, but the change is not *progress*, it is not *toward*, it is not *growth* in the sense of accumulation or expansion. It is *recurrence*. The same things happen again and again, not because they must but because they are what this place does, the way a heartbeat is what a heart does, and the heartbeat is not static even though it repeats, and the plateau is not static even though it has settled, and the people are not static even though they have been here for a thousand years, because a thousand years of the same thing is not the same thing as nothing, and the difference is the living, and the living is what they do.

---

There is one more thing.

Sometimes — not often, not rarely, but at the irregular intervals that constitute a rhythm of their own — a person will leave the settlement and walk out onto the plateau, alone, in the dim light, in the dust-fog, in the constant warm hum of the beetles. They will walk until the settlement is behind them, until they are surrounded by nothing but stone and bromeliads and the creeping ground-cover and the sound and the warmth and the light that is everywhere and nowhere. They will find a place — a slight rise, a bare patch of stone, a spot where the bromeliads thin out and the stone shows through, warm and smooth and old — and they will sit down.

They will sit. They will close their eyes.
