# Inhabitants

---

## The People (Such As They Are)

There are three. Maybe four. It's hard to count because they don't gather, and because the one you see at the far end of the chamber near the pump house might be the same one you saw ten minutes ago near the shipping containers, having circled the vast space while you weren't watching. They move along the walkways with a gait that has been shaped by the environment — slightly crouched, weight forward on the balls of the feet, hands never far from the railings. It's not fear, exactly. It's accommodation. They move the way you move on a boat, not fighting the motion but incorporating it.

The oldest one — and this is a guess, because the light down here is unkind to features and the humidity has made everyone's skin into the same texture — is probably not old. Maybe fifties. The work has aged him, or the place has. He has a way of standing at the pump house door with his arms crossed that suggests authority, or at least seniority, the posture of someone who has been here longest and has stopped explaining why. He wears a heavy cotton shirt that was once white and is now the color of the water that drips from the ceiling, and he has a cloth mask hanging around his neck that he pulls up when he's not speaking, which is most of the time. His eyes have adjusted to the dimness in a way yours haven't. He sees the beetles on the floor without looking at them. He tracks the membrane-creatures in the air without turning his head. He has incorporated this place into his sensorium so thoroughly that the fauna is no longer fauna to him. It's weather. It's furniture. It's the texture of his daily life, and he gives it no more attention than you give the walls of your kitchen.

The younger one is harder to watch because she moves. She walks the perimeter, the full circuit of the chamber, on a schedule you can't determine — not a patrol, exactly, because there's nothing to patrol against, but a circuit nonetheless, checking pipes and cable junctions and the places where the walkway grating has come loose. She has a tool belt that clinks softly as she walks, and she stops at intervals to tighten something or reposition a cable, and the stops are brief and economical, performed with the automatic competence of someone who has done this enough times that the actions are stored in her hands, not her head. She is the one who laid the new grating. You can tell because the sections she's installed have small chalk marks on their edges — not notation, just visible marks, the kind of thing you do to keep track of your own work in a place where no one else is going to check it. She doesn't wear a mask. She's stopped caring about the iron tang, or maybe she's become unable to taste it. Her hair is pulled back and damp, and the condensation beads on her forearms the same way it beads on the pipes, and she doesn't wipe it away.

The third one — and this is the one you're least sure about, the one who makes the count uncertain — lives in the lit shipping container. Or exists in it. The door is closed most of the time, and when it opens, the light from inside is warmer and steadier than the work lamps, and you see a space that is more inhabited than any other structure here. There's a cot. There are screens — small ones, older models, the kind with physical keyboards — and cables running everywhere, and what looks like specimen jars on a shelf, filled with fluid that holds shapes you don't examine closely. This is the person who knows. Who has decided to know. The others manage the infrastructure, but this one studies the vault, and the creatures in it, and the relationship between the heat and the water and the life that fills both. You see a hand reach out to adjust something on a shelf — thin fingers, stained at the tips with something dark, the same color as the fluid in the jars — and the door closes again. You hear the latch engage. You understand that the latches are not to keep the fauna out, though they do that too. They're to keep something in. Not a creature. A focus. An intensity of attention that can't tolerate interruption.

The radio is theirs. The crackle and fragmentary voice is something they're listening to, or waiting for. You can't make out words. Maybe they can't either. Maybe the listening is the point.

## The Surface Veneer (Ceiling)

These were described to you already, but the description was from the outside, the way a person on a riverbank describes fish. Here is what it's like to be beneath them.

They don't all behave the same way. The ceiling population is not uniform. There are clusters — dense, interlocked aggregations that look from below like a single textured surface, each individual pressed flush against its neighbors, shells overlapping, legs tucked, motionless. And there are dispersers — the same species, or a closely related one, but solitary, spaced out, each creature occupying its own patch of stone with a gap between it and the next. The clusters are where the condensation is thickest; you can see the water sheeting off them in a continuous film, running over shell after shell, gathering into droplets at the lowest edge of the cluster and falling. The dispersers are drier. Their shells bead with water but don't channel it. They seem to be doing something different. Absorbing, maybe. Feeding on what the moisture brings.

If you stand directly beneath a cluster and look up — and you shouldn't, but you do — you can see that their shells are not uniform. Each one has a pattern on its carapace, a ridge structure that catches the light differently, and the patterns vary. Not dramatically, not like fingerprints, but enough that once you notice it, you can't un-notice it, and the ceiling stops looking like a surface and starts looking like a crowd. They are not identical. They are not interchangeable. Each one is a specific organism occupying a specific position in a specific cluster, and the clusters themselves have shapes — lobed, roughly circular, elongated along the mineral veins where the condensation is richest — that suggest they have been there for a long time, growing outward from a founding individual, layering generation over generation.

They are the architects of this place, in the way that coral is the architect of a reef. The vault existed before them, blasted out of basalt by people with equipment. But the vault as it is now — the wetness, the dripping, the atmosphere thick with moisture — that is them. They made this. The settlement exists because of what they produce, and they produce it because the heat from the shallow magmatic source below makes their shells cooler than the air, and the differential pulls water out of the vapor, and the water falls, and the water is what the settlement is here for.

Or was. The second pump, unassembled, suggests the plan changed.

When they move — and they do, eventually, each cluster rearranging itself over a span of minutes so gradual you have to hold still to perceive it — the sound is the chitinous whisper you heard when you first descended. Not dry leaves. Shells against shells. A million small adjustments, each one almost silent, their sum a texture in the air.

## The Floor Population

The beetles are not beetles.

You learn this from the third person, the one in the container, if you're lucky enough to catch the door open and ask — or rather, if you're lucky enough that they decide to tell you without being asked, because this is someone who shares information on their own schedule, in their own phrasing, and doesn't respond well to direct questions. They are not beetles. They are something that has converged on the beetle body plan from a different lineage, the way crabs have — carapace, legs, a low flat profile, a posture of patient surveillance — but their anatomy is wrong in ways you'd need a specialist to articulate. The legs are too numerous. The carapace has a hinge that real beetles don't. The mouthparts, if you turn one over — and no one has, as far as you know, because no one down here picks them up — are not mandibular. They're something else. A filter, maybe. A sieve.

They live in the water. The shallow standing water that pools on the raw floor between the walkways is their habitat, and they sit in it with their backs barely submerged, the surface tension dimpling around them, their legs spread flat beneath them. They are filter-feeding. The water that drips from the ceiling carries mineral content — iron, calcium, whatever the basalt and the magmatic heat leach into it — and the floor organisms strain this from the water as it passes over them. They are processing the vault's exhalation. They are the digestive system of this place, converting the mineral-laden drip into bodies, and their bodies, when they die — and they do, you can see the empty shells here and there, pale husks half-dissolved in the pools — sink and contribute to a substrate that will, over geological time, become a floor of sedimentary rock. They are making the cave's future. They are making the cave.

There are more of them than you initially counted, because the ones you can see are only the ones near the lights. In the dimness beyond the work lamps' reach, the floor pools are full of them. You can hear it — a wet, shifting sound, like gravel in a shallow tide — and if you hold a light out over the edge of the walkway and cast it across the water, the surface comes alive with reflected points: their backs, wet and gleaming, hundreds of them, an audience, a parliament, a floor.

They are aware of you. Not in the way a dog is aware of you, or even a fish. In the way a plant turns toward light. When you step on the grating, the vibration propagates through the metal and into the stone, and the nearest beetles — they are beetles, you will call them beetles because the alternative is to call them nothing, and you need a word — orient. A slow turning. A shift of weight. They don't flee. They don't approach. They register.

The older worker, the one at the pump house, has stepped off the walkway. You didn't see him do it, but you see him standing on the raw floor, in the water, among them. He's checking a pipe junction that's inaccessible from the grating. The water comes up to his boots. The beetles are around his feet, and they've moved — not away from him, but into a formation, a cleared space, a respectful margin. He's not threatening them. They're not afraid of him. There's an arrangement here, a negotiated coexistence that happened before you arrived and will continue after you leave. He wades back to the walkway, pulls himself up, wipes his boots on the grating. One of the beetles has climbed onto the stone where he was standing. It's inspecting the impression his boot left in the mineral sediment. It stays there after he's gone.

## The Web-Builders

The structures have tenants.

The fibrous material in the upper corners of the prefab sheds is not spider silk. It's stronger, for one thing — you can feel the resistance if you pull at it, a toughness like fine fishing line, and it doesn't break under gentle tension. And the things that live in it are not spiders. They're quick, they're segmented, and they retreat from light with an urgency that suggests predation or prey, but their body plan is wrong for arachnids. They have too many eyes, or too few — it's hard to tell at the speed they move. They're small. The largest is the length of your thumb, and most are smaller, and they live in the webbing the way ants live in soil, using it as infrastructure, as architecture, as the medium of their existence. They don't catch things in it. Nothing gets caught. The webbing is not for trapping. It's for living in.

What they eat is the question, and the answer is: the structures themselves. They're consuming the prefab sheds. Slowly, methodically, from the corners in. The corrugated metal has a pitting in the webbed areas that isn't rust — it's ingestion. The organisms are eating the building, processing it through their bodies, and producing the webbing as a byproduct, or a shelter, or a colony structure, or all three. The sheds are being digested. Given enough time — and there is nothing down here but time — the sheds will be gone, and the webbing will remain, and the organisms will have transformed a human structure into a structure of their own.

The younger worker clears the doorways. She doesn't clear the upper corners. She has made a calculation: the doorway webs would interfere with foot traffic, and the upper-corner webs are doing no immediate harm, and removing them would be a job that needs redoing every week, and she has enough work. She's accepted the consumption as a rate. The sheds will outlast her posting here. That's sufficient.

If you watch the webs long enough — longer than you'd think to, longer than the attention span of a visitor — you see something else. The web-builders aren't random. The webbing has a structure. Not geometric, not the radial symmetry of spider webs, but a structural logic, a grain, a directionality. The fibers run along stress lines. They reinforce the corners where the shed metal is weakest. They're not just living in the building. They're maintaining it. The pitting and the webbing and the reinforcement are a single process: they're eating the structure and rebuilding it simultaneously, converting human engineering into something else, something organic, something that will last longer in this environment than corrugated metal ever could.

They're not destroying the sheds. They're inheriting them.

## The Airborne

You can't describe these. You've tried. You've stood in the light of a work lamp and watched them pass through the beam, and each time you get an impression — translucent, veined, moving with a deliberate control that's not flight exactly but isn't drifting either — and then they're past, and you're left with an afterimage that doesn't solidify. They're like words in a language you don't speak. You can hear them. You can't hold them.

They navigate by heat. You know this because the one in the shipping container told you, on one of the occasions when the door was open and the information was offered, unprompted, as if it had been waiting for someone to not-ask. The membrane-creatures — the container person calls them something, a word you don't catch, or catch but don't retain — follow thermal gradients. They ride the rising columns of warmth that emanate from the shallow magmatic source below the vault floor, and they orient toward the warmer air, and they aggregate where the heat is strongest. The center of the chamber, where the floor is warmest, has the highest density. You can't see this — the center is beyond the reach of the work lights — but you can feel it, the way you can feel a crowded room with your eyes closed. The air there is thick with them. You walk through them. They don't avoid you. They don't acknowledge you. You are warmer than the surrounding air, and they turn toward you as they pass, a slight deviation in trajectory, a momentary orientation, and then they continue. You are a heat source. You are a feature of their landscape.

What they are doing — and this is speculation, the container person's speculation, offered with the caveat that speculation is all anyone has — is breeding. The thermal columns are their mating grounds. The warmer air accelerates something in their life cycle, and the vault's geology has created a permanent incubator, a column of heated atmosphere that rises through the chamber and concentrates the way a lens concentrates light. They gather in it. They do something in it that you can't see clearly enough to describe. And then they disperse, and the next generation appears — smaller, paler, more translucent than their parents, drifting in the convection current like plankton in a thermal vent.

They're beautiful. This is a thing you don't expect to feel down here, in the wet and the dim and the iron air, but you feel it. They move with a precision that looks like intention, and their membranes catch the light the way soap films do, with that brief spectral flash, and they're silent, and they're everywhere, and the vault is full of them in the way the sky is full of birds at dusk. You are inside something living. The air is not air. It is a body, and they are its cells, and you are a foreign object within it, tolerated, unremarked upon, a warm spot moving through a warm medium.

## The Unseen

There is something else.

You don't see it. You don't hear it. You infer it from the behavior of the other organisms, the way you infer a predator from the silence of a forest. The ceiling clusters rearrange themselves periodically — not randomly, but directionally, shifting away from a specific section of the vault, a region in the deep dimness beyond the last work light, where the floor slopes down and the standing water deepens and the basalt walls converge into a throat, a narrowing, a passage that leads further down. The floor beetles don't enter that region. You've watched the younger worker walk the perimeter, and she stops thirty meters short of the narrowing and turns back. She doesn't approach it. She doesn't acknowledge the avoidance. She just doesn't go there.

The container person has a chart on the wall. You glimpsed it through the open door. It's a map of the vault, hand-drawn, detailed, with the structures and the walkways and the pipe runs carefully rendered. The narrowing is marked. It has a symbol next to it — not a word, a symbol, a small circle with a line through it, the kind of notation that means *here be something* in a private language of observation. There are notes. The notes are in handwriting so small you can't read them from where you stand, and the door closes before you can try.

The radio. The fragmentary voice. You listen to it one night, or what passes for night down here, when the workers' activity has dropped to its lowest level and the vault is at its most acoustic, every drip and tick and rustle magnified by the silence. The voice is human. The words are — maybe words. Broken by static, by distance, by the inadequacy of whatever transmitter is sending and whatever receiver is listening. But the cadence is there. Someone is speaking. Someone is saying something. The container person listens to this the way the beetles listen to vibration: with an orientation, a full-body turning-toward, an attention so complete it becomes a kind of stillness.

You don't know who's speaking. You don't know where they are. You don't know if they're above, in the world you came from, or below, in the narrowing, in the passage that leads further down, where the heat is stronger and the water is deeper and the things you can't see are.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

This place is an egg.

Not metaphorically. Or not only metaphorically. The heat from below is the warmth of something being incubated. The moisture is the fluid of a closed environment developing its own atmosphere. The creatures — the ceiling veneer, the floor population, the web-builders, the airborne — are not inhabitants. They are components. They are the systems of an organism that is still assembling itself, still differentiating, still deciding what it will be when it hatches. The people are components too. The pumps, the pipes, the walkways, the structures being slowly digested and rebuilt — all of it is part of the same process, the same slow gestation.

The container person knows this. The chart on the wall isn't a map. It's a diagram. The narrowing isn't a passage. It's a pore, or a mouth, or an opening that hasn't finished forming. The voice on the radio isn't coming from outside. It's coming from inside. From the thing that is becoming. From the vault itself, or the thing the vault is becoming, speaking in a frequency that the radio can barely catch, in a language that sounds like static because it hasn't been invented yet.

The workers don't know this, or they do and have decided not to know, which is its own kind of knowledge. The older one at the pump house has the look of a man who has been somewhere long enough that the place has stopped being a place and has become a condition. He is not living in the vault. He is living *as* the vault, or as part of it, the way an enzyme lives in the reaction it catalyzes. He is not separate from this. None of them are. The grating flexes under their feet, and the condensation forms on their skin, and the membrane-creatures turn toward their warmth, and they breathe the air that the ceiling organisms have processed, and they drink — they don't drink the water, but they bathe in it, they work in it, they exist within the cycle — and the cycle is the thing, the organism, the egg.

What lives here is something that is not yet alive in the way you understand life. It is pre-life, or proto-life, or life at a scale and tempo that your senses can't properly register. The beetles are its circulation. The ceiling clusters are its respiration. The airborne are its thoughts, or its dreams, drifting in the warm dark. The web-builders are its memory, converting the structures of a previous species into structures of their own, preserving the shape of what was while making room for what will be. The people are its immune system, or its nervous system, or its hands — maintaining, observing, pumping, listening, waiting for the signal that will tell them what they're for.

The radio crackles. A fragment of voice. The container person goes still.

The vault breathes. The condensation falls.

Something down in the narrowing shifts, and the beetles on the floor orient toward it, and the ceiling clusters rearrange, and the airborne creatures drift in a new direction, and the older worker at the pump house pulls his mask up and checks the engine and says nothing, nothing at all, because there is nothing to say. The egg is warm. The egg is whole. The egg is growing.

And you — you are a visitor, and you will leave, and the ladder will take you back up into the world above, and the iron taste will fade from your tongue, and the dampness will dry on your clothes, and you will stand in daylight and feel the clean air on your face and know, with a certainty that doesn't require evidence, that something is forming in the dark, something patient and whole and slow, and that the people down there are not studying it or surviving it but *serving* it, and that the voice on the radio is getting clearer, a word at a time, a syllable at a time, and that when it finishes speaking, the vault will open, and what comes out will not be what went in.

You will not be there for that. You will be in the world above, in the clean air, in the light. And you will remember the taste of iron. And you will hear, in the silence between heartbeats, the ticking of condensation falling in a chamber you can no longer reach, and the whisper of shells against shells, and the crackle of a radio receiving a message from something that is almost ready to send one.
