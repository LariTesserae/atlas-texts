# What Lives on the Carbonite Deck

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is a visitor. There is no migration, no season of arrival. Everything that lives on the plateau was born on the plateau, or more precisely *emerged* on it — not from a single origin point but from a slow convergence of chemistry and pressure and time so deep that the concept of "arrival" has no purchase. The mineral crust came first, or close enough to first. The filament-mats followed, or co-emerged, or were always part of the crust and simply became distinct when water gave them a medium to distinguish themselves in. The trunk-plants are older than the striders. The striders are older than the clickers. The flat basin-things are older than everything, or at least older than the categories that would make that claim meaningful, because they have been burying themselves and re-emerging for so long that their lineage is indistinguishable from the sediment they sleep in.

None of them know they are alive. This is not a limitation. It is a condition of the place. Knowing would require a kind of self-reference that the plateau does not support — not because it is hostile to consciousness but because consciousness would be redundant here. Everything the plateau does is already done by the systems themselves. There is no gap between process and outcome for a mind to occupy. The striders do not decide to move toward thicker crust; their legs carry them there because the crust they have been eating has altered the chemical composition of their blood, and their blood has altered the sensitivity of their footpads, and their footpads are drawn to the temperature signature of active growth, and the active growth is where the crust is thickest. The chain is unbroken. There is no moment of intention. There is no need for one.

---

## The Crust

It is not one thing. That is the first error, to call it "the crust" and imagine a uniform organism. It is a consortium. The dark maroon body of it is built in layers, and the layers are not successive growth stages but distinct communities that have agreed — chemically, metabolically, without ever negotiating — to occupy the same space. The deepest layer is almost entirely mineral: iron oxide, manganese dioxide, conductive sulfides that have been precipitated out of the thermal fluids rising from below. It is not alive in any way that a microscope would settle, but it is not inert, because it conducts current and the current changes it, reordering its crystal structure in ways that create finer and finer channels, more and more surface area, a more and more hospitable substrate for the things that come next.

Above the mineral base is a layer of something that is alive by any definition: a mat of heat-tolerant cells, not bacteria exactly, not archaea exactly, something older than the distinction and indifferent to it. They are chemolithotrophs. They eat rock and breathe sulfide and they have been doing this for so long that their metabolism has become indistinguishable from the weathering process it accelerates. They are the reason the crust grows outward in lobes rather than uniformly — their growth follows the veins of thermal fluid, and the veins branch, and so the crust branches, and the ridges of the plateau are colonized in patterns that look deliberate because they follow the fracture geometry of the stone, which is itself a kind of intention, if you stretch the word far enough.

The luminous blue-grey at the growing edge is the youngest community, the active margin, and it glows — faintly, faintly — because the electrochemical gradient across the boundary between living and non-living material is strong enough to produce photons. Not bioluminescence in the way that fireflies or deep-sea fish produce it. More like the glow of a heated filament, or the faint light that certain minerals emit under electrical stress. The crust's leading edge is a place where geology and biology are not yet distinct, and the light it produces is the light of that indistinction.

When a strider scrapes the crust away, the edge recedes. But the mineral channels beneath remain, and the thermal fluids still rise through them, and within a span of time that is long by animal standards but short by geological ones, the crust regrows. Thicker. More densely veined. The striders have taken the older, denser layers and left the substrate exposed, and the new growth that fills the gap is more vigorous because it does not have to push through accumulated dead material. This is the farming, though "farming" implies a plan, and there is no plan. There is only a feedback loop: eat the crust, the crust grows back stronger, eat the stronger crust, the stronger crust grows back stronger still. The striders and the crust have been doing this to each other for longer than either can remember, which is forever, because neither remembers anything.

## The Filament-Mats

They are the most fragile thing on the plateau, and also the most persistent.

Each filament is a single cell, drawn out to extraordinary length — a centimeter or more, which is vast for something that is also alive. The cell is hollow, or nearly so, its interior a channel through which warm water flows, carrying dissolved minerals and the faint electrical charge of the thermal fluids. The cell wall is translucent, not because translucence is advantageous but because the cell does not waste energy building pigment. It does not need to absorb light. It does not need to reflect it. It simply occupies the water and lets the water do the work.

The shimmering is mechanical. The filaments are so fine that the slightest current moves them, and their translucence means that as they shift angle, the light from the crust's luminous edges and from the faint overhead glow passes through them at different angles, refracting differently, producing a play of brightness that looks intentional but is structural. A pool full of filament-mats is a pool full of tiny prisms, and the prisms move because the air moves, and the air moves because the thermal engine pulses, and the thermal engine pulses because the mantle convects, and the mantle convects because the planet is still warm inside, and the planet is still warm inside because it was made that way.

The flat things in the basins eat the filaments. They have always eaten the filaments. Their mouthparts are adapted to it — not adapted in the sense of designed, but adapted in the sense of shaped by, the way a riverbed is shaped by the water that flows through it. The flat things and the filaments have been in each other for as long as either has existed, and the filaments grow back after being eaten because the warm water carries nutrients that the flat things' waste returns to the pool, and the cycle is so tight that it is more accurate to say the filaments and the flat things are one organism in two bodies than to say they are two organisms in relationship.

## The Trunk-Plants

They are older than they look. Each one — and there are perhaps ten thousand of them across the plateau, sparse as described, one every fifty meters in loose clusters — is the visible portion of a much larger organism that extends downward through the mineral substrate along the conductive veins. The trunk is not a stem. It is an outlet, a chimney, a place where the deep metabolic activity of the organism meets the air. The organism below is a network of fine tendrils following the conductive veins for tens of meters in every direction, and the tendrils are drawing current and using it to break the carbon-oxygen bonds in the mineral substrate, reassembling the carbon into the dark, rough tissue of the trunk itself. The trunk is a waste product, in a sense. It is what the organism builds with the carbon it has liberated. It is exhaust, but it is also architecture, because the trunk lifts the flat plates into the cooler air where condensation forms, and the condensation runs down the trunk and feeds the tendrils below, and the cycle tightens.

The plates at the top are not leaves. They are condensers. Their flat surfaces are cooler than the ambient air because they radiate heat upward into the cloud deck more efficiently than the dark ground does, and the moisture in the air condenses on their undersides and beads along their edges and drips in the cooler hours, and where it drips, the ground around the trunk's base stays wet longer than the surrounding basins, and the filament-mats grow there, and the flat basin-things gather there, and the area around a trunk-plant is a small ecosystem, a wet zone, a place of concentration in a landscape of dispersion.

The trunks do not move. They do not grow quickly. A trunk-plant that stands a person's height may be several thousand years old, and the plates at its top may have been there for centuries, slowly thickening, slowly darkening, slowly accumulating the mineral deposits that the condensation leaves behind. When a trunk-plant dies — and they do die, though rarely, though slowly — the trunk remains standing for a long time, hollow and dry, and the flat things use it as shelter, and the crust begins to colonize its surface, and eventually it falls and the tendrils below go dormant, and the conductive vein it was tapping finds another outlet or does not.

The hum. The subsonic frequency the plates produce in wind. It is not communication. It is not language. It is the sound of a surface vibrating because air moves across it, and the air moves because the thermal engine creates pressure differentials, and the pressure differentials are the plateau breathing. The trunks are the plateau's instruments, and the music they make is not for anyone. It is the sound of a process completing itself.

## The Striders

They are the closest thing to persons here, and they are not close.

A strider group moves across the plateau at a pace that would be described as unhurried if anyone were describing it, which no one is. They walk in a formation that is not a formation — it is a distribution determined by the crust beneath them, each individual following the chemical gradient that leads to the thickest growth, and because the crust grows in lobes that branch and diverge, the striders spread out and come together and spread out again in patterns that look, from above, like the patterns of a search party or a congregation, though they are neither.

They are warm. Their internal temperature runs between forty-two and forty-six degrees, depending on the thermal cycle, and their skin does not insulate so much as mediate — it is textured to create a thin boundary layer of air that slows heat loss without stopping it, because they do not need to stop it. They produce enough metabolic heat to supplement what they draw from the environment, but only enough to buffer against the coolest periods, the deep-dim hours when the thermal engine is at its lowest output. During those hours, the striders stand still. They do not sleep. They do not rest in the way that implies a return to activity. They simply stop, and their metabolism drops, and they wait, and when the current rises again, they move, and the transition is seamless because there is no boundary between waiting and moving. It is all one state, modulated by the ground beneath their feet.

Their mouthparts are broad and flat, like the blade of a trowel, and they scrape the crust with a motion that is more like chewing than like mining, a rhythmic working of the jaw that breaks the crust loose in flakes and presses it into the gut. The gut is long, much longer than the body would suggest, and it is divided into chambers, each of which hosts a different community of thermophilic microorganisms, each of which breaks down a different fraction of the mineral material. The strider does not digest the crust. The strider's gut digests the crust. The strider is the vehicle, the mechanism of transport, the thing that moves the gut from one part of the plateau to another.

They reproduce, but slowly, and in a way that is difficult to observe because it happens so rarely and without display. Two striders will stand close together for a long time — days, perhaps, standing still in the deep-dim, their flanks touching, their body heat shared — and then they will separate, and one of them will, after a span of time that is measured in weeks, produce a small, fully-formed strider from a ventral pore, and the small strider will stand within minutes, and will walk within hours, and will join the group, and the group will continue.

They do not parent. They do not teach. The small strider knows what it needs to know because its gut already contains the microorganisms it needs, inherited from its parent, and its feet already know the temperature of the ground, and its mouth already knows the taste of the crust. It walks because walking is what its body does. It eats because eating is what its mouth does. There is no learning curve because there is no curve. There is only the continuation of a process that has been continuing for longer than the plateau has looked the way it looks now.

The striders do not name each other. They do not distinguish each other in the way that would require naming. But they do distinguish — each individual carries a chemical signature in the secretions of its skin, a combination of the specific mineral content of its gut (which is determined by exactly which part of the plateau it has been feeding on) and the specific community of epidermal microorganisms that colonize its skin (which is determined by where it has been walking and how long it has been there). A strider can tell, by scent alone, whether another strider has been eating from the same crust-lobe or from a distant one, and it adjusts its position in the group accordingly, moving toward those whose chemical signature suggests they have found richer ground, drifting away from those whose signature suggests depletion. The group is a chemical conversation conducted in skin-secretions and foot-trails, and it has no more need for language than a river needs a map.

## The Flat Things

They do not have a name, and they do not need one, and even the impulse to describe them as "things" is a projection, a need to categorize what simply exists.

They are flat. They are many-legged — twelve to twenty, depending on age and nutrition, the legs emerging in pairs along the lateral margins of a body that is essentially a ribbon, a strip of tissue two to three centimeters wide and up to fifteen centimeters long, undulating through shallow water. The legs are not for walking. They are for filtering — each one is a fringe of fine, flexible filaments that sweep through the water as the body moves, catching the filament-mats and the mineral particulate that the filaments trap, and conveying the catch to a ventral groove that is the mouth, which is also the anus, which is also the respiratory surface. The flat things do not distinguish between these functions because there is no need to distinguish. Everything passes through the same channel. Everything is processed by the same community of symbiotic microorganisms that line the gut, which is the whole interior of the body, which is a tube, which is a living filter.

When a pool dries — and every pool dries, eventually, the water receding into the mineral substrate or evaporating into the slow air — the flat things bury themselves. They do this by flattening further, pressing their bodies into the soft mineral sediment at the bottom of the basin, and secreting a mucus that hardens into a shell, not a shell in the way a mollusk makes a shell but a casing, a seal, a barrier between the living tissue inside and the dry air above. They pull their legs in. They close. They wait.

They can wait for years. The sediment they are buried in is warm, because the thermal engine is always working, and the warmth is enough to keep their metabolism at the very lowest level that still qualifies as alive — a few chemical reactions per hour, a few exchanges of ions across cell membranes, a few tiny electrical impulses that keep the most essential systems ticking over. They are not dormant in the way that seeds are dormant. They are running, just very, very slowly. They are running at the speed of the mineral substrate around them, which is the speed at which stone thinks.

When the water returns — and it does, because the dew comes, because the thermal cycle changes, because the condensation gathers in the basins again — the flat things emerge. Not all at once. The first ones to emerge are those closest to the surface, those that were buried most shallowly, and they come out within hours of the water's return, unfolding from their casings like something that was never really gone. The deeper ones follow over the next days. Some do not emerge at all, because they were buried too deep, or because the casing failed, or because they have been waiting so long that the distinction between waiting and being dead has dissolved. These become part of the sediment, and the sediment becomes part of the crust, and the crust becomes part of the strider's gut, and the cycle continues.

There are millions of them. At any given time, the basins of the plateau contain more flat things than the surface contains striders, trunk-plants, crust-lobes, and clickers combined. They are the most numerous large organism on the plateau, if "large" includes something the size of a hand, which it should, because the flat things are large compared to the things they eat, and the things they eat are large compared to the things those things eat, and the scale of life here is not the scale of life elsewhere, and that is not a deficiency.

## The Clickers

They are the fastest thing on the plateau, and they are not fast.

They fly — or rather, they move through the dense, humid air with a motion that is more like swimming than flying, their wings broad and membranous and beating at a frequency that is slow enough to see, each stroke a distinct pulse that carries them forward a body-length or two. They are small. Ten centimeters across the wings, maybe twelve, with bodies no larger than a thumb. They are dark, like everything here, dark and matte and slightly iridescent in the way that thin films of mineral oil are iridescent, not because they produce color but because their surface structure interferes with the faint light that exists, splitting it into spectral components that are too dim to register as color but that give their bodies a quality of depth, a sheen that is not brightness but complexity.

Their clicks are their world. Each click is a single pulse of sound, produced by a membranous organ in the throat that snaps taut and releases, a motion like a tiny drumhead being struck. The click propagates through the dense air and reflects off every surface — the ground, the basins, the ridges, the trunks, the striders, the other clickers — and returns to the clicker's ears, which are not ears in the way that most animals have ears but are distributed pressure sensors across the surface of the body, each one tuned to a slightly different frequency, each one registering the returning echo with a precision that builds, click by click, a map of the space around them.

A clicker emits between twenty and sixty clicks per second, depending on what it is doing. In flight, the rate is high — forty to sixty, a rapid stream of pulses that builds a continuous image of the landscape in motion, updated with each click, each echo adding detail and discarding the stale echoes of seconds ago. At rest, the rate drops to twenty or below, a slow survey, a keeping-track. In the deep-dim hours, when the thermal engine is low and the striders stand still and the pools go dark, the clickers roost — hanging from the undersides of the trunk-plant plates, clustered in groups of five or six, their bodies pressed together for warmth — and their click rate drops to two or three per second, a slow heartbeat of sound that maintains the barest awareness of the space around them.

They eat the crust's growing edge. The luminous margin, the place where the new growth is softest and most chemically active, is where the clickers feed. They land on the crust and scrape at it with mandibles that are too small to see but that leave marks — tiny furrows in the soft leading edge, tracks that the crust will grow over and incorporate into its structure. The clickers are, in a sense, the crust's pollinators, though there is no pollen and no pollination. They carry the crust's cells on their legs and bodies from one growing edge to another, and where they land, the cells they carry may establish themselves, and a new lobe may begin, and the crust's reach may extend by a few centimeters in a direction it would not have reached on its own.

The clicking is the sound of the plateau's navigation system, and it never stops, not entirely, because there are always clickers in flight, always someone in the air, always someone mapping the space and the things in it. The sound is the plateau's awareness of itself, not conscious awareness, not reflective awareness, but the awareness of a system that is constantly sensing itself through the movements of its smallest members. The clickers are the plateau's eyes, and the striders are the plateau's hands, and the flat things are the plateau's memory, and the crust is the plateau's body, and the thermal engine is the plateau's heart, and none of them know this, and all of them are it.

## The Things That Are Not Described

There are other things. There must be. A system this large, this stable, this old, contains niches that no description has reached — decomposers that break down the strider's waste, parasites that live in the clicker's gut, microorganisms that mediate the boundary between the crust and the bare mineral substrate, things that live in the deep fractures where the thermal fluids are hottest, things that live in the cloud deck itself, if the clouds are hospitable, which they might be, because the clouds are made of water and mineral particulate and the faint electrical charge of the atmosphere above the plateau, and these are the materials of life, and life is what happens when these materials are left alone together for a long time.

There may be things that have never been seen because they are too small, or too slow, or too deeply embedded in the systems that support them. There may be things that emerge only rarely — once a century, once a millennium — when the thermal cycle reaches a particular resonance or the clouds thin in a particular way or the conductive veins shift their pattern in response to some deep tectonic adjustment. These things would be part of the plateau in the way that a heartbeat that comes only once a year is still part of the body's rhythm, still a beat, still a pulse.

There are no people. There is nothing here that builds or names or plans or mourns. There is nothing here that knows the plateau is a machine, nothing that recognizes the industrial character of the processes that sustain it, nothing that stands apart and observes and is moved by what it sees. There is only the machine, running, and the things that are part of the machine, running with it, and the darkness, and the warmth, and the quiet, continuous clicking that is the sound of a world sensing itself into existence, moment by moment, click by click, without end.
