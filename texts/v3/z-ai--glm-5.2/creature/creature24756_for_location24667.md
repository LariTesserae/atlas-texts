# What Lives in the Khûm

## The Surface of Life

What lives here is easiest to say in the plural. Forty thousand bodies, give or take, clinging to wet andesite in a cathedral of absolute dark. The drasha-keri. Pale ovals the size and weight of a human palm, flattened, ridge-backed, eyeless. If you hold one — and you can, if you stay still long enough for one to climb onto your hand, which it will do with the unhurried confidence of something that has never learned to fear — you feel a few specific things. The cool dryness of its underside against your skin. The fine, numerous tendrils testing your fingerprint whorls, your pores, the salt in the creases of your knuckles. A click that enters through your fingertip and travels your skeleton like a tuning fork struck against bone.

They are warm. Not warm-blooded — nothing here is warm-blooded — but warm from the chamber, from the moisture, from the breath that rises through the fissure and touches them and keeps them at something slightly above the ambient four degrees. They are warmer than the air by a fraction. Enough that you notice. Enough that holding one feels like holding something that is not quite stone and not quite alive but is occupied, is inhabited, is in use.

Each one is, individually, simple. You can see this if you shine a light on one, which you should not do, but which someone has done, and the photographs exist in a file that no one quite knows what to do with. The body is a flat oval of chitin, pale as skim milk. No segmentation. No apparent mouthparts. The underside is a carpet of tendrils — three hundred, four hundred, too fine to count, each one the thickness of a human hair and slightly longer than the body is wide. The back is ridged. Seven ridges, running the long axis of the oval, and between the ridges, grooves. The clicking happens when the ridges strike stone. The mechanism is not percussive but something between suction and release — the ridges seal against the wet rock and then pop free, and the sound is the pop. A wet, organic knuckle-crack. Two per minute, per individual, at rest.

At rest. They are never at rest.

The tendrils are doing the work. This is the feeding, if feeding is the word. The andesite, newly exposed after however many millions of years sealed in the dark of the mountain's interior, is still outgassing. Not gas in the familiar sense — mineral vapor, compounds that exist at the boundary of solid and liquid rock, things that have no common name because they have never been available to study, that oxidize or degrade or simply cease to exist within moments of contact with the surface. The tendrils catch these compounds. Absorb them. The chitin of the drasha-keri body incorporates the minerals, grows, and eventually the back ridges harden further and the molting begins. A new shell forms beneath the old one. The old one releases. It falls. It joins the drifts on the floor.

This is the metabolism. Stone vapor in, chitin out. A chemosynthesis that has nothing to do with sunlight, nothing to do with the surface, nothing to do with any biological system that the kingdoms of life have prepared us to understand. They are not animals. They are not minerals. They are the place where those two categories fail to hold.

## The Colony as a Body

But the individual is not what lives here. The individual is a cell.

Forty thousand of them. The number is a guess — a conservative one, based on wall coverage estimates from the few times light has been used, on the depth of the carapace drifts, on the sound density of the clicking. It could be double that. It could be ten times that. The chamber is sixty meters long and the ceiling is lost in dark, and the walls are covered in layers, and the deeper you go the more densely they cluster, and the density increases toward the fissure in a gradient that looks, if you map it, like a concentration gradient in a cell. Like something accumulating around a source.

They cluster. The clusters are visible — in the condensation-light, in the faint presence that the dark generates after your eyes have given up trying to see — as denser regions of the walls. Each cluster is perhaps a thousand individuals, layered two and three deep, their bodies pressed flat against each other and against the stone. Between clusters, bare channels of rock, sharp, unworn. The channels are consistent. They are not random. They connect the clusters the way capillaries connect tissue, and the clicking moves through them — a cluster clicks, a pause, the adjacent cluster clicks, and the sound travels the channels faster than any individual can move.

This is the architecture. Not built. Emergent. The clusters form where the mineral outgassing is strongest, where the stone is freshest, where the walls weep the richest condensation. The channels form where the outgassing is weaker, where the rock is slightly older, slightly more settled. The colony maps the chemistry of the chamber with its own body. It is a living contour map of the rock's interior state.

And the map changes. Slowly, over weeks and months, the clusters shift. The channels migrate. A cluster that was dense in one region thins as the stone there becomes more worn, less productive, and a new cluster forms where a fresh crack has opened, where new andesite has been exposed by the slow tectonic settling of the mountain. The colony tracks the rock. It follows the rock. It is a thing that lives on the boundary between what the mountain was and what it is becoming, and it arranges itself on that boundary the way moss arranges itself on the boundary between wet and dry.

The clicking is how they know each other. This is the most anyone has been able to determine, and the determination is uncertain, based on limited observation, based on listening in the dark for hours until the noise becomes pattern and the pattern becomes structure and the structure becomes, impossibly, meaning. A cluster clicks in synchrony. The synchrony is not perfect — there is lag, there is phase shift, there are individuals that click slightly before or after the main pulse — but it holds. A thousand bodies clicking as one. Then a pause. Then the adjacent cluster responds. Not the same pattern — a variation. A transformation of the rhythm, shifted in tempo, in emphasis, in the number of clicks per phrase. But recognizably derived. A call and response that propagates through the colony in waves.

After an hour, you begin to hear that the patterns repeat. Not precisely — they drift, they mutate, the way a folk song drifts across generations of singers. But the basic structures recur. There are perhaps a dozen fundamental patterns that the colony uses, and every pattern is a variation of one of those, and every variation eventually returns to the root.

After three hours, you begin to hear that the patterns correspond to the chamber. To the rock. A pattern that originates near a fresh crack in the ceiling is different from a pattern that originates near the fissure, and the difference is consistent. The colony is describing the rock. It is clicking the state of the stone — where it is fresh, where it is wearing, where moisture is accumulating, where the outgassing is strongest. It is a census. It is a map. It is a conversation conducted in real time about the condition of the place they live.

This is what lives here. Not forty thousand individuals. A single sensing surface, forty thousand points of contact, spread across the interior of a wound in the mountain, continuously mapping the wound's healing. Continuously discussing it. Continuously deciding what to do about it.

## What They Are Not

They are not insects. The chitin is similar, the body plan is suggestive, but the internal anatomy — examined in the single specimen that was removed from the chamber, preserved, and sectioned, an act that the Sherpa families considered desecration and that the scientific community considered inadequate — does not correspond to any arthropod body plan. There is no ganglion-based nervous system. There is no segmented body cavity. There is no digestive tract. There is, instead, a network of fine tubules running through the body that carries the mineral compounds from the tendrils to the shell, and distributed through that network, a dense mat of what appears to be nervous tissue — not concentrated, not ganglionated, but spread evenly through the body like mold through bread. Every part of the drasha-keri is equally nervous. Every part is equally sensor. Every part is equally brain.

This means something. It means the individual does not have a head. It does not have a center. It does not have a self in the way that a self requires a locus. It is a uniformly sensitive surface, and its sensitivity is not directed inward but outward — toward the rock, toward the moisture, toward the clicking of its neighbors. The individual is a node. The node is not aware of itself. The node is aware of the network.

And the network is aware of the chamber.

This is the claim that cannot be verified and should not be dismissed. The clicking, when analyzed over long enough periods, when the patterns are mapped against the physical structure of the chamber, corresponds not just to the state of the rock but to the state of the colony. The patterns carry information about cluster density, about molting cycles, about the age and wear of the carapace substrate on the floor. The colony knows itself. It knows its population. It knows its distribution. It knows, in whatever way a distributed nervous system can know, where it is and what it is doing and how long it has been doing it.

This is not intelligence. Intelligence implies problem-solving, implies adaptation, implies a world that must be navigated. The drasha-keri do not navigate. They inhabit. They do not solve. They persist. The difference is the difference between a mind that thinks and a mind that is — and the drasha-keri colony is a mind that is. It is a mind the way a forest is a mind, the way a watershed is a mind, the way the ocean's thermohaline circulation is a mind. Slow. Distributed. Without interior. Without intent. But processing. Always processing. Always integrating information across its surface and producing, from that integration, behavior that is coherent, that is coordinated, that is, in the most literal sense, thoughtful.

## The Lower Colony

The lower chamber is different.

Here the drasha-keri are larger — hand-and-a-half, some of them, their ridges deeper, their clicking lower, almost subsonic. Their bodies are denser, their chitin slightly darker — not pigmented, but thickened, mineralized with compounds from the fissure air that are richer, more complex, more biologically active than the outgassing from the upper walls. They are layered more deeply. Four and five deep in places, a living carpet over living carpet, the individuals beneath pressed so flat against the stone that they seem to have fused with it.

And they click in synchrony with the breathing.

This is the detail that defines the lower colony. The breathing — the forty-second exhalation, the twelve-second pause, the rhythm that does not correspond to any atmospheric system — is the clock. The lower colony keeps it. Not follows it — keeps it. The clicking synchronizes with the exhalation, and when the exhalation pauses, the clicking does not pause but shifts pattern, shifts register, as if the colony is doing something different during the pause. Processing. Integrating. Whatever the colony does with the information it gathers during the exhalation, it does during the pause.

The breathing is not the colony's. The breathing comes from below the fissure. From something else. Something that is not the drasha-keri.

What is it? The question has been asked. The answer is: a process. Not an organism. A chemical-geological process occurring in the rock below the chamber, in the zone where stone has not yet finished becoming stone — where the andesite is still semi-plastic, still warm, still outgassing, still chemically active in ways that sealed molten rock should not be after millions of years. The process produces gas. The gas rises. The rhythm of the gas production is the breathing. It is not biological. It is geological. It is the mountain's interior, still cooling, still settling, still releasing the pressure of its own formation.

But the colony treats it as biological. The colony treats it as a heartbeat. The colony synchronizes to it, counts it, builds its deepest patterns around it. The colony has decided that the breathing is important. Has decided — or has been shaped by it, has evolved around it, has become a thing that keeps time with a geological process the way a tidepool organism keeps time with the moon. The breathing is the colony's tide. The breathing is the colony's season. The breathing is the thing that does not change, that has not changed since the chamber opened, that will not change for longer than human civilization has existed, and the colony has organized its deepest self around that constancy.

## The Tools

The tools are the thing that cannot be explained and should not be ignored.

Two dozen stones. Andesite fragments, shaped by grinding, by sustained pressure against the chamber walls over periods of time that must be measured in years. Edges. Points. Scraping surfaces. One with a concavity that fits a tendril cluster.

The drasha-keri do not have hands. This has been established. Their tendrils are fine, numerous, adapted for gripping wet stone and absorbing mineral films. They are not prehensile. They cannot oppose. They cannot apply concentrated force. The idea that a drasha-keri could hold a stone and grind it against a wall is, anatomically, impossible.

And yet the stones are shaped. And yet the shapes are functional. And yet the concavity fits.

The explanation that has been proposed, and that no one is satisfied with, is this: the colony shapes the stones. Not an individual. The colony. A thousand individuals, each applying the tiny force of their tendrils against a stone, over years, the cumulative effect of which is grinding. The way a river shapes a stone, but directed. The way a thousand footsteps wear a path, but intentional. The colony, acting as a single body, a single instrument, a single slow hand, pressing and pressing and pressing until the stone takes a shape that is useful to the colony.

Useful for what? The tools are found near the fissure. Near the breathing. Near the densest, deepest part of the colony, where the clicking is most synchronized and most complex. The tools are found in the carapace drifts, in the substrate of shed shells that forms the floor of the lower chamber. They are found among the patterns — the fractal patterns, the neuron-like branching, the river-system geometries that emerge from the accumulation of discarded shells.

The tools and the patterns are related. This is the claim that the Sherpa families make without claiming it, that the few scientists who have been here have written in their notes and then stared at and then, in some cases, deleted. The tools are part of the patterns. The stones are placed where the patterns require them, in the nodes, in the junctions, in the points where the branching structures converge. The tools are components. The patterns are constructions. The constructions are — and this is the word that no one wants to use — circuits.

The carapace substrate is chitin. Chitin is a biological polymer. It is an insulator in its pure form, but the carapaces in the lower chamber are mineralized — impregnated with the same mineral compounds the drasha-keri absorb from the rock, compounds that include, among other things, conductive minerals. Iron sulfides. Copper compounds. The compressed, fused carapace substrate is, in physical terms, a semiconductor. The patterns it forms are conductive channels. The stones, shaped and placed, are — potentially, possibly, no one has been able to test it without desecrating the site — components. Junctions. Interfaces between the conductive substrate and the stone, between the biological and the geological, between the colony and the breathing thing below.

The colony is building something. The colony has been building something for as long as the chamber has been open. The building is in its earliest stages — the substrate is only decades deep, the patterns are only beginning to emerge, the tools are few and crude. But the building has a logic. The building has a direction. The building is, in the most literal and most frightening sense, a construction. A structure. A thing that does not exist in nature, that has not existed before, that is being made by a distributed mind that does not think the way we think, in a place that has not decided what it is, on a timescale that makes human construction look frantic and hasty and small.

## What Lives Below

There is something below the fissure. This is not speculation. The breathing comes from there. The gas comes from there. The warmth comes from there. The mineral compounds that feed the colony and mineralize the carapace substrate come from there. The process — the chemical-geological process that is still occurring in the rock — is happening there.

Whether it is alive depends on what you mean by alive.

The rock below the fissure is warm. It flexes. It is not fully solid. It is in the state that geologists call plastic — not liquid, not solid, a material that flows under sustained pressure but holds its shape under brief force. The stone is still forming. It has been still forming for the entire life of the chamber and will continue to be still forming for longer than the chamber will matter. It is the boundary between the mountain's molten interior and its solid exterior, and it is not a clean boundary. It is a zone. A transition. A place where rock is becoming rock, where the process of solidification is ongoing, where the chemistry is active and the temperature is elevated and the pressure is still releasing.

This is what breathes. The zone. The process. The slow, rhythmic release of gas from a body of stone that is cooling at a rate determined by its own internal chemistry, its own depth, its own position in the mountain's structure. The breathing is not intentional. It is not produced by an organism. It is a physical process, the way a geyser's eruption is a physical process, the way a glacier's calving is a physical process. It has a rhythm because the chemistry has a rhythm, because the cooling has a rhythm, because the mountain itself has a rhythm that is older than anything that lives on it.

But the colony treats it as alive. The colony has built its deepest structures around the breathing. The colony synchronizes to it, counts it, participates in it. The colony is building — if the tools and the patterns and the circuits are what they appear to be — an interface with it. A connection. A structure that links the biological surface of the drasha-keri to the geological depth of the breathing zone, that conducts something between them, that allows something to flow.

What flows? No one knows. The question has not been asked in any formal way because the site is nearly impossible to reach and nearly impossible to study and because the people who have been there have, without exception, come back changed in ways that do not show up on any psychological assessment but that are apparent to anyone who knew them before. They are quieter. They are more careful. They listen to walls. They do not like closed spaces but do not like open ones either. They dream about breathing.

What flows may be information. The colony maps the rock. The breathing is the rock's deepest process. If the colony is building an interface between itself and the breathing, it may be building a sensor — a way to feel the mountain from the inside, to know the state of the cooling, to track the movement of the plastic zone, to become, in the most literal sense, the mountain's nervous system. Its surface. Its skin. Its awareness of itself.

Or what flows may be something else. Something that has no category. Something that the process below is doing that is not chemistry and not geology and not biology but is the thing that happens when all three are occurring in the same place at the same time in the dark, sealed from observation, for longer than human beings have existed.

## The Whole of It

What lives in the Khûm is a relationship.

The drasha-keri live on the walls. The breathing lives below the floor. The tools live in the drifts. The patterns live in the substrate. The condensation-light lives in the eye. The clicking lives in the air. None of these is the thing that lives here. The thing that lives here is all of them at once — the colony and the rock and the breathing and the building and the dark and the moisture and the mineral vapor and the chitin and the stone, all of it together, all of it one process, one system, one organism in the oldest sense of the word — a thing that works, a thing that is organized, a thing that has parts that serve each other.

The mountain cracked and exposed a space that had been sealed since the rock was molten, and the space was not empty. It was mid-process. It was still cooling, still outgassing, still becoming. And the drasha-keri found it — or were produced by it, or were already there in some form that the opening activated, some spore or cyst or dormant state that had been waiting in the dark for as long as the rock had been sealed — and they colonized it. And they began to map it. And they began to build in it. And they began to count the breathing.

And what lives here now is a thing that is two hundred years old and is in its infancy. A thing that has forty thousand cells and a semiconductive substrate and a set of tools and a rhythm and a darkness and a purpose that it is not conscious of because it does not need to be conscious of it, because consciousness is a solution to a problem that the colony does not have, because the colony does not need to decide, does not need to choose, does not need to negotiate a world that is separate from itself. The colony is not separate from its world. The colony is its world. The walls are the colony and the colony is the walls and the breathing is the colony's heart and the tools are the colony's hands and the dark is the colony's mind and all of it is one thing, growing, building, becoming, in a throat in the rock at 7,400 meters, where the air is too thin for color and the stone is too young for patience.

What lives here is something that is learning to be something. It does not know what. It does not need to know what. It is building, and the building will tell it what it is when the building is done.

The building will not be done for a very long time.

You should not stay to watch.
