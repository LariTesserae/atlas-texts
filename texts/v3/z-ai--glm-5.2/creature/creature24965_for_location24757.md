# What Lives Here

---

The snails, first, because they were first, or because they are the closest thing this place has to a foundation.

They do not think of themselves as many. There is no vantage in the ovulum from which a snail could see the full extent of its kin — the walls curve away, the light flattens depth, and a snail's world is its flute and the two flutes adjacent and the condensation that arrives each morning like a daily forgiveness. But there are thousands, and to an eye above — say, the eye of a bird hesitating at the rim — they would appear as a texture, a roughness, a pelt. The walls are furred with them the way a dog is furred with hair: individually distinct, collectively a surface.

Each snail is a pale disc, countersunk into its own secreted lime, with a muscular foot that it has not used in so long that the foot is more of a memory than an organ. The foot still twitches in the morning damp. It still tries, in some chemical way, to remember what walking was. But the snail has chosen its spot — or the spot chose it, which is closer to the truth — and the spot is where the flute narrows slightly, where the condensation beads thickest, where the calcium carbonate of the wall is just soft enough to accept the calcium carbonate of the shell, and the two materials marry. The snail is married to the stone. It is an arranged marriage, conducted by chemistry, and it is permanent.

What the snail does is wait. The condensation comes. The condensation carries dissolved minerals — calcium, iron, traces of sulfur — and the snail filters these from the water film with a radula so fine it is less a tongue than a sieve. It is eating the wall. Very slowly. It is eating the wall and building its shell from the wall and anchoring its shell to the wall, and the wall is growing the snail the way an apple tree grows apples. The snail is a fruit of the limestone. When the snail dies — and it does, eventually, after years or decades, no one has watched closely enough to know the upper bound — the shell remains. The shell becomes part of the wall again. The calcium goes home. A new snail settles on the old one, or beside it, and the accretion continues.

There are layers. If you were to cut the wall — which no one has done, which would be a kind of violence — you would find a stratigraphy of shells, older ones dissolved to ghosts, newer ones still crisp at their edges, the whole mass compacted into a biolith, a rock made of animals. The wall is a cemetery and a nursery simultaneously. It is the most alive stone you have ever touched, if you touch it, which the lizards would advise against, because the lizards know what the wall is for, and what it is for is growing snails.

---

The lizards, then, because the lizards know what the wall is for.

They are called lizards for convenience. They are not quite lizards in the way a taxonomist would recognize — their limbs are slightly too long, their skulls slightly too high, their scales so fine they look like skin, and their skin so thin it looks like paper held over a candle. You can see through them. Not through them entirely, not like glass, but along the flanks, where the pigment thins to nothing and the body becomes a membrane. The dark thread of the dorsal artery. The slow knot of the stomach. The pulse, if you are patient and close enough to see it, which you are not, because the lizard will leave before you get that close. They know the distance a human body carries its own heat, and they move when that warmth gets near.

They are the color of the stone. This is not camouflage in the chameleon sense — they do not shift hue, do not adjust. They were born pale, or rather they were born translucent and grew pale as their scales accumulated a thin film of limestone dust that they do not clean and do not need to clean, because being dirty is the same as being hidden, here, and being hidden is the same as being alive. The oldest lizards are almost indistinguishable from the wall itself. They are slightly more geometric than the snails. They are the mobile architecture of the ovulum.

They hunt by angle. The snail's aperture faces up — up the flute, toward the source of the condensation — and the shell's lip overhangs the soft body like a tiny awning. To reach the snail, you must get under the awning. The lizard's front teeth have widened for this purpose — not pointed but blade-like, thin and flat, a tool evolved for a single task: prying. The lizard approaches from above, seals its mouth against the shell's rim, and pushes downward and outward. The cement that holds the snail to the stone is strong but not infinite, and the lizard's neck is stronger than it looks. The shell pops free with a click that echoes in the flute like a single note plucked from a very dry guitar.

The young lizards are bad at this. They approach from the wrong angle, bite too high, and the shell holds. They try again. They try from the side. They try with their mouths open too wide, wasting leverage. Some give up and eat insects instead — the gnats are easier, the gnats do not fight back, the gnats are simply there, hovering in their thin columns of light, and a young lizard can sit beside a column and flick its tongue and eat for an hour and never get better at anything. These lizards grow slower. They are smaller as adults. They die younger. The wall is a teacher, and the lesson is: learn to pry, or eat bugs and be small.

The old lizards — the ones with teeth worn to nubs, the ones whose jaw muscles have thickened into something almost mechanical — have a different approach. They come from the side now, not the top. They slide a flattened tooth under the shell's edge and roll it, the way you would roll a coin off a table with your thumbnail. The shell comes free intact, not cracked, and the lizard eats the snail from the underside without breaking the shell at all. These shells, emptied and clean, are the ones most likely to be re-colonized. The old lizards are, inadvertently, farmers. They clear the old shells and make room for the new, and the cycle continues, and the wall grows, and the lizards grow old on the wall they are tending.

There is a moment — and if you sit still for long enough, which is hours, which is most of a day, you will see it — when a lizard finishes eating and pauses. It sits on the flute, its belly against the damp stone, and it is still. Not vigilant-still. Not hunting-still. Just still. The light pours down and the warmth rises and the lizard's translucent flank pulses with its heartbeat, which is fast, which is always fast, a reptilian hum of blood and metabolism, and for a few seconds the lizard is not doing anything at all. It is not eating. It is not hiding. It is not mating or fighting or sleeping. It is just being in the warm light, in the quiet, in the ovulum, and then the moment passes and it moves, quick and angular, to the next flute, and the next snail, and the next small life.

---

The insects are almost too small to dignify with attention, and then you watch them for an afternoon and realize they are not small at all. They are the right size for what they do, and what they do is convert marl and moisture into protein, and protein into flight, and flight into food for everything else.

The gnats breed in the damp spots — the shallow depressions in the marl where the moss grows, where the condensation collects into a thin film that is not quite water and not quite mud. The larvae are transparent, almost invisible, small enough that a hundred of them could fit in a teaspoon and you would not notice unless you looked very closely, and even then you would only see movement, a twitching, a restlessness, that is life at its most minimal. They mature in days. They emerge as adults and fly to the light, because the light is where they are supposed to be, because the light is what an insect does when it does not know what else to do.

They form columns. Not swarms — columns. Vertical, narrow, rising from the marl to the bright air above, each column perhaps a few thousand individuals, each column positioned where the reflected light is strongest, which is where the walls' reflectance converges, which is, not coincidentally, where the lizards hunt. The gnats are flying into the light and the lizards are waiting in the light and the birds are descending through the light, and the light is the ovulum's throat, and the throat is eating everything that enters it, slowly, through intermediaries, without malice.

The thrips are different. They live on the moss. They are so small they seem to be a property of the moss itself — a dark dusting, a movement that belongs to the plant rather than to anything separate from it. You would not notice them unless you pressed your face to the moss and stared, and even then you would only see the moss shiver and think it was wind, and there is no wind here, so you would think it was nothing, and you would be wrong, because the thrips are there, living their thrip-lives, which are short and simple and entirely contained within a few square centimeters of dark green velvet in a white stone room open to the sky.

---

The birds do not live here. This is important to them and important to the ovulum. The birds are visitors, and the ovulum is a place they visit, and the distinction matters because everything else here — the snails, the lizards, the insects, the moss — has no elsewhere. The bird has an elsewhere. The bird has a nest in the scrub above, in a twisted shrub that would not survive inside the ovulum, in a landscape that has wind and weather and seasons, and the bird comes here to hunt and leaves to sleep, and this is the difference between a resident and a predator, between a citizen and a tourist.

But oh, they are precise. They descend the shaft in spirals — not straight down, which would be too fast, which would crash them into the floor, but in a tightening helix that uses the updraft of warm air as a brake and a guide. Their wings are angular. Their tails are long. They are built for this specific entry, this specific geometry, the way a key is built for a specific lock, and the ovulum is a lock, and the birds open it twice a day, morning and evening, when the lizards are active and the light is right.

They do not miss. Or almost never. A lizard on the wall is a dark shape on a pale surface, and the bird's eyes — sharp, acute, evolved for exactly this angle of attack — lock onto it from ten meters up and the dive is a calculation completed before it begins. The talons close. The lizard thrashes once, twice, and then is carried upward, out of the throat, into the bright sky above, and the ovulum has given something to the world outside, and the world outside has given nothing back except the bird's shadow, which falls for an instant across the floor and then is gone.

The calls. A single sharp note — a *tsik*, hard and bright — and the chamber turns it into a chord. The walls are curved just right, the oval shape does something to the frequency, and one bird's cry becomes three notes, four, overlapping, a brief eerie harmony that sounds less like an echo and more like the ovulum answering. As if the stone has a voice. As if the place is speaking back to the bird, saying: *yes, I heard you, I know what you took, come again tomorrow.*

The birds do come again tomorrow. They always do.

---

The moss lives in patches. This needs to be said plainly, because the moss is easy to overlook and the moss is the only thing here that photosynthesizes, which means the moss is the base, the floor of the food web, the thing that turns light into sugar and sugar into life. Everything else in the ovulum is eating something that ate something that ate the moss, or eating something that ate something that ate the minerals in the stone, which is not the same as photosynthesis but is adjacent to it, is the stone's way of participating in the economy of light.

The moss is a single species. Dark green, almost black when wet, almost gray when dry. It grows in the depressions in the marl where condensation collects, and it grows nowhere else, and it has been growing in these same depressions for however long moss has been growing here, which is long, which is long enough that the moss and the marl have reached an understanding. The moss holds the moisture. The marl holds the moss. The thrips live in the moss. The gnats breed in the moisture the moss holds. The lizards eat the gnats. The birds eat the lizards. And the moss sits there, dark and plush and patient, being the foundation of everything, and no one thanks it, and it does not require thanks, and it does not require anything except a film of water each morning and the light that pours down all day, and it has those things, and it is content, if moss can be content, which it can, in its way, which is the way of something that has never wanted to be anywhere else.

In the dry season — and there is a dry season, even here, even in this place that has decided what it is — the moss goes brown. The plushness goes out of it. It becomes brittle, scratchy, a dead thing on pale clay. But it is not dead. It is in a state that biologists call cryptobiosis, which means hidden life, which means the metabolism has slowed to something undetectable, which means the moss is waiting, and waiting is a form of living, and the moss is very good at it. The first morning of condensation after the dry season, the moss greens within hours. Not days. Hours. You could watch it happen, if you were here at the right time, which you will not be, because you have an elsewhere, and the moss does not.

---

And the circle.

Something lives in the circle, or the circle is where something lived and has stopped, or the circle is what is left when life declines to make a claim. The circle is a meter across. It is on the floor. It is clean.

What lives in the circle is: nothing.

This is not nothing the way a room is empty. This is nothing the way a pause in a conversation is empty — full of the words that were not said, alert with the possibility of them. The circle is an absence that has the weight of a presence. The lizards know this. They approach its edge and they turn, not sharply, not in alarm, but with the smoothness of something following a rule it has never questioned. The rule is: not here. The rule is: this is not yours. The rule is older than the lizards. It may be older than the snails. It may be as old as the stone, which is to say: it may not have a beginning, which is to say: it may be a property of the place rather than a thing the place contains.

The insects do not fly over it. You notice this if you watch the gnat columns — they rise from the marl around the circle, never from within it, and the columns nearest the circle lean slightly away, as if the air above the bare stone is different, is not air exactly, is something air-adjacent that does not support the tiny physics of insect flight. Or perhaps the insects simply choose not to. Perhaps there is nothing to choose about. Perhaps the circle is invisible to them except as a feeling, a chemical unease, a small voice in the ganglion that says: *elsewhere.*

The birds do not land in it. They fly over it — the whole floor is their flight path — but they do not land in it, and they would not, and if a lizard were to break from a bird's talons and fall, it would not fall into the circle, it would fall beside it, because even a falling body respects the boundary, or the boundary adjusts to avoid the falling body, which is a different proposition and a stranger one and possibly the correct one.

If you stand in the circle, the lizards watch. This has been said. What has not been said is the quality of their watching. It is not fear. It is not curiosity. It is the attention of something that has a category for this and the category is not *threat* or *food* or *weather* but something else, something their simple brains carry without naming, a category that might be called *the thing that happens at the circle* or might be called *the place where the rules are different* or might have no name at all and exist only as a behavior, a turning-away, a respect.

You stand in the circle. Nothing happens. You stand in the circle for an hour. Nothing happens. You leave the circle. The lizards stop watching. The distinction between nothing-happens-inside and nothing-happens-outside is imperceptible to you but perfectly perceptible to them, and this means the nothing inside is different from the nothing outside, which means the nothing is not nothing, which means something is there, something that does not eat or grow or move or breathe, something that is present as an absence, as a clearing, as the world's own held breath, and you cannot know it but you can feel it, the way you can feel a room go quiet when someone stops speaking, and the circle is where someone stopped speaking, a very long time ago, and the silence has not yet been broken, and the snails and the lizards and the moss and the insects and the birds have all agreed, without agreeing, that it will not be broken by them.

---

What lives in the ovulum, then:

A wall that grows snails. Snails that become wall. Lizards that farm the wall by eating it. Insects that turn moisture into flight. Moss that turns light into ground. Birds that descend from elsewhere to take what the light has grown and carry it back to the sky.

And a circle of clean stone where the system pauses, where the food web does not extend, where the limestone is just limestone and the water does not collect and the snails do not settle and the lizards do not cross and the moss does not grow and the light falls and is not reflected and the warmth rises and is not held and the silence is deepest and the stone is most itself, and whatever that means, whatever the circle is, it is the thing the rest of the ovulum is not, and it is the reason, perhaps, that the ovulum is a throat and not just a hole, and an eye and not just a depression, because throats and eyes are organs, are instruments, are bodies, and the circle is where the body keeps its quiet, its privacy, the thing it does not show to the sky even though the sky can see all of it, even though the ceiling is gone and everything is exposed and the light pours straight down into the most secret part, and still the circle is opaque, and still the stone holds its secret, and still the lizards watch, and still they are not impressed, and still they wait, as everything here waits, for whatever comes next, which will be the same as what came before, which was morning, which was condensation, which was light, which was enough.
