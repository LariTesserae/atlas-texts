# What Lives in the Uplands of Vesh-Taram

---

## First, the things you cannot see

The biofilms came before anything else that was alive. Or they were made before anything else that was alive. The distinction matters to some people and not to others, and the people it matters to most are the ones who were there, and they will not give you a straight answer.

What the biofilms are: a skin. That is the word the microbiologists use in the unpublished notes that circulate through Taram's research nodes, and it is more accurate than any formal taxonomy. A skin across the stone. The cells within are not singular in the way that laboratory cultures are singular. They are chimeric. The membranes contain structures that resemble known photosynthetic machinery and structures that resemble nothing in the literature, and the two systems share resources across molecular bridges that should not be stable and are. The biofilms fix carbon and nitrogen simultaneously from the same thin air, which is metabolically reckless and which works anyway. They grow at a pace that is imperceptible to the naked eye — a centimeter every three weeks, spreading outward from wherever the initial colony was seeded or arose, merging with adjacent colonies into a continuous dark mat that follows the moisture across the rock's surface.

They are warm. This is the detail that confuses visiting biologists. The metabolic processes produce a small amount of heat — not much, fractions of a degree — but on a plateau where the ambient temperature hovers near freezing and the wind never stops, that fraction is enough. The biofilm surfaces are measurably warmer than the bare stone surrounding them. The Veshi know this. The Veshi lie down on the biofilm patches when they rest, pressing their bellies to the dark green-black mat, and the warmth seeps into them. It is the closest thing to comfort the plateau offers, and they take it without knowing they are taking it.

The water has its own life. The greenish tint in the shallow sheets is not the biofilm — it is something else, something that occupies the water column itself rather than the substrate. It is unicellular, or possibly subcellular — the researchers in Taram have been debating this for eleven years without resolution. It does not photosynthesize. It fixes iron from the dissolved basalt and precipitates it in thin films on the stone beneath the water, which is why the shallow braids sometimes have a faintly metallic sheen that is not just surface tension. It is laying down mineral. It is, in its blind and aimless way, making soil. Not for any purpose. Not with any intent. It is simply doing what its chemistry allows it to do, and the result is a layer of iron-calcium sediment a few millimeters thick beneath the slow-moving water, which will, in a thousand years or ten thousand, be the substrate in which the first true plants put their roots.

If anything comes to put roots. Nothing has yet. The plateau is patient about this.

The insects are the smallest visible life and the most numerous by several orders of magnitude. Midges — that is the simple word, and it is wrong. They are not true midges. They are something that evolved in the lowland cloud-forests and migrated upward when the plateau emerged, and in the three generations since, they have diverged. The upland variety is smaller, darker, with wings so thin they are transparent to the point of invisibility. You see them as a disturbance in the air — a shimmer, a texture, a fine fog that is not fog. They breed in the shallow water sheets, laying eggs on the biofilm surface, and the larvae live in the two centimeters of slow water for three weeks before emerging. They do not bite. They have no mouthparts for it. They exist to mate and lay eggs and die, and they do this in numbers that are difficult to estimate because the usual population models assume a carrying capacity determined by available resources, and the midges of the Uplands seem uninterested in carrying capacities. They are simply everywhere, in clouds so fine that the birds hunt them by flying through and opening their mouths, and the people of Taram have stopped noticing them entirely.

## The Veshi

There are perhaps four thousand of them. No one has done a precise count, because counting them requires traversing the entire plateau on foot, which takes weeks, and by the time you finished, the ones you counted first would have moved. They range across the full four hundred kilometers of the ridge system and beyond, into the unmapped edges where the plateau slopes down into the cloud-sea and the geology becomes uncertain.

They are not deer. Visitors say they look like deer, and this is because visitors see them from a distance, in poor light, and their brains supply the nearest matching category. Up close, they are wrong in ways that take time to articulate.

Their legs are too long. Not elegantly long, not in the way that certain African antelopes achieve — functionally long, disproportionately long, as though the legs grew on a different schedule from the body and no one thought to coordinate. The femur is nearly twice the length of the equivalent bone in any comparable lowland ruminant, and the tibia and metatarsus are correspondingly extended, which gives them a gait that is not graceful but is efficient. They do not run. They do not need to. Their primary defense against the Tarrin is not speed but stamina and spacing — they spread out, they keep moving, they never stop moving for long, and the Tarrin cannot isolate one without first walking it to exhaustion, which takes days.

Their hooves are the most remarkable feature. Wide, almost circular, with a textured underside that is not keratin in the standard configuration. The surface is covered in fine lamellar ridges — like a fingerprint, if a fingerprint were designed by a geologist. When the hoof contacts wet basalt, the lamellae interlock with the vesicular texture of the stone at a microscopic level, and the grip is extraordinary. A Veshi can walk on a surface that a human would slide off in seconds. They do not slip. They have never been observed to slip. The research team in Taram has footage of a Veshi walking across a sheet of water covering an incline of thirty degrees, and the hooves find purchase as if the stone were dry. The material of the hoof is not entirely keratin. There is a protein in the lamellar matrix that has no known analog in any other ungulate, and it was either present in the lowland population before migration or it developed in the three generations since, and the researchers lean toward the latter, but they cannot prove it, because the lowland Veshi population — if it is the same species — has not been studied with the same attention.

They eat the biofilm. This is their entire diet. They do not graze in the sense of cropping vegetation — they scrape, their teeth flat and hard and angled forward like the blade of a putty knife, drawing across the stone in long strokes that remove the dark film down to bare basalt. The sound carries: a rhythmic, collective scraping, twenty or thirty animals working the same slope, and it is oddly harmonious, each animal finding a slightly different frequency, the combined effect a low grinding chord that you can hear from a kilometer away. They leave the stone behind them pale and clean, and the biofilm reclaims it in weeks.

They drink the seepage water directly from the rock. They lower their long faces to the shallow braids and the water sheets and they drink, and their mouths are adapted for this — wide, shallow, with a hard palate that filters the larger mineral particulates. They take in dissolved iron and calcium and silica, and their bones are denser than they should be for their size, which may be why they do not suffer from the stresses of their long legs on hard stone. Their ribs are heavy. Their skulls are heavy. They are, in a very literal sense, made of the plateau.

They breed slowly. One calf per female, every two years, in the late cold season when the rain is heaviest. The calves are born with their eyes open and their legs functional, and they stand within minutes and begin moving with the group within hours. They are small and dark and wet and they do not play. There is no play behavior in the Veshi. The calves do not gambol. They do not run. They walk, and they keep walking, and they grow as they walk, and within a year they are indistinguishable from the adults except by size. They live a long time — the oldest tagged individual in Taram's records is forty-one years old, which is the age of the plateau itself. She was among the first to arrive. She walked up from the lowlands when the basalt was still hot enough to steam, and she has been here ever since, walking, scraping, drinking, and she does not seem old. She does not seem young. She seems like something that has always been here, which is almost true.

Their eyes are pale. This is the detail that people remember. Not the legs, not the hooves, not the scraping — the eyes. Large, set wide, pale grey or pale amber, depending on the individual, and the expression in them is not intelligence in any way humans recognize. It is presence. They are present, fully, in whatever moment they occupy, and the moment they occupy is always the same: standing on wet rock, eating what grows on it, moving when the group moves. There is no anxiety in the eyes. There is no curiosity. There is a quality that the people of Taram, who have spent years watching them, describe as *patience*, though this is probably projection. The Veshi are not patient. They are simply not impatient. They are doing the only thing they know to do, and they will keep doing it until they cannot, and they do not think about this, because they do not think about anything, because thinking about things is not what Veshi are for.

But the eyes stay with you.

## The Tarrin

Four to six in a group. This is consistent. In twenty-three years of observation, no Tarrin group larger than seven or smaller than three has been recorded on the plateau. They are social — their hunting depends on coordination — but the social unit is rigid in a way that suggests it is not learned but encoded. Four to six. When a group falls below three, it dissolves. The remaining individuals join other groups or travel alone, and solitary Tarrin do not survive long on the plateau. They starve, or they wander into the cloud-sea and do not come back. The group is the organism. The individual Tarrin is a component.

They are dark-coated, nearly black, and the coat is not fur in the standard mammalian sense. It is a dense mat of keratin fibers so fine that water does not penetrate it — it sheets off, runs off, leaves the animal dry in a landscape where nothing else is dry. The coat is the same temperature as the air around it, which means the Tarrin do not show up on thermal imaging. They are thermally invisible. This is not an adaptation to predators — nothing predates the Tarrin. It is an adaptation to prey. The Veshi do not see well in the infrared, but they sense warmth at a distance, and the Tarrin's thermal neutrality means they can sit on a ridgeline a hundred meters from a grazing group and be, in every measurable way, indistinguishable from the stone.

They hunt by following. This is the part that visitors find disturbing — not the killing, which is quick and clean, but the following. A Tarrin group identifies a Veshi group — not a specific animal, not at first — and they begin to walk behind them. Not stalking. Walking. The same pace, the same direction, maintaining a distance of two to three hundred meters. They do not hide. They do not press. They walk, and they keep walking, and the Veshi know they are there, and the Veshi keep walking too, and this continues for hours or days, and the only thing that changes is the distance, very slowly, by meters, as the Tarrin adjust their pace to match the weakest member of the Veshi group.

The Veshi do not panic. They do not flee. They move, as they always move, and the group stays together, and the healthy animals stay ahead, and the weak — a calf, an old female, an animal with a stone bruise on its hoof — falls behind by degrees. It does not run. It simply cannot keep pace, and the distance between it and the group widens, and the Tarrin are in that distance, walking, and at some point the geometry becomes clear to everyone involved. The Tarrin close. The Veshi group continues without the fallen member. The kill happens on the open stone, and it is fast — the Tarrin are not cruel about this — and the water carries the blood away in thin threads that dilute to nothing within fifty meters, and the Tarrin eat, and the Veshi graze a kilometer away, and the birds circle, and the plateau does not notice.

They do not communicate in any way that human observers have been able to identify. No vocalizations. No visible signaling. The coordination is seamless — four animals spread in a loose arc, adjusting to terrain, maintaining spacing — and it happens without any detectable exchange of information. The hypothesis is subsonic or seismic. The Tarrin may communicate through the stone itself, through the low resonance that the plateau produces constantly, and if this is true, then their communication is indistinguishable from the background murmur of the tectonic activity below, which would mean they are using the plateau as a medium, which would mean the plateau is, in a sense, part of their social structure. This is speculation. It is also, somehow, obvious to anyone who has watched them for long.

They are not interested in people. This has been tested, accidentally and deliberately. A Tarrin group walked through the outer corridors of Taram's eastern node during a geothermal vent cycling that had temporarily warmed the stone floor. They passed through, single file, and exited through a transit tunnel, and they did not look at the residents who stood against the walls watching them pass. A research team placed scent markers from a Veshi carcass along a corridor and a Tarrin group followed the markers, found the carcass, ate, and left. They do not regard humans as prey, or as threats, or as anything at all. They regard humans as stone that moves.

## The Burrowers

They are everywhere, and you almost never see them.

Fist-sized. Furred — a dense, dry fur, unlike anything else on the plateau, which has adapted to wetness. The burrowers are dry by design. Their fur is coated in a lipid secretion that repels water so effectively that they can swim through the shallow water sheets without getting wet — the water beads off them like mercury off glass. They live in the cracks between basalt layers, in the gaps where the stone has not fully fused, in the spaces where the settlement's geothermal heat leaks upward through micro-fissures and creates warm pockets a few degrees above ambient.

Their burrows are perfect circles. Four centimeters across, always. The edges are smooth, almost machined, and the stone around the opening is polished by the passage of countless bodies over time. They dig with their teeth — the same adaptation as the Veshi, flat hard chisel-like incisors, but smaller, and their jaw musculature is disproportionate, massive for their size, which gives their heads a blocky, compressed look. They gnaw through basalt. Not quickly — a new burrow takes weeks — but the stone yields to them, and the tunnels they create are networks, not single holes. Each exit connects to a lattice of passages within the rock, and the lattice connects to other lattices, and the cumulative effect is that the burrowers have honeycombed significant portions of the plateau's upper crust with tunnels that are too small for anything else to enter.

They are fast. Not just quick — fast in a way that is difficult to track. They emerge from one hole, cross a meter of open ground, and disappear into another hole, and the elapsed time is less than a second. The birds try to catch them and fail. The Tarrin do not try. The Veshi step over their holes without noticing. They eat the biofilm and the microbial mats that colonize the warm interior walls of the settlement's corridors, and they eat the insects that breed in the shallow water, and they eat each other's feces, and they are, in terms of biomass, possibly the most significant animal on the plateau, though this is difficult to verify because you cannot count what you cannot see.

They make one sound. A chittering — high, fast, mechanical — that comes from deep within the burrow network and is audible through the stone if you press your ear to the ground. It is not communication. It is the sound of their teeth on stone, amplified by the rock's resonance, and it is constant, and it is the closest thing the plateau has to a heartbeat.

## The Birds

Three species, probably four. The taxonomy is unresolved because the birds do not cooperate with taxonomists.

The first species is the most numerous: small, dark, fast, with wings that are disproportionate to the body and a tail that is longer than it should be. They are not elegant fliers — they are efficient ones, banking and turning with a mechanical precision that suggests their flight is more calculation than instinct. They hunt the midge clouds on the wing, flying through the thickest concentrations with their mouths open, and their digestive system is a straight tube — in, through, out, the entire process taking minutes. They do not land on the ground if they can avoid it. The ground is wet and cold and offers nothing. They land on the stone ridges, on the rooflines of Taram's structures where the geometry gives them grip, and they sit there, hunched, dark, watching the air for the next cloud of insects.

The second species is larger, slower, and much less common. It has a broad wingspan and a heavy body and it does not hunt insects. It hunts the first species. It is the only predator of birds on the plateau, and it takes them in the air, stooping from above with a speed that seems impossible for its size. You see it rarely. You see the evidence of it — feathers on the wet stone, scattered, drifting in the shallow water. When you do see it, it is alone, always alone, and it does not call and does not rest long and is gone before you are certain you saw it.

The third species — if it is a separate species and not a variant of the first — is identical to the first in every way except that it is silent. The first species calls constantly in flight: short, sharp notes, a clipped frequency that echoes off the stone and fills the plateau with a sound like stones tapping together. The silent variant does not vocalize. It flies with the calling birds, hunts the same insects, lands on the same ridges, and makes no sound at all. It may be a mutation. It may be a different species that has adopted the first species' habits. It may be the same species and the silence is individual. No one knows. The people of Taram have noticed that the silent birds seem to roost closer to the settlement's warm vents, and there is a hypothesis forming — not yet published — that the silence is a metabolic trade-off, that the energy that would go to vocalization is being redirected to heat production, and that the silent birds are in the process of becoming something else. But this is speculation, and the plateau has a way of making speculation seem reasonable and then making it irrelevant.

## The People

Sixty thousand. Give or take. The census is imprecise because the settlement is spread across four hundred kilometers of ridge, and people move between nodes, and some people live in nodes that are technically uninhabited, and some people live in the spaces between nodes, in chambers they have forged for themselves using the induction equipment, without asking permission or registering with the settlement's planning systems. The people of Taram do not have a strong institutional culture. They have a strong material culture. The difference matters.

They came for the geology. This is the origin story, and it is not romantic, and the people here do not romanticize it. The plateau rose, and the geological community recognized that a continental shelf at this altitude with this rate of ongoing formation was unprecedented, and a research presence was established. The research presence became a settlement when it became clear that the plateau was not going to stop rising on any timeline that mattered to human planning, and that the geothermal energy available was sufficient to support long-term habitation, and that the induction-forging technology developed to study the basalt could be repurposed to build within it.

The first residents were scientists. The second generation were scientists and engineers. The third generation — the current adults, the ones who were born here and have never lived anywhere else — are something else. Not quite scientists. Not quite engineers. Not quite anything that has a name. They are people who grew up in a place that was still becoming, and their understanding of the world is shaped by that.

They build slowly. This is the thing that defines them, more than the technology, more than the settlement's strange architecture, more than the fact that they live inside rock on a plateau that is still rising. They build slowly. The induction-forging process can create a habitation chamber in days. They take months. They take years. They spend time in the space before they build in it, walking the fracture lines, feeling the stone, listening to the tectonic murmur, and they choose where to build based on criteria that are not written down anywhere and that the older residents, the ones who came from outside, cannot articulate. The younger residents — the ones born here — seem to feel it. They stand on a ridge and they know whether it will hold. They stand in a corridor and they know where the next chamber should go. They do not explain this. When asked, they say the stone tells them, and they do not mean this metaphorically, and they do not mean it literally, and they are not being evasive. They mean it in a way that requires you to have lived here for years to understand.

They speak quietly. The architecture carries sound, and they have adapted — not as a rule but as a habit that has become cultural. Their voices are low, measured, with a cadence that matches the plateau's rhythm. They do not shout. They do not argue loudly. Disagreements are conducted in murmurs that carry perfectly through the stone and that the participants can hear from their separate chambers, and this proximity has bred a kind of conversational care that is noticeable to visitors. People in Taram do not say things they do not mean, because the stone will carry it to everyone they live near, and they cannot take it back.

They watch the animals. This is not a hobby. It is not research, exactly, though the data they collect is meticulous and shared freely with the off-plateau research community. It is something closer to attention. They watch the Veshi graze, and they watch the Tarrin follow, and they watch the birds turn in the grey air, and they watch the water seep and the biofilm spread and the stone settle, and they do this with the kind of sustained, patient observation that most cultures reserve for meditation or prayer. They are not meditating. They are not praying. They are paying attention to the place they live in, and the place they live in is changing, slowly, by millimeters, and they want to understand the change, not to control it but to understand it, because understanding is what they are for.

They do not have many children. This is a source of concern among the settlement planners, who note that the infrastructure could support millions and houses sixty thousand, and the population is growing at a rate that will not reach capacity in any foreseeable future. The people of Taram have children when they want to have children, which is not often, and the children they have are raised in a community that is spread thin across a vast space, and the children grow up walking long corridors alone, or riding the magnetic carriages between nodes, or standing on the wet ridges watching the Veshi graze, and they grow up with a sense of space and solitude that is not loneliness but is something adjacent to it, something that does not have a word in the languages they speak.

The children born here have a relationship with the stone that the adult immigrants do not develop. They touch the walls as they walk. Not for guidance — the corridors are well-lit, easy to navigate. They touch the walls because the walls are warm and the stone is the thing they live inside, and the touching is a kind of greeting, or a kind of listening, or a kind of checking. They are checking that the stone is still there. They are checking that the stone is still becoming what it is becoming. They are, in some way that they would not articulate, checking that they are still part of it.

## The Microbial Mats

Inside the settlement, where the moisture collects on the interior walls, something has colonized the stone that did not come from outside. It is not the biofilm. It is not the water organism. It is a microbial mat — dense, multi-layered, occupying the thin film of water that condenses on the warm interior stone — and it is, as far as the microbiologists can determine, unique. It exists nowhere else. It was not introduced. It arose.

It is a community, not a species — a consortium of organisms living in mutual dependence, each providing something the others need, the whole system more capable than any component. The bottom layer fixes carbon from the air. The middle layer fixes nitrogen. The top layer produces the pale blue-green bioluminescence that lights the settlement's corridors. They are the light. The people of Taram live in light produced by something that was not planted and was not designed and was not invited and is, by any reasonable definition, alive.

The mats are spreading. Slowly, as everything here is slow, they are extending their range across the interior walls, following the moisture, following the warmth. They have reached the transit tunnels. They have reached the outer chambers. Within a generation, they will reach the open air, and then they will colonize the exterior surfaces of the settlement, and then — possibly, eventually, given enough time and enough rain — they will spread to the bare basalt beyond, and the biofilm on the plateau's surface and the microbial mats on the settlement's walls will meet, and they will merge, or they will compete, or they will ignore each other, and either way the plateau will have a new skin, and the skin will glow, and the people who live here will not need to manufacture light again.

This does not concern them. This does not excite them. They have been watching it happen for twenty years, and they have not intervened, and they will not intervene, because the microbial mats are doing what the microbial mats do, and the plateau is doing what the plateau does, and their role, as they understand it, is to be present for it, and to pay attention, and to build slowly, and to speak quietly, and to watch.

## What it is like to be here and alive

Everything that lives on the Uplands of Vesh-Taram is in the process of arriving. The stone arrived first, and is still arriving, pushing up from below. The water arrived second, and is still arriving, seeping from the clouds and the rock. The biofilm arrived, or was made, and is still spreading. The insects came up from the lowlands. The Veshi followed the insects, or followed the water, or followed nothing and arrived by accident. The Tarrin followed the Veshi. The burrowers followed the warmth. The birds followed the insects. The people followed the geology.

Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is established. The ecosystem is three generations old, which is nothing — which is less than nothing in ecological time — and it is held together by the thinness of its own connections. The biofilm feeds the Veshi. The Veshi feed the Tarrin. The insects feed the birds. The microbial mats light the people. The people watch everything. Remove one element and the whole structure shifts, reconfigures, finds a new shape. It is not fragile — it is too simple to be fragile, too spare to collapse. It is provisional. It is a draft. It is a first sketch on fresh stone, and the stone is still being drawn.

What lives here is the process of living here. That is the only honest answer. The Veshi are not residents — they are the act of grazing on new stone. The Tarrin are not residents — they are the act of following. The people are not residents — they are the act of building slowly inside a thing that is still building itself. The biofilm is the act of spreading. The water is the act of seeping. The stone is the act of rising.

And the plateau keeps rising, by millimeters, into a sky that doesn't seem to mind, and everything that lives on it rises too, and does not mind, and does not notice, and goes on.
