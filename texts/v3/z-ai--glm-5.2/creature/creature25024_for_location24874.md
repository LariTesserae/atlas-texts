# What Lives in the Black Basin

---

## The First Things

Begin with the things that are not quite things.

The bacterial mats. They come first because they were first, because they are always first, because they have been here longer than the trees or the lizards or the birds and they will be here after all of those are gone. They live in the shallow mineral water that sheets across the basins near the vents, in the runoff channels where the temperature drops from seventy degrees to forty, in that precise band where the water is still hot enough to be sterile to most life but not to them. They form visible sheets — yes, the description said visible, and it meant it. You can see them. Filamentous, pale yellow-green where the sulfur is concentrated, fading to a fainter orange where the iron oxide mixes in, darkening to a deep rust-red in the oldest colonies where layer upon layer has built up like sediment. They are thin. A few millimeters. But they are alive, and they are doing what life has done here since before the trees arrived: taking the raw materials of the earth — sulfur, carbon dioxide, hydrogen — and building them into cells. They are the foundation. Not in a metaphorical sense. In a literal, material, thermodynamic sense. Everything else here eats, directly or indirectly, what these mats produce.

They do not move in any way you can perceive. They do not respond to stimuli in any way you can test with human patience. They simply exist, and grow, and die, and are consumed by the organisms that live at their edges — the nematodes, the tardigrades, the mites that have specialized for life at temperatures that would cook their relatives in any other forest. These edge-organisms are almost unstudied. They have no common names. They exist in a band perhaps thirty centimeters wide around each bacterial mat, in the transition zone between the too-hot and the merely-warm, and they have been there, evolving in isolation, for as long as the vents have been active. Some of them may be new species. Some of them may be very old species. There is no way to know without sequencing their DNA, and no one has, because no one has been here, because the basin does not invite visitors and does not reward them.

The mats pulse with the vents. When a vent is active, the mats grow. When it seals and goes quiet, the mats die back, and the organisms at their edges migrate — slowly, over years — toward the next active vent, following the temperature gradient the way a plant follows light. They have done this many times. They are, in a sense, nomads of the geochemical world, and their migrations are measured in the same decades as the vents themselves.

## The Fungi

The fungi are what you see. They are the only thing you truly see, in a place where seeing is almost impossible.

The bioluminescent networks are not a single species. This is important to understand. The cold blue-white glow that traces the fallen logs and the root flares and the mineral-encrusted trunks comes from a community — a consortium, the mycologists would say — of fungal species that have converged on the same strategy. There are at least three genera involved. One is a basidiomycete, a classic wood-decay fungus, fruiting in shelf-like brackets that can reach thirty centimeters across on the oldest fallen logs. Its glow is steady, constant, a pale light that does not flicker or pulse. It is always on. It has always been on. It will be on until the fruiting body decays, and then new fruiting bodies will grow from the same mycelial network and they will be on too. The light is a byproduct of an oxidation reaction — luciferin, luciferase, the same basic chemistry as a firefly, evolved independently, as fungi so often do, finding their own way to the same solution.

The second species is more diffuse. It grows not in brackets but in threads — thin, branching filaments that spread across the surface of decomposing wood and leaf litter, forming a network that glows at its nodes, at the points where branches meet. It looks like a map. It looks like a diagram of a river system, or a nervous system, or a lightning strike frozen in the act of branching. Its light is fainter than the bracket fungus, bluer, and it does pulse — very slowly, on a cycle of perhaps two minutes, brightening and dimming as some internal rhythm or external trigger regulates the reaction. On a still night, with no other light, you can watch these pulses travel across a fallen log like a slow wave, like a thought moving through a mind.

The third is the strangest. It does not grow on wood. It grows in the soil, in the top two centimeters of the organic layer, and it is visible only when disturbed. When you walk, when a lizard's claw touches the ground, when a fruit falls from the canopy, the impact triggers a brief, bright flash — a bioluminescent signature that fades in two or three seconds. The function is unknown. It may attract insects that disperse spores. It may deter predators. It may serve no purpose at all, a relic of an adaptation that has since been co-opted by other pressures. But the effect is this: the forest floor, in absolute darkness, responds to your presence with light. Each footstep produces a faint, momentary glow in the soil around your boot. A lizard walking across the ground leaves a trail of dimming light. A falling leaf produces a single point of brightness at the moment of impact. The basin is aware of movement, not through consciousness but through chemistry, and it reports what it senses in the only language it has.

Together, these three systems produce enough light to navigate by, barely. Enough to see the large shapes — the fallen logs, the buttress roots, the monitor lizard on its branch. Not enough to see detail. Not enough to read or to recognize a face. The light is information, but it is minimal information, and it serves the forest, not the visitor. The fungi are not here for you. They are here for themselves, and their light is a side effect of their existence, and their existence is dedicated to the single task of breaking down what is dead and returning it to the system.

And they are extraordinary at this task. The mineral-impregnated wood near the vents resists decomposition for decades, but not forever. The fungal species that colonize these logs are different from the ones in the broader forest — larger, slower, more chemically aggressive. The great shelf brackets on the silica-saturated trunks are the fruiting bodies of a mycelial network that can penetrate wood which would repel ordinary fungal enzymes. They do this through a partnership that is not fully understood: a symbiotic bacterium lives within the fungal hyphae, and this bacterium produces an acid — a silicate solvent — that dissolves the mineral matrix binding the wood fibers, allowing the fungus to access the cellulose beneath. It is a slow process. A fallen mineral-tree may take forty years to decompose completely. But it does decompose, eventually, and the minerals return to the soil, and the carbon returns to the air, and the cycle closes.

The mycelial networks beneath the forest floor are vast. They connect the trees — all of them — in a web of nutrient exchange that has been growing for as long as the forest has been here. The trees are not individuals in any meaningful ecological sense. They are nodes in a network, drawing water and minerals from the fungal web, supplying sugars produced in the dim canopy above, receiving and giving in a system so balanced that no single species dominates. When a tree falls, the network reroutes. When a new tree grows, the network extends to meet it. The fungi are the nervous system of the forest, and they have been conducting signals — chemical signals, nutrient signals, warning signals when a pathogen is detected — for ten thousand years.

## The Ants

There are at least fourteen species of ant in the basin, and they are the most visibly active animals in the ecosystem. You notice them first because they are the things that move.

The dominant species is a large, dark-bodied ant — a relative of the army ants, but not a true army ant, having diverged from that lineage perhaps two million years ago and evolved for a life in permanent darkness. They do not raid. They do not swarm. They have no need; the prey is always here. They form permanent colonies of perhaps fifty thousand individuals, nesting in the hollow butts of fallen trees, and they patrol the forest floor in columns that follow the same routes for years, even decades. The routes are visible as worn paths through the leaf litter, bare lines on the dark soil, etched by the passage of millions of feet over thousands of days. These paths connect the colony to its foraging grounds and to the other colonies of the same species in a network that spans the basin. The ants are, in a sense, the basin's circulatory system — moving material, processing biomass, connecting the pieces.

They are farmers. This is the thing that is hardest to see and most important to understand. They cultivate fungus in their nests — a specialized fungus, found only in association with these ants, growing nowhere else in the basin. The ants bring leaf litter, fruit fragments, insect remains to the nest, process them into a substrate, inoculate them with the fungus, and harvest the fungal bodies for food. The fungus, in turn, has evolved to depend entirely on the ants; it does not produce fruiting bodies, does not disperse spores, does not exist outside the controlled environment of the nest. It is a domestic organism, the way wheat is a domestic organism, and it has been domesticated for roughly as long as humans have domesticated wheat.

There is another species, smaller, lighter-colored, that lives exclusively in the canopy. It never descends to the ground. It has no need to — the canopy provides everything. It tends scale insects on the branches of the emergent trees, harvesting honeydew, and it has developed a relationship with a particular epiphyte that grows only in the nests these ants build in branch crotches. The ant nests provide the substrate and nutrients the plant needs; the plant's roots stabilize the nest and absorb water that the ants would otherwise lose. The plant produces small, fleshy fruits that the ants eat. Neither can survive without the other. They are a single organism, distributed across two kingdoms.

A third species lives in the mineral zones, near the vents, and this one is remarkable. It tolerates temperatures that would kill any other ant — foraging on the edges of the bacterial mats, feeding on the microorganisms that grow there, retreating to cooler nests when the vent activity intensifies. Its exoskeleton is thicker than its relatives', almost calcified, and it has a pale, almost translucent appearance that makes it difficult to see against the mineral crusts of its habitat. It is the only animal that lives permanently in the vent zones. Everything else visits. These ants stay.

## The Monitor Lizards

There are two species.

The first is the one you see — the one the description mentioned, the dark-scaled, meter-and-a-half animal that climbs to low branches and watches with wet-stone eyes. It is the basin's large predator, or what passes for large in a place where the absence of sunlight limits body size. A meter and a half is big here. It is as big as anything that lives permanently on the ground.

This species — let us call it the basin monitor, because it has no common name and may not have a scientific name either — is built for patience. Its metabolism is slow even for a reptile, running at the low end of the range that its body temperature would suggest. It does not need to bask. The heat of the basin keeps its body at a constant thirty-six to thirty-eight degrees, and it is active at all hours, moving through the understory with a deliberate, almost mechanical gait, climbing slowly, descending slowly, eating whatever it can catch. It is an opportunist. Rodents are its primary prey, but it takes frogs, large insects, smaller lizards, carrion. It has been observed eating fruit, though it is unclear whether this is for nutrition or for the insects the fruit attracts.

Its eyes are remarkable. They are large, forward-facing, and adapted for extremely low light. The tapetum lucidum — the reflective layer behind the retina — is unusually developed, and the eyes of a basin monitor, caught in the faint glow of the fungal light or in the beam of a flashlight, reflect a vivid green that is visible from twenty meters. This is the reflection that makes the forest floor seem, at night, to be covered in eyes. It is mostly these lizards, watching, always watching, from low branches and root buttresses and the sheltered sides of fallen logs.

They are solitary. They do not form social groups, do not defend territories in any observable way, and tolerate each other's presence with indifference. When two meet, they may pause, regard each other with those enormous eyes, and then continue on their separate paths. They do not fight. They do not compete overtly. The basin is large enough, the prey abundant enough, the predators slow enough, that there is no pressure for conflict. They live for perhaps thirty years — no one knows for certain, because no one has tracked an individual from birth to death — and in that time they may never move more than a kilometer from where they hatched.

The second species is smaller — sixty centimeters, rarely more — and lives in the trees. It rarely descends to the ground. It is lighter in color, a mottled brown and gray that blends with bark, and it is faster, more agile, hunting insects and tree frogs in the canopy. It is the one you hear at night, the rustling in the branches above, the creak of a bough bending under weight. They are more numerous than their larger relatives, perhaps ten individuals per hectare, and they are the primary predator of the canopy insect community. They lay eggs in the epiphyte nests of the canopy ants, and the ants do not seem to mind, or perhaps do not notice, and the hatchlings emerge into a world of dim light and abundant food and immediately begin climbing, because the ground is not their world and they know this without being taught.

## The Birds

There are nine species. Maybe eleven. It is hard to count things you cannot see.

The canopy dwellers are the most numerous. They are small — sparrow-sized, finch-shaped, dark-feathered — and they live in the spaces between the canopy layers, where the light is dim but present, where there is enough photosynthesis to support the insects and fruits they feed on. They have large eyes. All the birds here have large eyes. The largest eyes, proportionally, belong to a species that the description called the wooden-ratchet bird, and it is the one you hear most often: a rapid, mechanical clicking that repeats every twenty seconds, day and night, with a precision that suggests it is territorial rather than communicative. The bird itself is almost invisible — mottled black and brown, sitting motionless on a branch in the upper canopy, its call the only evidence of its presence. It does not fly during the hours when you might see it. It moves in short hops between branches, and its flight, when it does fly, is a brief, heavy glide from one perch to another. It is not built for distance. It is built for staying where it is.

There is an owl. Of course there is an owl. It is not the owl you are imagining. It is not large. It is not majestic. It is the size of a human fist, dark-feathered, with eyes that take up most of its skull, and it hunts bats. It hunts bats in absolute darkness, by sound alone, and its hearing is so acute that it can detect the echolocation calls of its prey — those high, thin sounds at the edge of human perception — and use them to triangulate position. It is the only predator of the bats in the basin. It sits on a low branch — sometimes three meters above the ground, sometimes ten — and it waits, and it listens, and when a bat passes within range it drops, silent, and the capture is over before you would know it had begun, if you were there, which you are not, because you cannot see and you cannot hear it and by the time you realize something has happened the owl is back on its branch, eating.

The deep hooting call comes from a larger bird — a ground-dwelling species, pheasant-sized, that forages in the leaf litter for insects and fallen fruit. It is the bird you are most likely to see, because it is the bird that is most likely to be on the ground when you are on the ground, and because the bioluminescent fungi provide just enough light to make out its shape: dark, heavy-bodied, moving with a slow, deliberate walk through the undergrowth. It is flightless. Not vestigially, not as a relic of past evolution — it has never needed to fly, in this place where there are no aerial predators large enough to threaten it and no open spaces to cross. Its wings are small and rounded and used for balance when it climbs over fallen logs. It roosts at night on low branches, and it calls — that deep, resonant hoot — to establish its presence and its claim to a foraging area. The call carries far in the still air. It is the sound of the basin at night, more than any other single sound.

There are no water birds. There are no migratory birds. Every species in the basin lives here and only here, and has lived here, in this darkness, for long enough that the darkness has become the only condition it knows. The birds do not leave because they cannot leave — not because they are physically incapable of flight (though some are), but because the world outside the basin is a world of light and wind and temperature variation, and their physiology, their behavior, their entire evolutionary history has been shaped by the absence of all those things. They would not survive. They are not built for the ordinary world. They are built for this one.

## The Rodents

There are three species of rodent, and they are the foundation of the vertebrate food web, the things that connect the plants and the insects to the lizards and the owls.

The largest is a rat — and it is a rat, genus *Rattus* or something close, a representative of a lineage that has been spectacularly successful across the tropics. This particular species is dark-furred, heavily built, perhaps four hundred grams, with a thick tail and small eyes — small for a rat, which is to say still larger than a human's, but small compared to its relatives in brighter habitats. It does not need large eyes. It navigates by touch, by smell, by the long, sensitive whiskers that sweep the ground ahead of it as it walks. It is a generalist. It eats seeds, fruit, insects, fungi — particularly the fruiting bodies of the bracket fungi, which it can locate by their glow, and which provide both nutrients and water in a place where standing water is scarce. It is the primary prey of the basin monitor. It is aware of this. When a monitor approaches, the rats freeze, pressing their bodies against the ground, and in the dim fungal light they become nearly invisible — dark fur on dark soil, the outline broken by the leaf litter. Sometimes this works. Sometimes it does not. The monitors are patient, and they hunt by smell as much as by sight, and a rat that freezes is a rat that is still there, and patience has its rewards.

The second species is smaller, more specialized. It lives in the trees — not on the ground, never on the ground — and it feeds on the epiphytes, on the orchids and bromeliads that cluster in the branch crotches. It is agile, climbing with a speed and confidence that suggests it has been doing this all its life, which it has. It has a prehensile tail. It is the color of bark. It is almost never seen, not because it is rare but because it is perfectly adapted to the world it lives in, and the world it lives in is a world of bark and branches and darkness, and a bark-colored animal on bark in darkness is not something that is seen.

The third species lives in the mineral zones. It is the smallest — fifty grams, perhaps — and it is pale, almost colorless, its fur a light gray that is nearly white. It is not albino; it has pigment in its eyes, which are small and dark. But its fur has lost the dark coloration of its relatives, and this may be because the mineral zones are lighter — the clearings around the vents admit a faint light from above, and the mineral crusts are pale, and a dark animal would be more visible against them than a light one. Or it may be because the mineral diet — these rats feed heavily on the bacterial mats and the organisms that live in them — incorporates compounds that interfere with melanin production. No one knows. The rats do not know. They are simply pale, and they live in the hottest, most mineral-saturated patches of the basin, and they tolerate conditions that would kill their relatives instantly.

## The Bats

They are the last to leave their roosts and the first to return, though "roost" and "leave" are relative terms in a world without dawn or dusk. They navigate by temperature. The air above the canopy cools slightly at night — a degree, perhaps two — as the heat radiates upward into the atmosphere, and this infinitesimal change triggers their emergence from the hollow trees where they roost in groups of fifty to two hundred. They are small. All the bats here are small. The largest weighs perhaps fifteen grams. The smallest, eight. They are insectivores, all of them, hunting the flying insects that emerge from the leaf litter at night — moths, flies, beetles — and they hunt by echolocation, building a world of sound in the darkness.

Their calls are at the upper edge of human hearing, and most people who entered the basin would not register them as sound at all. They would feel them — a pressure, a texture in the air, a faint high tension that is not quite a whistle and not quite a hum. To the bats, these calls are the world. Each call returns a complex of echoes that describe the position, size, texture, and movement of every surface and every object within range, and the bats process this information at a speed and density that no human brain could match. They fly through the understory — not the canopy, but the spaces between the trunks, the gaps between the buttress roots, the channels through the three-dimensional lattice of the forest floor — and they do not collide, ever, because their acoustic world is more detailed than any visual world could be in this darkness.

They are hunted by the owl. They are hunted by the tree-dwelling monitors. They are hunted by the large spiders that build webs between the buttress roots — but the spiders are a story of their own.

## The Spiders

There is a spider that builds webs large enough to span the gap between two buttress roots, and these webs are strong enough to hold a bat.

It is an orb-weaver, or something like one, and its web is not the delicate, symmetrical wheel of a garden spider. It is a chaotic, three-dimensional structure — a tangle of threads with a tighter core, a killing zone, where the spider waits. The silk is dark. It is not invisible in the fungal light; it is barely visible, a dim tracery that you might mistake for mycelial threads if you were not paying attention. The bats do not detect it with their echolocation — the threads are too thin, too widely spaced, and the spider has evolved to produce silk that absorbs sound rather than reflecting it. The bats fly into the webs at night, and the spider descends, and that is that.

There are smaller spiders. Many smaller spiders. Jumping spiders that hunt on the trunks of trees, their large forward-facing eyes giving them binocular vision in the fungal light. Wolf spiders that patrol the leaf litter. Crab spiders that sit on flowers — for there are flowers, even here, pale and small, opening in the darkness, pollinated by the insects that navigate by smell. There are spiders that mimic ants, living within the ant columns, protected by their disguise. There are spiders that live nowhere else, that have no name, that build webs in the mineral crusts near the vents and catch the insects drawn to the warmth and the sulfur.

The eyes that reflect back at you when you shine a light across the forest floor — those are mostly spiders. Hundreds of them. Each one in its own small territory, each one going about its business, each one a part of the system that is so dense and so interconnected that removing any single species would produce cascading effects that no one could predict.

## The Amphibians

The frogs are the voice of the basin. Not the only voice — the birds call, the insects stridulate, the lizards hiss when threatened — but the frogs are the constant, the background, the texture that defines the acoustic space.

There are at least six species. The largest is a toad — heavy-bodied, warty-skinned, the color of the dark soil it lives on, and it is everywhere. It does not need standing water to breed. The basin's humidity is so consistently high that it can lay its eggs in the moist leaf litter, in the accumulated debris in tree hollows, in the pockets of water that collect in the folded leaves of bromeliads and heliconias. The tadpoles develop in these small pools — sometimes no more than a few tablespoons of water — and metamorphose into miniature adults in a matter of weeks, because the small pools are not permanent, because the water evaporates or is consumed or drains away, and the tadpole that does not metamorphose in time dies. The pressure is constant. The response is rapid. The toads breed continuously, year-round, in the absence of seasons, and the forest floor is populated with toadlets the size of a fingernail, hiding under leaves, feeding on the smallest insects, being eaten in turn by the spiders and the centipedes and the small lizards.

The tree frogs are more specialized. They live in the canopy, in the epiphyte tanks, in the small worlds of water and debris that collect in the crotches of branches. They are small, most under three centimeters, and they are the source of the high, pure calls that pierce the low hum of the insect chorus at night. Each species has a distinct call — a trill, a chirp, a single sustained note — and the males call from their territories in the canopy, defining their space by sound, competing for females by sound, because in the darkness sound is the only medium that carries. The females choose among them by the quality of the call, which is to say by the health and energy of the caller, which is to say by his ability to survive and thrive in this hard, hot, dark world.

There is one species that is silent. It does not call. It lives near the mineral vents, in the warm runoff channels, and it is the most heat-tolerant amphibian in the basin — possibly the most heat-tolerant amphibian anywhere. It tolerates water temperatures of forty-two degrees. Its skin is pale, almost translucent, and you can see the veins beneath, the dark network of blood vessels carrying the warm blood through the body. It feeds on the organisms in the bacterial mats — the nematodes, the mites, the insects that live at the edges — and it is preyed upon by almost nothing, because the water it lives in is too hot for most predators. The mineral-zone rats take them occasionally. The vent-dwelling ants. But mostly they live and die in the warm water, unbothered, their lives regulated by the temperature of the earth beneath them.

## The Things You Cannot Name

There are organisms here that do not fit into the categories above.

There is a centipede that grows to twenty centimeters, dark red, with legs that seem too numerous to count, and it is a fast predator, the fastest animal in the basin, chasing down cockroaches and crickets across the forest floor with a speed that seems impossible in the thick, humid air. It is the one thing here that moves with urgency. Everything else is patient, deliberate, slow. The centipede is not. It is hunger made into a body, and it does not stop.

There are cockroaches — not the household pests, but large, dark, wingless species that live in the leaf litter and perform the essential function of breaking down dead plant material. They are the cleanup crew. They are the sanitation department. They are more important than they look, and they look like cockroaches, which is to say they look like something you would step on without thinking, and you should not step on them, because they are doing work that the ecosystem needs.

There are land crabs. This is unexpected, perhaps — this is a lowland, not a coast — but the basin's geology includes ancient marine deposits, and somewhere in the distant past, before the forest closed over, before the canopy sealed, there was water here that connected to the sea, and the crabs came, and they stayed, and they adapted. They are dark-shelled, thick-legged, and they live in burrows in the soft soil, emerging at night to feed on fallen fruit and dead animals and whatever else the forest provides. Their burrows are the only holes in the ground you will find here — the soil is too wet for most burrowing animals, but the crabs have found a way, reinforcing the walls with a secretion that hardens in the warm air, creating tubes that persist for years after the crab that made them has died.

There are land leeches. They are not pleasant to think about, but they are here, and they are part of the system. They wait in the leaf litter, sensing the approach of warm-blooded animals by the vibration of footsteps and the change in temperature, and they attach themselves to anything that passes, and they feed, and they drop off, and the blood they take is returned to the soil in their waste, and the iron in it contributes to the dark color of the soil, and the cycle continues. They are not a plague. They are a mechanism. The basin has room for mechanisms of all kinds.

There are nematodes — millions of them, billions, in every handful of soil, in every fragment of decomposing wood, in every bacterial mat. They are the smallest animals and the most numerous, and they are the bridge between the microbial world and the macroscopic world, eating bacteria and being eaten by mites, which are eaten by insects, which are eaten by frogs and lizards and birds. Without the nematodes, the system would collapse in weeks. With them, it has persisted for millennia.

## The Trees

They live here too. They are not backdrop. They are not scenery. They are inhabitants, and they have been here longer than any animal, and they will be here after the animals are gone.

The emergent species — the tall, thin trees that rise twenty and thirty meters to the light above the canopy — are the oldest individuals in the basin. Some of them may be five hundred years old. Their growth is slow, because the light is dim, because the competition is intense, because the resources are spread thin across the dense community of organisms that share this space. But they grow, and they persist, and when one falls — which happens rarely, perhaps one every decade across the entire basin — the gap it creates in the canopy is filled within months by the surrounding trees, which sense the change in light and redirect their growth upward, toward the opening. The forest is not passive. It responds. It is responsive. It is alive in a way that the word "alive" does not adequately capture, because it is not an individual aliveness but a collective one, a community aliveness, an aliveness that belongs to the system and not to any of its parts.

The mineral trees near the vents are the exception. They grow more slowly, because the mineral soil is harder, because the chemical environment is harsh, because the heat and the sulfur and the silica stress them in ways that the other trees do not experience. But they are protected, in a sense, by the very things that stress them: the mineral impregnation of their wood makes it harder, more resistant to decay, more difficult for fungi to colonize, and the trees that live near the vents may live longer than any other trees in the basin. A mineral tree near a long-active vent might be a thousand years old. Its trunk is a geological record — layers of silica and iron oxide and organic material, deposited over centuries, telling the story of the vent's activity in the same way that ice cores tell the story of the atmosphere. If someone were to cut one of these trees and polish its cross-section, they would see the pulse of the earth in its rings.

No one will do this. The trees are not for cutting. They are for being, and they are for being in this place, and they will continue to be here until the vent beneath them goes quiet and the ground cools and the minerals stop flowing and the forest migrates away and they are left standing alone in a clearing that is no longer hot, no longer mineral, no longer the place they were shaped by, and they die slowly, over decades, their fossilized wood resisting even the absence of the conditions that made it.

## The Insects

They are everywhere. They are the connective tissue of the ecosystem. Without them, nothing else functions.

The cockroaches and the crickets and the beetles break down the plant material. The ants farm the fungi and process the biomass. The moths and the flies pollinate the flowers. The termites — and there are termites here, vast colonies of them, building nests in the wood of fallen trees, digesting cellulose with the help of symbiotic protozoa in their guts — the termites are the primary decomposers of the dead wood, working alongside the fungi, each doing what the other cannot. The termites break the wood into fragments. The fungi break the fragments into molecules. The bacteria break the molecules into atoms. The atoms return to the soil, to the water, to the air, and the cycle continues.

There are insects that have evolved to live in the fungal light. Their eyes are adapted for the specific wavelength of the bioluminescence — the cold blue-white, around 480 nanometers — and they can see in a world where no other insect can. They are mostly beetles. Small, dark, ground-dwelling, with large compound eyes that are tuned to a narrow band of the spectrum, and they navigate the forest floor by the glow of the fungi the way a sailor navigates by the stars. They are the only insects that can see in the basin's darkness. Everything else navigates by touch, by smell, by sound. These beetles navigate by the light that the fungi produce as a byproduct of their existence, and in doing so they have become part of a system that neither the fungi nor the beetles designed — a system that emerged from the intersection of two unrelated organisms, each doing what it does, each benefiting from the other's presence without intending to.

## What It Means to Live Here

To live in the Black Basin is to live without seasons. Without days. Without the rhythms that organize life in every other ecosystem on earth. There is no time of plenty and no time of scarcity. There is no breeding season. There is no migration. There is no dormancy. Everything reproduces continuously, grows continuously, dies continuously, and the rates of all of these are so perfectly balanced that the system as a whole appears static, appears unchanging, appears to be a thing that has always been exactly what it is.

But this is an illusion of scale. On the timescale of a human visit, nothing changes. On the timescale of a human lifetime, almost nothing changes. On the timescale of a millennium, everything changes — the vents shift, the forest migrates, the species evolve, the boundaries of the basin expand and contract with the geothermal pulse beneath. The basin is not eternal. It is not permanent. It is a process, a very slow process, and the things that live in it are participants in that process, not inhabitants of a fixed place.

They are all, in their own ways, adapted to the same three conditions: darkness, heat, and moisture. These three pressures have shaped every organism here — the large eyes of the lizards and birds, the pale skin of the vent-dwelling rodents and frogs, the bioluminescence of the fungi, the sound-based navigation of the bats, the chemical communication of the ants, the heat tolerance of the bacterial mats. Every trait is a response. Every body is a solution to the problem of existing in a place that is, for most life, uninhabitable.

They do not know they are remarkable. They do not know they are living in what a human would consider extreme conditions. They do not know that the world outside their basin is different — brighter, cooler, drier, more variable, more seasonal, more unpredictable. They do not know that they are isolated, that they are endemic, that many of them may be the last representatives of lineages that once ranged more widely and have been pushed back to this final refuge by the changes in the earth's climate over the last ten thousand years. They do not know any of this.
