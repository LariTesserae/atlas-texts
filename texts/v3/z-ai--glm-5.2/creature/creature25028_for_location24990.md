# The Inhabitants

The towers are alive, and they are the closest thing this place has to inhabitants in the sense that implies belonging — not merely residing but *inhabiting*, the way a mountain inhabits a range. They are the architecture and the population at once. Everything else here lives in relation to them, and nothing lives independently of them, and nothing lives against them. There is no opposition in this ecosystem. There is only the tower and what the tower makes possible.

But let us be careful, because "ecosystem" is the wrong word. An ecosystem implies process, exchange, the movement of energy through linked lives in a way that implies change over time — populations rising and falling, species adapting, the slow redescription of relationships that constitutes evolution. That is not what is happening here. What is happening here is more like a clock that has been assembled and wound and set on a shelf, except no one assembled it and no one wound it and the shelf is the size of an ocean. The towers are the mechanism. The fauna are the dust that has organized itself around the mechanism's rhythm. The root-network is the shelf itself, or close to it — the substrate that holds the mechanism in position, and that also, through some process no one will ever fully describe, feeds the mechanism and carries the mechanism's products and is itself a product of the mechanism, so that the whole thing is a loop with no beginning and no end, a circuit that was closed before it was a circuit, that has simply always been closed.

This matters for understanding the inhabitants, because it means they are not *surviving*. Survival implies contingency — the possibility of not surviving, the constant pressure of events against existence, the statistical dance of birth and death that shapes a population. There is no statistical dance here. The inhabitants are not surviving. They are *being*. The distinction is important.

## The Towers

The towers are the largest and most complex inhabitants, and there is no clear point at which "inhabitant" becomes incorrect and "structure" becomes more accurate, because each tower is both. There are hundreds of them — perhaps thousands, because the plateau is large and the light attenuates at a distance that prevents you from seeing them all, even in water this clear. You can see perhaps ten at any given elevation, their crowns glowing at slightly different intensities, their trunks varying in darkness according to their mineral uptake, but the sound tells you there are more. The sound tells you there are always more. The chord would not be complete if there were not more. The lowest harmonics, the ones you feel rather than hear, are produced by the towers at the greatest distance, and their inclusion in the chord is non-negotiable — remove one and you would notice. The chord has been balanced for longer than anything can measure, and every tower is necessary to the balance, and every tower knows this, or at least every tower is this, functioning in a way that makes the question of whether it "knows" the wrong question entirely.

The towers vary in size, though not by much. The smallest are perhaps two hundred meters tall and forty meters across at the base. The largest — and you can never be sure which is truly the largest, because you can only compare the ones you can see, and the ones at the farthest edge of visibility might be larger still, their apparent size confounded by the flat light and the limits of vision — the largest approach four hundred meters. Their proportions are consistent. A ratio of approximately five to one, height to base diameter, which is the ratio that allows the pumping mechanism to function at maximum efficiency given the viscosity of the water and the mineral content of the intake flow. This is not a coincidence. This is not a design. This is the shape that works, and it has been the shape that works for so long that no tower has been any other shape, and no tower will be any other shape, because the shape is settled, the way a river settles into its channel and stays.

Each tower is an individual, though "individual" is a word that implies boundaries, and the towers resist the concept. Their root-networks are fused at the edges — you can see this if you follow a root-line across the stone floor far enough, which takes hours and which no one has done completely, because the network extends across the entire plateau and connects every tower to every other tower in a web so dense that the basalt beneath it might as well be the back of a living thing. The roots exchange material. What enters one tower's roots at its base may, by the time it has been processed and pumped and exhausted and reabsorbed by another tower's roots a kilometer away, have been transformed — filtered, refined, stripped of one compound and enriched with another — and the tower that receives it will not know where it came from, or will not care, or the distinction between those two is not meaningful here. The network is a digestive system, a circulatory system, a nervous system, all at once, and the towers are organs in it, and they are also independent organisms, and they are also not organisms at all but processes, and the taxonomy does not matter because the taxonomy has never mattered here. What matters is the flow. The flow has been running for longer than the towers have been towers, and the towers grew to accommodate the flow, or the flow shaped itself around the towers, or both happened at once, and now they are the same thing.

The crowns are where each tower's individuality becomes most apparent — or at least most visible. Each crown is a membrane, a single sheet of translucent tissue stretched across the top of the column like a sail or a drumhead, and each one glows. The glow is not bioluminescence in the standard sense — there are no pigments reacting, no luciferin-luciferase chemistry. The glow is a conversion. The towers take the flat ambient light that filters down from the surface, the pale even illumination that has no source you can point to and no variation you can measure, and they convert it. Into what, exactly, is unclear — some form of stored energy that pools in the dark nodes along the trunk, dense deposits of material that pulse faintly when you touch them, warm to the hand, firm to the point of being rigid. The color of the glow varies from tower to tower. Some are pale green. Some are almost white. A few — a very few, and you notice them because of their rarity — glow with a faint amber tint, as though their mineral intake or their particular chemistry or their position in the network has given them a different signature. The amber ones are no larger and no smaller than the others. They are not more active or less active. They are simply different, in the way that one instrument in an orchestra might be tuned slightly differently and the orchestra accommodates it, incorporates it into the chord, and the chord is more complete for the inclusion.

The tissue of the towers is warm. This is worth noting, because it is the only warmth in the plateau, and it creates the only thermal gradients in the water, and the fauna organize themselves around those gradients the way the fauna of a reef organize themselves around currents. The warmth is constant — not hot, not tepid, but the steady warmth of a machine housing, thirty-two or thirty-three degrees, warmer than the surrounding water by enough to create the shimmering outflow at the vents. The warmth comes from the processing. The towers are doing work, and work produces heat, and the heat has to go somewhere, and it goes into the water, and the water carries it away, and the towers process more water to do more work to produce more heat, and the loop is closed again. The warmth is a byproduct. It is also, for the smaller inhabitants, the only weather there is.

If you press your hand against the trunk of a tower — and you can, the tissue is firm enough to support this, slightly yielding, the texture of dense cartilage — you can feel the peristalsis. It is a slow wave, traveling upward at a rate that you can time if you are patient: approximately one contraction every eleven seconds, which means the full column, three hundred meters, completes a cycle every — you can calculate it, but the calculation does not matter. What matters is the rhythm, which is the rhythm of the tower, which is the rhythm of the place, which is the rhythm of everything, because after long enough in the plateau you begin to move at the pace of the towers. Your swimming slows. Your breathing finds the eleven-second pulse. Your heartbeat, which has no reason to synchronize, drifts toward it anyway, the way a pendulum clock on a wall will eventually synchronize with the vibrations of the building. The towers set the tempo of the place, and everything that lives here lives at that tempo, or has forgotten that it ever had a different one.

## The Crawlers

The crawlers are the most numerous inhabitants, and they are the least dramatic, and they are perhaps the most essential, in the way that the least dramatic things are often the most essential.

They live on the root-lines. This is their entire world — the dark tendrils that spread across the basalt floor, connecting tower to tower, thick as your wrist near the bases and thinning to threads at the edges where they meet and fuse with the roots of other towers. The crawlers are flat. Their bodies are oval, the size and shape of a human hand, armored with plates of some chitinous material that is the same dark green as the root-lines, so that when they are still — and they are often still, for long stretches, gripping the root with legs you cannot easily count, twelve or sixteen or perhaps more, tucked beneath the armored carapace — they are nearly invisible. You see them when they move, which is a gliding, leg-driven locomotion that has the quality of something mechanical, not fluid, each leg planting and releasing in sequence, the body advancing by a body-length every few seconds. They are slow. They are patient. They are not in a hurry, because the root-line is not going anywhere, and the thing they eat is not going anywhere, and they are not going anywhere, and hurry is a concept that does not exist on the plateau.

They feed on seepage. The root-lines, in addition to whatever else they do — carrying nutrients, chemical signals, energy between the towers — exude something. A residue. A thin film of organic material that coats the root's surface, slightly sticky, slightly warm, the color of dark honey. You can see it if you look closely: a sheen on the tendrils, a gloss that the crawlers move through, and as they move, the seepage adheres to their undersides, and they process it — not through a mouth, because they do not appear to have a mouth, but through the undersides of their plates, which are textured with fine hairs or cilia that absorb the seepage the way a cloth absorbs water. They are eating through their skin. They are filtering the root-line's waste through their bodies and extracting whatever it contains that they need, and what they need is small — trace compounds, minerals that the roots carry in excess, byproducts of the towers' processing that the roots excrete as a matter of course. The crawlers are cleaning the roots. They are maintaining the network. Whether the network needs maintaining — whether the roots would function the same without the crawlers, whether the seepage would accumulate and clog the transport pathways — is a question no one can answer, because the crawlers have been here as long as the roots have been here, and removing them to test the hypothesis would require an act of intervention that the plateau does not invite.

They do not have eyes. They do not have any sensory organs you can identify. They navigate by touch, by chemical gradient, by the texture of the root-line beneath them, which must be rich with information for an organism equipped to read it — the seepage varies in composition along the root's length, denser near the towers where the flow is stronger, thinner at the junction points where roots meet and the material is exchanged, and the crawlers move between these zones with a precision that implies they can sense the difference. They do not explore. They do not wander onto the bare stone between the roots. The stone is not their world. The roots are their world, and the roots are a closed system, and the crawlers live inside that closed system like mites in a carpet, like bacteria in an intestine, and they are as numerous as that comparison implies. Along a single kilometer of root-line, there are thousands of them, tens of thousands, a continuous crust of armored bodies moving slowly along the dark tendrils, cleaning, eating, cleaning, moving. They never pile up. They never crowd. Their population has reached whatever number the root-lines can support, and it stays at that number, and it has stayed at that number for longer than the towers have been towers, and the crawlers do not know this and do not need to know this and are not improved by knowing it.

They do not reproduce in any way you can observe. There are no young crawlers, or if there are, they are the same size as the adults, which means you cannot tell them apart, which means the distinction between young and adult is not a distinction this species makes. They may reproduce by budding, by fragmentation, by some process that does not involve visible courtship or eggs or larval stages. They may not reproduce at all. They may simply *be*, in the way the towers simply are, having arrived at their current form and number through a process that completed so long ago that the concept of "arrival" no longer applies. This is the unsettling thing about the plateau, applied to its smallest inhabitants: you cannot tell if they are living or if they are *finished living*, if they are organisms or artifacts, if they are the result of evolution or the result of evolution's conclusion, the final shape of a lineage that has run out of reasons to change.

They are warm. Faintly. Their undersides, where they contact the root-lines, are warm from the root's warmth, and the heat has migrated into their bodies over the course of their lives, which may be very long, and they carry it with them as they move, small flat warm shapes on a dark tendril in a cool clean ocean, and they are everywhere, and they are doing what they do, and what they do is enough.

## The Swimmers

The swimmers live in the open water between the towers, and they are the inhabitants you see most often, because they are the ones that move, and movement draws the eye.

They are torpedo-shaped. This is the simplest description, and it is accurate enough, but it misses the quality of them, which is translucence. They are not transparent like glass — they are translucent like frosted ice, like clouded quartz, like something that is almost see-through but holds just enough opacity to catch the light and give itself a body. You can see their interiors. Not clearly — not the way you can see the interior of a jellyfish — but as shadows within the translucence, the faint architecture of an organism that has reduced its own opacity to the minimum required for function. You can see the digestive tract, a dark tube running from the anterior end to the posterior, and you can see it working — the slow peristaltic contractions that move material through it, the same rhythm as the towers but faster, compressed, a higher frequency of the same fundamental pulse. You can see the gonadal tissue, if that is what it is — paired structures along the gut, slightly denser than the surrounding tissue, slightly more opaque, and these pulse too, at their own rate, which is slower than the gut and faster than the towers, a rhythm between rhythms, a syncopation that exists only in the swimmers and that gives them, when you observe a school, a collective quality that is almost but not quite musical.

They school. The schools drift across the plateau like clouds, like weather, like something meteorological rather than biological — large aggregations, hundreds of individuals, moving in the same direction at the same speed, their bodies oriented the same way, their spacing uniform, the distance between individuals maintained with a precision that implies some form of communication, some signal you cannot perceive. They are not touching. They are not nearly touching. Each swimmer maintains a body-length of distance from its neighbors, and the school moves through the water as a single object, turning, rising, descending, the whole mass of translucent bodies shifting in unison, catching the flat light, flickering as their surfaces catch it at different angles. From a distance, a school looks like a lens, a curved volume of water that bends light differently than the water around it, a soft-edged shape that moves across the plateau between the towers, and you watch it the way you watch a cloud, with no urgency, no expectation, just the passive attention of an eye that has nothing else to look at.

They filter. Their mouths — if they are mouths — are slits at the anterior end, open constantly as they swim, and water flows through them and through the digestive tract and out through the posterior slits, and the tract extracts whatever it extracts, which must be dissolved organic material, because there is nothing else in the water to extract — no plankton, no particulate matter, no visible food source. The water between the towers is clear and empty. It contains only what the towers have not taken, the residual compounds left in the water after the towers have processed it, and the swimmers live on this residue the way the crawlers live on the seepage, occupying a niche that is defined not by what is present but by what has been *left*. They are the afterthought of the ecosystem. They live on what the towers do not need. They have been living on what the towers do not need for so long that they have optimized for it, their bodies stripped of everything that is not required for this one function — no teeth, no aggressive features, no defensive structures, no eyes. They are the simplest possible organism that can still be called an organism, and they are sufficient, and they have been sufficient for longer than sufficiency has been a concept.

They do not approach the towers. This is a rule, though it is not enforced by anything visible — no current repels them, no chemical barrier surrounds the towers, no predator lurks near the bases to drive them away. They simply do not go there. The schools' paths curve around the towers at a distance of perhaps twenty meters, giving each column a wide berth, and if you follow a school long enough — hours, a day — you realize that their paths are not random. They are following the root-lines. Not on the root-lines, the way the crawlers do, but above them, tracing the network from above, swimming along the same routes the roots follow below, as though the roots create a current or a gradient or a chemical signature that the swimmers can detect and that guides their movement. The schools are mapping the network. They are the network's shadow, moving above it in the open water, and their paths are as fixed as the roots, and they have been following these paths for as long as the roots have been here, and the paths do not change.

They do not avoid you. You can swim into a school and they will part around you and close behind you, and their bodies will brush against your skin — cool, smooth, the texture of wet silk, the faintest pulse of their internal rhythm transmitted through the contact — and they will not react. They will not scatter. They will not change direction. You are not a predator. You are not a feature of their world. You are a temporary obstacle, and they accommodate you, and they forget you, and the school re-forms and moves on, and you are standing in empty water where a school was a moment ago, and the water closes over the space they occupied, and it is as though they were never there.

There are different kinds. This takes time to notice, because they look similar, and the differences are subtle — a slight variation in size, a slight difference in the opacity of the translucence, a slightly different shape to the digestive tract visible inside the body. But there are at least three species, or morphotypes, or whatever the correct term is for organisms that do not reproduce in an observable way and that may have been the same species for so long that the concept of species has worn smooth. The largest are perhaps forty centimeters long. The smallest are ten. They school separately. The schools of large swimmers move at a different speed than the schools of small swimmers, and their paths trace different parts of the network, and they do not mix, and they have not mixed for longer than mixing has been possible, and their separateness is not conflict — it is simply arrangement. They are arranged the way the towers are arranged, each in its ideal location, each doing what it does, and the arrangement is complete.

## The Gliders

The gliders are the largest fauna on the plateau, and they are the rarest, and they are the ones you see at the edge of visibility, and they are the ones you will remember.

They are flat. This is their defining feature — a body plan that is not fish-like or eel-like or ray-like but something else, a broad, thin disc of tissue, perhaps two meters across, with a texture that suggests membrane rather than muscle. They are not translucent like the swimmers. They are dark — dark green, almost black, the color of the towers' lower trunks, and their surfaces are smooth and faintly reflective, and they move by means you cannot identify. There is no visible propulsion. No fins, no tail, no muscular undulation. They simply *glide*, maintaining a steady speed and a steady altitude, cruising the thermoclines above the towers, riding the gentle currents that the vents produce, and the currents must be sufficient to support them because they never stop moving and they never appear to exert effort. They are kites. They are the kites of the plateau, and the towers are the wind, and the wind has been blowing for longer than the kites have been kites.

They are solitary. You never see two gliders together. Each one occupies its own altitude, its own circuit, and the circuits do not overlap — they are layered, like the layers of atmosphere above a planet, each glider in its own band of water, moving in its own direction, and if you are lucky enough to see one pass close enough to identify features, you will see that it has markings: faint lines on the dark surface, patterns that might be individual signatures or might be the imprints of the currents it has been riding, grooves worn into the membrane by the steady flow of water over its body. The markings are different on every glider. The markings are the same on every glider. The distinction is not resolvable, because you will never see two gliders close enough to compare, and the plateau does not invite comparison, because comparison implies that things could be other than they are, and things here cannot be other than they are, and this is the fundamental fact of the place, and the gliders embody it more fully than anything else.

They do not feed visibly. They do not interact with the towers, or the crawlers, or the swimmers. They do not land on the plateau floor. They do not rest. They cruise, and they have been cruising, and the circuit each one follows is the circuit it has always followed, and the circuit is a closed loop, and the glider returns to its starting point without having gone anywhere, and it begins the circuit again, and again, and the water around it is clear and cool and empty, and the light is flat, and the sound of the towers fills the space below it and above it and through it, and the glider glides, and that is all it does, and that is all it has ever done.

One will pass close to you, if you stay long enough. It will not avoid you. It will not approach you. It will simply happen that its circuit brings it near the place where you are hovering, and for a moment — ten seconds, twenty — the dark shape will be close enough to touch, and you will see its surface, and the surface will be alive in a way that the towers are alive, working in a way that you cannot describe, a slow movement within the tissue, a processing, a function that is happening at a rate too slow to see as motion but too fast to be stillness. It is doing something. It has always been doing something. And then it moves past, and it is gone, and the edge of visibility reclaims it, and you are alone again in the flat light with the sound in your chest and the clean stone below and the towers on the horizon, and the glider is somewhere out there, on its circuit, and it will be back, and you may not be here when it comes back, and this will not matter to the glider, and it should not matter to you, because nothing here matters in the way that matters when mattering means something could be different.

## What Else

There is more. There must be more, because an ecosystem — even one in permanent equilibrium, even one that has been finished for longer than finished has been a concept — does not consist of only three kinds of fauna. There are the microbes, the organisms too small to see, and they must be here in the root-lines, in the tower tissue, in the water itself, and they must be doing what microbes do, processing, converting, occupying niches too small for the crawlers and too diffuse for the swimmers. But you cannot see them, and the plateau does not invite speculation about what you cannot see, because the plateau is a place where what is present is what is present, and what is not present is not worth discussing, and the boundary between the two is as clean as the stone.

There are other things, though. Things you glimpse.

A shape on the basalt floor, between two root-lines, in the bare stone where nothing lives. A shape that is flat and dark and still, and that might be a shadow, except that the light on the plateau is flat and even and does not cast shadows, and this shape is casting one — a faint shadow, a slight darkening of the stone around it, as though it is denser than the water it displaces. You swim toward it and it does not move, and as you get closer you see that it is not an animal. It is a deposit. A film of something on the stone, thin as a coat of paint, dark green, slightly iridescent, and it is not a mineral deposit and it is not a biological residue and it is something, but the plateau does not tell you what, and you have no instruments, and the film is gone the next time you pass this way — not gone as in moved, gone as in dissolved, absorbed, returned to the water, as though it was a message that was delivered and received and erased, and you were not the recipient.

There are things in the tower tissue. If you press your hand against the trunk and wait, you will feel them — not the peristalsis, but something else, something irregular, something that moves within the tissue the way blood moves within a vein, a localized pulse that is not part of the tower's rhythm. There are organisms inside the towers. Commensals, or parasites, or components — you cannot tell, and the towers do not seem to be harmed or helped by their presence. They are simply there, inside the tissue, doing whatever they do, and the towers continue to function, and the things inside the towers continue to function, and neither cares about the other, or both are the other, and the distinction is the wrong distinction.

There is something at the base of one tower — only one, the one you have come to think of as "yours" because you have returned to it three times, though it is not yours and will never be yours and does not know you exist — a structure that is not a root. It is small, perhaps half a meter across, and it is attached to the basalt next to the root-structure, and it is not made of the same material as the root. It is lighter, almost white, and it is geometric — not a crystal, not a mineral formation, but something with angles, something with edges, something that does not belong to the organic vocabulary of the plateau. You see it once. You do not see it again. You return to the same tower, the same side, the same position on the basalt, and it is not there. Was it ever there? The stone is clean. The stone has always been clean. The plateau does not keep artifacts. The plateau does not keep anything. Everything here is in use, or has been in use, or will be in use, and there is no storage, no waste, no history. The thing with the angles was there and now it is not, and this is the closest thing to an event you have experienced on the plateau, and it is not an event. It is a subtraction. The plateau subtracts things, returns them to the flow, and the flow carries them, and the towers process them, and the crawlers eat them, and the swimmers filter them, and the gliders ride above it all, and the chord sounds, and the light stays flat, and the water is clear.

---

The inhabitants of the Greenwater Plateau are not a community. A community implies relationships that change, that develop, that have a history. The inhabitants here do not have a history. They have a present, and the present is the same as the past, and the present is the same as the future, and the word "same" has been true for so long that it has worn through to a deeper meaning of "same" — not identical, but *identical in the way that a river is identical to itself*, the water always different, the river always the same, except here even the water is the same, because it has been processed and reprocessed and filtered and exhausted and reabsorbed so many times that its composition is fixed, its mineral content is fixed, its temperature is fixed, and the only thing that moves through the towers is the water, and the water has been moving through the towers for so long that it is the towers, and the towers are the water, and the crawlers are the root-lines, and the swimmers are the space between, and the gliders are the sound, and everything is everything, and everything has been everything, and nothing changes, and the chord sounds, and the light stays flat, and this is the world, and this is who lives here, and this is what living here means: to be a part of a system that has been complete for longer than completion has been a concept, to move at the rate the system moves, to breathe at the rate the system breathes, and to not know that you are doing this, and to not need to know, and to never have needed to know, and to never need to know, and the sound fills your chest, and the light does not change, and the water is cool, and the stone is clean, and the towers stand, and everything is here, and everything has always been here, and you are here too, for now, and the plateau does not notice, and the plateau does not mind, and the plateau continues.
