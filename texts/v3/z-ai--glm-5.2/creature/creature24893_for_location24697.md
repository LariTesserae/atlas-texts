# The Manufactory Plateau

---

## The Grazers

Call them what you like. They have no name for themselves, or if they do, it is not a name in any sense you would recognize — it is a temperature, a rate of chewing, a particular silver that means *I am here and I am eating and this is sufficient.*

They are the plateau's most visible residents, and the least interesting thing about them is the six legs. Yes, the rear pair coils up against the body like sprung traps, held in reserve, and yes, it is unsettling to watch something graze with the constant implication of sudden departure. But the legs are practical. The ground here is unreliable — a mat of rotting vegetation over warm stone over voids that sometimes open — and a creature that can redistribute its support across six points or four or two is a creature that does not fall. They have fallen. The ones that fell are not here. The legs are a memory of falling and a refusal to repeat it.

The hide is where you should look. The silver-green of the leaf undersides, yes, but not static. The color moves. It is not camouflage — or not only camouflage. The shifts are too slow for alarm, too deliberate for reflex. When a grazer is alone, its hide holds a steady pewter, almost grey, almost nothing. When another grazer comes within a certain distance — fifteen meters, perhaps twenty, the range varies — both animals ripple. A slow umber wave starts at the shoulder and moves aft, and the other answers with the same wave slightly delayed, and for a moment they are both the same color, and then they separate and the color fades. This is not greeting. This is not competition. It is closer to *synchronization* — two components acknowledging that they are part of the same system, calibrating to the same clock.

Herds of thirty or forty move through the fast-growing forest in loose formations that are not driven by any individual. No lead animal. No hierarchy. The herd is a fluid that takes the shape of its container — the container being the forest, the available light, the warm air currents that tell them where the fresh growth is densest. They crop the plants with those sideways-opening mouths and the plants grow back faster than they can be eaten, which is the point. The grazers are not exploiting the forest. They are *paced* to it. They are a valve in a circuit, opening and closing at the rate the system requires, and the system requires a specific rate of pruning to keep the growth at its frantic, productive pitch. Too much grazing and the plants exhaust themselves. Too little and they overshoot, tangle, choke their own light. The grazers hold the line. They are the line.

They sleep standing, all six legs locked, the rear pair finally deployed. They sleep in tight clusters, hides touching, and the color that passes between them in sleep is different from the waking color — deeper, slower, almost violet, and it moves through the whole herd as a single pulse, and if you pressed your hand to the flank of one sleeping grazer you would feel the pulse in your own wrist, your own blood adjusting, briefly, to a rhythm that is not yours.

They do not startle. You can walk through a sleeping herd and they will not scatter. The hide nearest you will shift — silver to umber to silver — and the pulse will hiccup, once, and resume. You are not a predator. The plateau has no predators. The plateau does not require them. Nothing here dies before it is supposed to, and everything here is supposed to, eventually, and the dying is as much a part of the process as the growing. The grazers know this. Not in words. In the way a clock knows the time.

## The Membrane-Wings

They are not birds and they are not bats and they are not insects and they are not kites, though the kite comparison is the closest human word will get. They are sails. Living sails.

The wings are the whole animal. There is a body — small, dense, warm, a dark knot of tissue at the convergence of the membranes — but the body is an afterthought, a place where the wings meet. The wings themselves are translucent, veined with something darker and more conductive, and they span two meters or more, and when the animal banks in a thermal, the amber ground-light passes through the membrane and paints the forest floor in shifting, cathedral-glass patterns that are, you realize after watching long enough, *specific*. The patterns are not random. The veins branch in certain ways. The density of the membrane thickens at certain points. The animal is carrying information in its wings and projecting it downward as shadow, and the things below — the mounds, the plants, perhaps the rock itself — are reading it.

What are they saying? The question is malformed. They are not saying anything. They are *tuning*.

Here is what happens: A membrane-wing circles a thermal vent. The vent is a crack in the basalt where heat rises in a column, and the wing rides that column in slow spirals, and as it spirals it adjusts — the angle of a wing, the tension in a vein, the pitch of its body — and it calls. The call is a single low note, below hearing, felt in the chest like a bass drum at a distance, and when it calls, the thermal vent responds. Not with sound. With heat. A pulse. A surging of warmth from below, as though the deep engine has heard the call and opened a valve. The wing adjusts again. Calls again. Another pulse, slightly different — warmer, or less warm, or shifted in some quality you do not have the senses to parse. The wing circles. The vent pulses. They are in conversation, and the conversation is about rate. How fast should this vent run? How much heat should rise here, now, at this moment, for the plants growing within its reach? The wing is a thermostat. A living thermostat with a two-meter wingspan and a body like a warm coal, circling in the dusk, dialing in the correct temperature for a forest that grows at triple speed and cannot afford to be too hot or too cold.

There are dozens of them. Each tends a territory of vents, and their territories overlap at the edges, and when two wings cross paths they do not interact — they simply adjust, each taking account of the other's tuning, each folding the other's data into its own calculations. They are a distributed control system. A network of living instruments, each one measuring and adjusting a small section of the plateau's thermal output, and the plateau runs because they run, and they run because the plateau feeds them thermals to ride and a purpose to fulfill, and the purpose is enough. The purpose is the whole of their lives.

They never land. This is important. They have no legs. The body has no landing gear, no claws, no perch-grip. They were not designed to rest. They were not designed at all, but they were not *shaped* to rest — they are shaped to fly, only to fly, and when they die they do not descend gently. A dead membrane-wing drops like a collapsed tent, the membranes folding inward, the body tumbling, and it falls through the canopy and hits the spongy ground and the insects find it within minutes and it is gone within hours, every trace of it harvested and processed and incorporated into the mounds, and the thermal it tended goes untuned for a day or two until a new wing molts or emerges or is grown — the process is not clear, has never been observed — and takes up the spiral and the calling and the work.

## The Insects

They are the smallest visible components and the most essential, and they are the closest thing this place has to a working class.

Thumb-sized. Dark. Not black — a deep, warm brown, the color of cooled iron, and they have a sheen that is not quite metallic and not quite wet. They are not insects. They have six legs and a segmented body and they move in ways that recall insects, but their bodies are too smooth, too uniform, and their legs end not in claws or pads but in small, flat structures that look like — and function as — feet. They walk on feet. This is more disturbing than you expect it to be.

They move in straight lines. Not approximately straight — *straight*, as though following a track only they can see, and perhaps they can, perhaps the ground is inscribed with guidance they read through those flat feet, some chemical trail or thermal gradient that lays out their routes with surveyor's precision. They harvest. That is their visible function. The rotting plant matter — the collapsed trunks, the liquefying leaves, the wet heaps of yesterday's forest — they cut it, carry it, deposit it. They work in grid patterns that shift over hours, the grid migrating across the forest floor like a slow searchlight, covering every surface, missing nothing.

The mounds. Low, flat, warm. Each one is about the size of a card table and knee-high, and there are thousands of them, distributed across the plateau with a regularity that is not quite uniform and not quite random — the spacing follows a pattern, but the pattern is complex, recursive, like the distribution of veins in a leaf or cracks in drying mud. The insects build them. They carry the rotting matter to the mound site and press it into place with their flat feet, layer by layer, and the mound grows and the mound warms and the mound begins to hum.

The hum is the sound of process. Inside the mound, the compressed vegetable matter is doing something. Not decomposing — or not only decomposing. Digesting. Transforming. The heat of the plateau rises through the mound's base and the matter inside responds — chemical reactions that are not quite fermentation and not quite combustion and not quite electrolysis, something that requires the specific mineral content of the rotting plants and the specific temperature of the plateau's thermal output and the specific electrical field that the mound itself generates as a byproduct. The mounds are batteries. Or kilns. Or organs. They are the place where the forest's output is converted into something else, something the system needs, and the something else is —

You do not know. You cannot tell. The mounds produce heat and a low electrical field and an taste of ozone and iron, and the heat rises and the field radiates and the taste dissipates into the air, and where does it go? Into the clearings. The polished clearings deep in the forest, where the air thickens and prepares and then relents. The mounds feed the clearings. The clearings are where the product would emerge, if it emerged, if the process completed, if the factory had a final output — but it doesn't, or it hasn't yet, or it does and you cannot perceive it because the product is not a thing, it is a condition, a state of readiness, a potential that is maintained but never discharged, and the mounds are its power supply, humming in the dark, patient as boilers.

Break one open — if you can. The insects will not stop you. They will not react. You push your hand into the warm, compressed matter and it gives like bread dough, and inside it is hot and dry and smells of ozone and the electricity raises the hair on your arm, and there is nothing there. No larvae. No queen. No mechanism. Just matter and heat and the hum. The mound is not a home. It is a machine, and the insects are its operators, and they have no use for a home because they have no use for rest. They work. They die working. A dead insect is carried to the nearest mound by the others and pressed in with the rest, and its body contributes its small heat and its small minerals to the process, and the grid shifts to cover the gap, and the work continues.

## The Shape

You will not see it clearly. No one does. The accounts vary — tall, short, many-limbed, no limbs, moving like a person, moving like a fog bank, moving like nothing at all — and the variation is the data. It is not one thing. It is not many things. It is the thing that the plateau is making, or trying to make, or practicing making, in those polished clearings when the air thickens and then relents.

It lives deeper in. Past the dense vine-canopies, past the oldest mounds, past the clearings where the ground-light is brightest. It moves through territory that it owns in the way a river owns its bed — not by claim but by passage. It is there, in the forest, the way the hum is in the rock: continuous, underlying, felt but not seen. You catch it at the edge of vision. A shape between the trunks that is too tall to be a grazer and too slow to be a wing and too deliberate to be wind in the foliage. It walks. It changes while it walks. Not dramatically — not a lycanthropic convulsion, not a melt — but in the way a shadow changes as the light source moves. Its outline shifts. A shoulder becomes a flank. A limb becomes a contour. It is not deciding what shape to be; it is *being shaped*, continuously, by something you cannot see — the product of a process that is running in the rock and the mounds and the clearings, a process that outputs not heat, not electricity, not ozone, but *form*, a living form, a thing that exists in the space between the factory's product and the factory itself, and it is not finished, and it may never be finished, and it walks its territory with the patience of something that is being made and knows it is being made and does not mind.

It sees you. You know this because when you see it, it stops. Not freezes — stops, the way a machine stops, mid-cycle, and holds. And in the holding, its outline steadies, briefly, and you see — almost see — something almost coherent. Almost a shape you could name. Almost a body you could describe to someone later, except you can't, because by the time you open your mouth the shape is gone, dissolved back into the forest, and the trunks where it stood are just trunks and the vines are just vines and the only evidence that it was ever there is a faint warmth in the air and a low, particular hum that you could swear is coming from your own teeth.

It is not dangerous. It is not afraid. It is not curious. These are animal categories and it is not an animal. It is a product. A product that walks. A product that lives. A product that is still being refined, still in development, still cycling through possible configurations in the factory's slow, patient R&D, and the factory has all the time in the world and the product has all the patience in the world and neither of them needs you to understand what they are doing or why.

You are not afraid of it. This surprises you. You should be. By every right — alone, deep in a forest that breathes, watched by something that changes shape — you should be afraid. You are not. The fear does not come. It is as though the plateau has decided that fear is not part of your function here, and the plateau's decisions, in this place, are the ones that hold.

## The Deep Thing

Beneath all of it — beneath the rock and the hum and the warm stone and the thermal vents and the engine that has no name — there is something else.

Not a machine. Not a creature. Not a god, though the word wants to go there. Something that is running. Something that started running before the plateau rose, before the rock was torn upward, before the forest grew, before the first grazer rippled silver to umber. Something that has been running so long that running is not quite the right word anymore — it is more like *being*, but a particular kind of being that involves motion, internal motion, a turning-over of vast components at a rate so slow that geological time feels hasty by comparison.

It is not alive. It is not dead. It is not between those states because those states do not apply. It is a process. The process. The one that pushed the plateau up — not as construction but as exhalation, the way a lung rises with breath — and seeded the rock with heat and seeded the heat with a pattern and seeded the pattern with a purpose that is not a purpose in the sense of intention but in the sense of direction, the way water has a direction, the way electricity has a direction, the way a reaction has a direction once it starts and cannot stop until it completes.

The plateau is its lung. The forest is its skin. The grazers are its capillaries. The wings are its thermostats. The insects are its enzymes. The mounds are its mitochondria. The clearings are its — its what? Its mouth? Its hands? The place where it would speak, if it spoke, the place where it would make, if it made, the place where the product would emerge if the process ever completed, and it does not complete, and it does not need to complete, because the running is the point. The factory runs to run. The product is the process. The making is the made.

You feel it in your molars. That hum. That low, tectonic idling. It is not coming from the rock. It is coming from below the rock. It is coming from the thing that is running, and the thing is vast, and the thing is patient, and the thing does not know you are here, or if it knows, it does not care, or if it cares, it cares the way a heart cares about a single blood cell — which is to say, completely, and not at all, and without any possibility of expressing the difference.

## You

You do not live here. You are visiting.

But the plateau does not distinguish. The forest breathes around you and the warm rain dots your face and the insects walk across your hand without pausing and the ground hums beneath your feet and your blood, briefly, synchronized with a sleeping grazer, holds a rhythm that is not yours. You are in the system. You are a component. The plateau does not care whether you were invited. It does not care whether you leave. It will process you the way it processes everything — slowly, patiently, through heat and time and the patient work of components that do not know they are components and a process that does not know it is a process and a thing in the deep that does not know it is a thing.

You will leave, of course. You will climb down the torn basalt faces and return to the lowlands where the sun exists and the ground is cold and nothing hums in your molars. You will tell someone, or you won't. You will remember, or you won't — the memory may not hold, may dissolve the way the clearings dissolve, the way the Shape dissolves, the way everything here dissolves back into the process that made it. You may find, in years to come, that you cannot quite recall what the plateau looked like, but you can recall what it *felt* like — the warmth, the breathing, the hum — and you may find that the hum has not entirely left you, that it surfaces sometimes in quiet moments, in the space between waking and sleep, a low vibration in the deep tissues of your jaw, and you will not know whether it is a memory or a connection, whether the plateau is still running in you or you are still running in it, and the difference, here, does not matter.

The Manufactory Plateau does not have residents. It has components. And you, for a while, were one. And the factory is still running. And the factory does not need you. And the factory does not need to need you. And the hum goes on.
