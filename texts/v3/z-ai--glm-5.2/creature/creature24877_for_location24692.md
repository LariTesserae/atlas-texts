# The Inhabitants of the Hollow Ward

They are the kind of people who were once called something, but the name they had before is less interesting than the name this place is giving them.

There are not many of them. That is the first thing to understand, and it is the most important thing, and it explains almost everything else. There are not many because there cannot be many. The hollow can support a certain quantity of breath, a certain weight of footfall, a certain demand on its seeps and its warmth and its slow, patient geometry, and the people who live here know this quantity the way you know how much water a cupped hand can hold — not by measurement but by the feel of it, by the moment when the water begins to spill between your fingers. They live at the edge of that moment. They have always lived there.

---

## What they are, approximately

They are probably human. Probably. It is hard to say, and harder to say the longer you think about it, and they themselves do not seem to consider the question worth answering.

They are the right general shape. Two legs, two arms, upright, with faces that have the expected number of features in the expected arrangement. But they are slight in the way that things are slight when they have been shaped by a confined space — narrow-shouldered, compact, with hands that are disproportionately large and disproportionately careful. Their fingers are long. Their palms are wide and flat and calloused in specific, asymmetric patterns that tell you: these hands stack stone, press clay, weave leaves, and sweep floors. These hands do not do much else. The calluses are a biography.

Their skin has taken on the coloration of the hollow. Not by intention — by proximity, by years, by the slow exchange that happens when a living body occupies a place long enough. They are the grey-yellow of the limestone, the pale matte of the dried mineral floor, with a warmth beneath that is faintly amber, like the light. They are difficult to see against the walls. This may be accidental. It may not be.

Their eyes are the most telling feature. They are large, and they are dark, and they are slow to adjust — not because they are weak but because they are calibrated for the specific light of this place, the dim amber-grey ambience that comes from no source and casts no shadows. Take one of them outside, into full sun, and they would be blind for an hour, perhaps longer, their pupils contracting to points in faces that had forgotten what brightness was. In the hollow, though, they see everything. They see the condensation forming. They see the insects moving in their columns. They see the way the mineral floor has changed since yesterday, which is a thing that almost no one else would notice, because almost no one else has spent enough time in one place to develop that kind of attention.

They do not speak the way you might expect.

They speak, yes. Their mouths make shapes and their throats make sounds, but the sounds are quiet — low-pitched, close to the chest, shaped more by the tongue and lips than by the breath. They do not project. Their words do not carry. They are calibrated for a space where sound is precious because there is so little of it, where a raised voice would be a kind of violence. They speak to each other at distances of two meters, three, sometimes across a dwelling and no further. If they need to communicate at a greater distance, they touch. A hand on a shoulder. A palm against a forearm. The pressure of fingers in specific patterns — a language of contact that is older than their spoken language, or at least more essential. You would not hear it. You would see it, if you were watching, and you would not understand it, and you would feel that you had seen something private, which you had.

## What they do

They build. That is the primary work — not a task they perform but a condition they inhabit, the way a river inhabits its channel. The building is never finished because the floor is never finished. The mineral deposits spread outward from the seeps, and what was solid ground a year ago is now a slightly different elevation, a slightly different texture, and the dwellings must be adjusted. A wall re-stacked. A threshold re-leveled. A roof re-pitched to accommodate the fact that the floor has risen by a centimeter and the ceiling has not. They do this with their hands. They have always done this with their hands. Tools would be faster, but speed is not a value here. The hands know the stone. The hands know the clay. The hands can feel whether a joint is true in a way that a tool cannot, because a tool does not have skin, and skin is how you know things in the hollow.

They sweep. This sounds trivial and it is not trivial. The emptiness between the dwellings is the most deliberate feature of the settlement, and maintaining it is a practice, a discipline, a form of attention. They sweep with the flat of their hands or with smooth stones held in the palm, pressing the fine mineral dust toward the edges, toward the vegetation, where it can be absorbed. The center is kept clear. The center is the negative space that makes the settlement a settlement rather than a pile of structures. It is also the place where they gather, when they gather, which is not often, and never on a schedule.

They tend the vegetation. Not by planting — they do not plant — but by guiding. A root that is growing in the wrong direction is redirected. A climbing plant that has reached too high is looped back down. The broad-leafed plants at the base of the walls are thinned when they become too dense, the excess leaves carried to the roofs for weaving. They do not take more than the hollow can replace. They do not take less than they need. This balance is not calculated; it is felt. It is the same feeling that tells them how many people can live here, and it is the same feeling that tells them when to leave.

They leave. This is the thing that is hardest to understand from outside, because from outside the settlement looks abandoned, and it is not abandoned. It is *cycled*. The inhabitants live here in shifts — not shifts of hours but shifts of weeks, months, seasons that do not correspond to any season above ground. There is a rhythm to it, a pulse, and the pulse is tied to the hollow itself, to the rate at which the floor builds, the rate at which the condensation forms, the rate at which the seeps weep. When the hollow is active — when the mineral deposits are growing quickly, when the water is abundant, when the air is warm and the plants are thick — the inhabitants are here. When the hollow slows, or when it has given enough and needs to be left alone to continue, they leave. They go somewhere else. They have another place, or several other places, or a network of hollows and cracks and seams in the limestone that they move between, each one tended in its turn, each one occupied and then emptied and then occupied again.

They do not speak of where they go. They may not think of it as worth speaking about. The hollow is the place that matters. The hollow is the place they return to. Where they go in between is logistics.

## The grey creatures

The grey creatures that circle the dwellings are not pets. They are not livestock. They are not, exactly, companions.

They are a part of this.

The inhabitants and the grey creatures exist in a relationship that has no name in any language you would recognize, because the languages you recognize are built around categories — owner and owned, wild and domesticated, self and other — that do not apply. The grey creatures were here before the inhabitants, or they were not. The inhabitants were here before the grey creatures, or they were not. It does not matter. They are here now, together, and their togetherness is structural. The grey creatures circle the dwellings. They inspect the walls. They move in patterns that look like ritual because they are ritual — not in the sense of symbolic performance but in the sense of a repeated, necessary action that maintains a relationship between things. The creatures are checking the dwellings the way the inhabitants check the floor: for change, for movement, for the slow geological drift that is always happening. They feel the walls with their heads — their scaled, smooth, bird-lizard heads — and they know, somehow, whether the stone has shifted, whether a joint has loosened, whether a root has begun to push from the inside.

When the inhabitants return from wherever they go, the grey creatures are the first thing they greet. Not with words. With hands on heads, palms on scales, the same contact-language they use among themselves. The creatures lean into the touch. Their eyes close, or something like closing — a nictitating membrane, grey and translucent, sliding across. Then the inhabitants go to the dwellings, and the grey creatures go back to circling, and the rhythm resumes.

The creatures do not enter the dwellings. The dwellings are the inhabitants' space. The space between is the creatures' space. This division is not negotiated. It is understood. It has been understood for as long as either of them can remember, which is longer than you might think, because memory in the hollow is not stored in brains alone. It is stored in the floor, in the walls, in the layered mineral deposit that records each year the way a tree records each summer. The inhabitants can read this record. The grey creatures can feel it. Together, they remember the hollow back to its beginning, or near enough.

## What they eat

They eat the fungi. The flat, shelf-like growths in the dimmest corners, the pale cream and soft pink ones that have already arrived at whatever they were becoming — these are food. They are warm and dense and slightly sweet, with a texture that is somewhere between bread and cheese, if you can imagine bread and cheese that grew out of limestone in the dark. The inhabitants harvest them by hand, snapping off the outer edges, leaving the centers to continue growing. The fungi regenerate. The inhabitants know the rate of regeneration, and they harvest accordingly.

They eat the insects. Not directly — they are not insectivores in the obvious sense. But the amphibians eat the insects, and the inhabitants sometimes eat the amphibians, though this is rare and done with a kind of reluctance that suggests it is not their preference. The amphibians are too few, too slow, too much a part of the hollow's breathing to be taken lightly. When one is taken, it is taken with an acknowledgment that is not prayer but is adjacent to prayer — a pause, a held breath, a moment of stillness before the hands close.

They eat what they bring from outside. When they return from wherever they go, they carry things. Small things — wrapped in the broad leaves, tied with the jointed stems. Root vegetables, perhaps. Seeds. Hard, dark objects that might be nuts or might be something else entirely. They bring enough to supplement what the hollow provides, never enough to replace it. The hollow feeds them. The outside tops them up. The proportion matters. Too much from outside and the settlement becomes an import, a thing that is here only because it is being supplied, and then it would not belong to the hollow anymore. It would belong to the supply chain. The inhabitants do not want this. They want to belong to the hollow.

## The children

There are children. Or there were. Or there will be.

This is the hardest thing to know, because the hollow does not advertise its futurity, and the inhabitants do not speak of it to outsiders, and there are no outsiders, so the question circles itself.

But. The dwellings are not all the same size. Eight or ten structures, you might count, but if you look more carefully, two of them are smaller — lower, tighter, with openings that are narrower and thresholds that are closer to the floor. These are not storage. These are not workshops. These are dwellings built for bodies that are smaller, and the smaller dwellings are maintained with the same care as the larger ones — the same patched roofs, the same swept floors, the same deliberate, generous emptiness around them. Someone is keeping these dwellings ready. Someone is waiting for someone smaller to come home.

If there are children, they are not here now. They are wherever the inhabitants go when they go. But they will come back, because the dwellings are waiting, and the grey creatures circle the small dwellings with the same attention they give the large ones, and the fungi in the corners near the small dwellings are harvested less frequently, as though someone is saving them. As though someone knows that small mouths prefer the softer edges, the ones that come off easily in small hands.

---

## What they are becoming

This is the question the hollow asks of them, and the question they ask of themselves, though they do not ask it in words. They ask it in the way they build — in the impermanence of their walls, in the way they let the floor rise beneath them without fighting it, in the way they leave and return and leave again. They are not trying to make the hollow into a permanent home. They are trying to learn how to live in a place that is not permanent, that was never going to be permanent, that is still in the process of deciding what it is. They are learning to be the same way.

They are becoming creatures of the in-between. Of the seam. Of the crack in the stone where the world hasn't finished. They are becoming the kind of people who do not need the earth to be done before they can live on it, who do not need the floor to be final before they can stand on it, who do not need the light to have a source before they can see by it. They are becoming patient. Not patient in the way that implies waiting for something — patient in the way that the hollow is patient, which is to say: ongoing. Continuing. Becoming.

The fungi have arrived at what they were becoming. The inhabitants have not. They are still on the way. The dwellings are way-stations, not destinations. The grey creatures are fellow travelers. The hollow is the road.

And when you leave — through the fissure, through the crack, turning your shoulders sideways to fit — and the grey-yellow stone closes behind you and the cool air of the outside world finds your skin again, you will realize that you did not see them. You were not meant to see them. You were meant to see where they live, and to understand that where they live is not a place but a process, and that the process does not require an audience. It does not require permission. It does not require you.

But it allowed you. For a moment, in the warm dark, in the deep quiet, it allowed you to stand in the empty center and feel the floor building beneath your feet, and that was not nothing. That was the hollow, and the people who live in it, letting you know that they exist, and that they are not finished, and that they are not worried about it.
