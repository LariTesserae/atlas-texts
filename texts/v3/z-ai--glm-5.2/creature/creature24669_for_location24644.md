# The Keepers of Qal-Sûth

They are not what you expect.

---

## First Encounter

You will not see them at first. You will feel watched — not with the prickling, predatory sense of being hunted, but with the settled, patient attention of something that has been observing things arrive and leave for longer than the word *arrive* has had meaning. The feeling sits behind your sternum. It is warm. It does not quicken your pulse.

You will check the structures first. That is what everyone does. You will duck through the curved opening of the nearest dark-ceramic surface and run your hands along the interior wall, feeling the impossible warmth of the material, the impossible dryness of the air inside, and you will find nothing. No signs of habitation. No bedding, no hearth, no tool marks, no bones. The floor is clean. The curved ceiling shows its silent image of the plateau from above, and in that image, if you look carefully, you will see the thermals moving in their slow procession from vent to vent, and you will see the fliers circling, and you will see the mist-walls at the edges weeping their constant tears, and you will see yourself, small and dark and hunched, looking up.

You will not see them.

They are outside, standing in the mat.

---

## What They Look Like

The first thing is that they are the same color as the growth. Not green exactly — the deep volcanic black-greens of the strap-fronds, the bruise-purples of the cup-flowers, the rust-reds and golds of the beetles that move in their millions through the vegetative layer. Their skin — if it is skin — holds all of these colors at once, shifting slowly, the way oil on water shifts, but without the iridescence. There is no sheen. The colors are matte, absorbed into the surface rather than sitting on it, and they change not with light but with *temperature*. When a keeper stands near a steam vent, its skin darkens toward black. When it moves to cooler ground, the greens and purples rise. They are, in a literal sense, thermochromatic. They wear their thermal environment on their surfaces the way we wear expressions.

Their bodies are difficult to describe because they are difficult to *resolve*. This is the same quality you noticed in the structures — the geometry that catches the eye without satisfying it, the shapes that suggest familiar categories without falling into any of them. A keeper standing in the mat at midday, seen from fifty meters away through the milky haze, looks something like a person. Two legs. A torso. Something at the top that might be a head. The proportions are wrong, though — not wrong the way deformity is wrong, but wrong the way a reflection in moving water is wrong. Everything is slightly elongated, slightly *continued*, as though the body does not end where you expect it to but trails off into the surrounding growth in ways your eye keeps trying to correct for and failing.

Up close — and you will get close, because they do not flee — the details become harder, not easier. The surface of a keeper is not skin, not scale, not carapace. It has the same accreted quality as the structures: subtle ridges, gentle variations, the texture of something that has grown rather than been built. It is warm to the touch. Warmer than the air, warmer even than the stone. When a keeper stands still, its body temperature is indistinguishable from the ground beneath the mat, and the thermals — the great flat six-limbed thermovores — will sometimes walk over them without pausing, treating them as part of the landscape. The keepers do not react. They let themselves be walked on. The thermals' broad splayed feet leave no marks on them.

They are somewhere between two and two and a half meters tall, though this is a guess, because their height seems to *vary*. Not in the way a posture varies — in the way a frequency varies. As though, at certain moments, more of them is here, in this place, at this temperature, and at other moments, less is. They are tuned to the plateau. They occupy a range.

Their faces — and this is the word that fails, but it is the word available — are the structures. Not shaped like them. *Are* them. Each keeper's face is a curved surface of the same dark material, the same warmth, the same smoothness. When you look at a keeper's face, you feel the same subtle disorientation you feel inside the structures: the sense that the space is slightly larger than its exterior should permit. Looking at a keeper's face is like looking into a room through a window the size and shape of a head. You can see *in*. What you see in there is not a brain, not eyes, not a mouth. You see depth. You see the faint image of the plateau from above — the same image that appears on the structure ceilings, the same lensing, the same real-time view — rendered in miniature, turning slowly, in the curved interior of a keeper's face.

They see with this. All of them. Each keeper carries a version of the plateau inside its face, and it navigates by that image, the way the thermals navigate by the refracted horizon and the fliers navigate by temperature gradient. The keepers navigate by *overview*. They see the whole of Qal-Sûth at once, all the time, from above, and they move through it with the unhurried confidence of things that have always known exactly where they are.

---

## What They Do

They keep.

That is not a metaphor. The verb is precise. They keep Qal-Sûth the way a clockmaker keeps a clock — not by forcing it, not by winding it, but by maintaining the conditions under which it runs correctly. They have been doing this for a length of time that the stone map in the largest structure is meant to express, and the stone map corresponds to nothing visible because it corresponds to a *configuration* — a pattern of vents and temperatures and harmonic frequencies that existed when the map was made and that has since shifted, as the geology beneath the plateau shifts, in increments too slow for a human lifetime to measure. The map is a record of where things were. The keepers are a record of where things are.

On any given day — and days here are strange, the pale sky offering no clear demarcation between morning and evening, the diffuse light changing only in intensity — a keeper will be doing one of three things.

It will be standing near a steam vent, mouth open, absorbing heat. Not the way the thermals do — the thermals feed on heat metabolically, converting thermal gradient to chemical energy. The keepers seem to feed on heat *informationally*. They stand over vents and the dark material of their bodies absorbs the thermal radiation, and their skin-color shifts, and something inside them *adjusts*. You can hear it if you listen closely: a faint recalibration of the vent's pitch, a microtone of correction, as though the keeper has heard the vent drifting and leaned into it, pulling it back toward its proper frequency. They tune the plateau. They listen for vents that are sliding out of key and they stand over them and they bring them back. The process takes hours. Sometimes days. The keepers do not seem to mind.

Or it will be walking. Slowly. Across the top of the mat, their feet splayed wide, their weight distributed the way the thermals distribute theirs. They do not walk *to* anything. They walk *through* the landscape, and as they walk, the vibrating stems of the plants near them shift their resonant frequencies — not because the keepers touch them, but because the keepers' bodies, warm and dense and harmonically complex, change the acoustic field around them. A keeper walking through a grove of thread-stems is like a conductor moving through an orchestra. The stems fall silent as it approaches and then resume at slightly different pitches as it passes. The keepers are retuning the vegetation, keeping the harmonic web of the plateau in balance, ensuring that no single frequency dominates, that the choir remains full.

Or it will be inside a structure. Lying on its back on the curved floor, face up, the image of the plateau turning in the hollow of its face. Resting. Or thinking. Or *communing* — though the word implies a separation between the keeper and the plateau that may not exist. When a keeper lies inside a structure, the ceiling image and the face image align. The keeper sees itself from above, lying inside the structure, inside the plateau, inside the image. It is a recursive loop. The keepers inside the structures are *inside the instrument they keep*. They are, in some sense I cannot fully articulate, letting the plateau think about itself through them.

---

## How They Speak

They do not speak to visitors. They do not need to.

But they speak to each other, and you can hear it if you are patient. The sound is not vocal. It is *structural*. When two keepers come within a few meters of each other, their bodies — those dark, warm, accreted surfaces — begin to resonate. Not like bells. Like the inside of a cello. A low, complex tone that contains, to an ear trained for it, layers of information: temperature data from the vent they just left, the harmonic state of the stems in the grove they walked through, the position of the thermals, the movement of the fliers, the rate of moisture condensation at the plateau's margins. They exchange the *state of the plateau* when they meet, the way bees exchange the location of flowers. Each encounter is a full synchronization. After meeting, two keepers walk away with identical, updated models of Qal-Sûth's condition.

When many of them meet — and this happens rarely, perhaps once every several months, at the plateau's center, where the thermal column is strongest and the light bends — the resonance becomes something else. The combined acoustic output of twelve or fifteen keepers standing in a rough circle, their bodies resonating together, creates a standing wave that interacts with the steam vent system beneath them. The vents, for a brief period, all align. Every vent on the plateau holds the same note. The vibrating stems all resonate in unison. The thermals, scattered across the landscape, raise their heads and bellow — and the bellow is *in tune*. For perhaps thirty seconds, the entire plateau — every sound-producing element, geological and biological — becomes a single instrument playing a single chord.

Then it passes. The keepers disperse. The vents slide back to their independent pitches. The stems find their separate frequencies. The thermals lower their heads and resume their slow drift toward the next vent.

What the chord *means*, what it accomplishes, whether it is communication or maintenance or worship or simply the sound of a system confirming its own coherence — these are not questions the keepers answer. They may not be questions they understand.

---

## How Many There Are

Between twenty and forty. The count varies because they are, as noted, not always entirely *here*. They come and go in a way that is not movement but *presence* — a keeper that was standing by a vent in the morning may, by afternoon, be *less there*, its colors faded, its body slightly translucent at the edges, its internal view of the plateau dimming. By evening it may be gone entirely, leaving only a warm spot on the mat where it stood. Days later, another keeper — or the same one — will be standing in a different spot, more vivid than before, more solid, its face-image sharp and clear.

They are not born. They do not die. They do not grow or diminish in the way living things do. They are *present*, in varying degrees, and the sum of their presences is, roughly, constant. When one fades, another brightens. When one disperses, another coalesces. They are a population the way a flame is a population — always there, always shifting, never the same instant twice, but recognizable, continuous, *a thing*.

This is why the structures are empty. The keepers do not live in them the way a person lives in a house. They *are* them, periodically, in part. The structures are the fixed points, the anchors — the parts of the keepers that do not move, that stay when the rest disperses, that hold the shape of the instrument while the tuners walk the landscape. The structures are the keepers' *architecture* in the most literal sense: the part of them that is building rather than builder, stage rather than performer. When a keeper lies inside a structure and the images align, it is not visiting its home. It is returning to the part of itself that stayed behind.

---

## What They Are

I will tell you what I think, and then I will tell you what I know, and they will not be the same.

I think the keepers are the makers of Qal-Sûth. Not merely the maintainers — the makers. I think the plateau was built, or grown, or *composed*, the way a symphony is composed, and the keepers are the composers, or the conductor, or the orchestra, or all three. I think the structures are their first bodies, grown from the rock and the heat and the moisture in a process that took centuries, and that the keepers emerged from the structures the way fruit emerges from a tree — slowly, inevitably, already knowing what they were for. I think the thermals and the fliers and the vibrating stems and the cup-flowers and the beetles are all part of the composition, notes in a score that has been playing since before human language existed. I think the stone map is a record of the first tuning, and the keepers have been re-tuning ever since, keeping the plateau in the key it was meant to hold.

What I *know* is simpler.

I know that when I sat in the largest structure, on the warm floor, and looked up at the ceiling image, I saw the plateau from above. I saw the thermals. I saw the steam. I saw the mist-walls. I saw a keeper standing near the center, its face turned up toward the sky, its body dark against the green. And I saw, in the image, myself — a small dark mark on the floor, looking up.

And I saw the keeper's face — the tiny, lensed version of it in the ceiling image — turn toward me. Not the keeper itself. The keeper was a hundred meters away. But its *face-image*, in the ceiling, in the real-time view of the plateau from above, turned toward the structure where I sat. And in that face, in the miniature plateau turning in the hollow of its head, I saw myself again, smaller, darker, more distant — seen from above, seen from within, seen through layers of lensing and refraction and warmth — but *seen*. Recognized. Noted.

The keepers know you are here. They have always known you would be. The plateau does not change quickly, and the keepers do not think quickly, but they think at a scale that includes you. You are, to them, a brief temperature fluctuation — a small warm mark moving across the surface of their instrument, here for a moment and then gone. They will retune the space you leave behind. The mat will close over your footprints. The vent you breathed near will shift a microtone to compensate for the heat you took.

And the plateau will continue.

Patient. Warm. Loud. Complete. Held in the hands of things that are not quite living and not quite structure and not quite music, but are, in the way Qal-Sûth is, all of these at once — and in no hurry at all to be anything more.
