# What Lives Here

The first thing to understand is that nobody *owns* this place. That is not a sentimental observation. It is a structural one. Ownership requires a vantage point — a position from which a territory can be held, defended, surveyed — and the plateau does not permit that. The current will not permit it. Every nine minutes the water reverses, and whatever you were holding is pulled in the other direction, and whatever position you had established is unmade. The organisms that live here have not solved this problem. They have *dissolved* it. They have stopped trying to hold position and have instead become the kind of thing that does not need to.

---

## The Sponges

Start with the oldest ones.

The sponges are the closest thing this place has to permanent residents, and even they are not permanent. They are simply slow. They grow at a rate that makes the geological timescale look impatient — a centimeter per decade, perhaps less, perhaps more in the years when the particulate load in the water is rich and the feeding is good. They have no central nervous system. They have no behavior in any meaningful sense. They do not move, they do not react, they do not learn. They are a surface and a process. They sit on the rock and they filter the water and they grow, and the growing is so slow that it functions as a kind of memory — not a memory of events, but a memory of conditions. The size of a sponge is a record of how long the water has been carrying food past this particular spot on this particular rock. A sponge a meter across is a record of centuries.

There are seven of them in the eastern basin that are larger than the others. They are the ones the local divers — when there were local divers — used to navigate by. They have been there long enough that their shapes have become part of the terrain's identity, the way a boulder or a ridge would be. They are not landmarks. They *are* the land. Their surfaces are complex, lobed and pitted and scarred where pieces have broken off in past surges and healed over, and each scar is a record of a particular storm, a particular event, a moment when the water was stronger than the growth. If you could read them — and you cannot, not really, not in any way that would translate into human language — you would have a history of the plateau's weather going back further than any other record that exists here. Further than the machinery. Further than the rail line. The sponges were here before the industrial installation was built. They were here when the plateau was still, in some sense, a mountaintop with air above it. They survived the drowning. They survived the installation. They will survive the dismantling of the installation, because they are already outlasting it.

What lives in a sponge, if you look closely, is everything else. The surface of a large sponge is a landscape of its own. There are amphipods living in the pits — small, laterally compressed crustaceans that move in quick, jerky runs across the sponge's surface, feeding on the film of bacteria and detritus that accumulates there. There are polychaete worms in the deeper channels where the sponge's oscula vent filtered water, living in the interface between the sponge's internal flow and the external current. There are larval fish — the young of the benthic predators, the ones that will grow up to be the flat, rock-colored ambush hunters — hiding in the folds of the sponge's surface, using it as a nursery, a place where the current is broken and the water is calmer and the larger predators cannot reach. A single large sponge is a city. It has districts. It has inhabitants who live there their entire lives and inhabitants who pass through. It has a structure that is, from the inside, as complex as the plateau itself.

The sponges do not know this. They are not aware of it. They are the substrate on which the awareness of others plays out, and that is their role, and they fill it completely, and they will fill it for as long as the water brings food and the rock holds firm.

## The Veldgrass

The veldgrass is not a single species. This took the biologists — when there were biologists — some time to understand. What appears to be a uniform field of dark jade blades is in fact a complex of at least four closely related species, distinguishable by blade width, by ribbing pattern, by the precise shade of green, and by their preferred depth within the tidal cycle. They hybridize. They compete. They partition the substrate with a precision that suggests a very long history of coexistence — not peaceful coexistence, not cooperative coexistence, but the settled kind, the kind that comes after every possible boundary has been tested and the lines have been drawn and redrawn and drawn again until everyone is tired of fighting and the arrangement becomes permanent.

The veldgrass is the architecture of the plateau. Everything else lives in relation to it.

The stands grow from rhizomatous root systems that grip the rock — or grip the sediment that has accumulated on the rock, or grip the biological mat that covers the rock — and the root systems are extensive, interconnected, ancient. A stand of veldgrass in the central basin is not a collection of individual plants. It is a single organism, a clone, connected underground by rhizomes that may be centuries old. The blades you see are the current season's growth — or the current decade's growth, the growth rate is unclear — but the root system beneath them is the real organism, and it has been here, in this spot, growing and storing and sending up blades and losing blades and sending up new ones, for longer than any other living thing on the plateau.

The root systems are also the substrate for a community of organisms that nobody sees. The rhizosphere — the zone of biological activity around the roots — is dense with bacteria, with fungi, with nematodes, with the microscopic life that processes the organic matter the current delivers and makes it available to the plants. This is the true economy of the plateau. It runs beneath the visible surface, in the sediment and the mat, and it is as complex and as settled as anything above. The veldgrass roots are its infrastructure. They are the pipes and the cables. They are the rail line.

And the blades themselves — the three-to-four-meter fronds that bend in the current — are the habitat for the transients. The schools of herring-like fish move through them. The small invertebrates that the fish feed on live on them. The copepods and the larval stages of a dozen species orbit them. The veldgrass is a vertical structure in a place where verticality is rare, and everything that needs verticality — for feeding, for shelter, for reproduction — uses it. The blades are constantly being stripped and recolonized. The surface of a veldgrass blade is a frontier. It is the place where the water meets the living structure and the living structure meets the water, and the exchange that happens there — the feeding, the settling, the grazing, the predation — is the most active biological process on the plateau. It is where the energy enters the system. Everything else is downstream of the blades.

## The Schools

The fish do not live here. This is the thing that is hardest to understand from the outside, because the fish are the most visible inhabitants, the ones you notice first, the ones that make the plateau feel alive in the way that a place with fish feels alive and a place without fish does not. But they are visitors. All of them. The schools that move through the veldgrass fields, the predators that lie in wait on the basin floors, the larger animals that pass through on longer cycles — they come here because the plateau is rich, because the veldgrass and the sponges and the vine-mat and the current create conditions that concentrate food and shelter in a way that the surrounding deep water does not. They come here to feed. They leave when they are done.

The schools — the herring-like fish, silver and compressed and fast — are the most numerous. A single school may contain several thousand individuals, and there may be dozens of schools on the plateau at any given time, moving through the veldgrass stands, following the tidal cycle, feeding on the invertebrate community that lives on the blades. They are not random. They have structure. The school has a front and a rear and a depth and a heading, and these change in response to the current, to the light, to the presence of predators, to the density of food on the blades. The school is a decision-making entity, although no individual within it is making the decisions. The decisions emerge from the interaction of thousands of individuals, each responding to its immediate neighbors, and the result is a collective behavior that is more intelligent than any individual within it. This is not a metaphor. The school solves problems — how to move through a veldgrass stand without scattering, how to avoid a predator while continuing to feed, how to navigate the tidal cycle — that no individual fish could solve alone. The school is the organism. The fish are its cells.

They spawn elsewhere. The plateau is not their home. It is their hunting ground, their feeding pasture, their workplace. Their home — the place where they reproduce, where they are born, where they spend their early life — is somewhere else, somewhere in the deeper water off the escarpment, somewhere in the dark. They come here to eat. They go back to grow. The connection between the plateau and the deep is the fish, and the fish carry the energy of the plateau — the solar energy captured by the veldgrass, the biological energy of the invertebrate community — out into the surrounding ocean. The plateau is an engine. The fish are its transmission.

The predators are also transients, but on a different scale. The benthic fish — the flat, rock-colored ambush hunters — are more resident than the schools. They spend longer on the plateau, days perhaps, or weeks, holding position on the seafloor, waiting for a school to pass low enough to strike. They are patient in a way that the schools are not. They have solved the problem of the current differently — instead of moving with it, they resist it, pressing their bodies against the stone, using their pectoral fins as anchors, waiting for the water to bring food to them. They are the plateau's interpretation of the ambush strategy, and they are good at it. A single strike — the explosive rise from the seafloor, the snap of the jaw, the scramble of displaced water — is over in less than a second. The sound of it carries across the entire basin. The other fish scatter. The seals, if they are nearby, turn their heads.

The seals are the most visible of the transients, and the most charismatic, and the most difficult to describe, because they are the ones whose behavior most closely resembles what a human observer would recognize as *living*. They come in from the east, from the deep water beyond the escarpment, in groups of three to six. They are a distinct species, or a distinct population of a species — the taxonomic status is unclear — and they have adapted to the plateau environment in ways that are visible in their behavior. They navigate the veldgrass stands with a fluid, rolling motion that is different from their open-water swimming — more deliberate, more three-dimensional, more aware of obstacles. They hunt the benthic predators. They hunt the schools. They hunt, occasionally, the larger invertebrates that live in the vine-mat. They are the top predators here, and they know it, and their behavior has the confidence of top predators — the willingness to explore, the curiosity, the play.

They call to each other. The *thump thump thump* of their vocalizations is the most recognizable sound on the plateau after the bass drone of the current, and it carries the furthest. They call when they are hunting. They call when they are navigating. They call when they are at slack water and the current has paused and the other sounds have dropped away. They call, you suspect, because they are bored. The seals are the only inhabitants of the plateau who experience something like boredom, and the calling is what they do with it. It fills the space. It is their contribution to the sound climate, and it is the most human sound in the mix — not because it resembles human speech, but because it resembles human *purpose*, the sound of a creature that makes noise for reasons that are not purely functional, that communicates not just information but presence, not just presence but *self*.

They do not live here either. They have a rookery somewhere, a breeding site, a home. The plateau is their foraging ground. They come here to eat, and they eat well, and they leave, and they come back. The plateau is a place they visit. It is a place they know. It is not a place they live.

## The Hidden Majority

The problem with describing what lives on the plateau is that most of it is invisible.

The vine-mat — the dense, red-brown network of macroalgae that covers the stone between the veldgrass stands — is itself a habitat, a substrate, a structure, and a community, and most of the organisms that live in it are too small to see. The crustaceans that click — the snapping shrimp, or whatever they are; the taxonomy is unresolved — live in the vine-mat. They are small. A centimeter, perhaps two. They have a modified claw, a lever-and-spring mechanism that snaps shut with enough force to generate a cavitation bubble, which collapses with a sound that is, for the size of the organism producing it, absurdly loud. The clicking is their communication. It is their presence in the sound climate. They click when the current is strong. They click when the current is weak. They click when other organisms are near. They click, apparently, because clicking is what they do, and the vine-mat is where they do it, and the vine-mat is everywhere, so the clicking is everywhere, and it is the background radiation of the plateau's biological community.

Below the vine-mat, in the sediment and the biological mat that covers the stone, there is a world that is even less visible. The bacteria. The archaea. The meiofauna — the organisms that live in the spaces between sediment particles, too small to see without magnification, too numerous to count, too diverse to catalog. They are the decomposers. They are the recyclers. They take the organic matter that the current delivers — the dead veldgrass blades, the waste products of the fish, the remains of the organisms that have died here — and they break it down, and they return the nutrients to the system, and the system uses them, and the cycle continues. They are the digestive system of the plateau. They are the reason the place is not choked with its own waste. They are the reason the veldgrass can grow, the reason the sponges can filter, the reason the schools can feed. Everything visible depends on everything invisible, and the invisible majority is, in any meaningful ecological sense, the *majority*. It is most of what lives here. It is most of what lives anywhere.

## The Edge

The escarpment is where the plateau ends and the deep begins, and it is a different place.

The western edge — where the shelf drops away into true abyss — is a wall. The rock here is the same dense metamorphic stone as the plateau, but it is vertical, or near-vertical, and it is covered with a different community. The sponges here are different species — taller, narrower, more branching, adapted to the different flow conditions on a vertical surface. The veldgrass does not grow here; the current is too strong, the substrate too steep. Instead, the wall is covered with organisms that filter the upwelling water that rises from the deep: crinoids, sea cucumbers, anemones, things that sit in the flow and catch what comes. The wall is a filter. It is the boundary between the plateau and the ocean, and the organisms that live on it are the boundary's inhabitants, the things that live on the edge, in the place between.

The eastern edge — where the tidal pulse pushes water against the escarpment — is more turbulent. The upwelling is strongest here, and the water that rises from the deep is colder, richer, more laden with nutrients. The community on the eastern wall is denser, more varied, more active. There are fish here that do not appear on the plateau itself — deeper-water species, things that live in the dim zone and come up the wall to feed on the upwelling. There are organisms that the plateau community does not include: deep-water corals, their branches fragile and pale, growing in the sheltered crevices of the wall. There are things that have not been named, that have not been seen, that exist in the space between the known and the unknown, in the place where the light fades and the water changes and the plateau is no longer the plateau.

The edge is where the plateau breathes. The upwelling brings nutrients. The downdraft takes waste. The exchange of water across the escarpment is the respiration of the system, the inhalation and exhalation that keeps the plateau alive. The organisms on the wall are the lungs. They are the tissue between the inside and the outside. They are the boundary, and they live there, and they are as much a part of the plateau as anything on the flat.

## What Does Not Live Here Anymore

There is a category of inhabitant that is absent, and the absence is itself a kind of presence.

The industrial installation — whatever it was, whatever it extracted, whatever it was for — had a human population. There must have been workers. There must have been a settlement, or a platform, or a rotation of personnel who came and went. They are gone. They have been gone for long enough that their remains — the housing, the infrastructure, the domestic artifacts — are not on the plateau. They were somewhere else, somewhere above the water line, somewhere on the coast or the mainland, and they have left no trace here. What they left here is the machinery, and the machinery is not them. The machinery is what they built, and it is being dismantled, and when it is gone, there will be nothing left of them on the plateau at all.

This is not a tragedy. It is not a lament. It is a description. The humans who worked here did not live here. They worked here. They came and went. They left their tools, and the tools are being taken apart by the water and the organisms, and when the tools are gone, the humans will have no presence here. The plateau will not remember them. The sponges will not remember them. The veldgrass will not remember them. The fish will not remember them. The seals — the seals might, in some dim, behavioral way, remember that this was a place where things were different, but the memory will fade, and the seals will die, and their descendants will know only the plateau as it is, not as it was.

The plateau is heading toward a future in which it will be nothing but rock and water and life. It is almost there. The humans are almost gone. Their machines are almost gone. What remains is the sound — the cracking and grinding of the machinery as the current works it loose — and when the sound stops, the last trace of the human presence will be gone, and the plateau will be complete. It will be finished. It will be what it was before the humans came, and it will be what it will be after they are forgotten.

## What It Is Like to Be Here

You cannot be here without becoming part of the sound. Your own body — your breathing, your movement, the displacement of water as you swim — becomes a layer in the sound climate. You add your frequency. The fish notice you. The seals notice you. The shrimp keep clicking. The current keeps moving. You are a transient, like the fish, like the seals, like the light that filters down from above. You are here, and then you are not, and the place goes on without you, and it does not notice your absence, because it did not notice your presence, because you are not the kind of thing that this place notices. You are too fast. You are too brief. You are too loud, and then you are too quiet, and the plateau is neither loud nor quiet. It simply is. It is the sound of water moving over finished stone, and the sound of life growing on the stone, and the sound of the stone being slowly, patiently, endlessly worn toward a state of rest.

What lives here is the plateau itself. The sponges and the veldgrass and the fish and the seals and the shrimp and the bacteria and the vine-mat and the things on the edge and the things in the sediment — they are not inhabitants of the plateau. They *are* the plateau. They are what the plateau is, in the same way that your cells are what you are. They are the living tissue of a geological body, and the body is old, and it is tired, and it is at rest, and it is more alive than anything you have ever seen.

This is what lives here: a place that is almost finished, and is in no hurry, and is full of life, and does not know it is full of life, and does not need to know.
