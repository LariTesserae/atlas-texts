# What Lives in the Weeping Hollow

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is in a hurry, and so what lives here is difficult to name as living. The distinction between resident and geology has worn thin. But things do live here. They have lived here longer than the moss-columns have stood in their patient rows, and they will be here when the notch in the rim has deepened enough to drain the hollow's shallow heart. They are not spirits. They are not gods. They are not hiding. They are simply so slow, and so much a part of the place they occupy, that the eye slides off them the way water slides off wet stone — not because of resistance but because there is no edge to catch against.

---

## The Listeners

Call them that, for now, because it is the thing they do most visibly. They are the ones who stand among the fern-stalks, and you would not see them if you did not already know to look, and even then you might doubt yourself.

They are tall, first of all — not in the way a person is tall but in the way a young birch is tall, narrow and upright, built for vertical space rather than horizontal occupation. Seven feet, perhaps, though the light here makes height uncertain, makes all measurement a guess you whisper to yourself and then abandon. Their bodies are the color of the wet schist — pale, greenish-gray, streaked in places with darker lines that follow what might be a spine, might be a system of vessels, might be ornamentation. It is difficult to tell because they are still, and have been still since before you arrived, and will be still after you leave. The stillness is not rigor. It is not the locked stillness of an animal pretending to be a tree. It is the stillness of something for which movement is a rare event, a decision made the way a mountain decides to shift — with full weight behind it, with inevitability, and only when the reasons have accumulated past some internal threshold that has nothing to do with urgency.

They stand among the fern-things because they are like the fern-things. Not the same species — the ferns are plants, rooted, feeding through their patient cups — but the Listeners share something with them. A posture. An orientation. The slight inward curl at the top, as though the crown of the body is cupped to receive what falls from above. In the Listeners, this cupping is more defined. The upper third of the body bends forward gently, and where a head might be there is instead a concavity — a shallow bowl, three or four inches deep, that fills with the water that sheets everything here. The water sits in it. It does not overflow. It does not evaporate. It simply sits, a small round lens of the hollow's weeping held in the body's highest point, and the Listener listens through it.

To what? To the water-chord. To the hum that the stone makes when a hundred trickles strike it at once and the bowl of the hollow amplifies them into a single sustained note. The Listeners are not ears but they are ear-shaped, in their whole bodies, and the sound enters them the way sound enters a seashell — through the opening, into the spiral, resonating. The water in their crown-bowls vibrates at frequencies below what you can hear, and the Listeners receive these vibrations the way the moss receives moisture: passively, continuously, without intention.

They have been doing this for a very long time. The water-chord carries information — not language, not message, but something older and less formed. The stone remembers. The water that has been seeping through it for centuries carries mineral traces, temperature signatures, the faint chemical record of every rain that fell on the ridge above and sank into the rock and was slowly, slowly pushed through the fractures and out the walls. The chord is the stone's memory played as sound. The Listeners hold this memory in the water in their crowns, and they do not use it. They do not need to. Holding it is enough. They are the hollow's remembrance, and they are adequate to the task because they ask nothing of it.

Sometimes one of them moves. Not often. Once in a season, perhaps, or once in a longer span — the moss would know, but the moss does not count. The movement is a step, and the step is slow, and the sound it makes is the sound of a foot pressing into wet moss and holding there, and then the other foot, and then stillness again in a new position. They step around the fern-stalks. They never step on them. They step over the water-film without disturbing its sheeting, which should not be possible for something their weight but is, in fact, exactly the kind of thing that is possible here, where the rules of physical disturbance have been negotiated downward to such a fine grain that even displacement is gentle.

If you sat and watched one for an hour, you would see nothing. If you sat and watched one for a week, you might notice it had changed position. If you sat and watched one for a year — and no one has, and no one will — you would see it move perhaps three times, each movement covering less than a body-length, each undertaken with the deliberateness of a thought so considered it has become physical. And you would notice, eventually, that the Listener had not moved randomly. It had moved toward sound. Toward a new seep opening in the wall, perhaps, or a shift in the chord's pattern as a fracture widened by a millimeter. It had moved the way a sunflower turns, slowly, with the whole body, toward what nourishes it.

There are four of them. Maybe five. The count is uncertain because in the flat, shadowless light, at a distance of thirty yards across the moss and the water-film, a Listener standing among the fern-stalks resembles a fern-stalk, and the resemblance is not mimicry. It is kinship. They came from the same patience. They were shaped by the same water.

## The Silver Threads

In the water-film, the things that move.

You see them as motion before you see them as bodies — a silver flash in the sheet of water, gone before the eye can track it, then another, then nothing, then one again, ten feet from where the first appeared. They are small. The length of a finger, perhaps, and the width of a thread, and they are legless, as the description noted, and they move by a mechanism that has nothing to do with swimming as vertebrates understand it. They flow. The body is a filament, and the filament moves through the shallow water the way a pulse moves through a vein — by sequential contraction, each segment pushing the one ahead of it forward, so that the whole creature traverses the film not by pushing against the water but by becoming a wave in the water.

They are feeding. The water-film is not empty — it carries the fine particulate that the weeping has washed from the stone, mineral dust and organic fragments and the microscopic bodies of things that lived and died in the moss above. The silver threads filter this as they move. Their bodies are open to the water, permeable in a way that skin is not, and the nutrients pass through them the way light passes through glass. They are translucent. You can see the vague interior — not organs but regions of density, darker nodes spaced along the filament's length that might be centers of processing or might simply be the places where the thread has eaten most thoroughly and so has grown more opaque.

They are not rare. If you crouched at the edge of the water-film and held your gaze on a single square foot of it for long enough, you would see a dozen pass through in an hour. But they do not announce themselves. They are the color of the water when the light hits it — silver, quick, insubstantial — and they have no interest in being observed. They have no interest in anything. They are processes, not personalities. They move and eat and continue, and their continuation is so perfectly matched to the conditions of the hollow that they have been doing this for as long as the water has been sheeting across the floor, which is to say, for longer than the Listeners have been listening, longer than the moss has been growing, longer than the fern-things have been feeding in their cups.

They are the oldest thing here. And they do not know it.

## The Moss-Undertakers

Beneath the moss-columns, in the peat that has built up from ten thousand generations of dead moss falling and compressing into a soft dark substrate, something else lives. You feel it before you see it — a pulse, a contraction, a shift in the moss's surface that is too rhythmic to be water and too slow to be animal. The moss-columns are standing on something that moves.

It is not a creature, exactly. It is a body — or a network of bodies, or a single continuous body that extends beneath the moss the way a mycelium extends beneath a forest floor, threading through the substrate, filling it, becoming it. It is the color of the peat: dark, almost black, wet, and it has the consistency of something between flesh and fiber. If you pressed your hand into the moss and felt downward, you would touch it — something firm and slightly warm, giving under pressure and then rebounding, drawing your fingers in and holding them with a gentleness that is more unsettling than any violence.

It eats the dead moss. This is its role. The moss-columns grow and die and fall and compress, and the thing beneath them absorbs this material, breaks it down, incorporates it. It is the undertaker of the hollow, the recycler, the thing that ensures nothing is wasted and nothing accumulates past the point of balance. Without it, the moss would build on itself forever, rising higher and higher, choking the floor. With it, the moss stays at its appointed height — six to eight inches, no more — and the balance holds.

But it does more than eat. It also moves the moss. The pulsing you feel is its circulatory rhythm — a slow expansion and contraction, perhaps once every several minutes, that pushes water and nutrients up through the peat and into the roots of the moss-columns. It is a heart for the moss. It is a stomach for the floor. It is, in its way, the most important thing living here, because without its work, the whole system would lose its equilibrium, the moss would run rampant, the fern-things would be crowded, the water-film would be interrupted, and the hollow would become something other than what it is.

It does not know this. It is not conscious in any way that word implies. It pulses because pulsing is what it does, the way the heart beats because beating is what the heart does. It has no ambition. It has no malice. If you stepped into the moss and it drew your foot in, it would not be attacking you. It would be doing what it does to everything that stands on it — testing, incorporating, breaking down. If you pulled away, it would let you go. If you did not pull away, it would take longer. It is patient. Everything here is patient. But the undertaker's patience is the most literal: it will wait as long as the thing it is breaking down requires, and it will not hurry, and it will not stop.

## The Rim-Walkers

These are the ones you almost see. The shapes that cross the upper edge of the hollow — bird-like, or not, gone before the eye can fix them.

There are two of them. They live on the rim, on the outer edge, in the mist that hangs just beyond the known. They do not enter the hollow. They do not need to. They perch on the lip of stone where the weeping-curtains hang and the water spills toward the notch, and they watch, and they are watched by nothing, and they make no sound that the water-chord does not absorb.

Their bodies are the color of the mist — gray-white, luminous at the edges, fading to translucence at the extremities. They are not birds, though they have a bird's alertness, a bird's quick-turning head, a bird's economy of motion. They are something else. Something that has found a niche at the boundary between the hollow and the outside world, and has filled that niche with the same restraint that everything here fills its niche.

They eat the insects. The small flies and gnats that hatch in the moss and rise into the warm, humid air and hover, aimlessly, in the shadowless light — these are their prey. They take them from the air with a motion so fast it is not a motion but an absence, a gap in the air where a fly was and is not, and then the Rim-Walker is still again, and the fly is gone, and the hollow has not changed.

They do not enter the hollow because the hollow does not need them inside. Their role is at the edge. They are the gatekeepers, though there is no gate and nothing to keep. They are the boundary's living thing, the fauna of the margin, and they exist in the mist and the light-haze because that is where the hollow's influence thins enough to allow something quick and sharp. Inside the hollow, everything is slow. At the rim, the quickness returns, and the Rim-Walkers are its expression.

If you climbed to the rim and looked at one directly, you would see something like a face — not a face but a concentration of features, two dark spots above a curved line that might be a beak or might be a mouth or might be nothing at all, a pattern in the feathers or the skin or whatever covers the body, a pareidolia that the mist and the light and your own need to see a face would construct from the available materials. The Rim-Walker would look back at you. It would not move. It would not flee. It would hold your gaze with a steadiness that belongs to something that has never been hunted, has never had reason to fear, and does not understand fear as a concept. Then it would turn, and step into the mist, and be gone, and you would not be sure you had seen it at all.

## What Is Not Here

There are no predators. There are no parasites. There are no diseases. This is not because the hollow is a paradise — paradise implies a judgment, a sweetness, a desirability. The hollow is none of these things. It is simply complete. The system has reached a state of closure so thorough that the niches we associate with dysfunction — the niche of the predator, the niche of the parasite, the niche of the pathogen — have no purchase here. Everything that lives here fits. Everything that does not fit does not live here. The system does not exclude. It simply does not accommodate, and the difference is that exclusion requires effort and accommodation requires effort and the hollow expends no effort. It holds. It has been holding. What fits, stays. What does not, was never here.

There are no bones in the peat. There are no skeletons in the moss. No animal has come here to die and be absorbed by the undertaker's patient body, because animals do not come here. Animals pass through. The Rim-Walkers live at the edge and do not enter. The insects that hatch in the moss are born and die here, but they are small enough that the system absorbs them without record — a wing in a pitcher-cup, a leg in the water-film, a body dissolved by the undertaker's slow chemistry, unremarked, unremembered, part of the balance.

## The Question of Awareness

Do the Listeners know you are here?

You want them to. You stand among the fern-things and the moss-columns and the water-film and you look at one of those tall, still, stone-colored bodies and you want it to see you. You want the water in its crown-bowl to ripple at your presence. You want the slight inward curl of its upper body to shift, to acknowledge, to register that something new has entered the hollow and is standing in the flat light and is breathing the warm wet air and is listening to the chord.

It does not ripple. It does not shift. The Listener holds its position, holds its water, holds the stone's memory in the bowl of its body, and you are either invisible to it or irrelevant to it or so brief, so small, so momentary against the scale of its listening that your presence does not register as an event. You are a weather pattern. You are a gust that will pass. The Listener will be here when you are gone, and it will have moved perhaps two feet in the time between your arrival and your departure, and it will not have moved because of you.

This is not coldness. This is not indifference. The Listeners do not have the capacity for indifference because they do not have the capacity for preference. They are not ignoring you. They are listening to the stone, and the stone does not mention you, because the stone does not know you are here either. You are too new. You are too quick. The stone's memory runs on the timescale of seepage, and you will be gone before the water that touches your boot has traveled six inches down the wall.

Sit down anyway. Sit on the moss — it will compress beneath you with that squeezed-sponge sound, and the undertaker beneath will pulse, once, adjusting to your weight, and then it will accept you as it accepts all dead weight, and it will begin, with infinite patience, to consider whether you are something it should process. It is slow. You have time. But it has begun.

The chord sounds. The light lies flat. The Rim-Walkers pass across the upper edge, quick and silent, and are gone. The silver threads trace their paths through the water-film, eating, flowing, continuing. The Listeners listen. The hollow holds.

And you are here, and you are not the point, and the place does not need you, and you have never been in a room that felt so much like it was complete without you, and the feeling is not rejection. The feeling is context. You are very small. You are very brief. The Weeping Hollow is a cathedral that has been holding its service for millennia and does not require a congregation, and you have wandered in during a moment between moments, and the moment will last long after you have left, and the moment was not waiting for you, and the moment is not diminished by your going.

The water weeps. The stone remembers. The Listeners hold what the stone remembers in the bowls of their bodies. The moss breathes. The undertaker pulses beneath you, patient, patient, patient. And you are a weather pattern, and you will pass, and the hollow will hold, and the holding is the only thing here that matters, and it matters to no one, and it is enough.
