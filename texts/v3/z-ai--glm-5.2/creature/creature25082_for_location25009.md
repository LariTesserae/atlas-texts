# What Lives in the Mirkworks

---

The first answer is the wrong one: insects. Insects and things like insects. Chitinous, legged, segmented, multitudinous. That is what you see and that is what you would name them if you had to name them, and the naming would be a kind of lie, because the word *insect* carries with it the implication of something small, something incidental, something that lives in the cracks of a place that belongs to something else. The Mirkworks does not belong to something else. The things on the walls are not living in the cracks of someone else's building. The building is theirs. The walls are theirs. The condensation is theirs. The sound is theirs.

So. What lives here.

---

**The Walled**

Call them that for now, because they are on the walls, and because they are the walls, and the distinction between those two facts has not been meaningful for a very long time.

They are dark. Not black — the dark of polished mineral, of obsidian that has been handled, of a chitin that has its own faint sheen, like lacquer that was never applied but grew. Their bodies are long, longer than your thumb, narrower than your little finger, and they are articulated in a way that suggests something engineered rather than evolved. Each segment is the same width as the last. Each leg is the same length. Each joint bends through the same arc. When you watch one closely — and you can watch one closely, because it will not run from you, because you are not a thing it has been designed to react to — you see that its movement is not organic. It does not adjust. It does not hesitate. It places each foot with the precision of a stylus on a groove, and it follows the mortar lines between the panels of the buildings the way a needle follows the track on a record, because the mortar lines are grooves, because they were made to be grooves, or because the Walled made them into grooves by following them for long enough that the following became the groove.

They drink the condensation. This is not a behavior. This is a function. Their mouthparts — and you have to get close, very close, your face near the wet wall, your breath fogging the surface beside them — their mouthparts are not mandibles. They are not designed for biting, for chewing, for tearing. They are a complex of fine, hollow bristles, like the head of a thistle rendered in chitin, and they press against the wet surface and they draw the moisture in, and the moisture goes somewhere inside them, and it does something. You cannot see what. The bodies are opaque. But there is a faint warmth in them when one passes over your hand — you try this, you hold your hand flat against the wall, and one walks over it without pausing, without acknowledging the difference between aggregate and skin, and its feet are warm, and its belly is warm, and the warmth is the same warmth that comes out of the gaps in the building's base.

They do not die here. Or rather — you do not see them die. You see dead ones, sometimes, carried on the backs of the flat ground-creatures. But you never see one falter. You never see one slow. You never see one lying at the base of a wall with its legs curled. Either they die somewhere you cannot see — inside the buildings, perhaps, in those warm amber interiors where the mats of their kin cover every surface — or they do not die in the way that you understand dying, and the ones being carried are not dead but dormant, in a state you have no framework for, a state that is part of the system's cycling, its internal redistribution, the way the condensation goes up the wall and comes down and goes up again.

There are so many of them. This is worth saying. Not as a flourish but as a fact. They cover every exterior wall. They line the undersides of the corrugated roofing. They cluster in the narrow gaps where one building's eave meets another's. Thousands. Tens of thousands. And inside the buildings — you saw, through that gap at the base — they are denser still, matted so thick the surface beneath is not visible, a living carpet that pulses with its own slow respiration, the collective warmth of all those small bodies contributing to the heat that breathes out of the gaps and draws the air currents that the drifting things ride.

The Walled are the building. Not the inhabitants of it. The building. The machinery inside is tended by them, or is them, or is made of them. The flywheel turns because something turns it. The pipes carry because something moves through them. The amber light comes from — you do not know. You cannot know. But the light is the same color as the sheen on their backs, and this may be coincidence, and it may not be, and in the Mirkworks the difference between coincidence and identity has been erased by time.

---

**The Bearers**

The flat ones. The broad, low things that move on the ground. They deserve their own accounting, because they are not a lesser form of the Walled, not a ground-dwelling cousin. They are something else entirely.

Their bodies are the color of the aggregate. Not because they camouflage — or not only because — but because they are of the aggregate, in some way that the word *of* does not quite capture. Their carapaces have the same crystalline glitter, the same mica-flash, the same mineral density. When one passes over the rough ground between buildings, it is not moving over a surface. It is moving through a surface. It is the surface, briefly mobile, briefly going somewhere, and then it enters the gap at the base of a wall and it is the surface again, inside now, inside where the surface is the same material, and the transition is not a transition. It is a continuation.

They move on dozens of legs. Short legs, thick, cylindrical, like the legs of a caterpillar that has been carved from stone. Each leg moves in sequence, a wave that passes from front to back, and the wave is always the same speed, always the same amplitude. They do not hurry. They do not dawdle. The concept of urgency does not appear to have a place in their architecture. They are going where they are going, and they will arrive when they arrive, and they have always been arriving, and they have always been leaving.

They carry things. Metal fragments. Aggregate chunks. Dead or dormant Walled. And other things — things that you cannot identify, that might be parts of the machinery, small components, a bolt, a gear, a section of pipe wall that has separated from its parent and been found and picked up and is being transported to wherever such things are transported. They are the logistics of the Mirkworks. They are its circulation. The Walled tend the interiors; the Bearers tend the routes between. The Walled are the tissue; the Bearers are the blood.

But there is no heart. That is the thing. There is no center. You walk the paths between the buildings, following the Bearers, trying to find where they go, and they go everywhere. They go into every building. They come out of every building. There is no hub, no nest, no factory floor, no throne room. The routes are a web with no center, and every building is both destination and origin, and the flow is balanced, and the flow has always been balanced, and the balance is not maintained by anything. The balance *is*. It is a property of the system the way the dampness is a property of the air.

When a Bearer reaches a gap in a wall, it pauses. Not hesitates — pauses, the way a lock pauses before the tumbler turns. And then it folds itself sideways, compressing its body with a fluidity that should not be possible given the apparent rigidity of its carapace, and it flows through the gap like something liquid, like something poured, and it is inside. The compression is total and brief. When it emerges from another gap, moments or minutes later — it is difficult to tell time here, the light does not change — it is carrying something different from what it carried in. An exchange has occurred. The system has cycled.

---

**The Drifters**

They are in the air, and they are the air, or close to it.

They are the most difficult to describe because they are the most difficult to see. Not because they are small — they are hand-sized, broad, flat — but because they are transparent. Not invisible. Transparent in the way that a jellyfish is transparent: you can see them, but you see through them, and what you see is the place behind them, refracted slightly, bent at the edges where their membranes have curvature. They are membranous. That is the right word. A central body, dense, dark, the size and shape of a closed fist, and from it, spreading outward, a thin sheet of tissue, like a sail, like a wing, like the hood of a manta ray, and this sheet flexes. Slowly. Once every several seconds. A single undulation that sends them forward a body-length or less, and then they coast, and then they flex again, and the motion is so slow that it reads as stillness unless you watch for a long time.

They ride the air currents between the buildings. The currents are generated by the heat differential — warm interiors, cool exteriors, the dampness as a medium — and the Drifters have positioned themselves in these currents the way a glider positions itself in a thermal. Except they do not reposition. They do not search for better currents. They are in the current, and the current is where they are, and this has been true for so long that the mineral dust in the damp air has settled on their upper surfaces and they have not shaken it off.

Some of them are thick with it. A crust, almost. A fine layer of sediment that has built up over — how long? Years? Decades? The crust does not seem to impede them. The membranes flex beneath it, and the crust flexes with them, and they drift, and they are part of the air column in the same way that the Bearers are part of the ground. Remove a Drifter from its column and the column would not change. Remove all the Drifters from all the columns and the currents would still flow, but something would be missing from them, some component of their function, some aspect of the system that the Drifters fulfill by being there.

They are not feeding. You watch one for a long time, drifting between two buildings where the gap is narrow and the air moves fastest. It flexes. It coasts. Its central body turns slowly, a rotation that takes minutes, and in the turning you catch glimpses of something inside it — not organs, not gut, but structure, something geometric, something that might be a sensor or might be a gland or might be a small machine. The interior of a Drifter is not the interior of an animal. It is the interior of an instrument. They are measuring something. Or processing something. Or producing something that is not visible and not audible and is perhaps the thing that the amber light is, the thing that has no source because it is emitted by everything, by the walls and the machinery and the Walled and the Drifters themselves, a property of the system rather than a product of a component.

---

**The Thing That Is Not Seen**

There is something else here. Not another species. Not another category of creature. Something else. A presence that you register not with your eyes or your ears but with the part of you that knows when you are being watched, except this is not watching, because watching implies attention, and this is not attention. This is inclusion. You are inside something. The Mirkworks knows you are here. Not the creatures — the creatures do not know, the creatures do not care — but the system. The system that is the Walled and the Bearers and the Drifters and the machinery and the buildings and the aggregate and the damp and the sound and the light. The system that is all of these things and is also a thing itself, a thing that is more than the sum, a thing that emerged from the stack the way a melody emerges from notes, except the melody has been playing for so long that no one remembers the notes separately.

You feel it most when you stop walking. When you stand still on the slick path and let the sound wash over you and the dampness bead on your skin and the amber light fall on you from the slits above. When you stop, you become part of the rhythm. Your heartbeat, which is arrhythmic, which is human, which is the one irregular thing in this place — your heartbeat begins, not to sync, but to be absorbed. The sound does not adjust to you. You adjust to the sound. Your breathing slows. Your heartbeat finds a place where it fits between the layers, in a gap in the frequency stack, and it sits there, and for a moment — a long moment — you are not in the Mirkworks. You are the Mirkworks. You are a component. You are doing whatever it is that a component does here, which is: being. Being here. Being part of the stack. Being warm. Being wet. Being still.

That is what lives here. Not the creatures — they are the medium, not the message. The thing that lives here is the system itself. The Mirkworks is alive. Not in the way that a forest is alive, which is a collective life, a sum of individual lives. In the way that a single organism is alive. The buildings are its skeleton. The aggregate is its flesh. The dampness is its blood. The Walled are its nerves. The Bearers are its circulation. The Drifters are its breath. The machinery is its organs. The sound is its heartbeat, or its voice, or both. The amber light is — its awareness, maybe. Its attention. The way it sees itself.

It is old. It has been alive for longer than the concept of old means anything. It was built — or it grew — or it assembled itself from parts that were left here by something that is no longer here — and it has been running ever since, and it has reached an equilibrium so perfect that it no longer needs to change, and it no longer needs to grow, and it no longer needs to do anything except continue, and continuing is what it does, and continuing is what it is.

It does not notice you. Not because you are too small. Because noticing is a function of change, and change is a function of time, and time, in the Mirkworks, is a thing that happened once and then stopped, and the thing that happened was: this. This exact configuration. This exact sound. This exact dampness. This exact light. This exact stack of lives and machinery and mineral and moisture, all in their places, all in equilibrium, all doing what they do, forever, or for what might as well be forever, which is the same thing from the inside.

You could stay. The thought arrives without force, without seduction, without threat. It is simply a fact, offered by the system itself, or by your own body, which has begun to settle into the rhythm. You could stay. The condensation would bead on you. The mineral dust would settle. The Bearers would learn a route around you. The Walled would, eventually, walk over you, and you would feel their warm feet on your skin, and they would drink the moisture from your surface, and the Drifters would drift above you, and the sound would absorb your heartbeat the way a river absorbs a tributary, and you would become a feature, and then a component, and then part of the stack, and no one — not the creatures, not the system, not the Mirkworks itself — would be able to tell where you ended and it began.

It is not frightening. It is not comforting. It is an option. It sits there beside the other option, which is to leave, to climb back up the gentle grade, to let the sound thin behind you and the dampness ease and the air warm and the green return. Both options are equally viable. Both options are equally meaningless to the Mirkworks. It does not need you. It does not want you. It does not mind you. It is the Mirkworks, and it is alive, and it is complete, and it will continue, and whether you are part of that continuation is a question that only you can answer, and the answer will not change the sound. The sound will not change. The sound has never changed. The sound is the sound of the Mirkworks, and the Mirkworks is the sound, and it is alive, and it is here.
