# What Lives Here

They are not a people who were meant to be found, and so the describing of them feels like a kind of trespass. But they are here. They have been here. And they are worth the trespass.

---

## Bodies

The first thing you would notice, if you met one of them in the water, is that they do not move like swimmers. They move like *inhabitants*. There is no stroke, no kick, no efficient horizontal passage through the water. They move the way a person moves through a house they have lived in for decades — with familiarity, with contact, with the minimum effort required to arrive where they are going. A hand on a *thara* stalk. A foot braced against a stone lip. A shoulder turning against a current that is barely there. They do not fight the water. They lean into it, the way you lean into a familiar weight.

Their bodies are adapted to the work of this place in ways that are not dramatic but are thorough, the way a tool becomes shaped by its use over years. They are lean — not from scarcity, but from the constant thermal demand. The water is warm by oceanic standards, twenty-four, twenty-five degrees, but water pulls heat from a body faster than air, and they live in it. They live in it the way a mountain people live in cold: not by fighting it but by carrying more of it inside. Their metabolism runs high. They eat often, and they eat rich — the pale fish they catch in the canopy, the microbial mats they harvest from the sediment between stalks, the fibrous inner tissue of younger *thara* stalks, which is starchy and faintly sweet. They eat raw, because there is no fire here. There has never been fire here. The idea of fire would be as strange to them as the idea of drowning.

Their skin is dark — not simply pigmented, but dark in a way that seems to absorb the dim green light rather than reflect it. In the water, at any distance, they are visible mostly as absences: darker shapes against the dark stone, or silhouettes against the pale glow of a *thara* canopy. This is not camouflage by design. It is the result of generations living in a place where light is scarce and the advantage goes to those who are not seen. They do not think of themselves as hidden. They think of themselves as *here*, and the darkness is part of here.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely, but noticeably — the irises wider, the pupils more dilated in the dim, the whites less prominent. This is the adaptation that is most immediately visible and most immediately unsettling to a surface-dweller, because it reads as hunger, or intensity, or something not quite human. It is none of these. It is simply the eye of an animal that has evolved to gather photons in a cathedral of green shadow. They see better here than you would. They see the *thara* fronds' veins, the mineral grain of the soft stone, the slow drift of particulate through the still water. They see each other. They read each other's bodies — the tension in a shoulder, the angle of a head, the position of hands — with a fluency that makes their gestural language possible, because the language depends on seeing, and seeing depends on light, and the light here demands eyes that can work with almost nothing.

Their hands are their most expressive feature, and the one they would identify as central to who they are. A person here is known by their hands — by the calluses on the palms from shaping soft clay, by the small scars from stone edges, by the particular way they hold a fiber basket, by the sensitivity of their fingertips to the temperature of emerging stone (a degree of warmth tells you how long you have to work it before it hardens). Children learn the world through their hands before they learn it through their eyes. A soft-ground tender can tell you the mineral content of a fresh stone flow by pressing a thumb into it and reading the resistance, the warmth, the texture. They can tell you whether a *thara* stalk is healthy by closing their hand around it and feeling the faint pulse of fluid through its vascular tissue. Their hands are their primary sensory organs. Their hands are their language. Their hands are how they know what is real.

They are not tall. This is not a deficiency; it is an adaptation. The dwellings are low-ceilinged, built around the *thara* stalks, and the spaces between structures are narrow, and the water is thick enough that a large body is a body that loses more heat and expends more energy per movement. The ideal body here is compact, dense, efficient. They are built for the space they inhabit, the way a root is built for the soil it grows in.

## Breath

The breath is the rhythm that structures everything, and it is the thing that is hardest for an outsider to understand, because it is not a limitation they resent. It is the *tempo* of their lives.

Here is what it is like: you surface — they do not call it surfacing, they call it *returning* — you swim to a capture chamber, you find the air pocket trapped under its stone dome, you press your face up into it, and you breathe. The air is warm. It is wet. It tastes of iron and sulfur and the deep mineral signature of the earth's interior. It is not fresh. It is not clean. It is *alive* in a way that surface air is not — it carries the memory of the stone it came from, the heat of the mantle that drove it upward. Breathing it is like drinking from a spring that has never seen sky.

You fill your lungs. You hold it. You feel the pressure of the water around your body, the weight of sixty meters of ocean above you, and you dive back down into the green dim, and you work until the breath runs thin, and then you return.

Four minutes. Five, if you are resting. Six, if you are very skilled and very calm and the work is gentle. This is not much. But the work here is not much in any given moment. The stone emerges slowly. The *thara* grows slowly. A wall is shaped over days, not hours. A garden is tended over a lifetime, not a season. The breath is not a constraint on the work; the breath and the work are *the same pace*. They have evolved together. The people and the place have found a shared tempo, and the tempo is: slow enough to live on a breath, fast enough to matter before the breath runs out.

Children learn the breath early. An infant is carried to the capture chamber by its mother or father, held up into the air pocket, brought back down. The child does not understand what is happening, but the body learns: the rhythm of air and water, the cycle of filling and emptying, the understanding that the world above the air is where you *are* and the world below it is where you *do*. By the time a child can swim independently — four, five years old — they can hold a breath for a minute. By adolescence, three. By adulthood, they have stopped counting, because counting implies that the breath is something separate from the work, and it is not. It is the work.

There is a skill, taught to adolescents, that has no surface equivalent. It is called *holding the work in the breath*, and it means: learning to feel the moment when the air is beginning to thin, and pacing the work so that it arrives at a natural stopping point at the same moment the breath does. A wall that is half-shaped when the breath runs out is a wall that will harden wrong. A *thara* root that is half-guided when you must return for air is a root that will grow wrong. So you learn to feel the breath and the work as one thing, to let them end together, to finish a gesture as you finish the air, so that the return to the chamber is not an interruption but a cadence. This is the deepest skill of this culture. It is what makes them not just survivors but *inhabitants*. They have learned to live at the pace of their own breathing, which is also the pace of the stone, which is also the pace of the *thara*, which is also the pace of the earth.

## Gesture

They do not speak. This is not a loss. It is a fact of the environment — sound travels differently underwater, and the air they breathe is too precious to spend on vibrating their throats — that has been so thoroughly absorbed into who they are that the absence of speech does not feel like an absence. It feels like a design choice. The water is a place of silence, and they are a people of the silence, and their language is the language of the body, which is the only language that works here.

The gestural system is dense. It is not a sign language in the way that terrestrial sign languages are — it is not a encoding of a spoken language, because there is no spoken language to encode. It is a language that developed in the water, for the water, by people whose hands were already their primary way of knowing the world. Its grammar is spatial. A gesture's meaning depends on where it is made — in front of the body, beside it, behind it; high, low; toward the stone, toward the *thara*, toward the open water above. The same hand-shape means different things depending on its position relative to the body and to the place. This is a language that *could not be transplanted*. It belongs to these bodies in this place.

There are gestures for the practical things: *the stone is ready to shape*, *this vent is slowing*, *the thara here is dying*, *come help me hold this*. There are gestures for the organizational things: *this is my work today*, *that is your work*, *we should build here, not there*. There are gestures for the social things: *I am tired*, *I am content*, *I am thinking about something*, *leave me be for a while*.

And there are gestures for the things that are harder to categorize. There is a gesture that means *the stone is still warm*, which is used literally, to indicate that a fresh flow has not yet hardened — but which is also used metaphorically, to mean *this is not finished*, *this is still in progress*, *this is still becoming*. There is a gesture that means *the thara roots have reached the stone*, which is used literally when a gardener reports that a planting has taken hold — but which is also used to mean *the foundation is set*, *we can build on this now*, *this will hold*. There is a gesture that means *I can hear the vent*, which is used to say *the earth is still breathing*, which is used to say *everything is as it should be*.

The language has no tenses. This is significant. The *thara* does not grow in tenses — it grows continuously, the past of its roots present in the stone, the future of its fronds present in the dim light above. The stone does not harden in tenses — it is always in the process of hardening, always between soft and set, always becoming. The people do not think in tenses. They think in *states of becoming*: this stone is further along than that stone; this *thara* is closer to maturity than that one; this dwelling is closer to completion than its neighbor. The past is present in the hardened stone around them, in the dead gardens, in the old foundations. The future is present in the soft clay emerging from the vents, in the young *thara* just planted, in the unshaped possibilities of the next flow. Everything is always in the middle of becoming, and the language reflects this. There is no *was*. There is no *will be*. There is only *is, and is further along, and is not yet*.

They have a gesture for death. It is subtle — a closing of the hand, slowly, as if around something that is no longer there. It is used when a *thara* stalk dies. It is used when a vent quiets. It is used when a person does not return from the water, which happens — not often, but it happens, a breath held too long, a body caught in a current, a person who went to the edge of the plateau and did not come back. The gesture is the same in all cases. Death here is not a category distinct from other endings. It is one more thing that has finished becoming, one more structure whose stone has hardened and whose roots have gone quiet. They do not fear it the way surface people fear it. They regard it the way they regard a vent that has gone silent — with attention, with a kind of respect, with the understanding that what has hardened was once soft, and that the softness does not disappear, it just changes into something else.

## Social Life

Four hundred people is a specific kind of number. It is small enough that everyone knows everyone, and large enough that not everyone is close. It is the size of a village, but the village is not a village in any familiar sense — it is not a cluster of houses around a common space. It is a *layered* settlement, vertical and horizontal at once, its structures interpenetrating, its spaces shared in ways that would be impossible on land.

A dwelling is not private in the way a surface dwelling is private. It is a shaped space — stone walls, *thara* pillars, a canopy overhead — but it is open to the water, and water does not respect walls the way air does. Currents pass through. Neighbors are present as silhouettes, as the sound of their tools, as the movement of their bodies in the dim green light. You can see the glow of someone else's capture chamber from your own dwelling. You can feel the vibration of someone shaping stone three chambers away. Privacy here is not seclusion. Privacy is *attention*: when the people around you are attending to you, you are not private; when their attention is elsewhere, you are, regardless of proximity.

This has shaped their social life in ways that are hard to fully convey. There is no hiding here. A person's moods are visible in their body — in the way they move, the way they hold their hands, the way they breathe (or do not). There is no facial expression to mask, because faces are less expressive here; there is no voice to modulate, because there is no voice. The body is the only text, and it cannot lie. This has made them honest in a way that is not moral but practical. Deception would require a private space, and there are no private spaces. The community is the space, and the community is always attending.

Roles are not assigned. They are *recognized*. A child who shows an early sensitivity to the temperature and texture of emerging stone becomes a soft-ground tender. A child who is patient with young *thara*, who sits still for long periods beside a new planting and watches its fronds for signs of health, becomes a gardener. A child who is strong and steady with a stone blade becomes a builder, or a *thara*-wood cutter, or a toolmaker. The community recognizes the aptitude and creates the opportunity, and the person grows into the role over years, the way a *thara* grows into the stone — slowly, through contact, through the patient integration of one thing with another.

There is no hierarchy. This is not ideology. It is structure. Four hundred people cannot support a hierarchy — the overhead is too high, the distance between top and bottom too expensive in breath and time. Decisions are made by the people who will be affected by them, in the places where they will have effect. A decision about a garden is made by the gardeners. A decision about a capture chamber is made by the people who breathe from it. A decision about the settlement as a whole — rare, because the settlement is not a whole, it is a cluster of overlapping concerns — is made by whoever shows up, in whatever space is large enough to hold them, by gesture and by consensus and by the kind of patience that comes from knowing the decision does not need to be made now, because the stone is still soft, and the *thara* is still growing, and the thing being decided will not be built for years.

They care for each other in ways that are constant and small. A neighbor checks your *thara* while you are at a distant vent. A colleague shapes a wall for you when your breath is short. A child is held up to a capture chamber by whoever is nearest, not necessarily a parent — because the air belongs to everyone, and the child belongs to everyone, and the distinction between yours and mine is a distinction that costs energy and gains nothing. They share food. They share tools. They share warmth — and warmth is shared by proximity, by sitting close in the still water between two *thara* stalks, by letting the body's heat stand against another body's heat, a mutual conservation that is not intimacy in the surface sense but is something deeper: a recognition that the cold is the shared condition, and the warmth is the shared solution, and neither one is yours alone.

## Children

Children are born in the capture chambers.

This requires explaining. A capture chamber is a stone dome, a few cubic meters of trapped volcanic gas, warm and mineral-thick, above the warm water. It is not a comfortable space by surface standards. It is cramped, dark, wet, and the air is barely breathable. But it is *air*, and for a people who live on borrowed breaths, the capture chamber is the closest thing to a place where the body can relax its discipline. A woman in labor can breathe continuously in a capture chamber. She does not need to time her contractions to her breath. She can push, and rest, and push again, in air, while the water below holds her body's weight and the stone dome holds the air.

The birth is attended by whoever is near. This is not a formal gathering. It is a practical response to a need: someone is in the chamber, working through something difficult, and the people around attend. They bring food. They bring fiber for wrapping the newborn. They hold the woman's body steady in the water when she descends from the air pocket to cool, to let the water take the weight. They hold the infant up into the air, into the breath, for the first time.

The infant's first experience is breath. Not light — the light in a capture chamber is dim, filtered through stone and water and *thara*. Not warmth — the air is warm but the water is warmer. Breath. The mineral taste of volcanic air filling lungs for the first time. This is the foundational sensory experience of a life here: the air, and then the water, and then the air again, and the understanding — which comes not as thought but as rhythm, as body-knowledge — that life is the passage between the two.

Children grow slowly here. Not physically — they grow at the rate their bodies dictate, which is the rate of any human body — but in the sense of becoming a person. A child is not a *person* in the full sense until they can hold a breath and do work. Before that, they are carried. They are held in the water by parents and neighbors, brought to the air, taken back down. They are present but not yet participating. They watch. They feel. They learn the rhythm of the place through the bodies that hold them — the rhythm of going to the air and coming back, of working and resting, of the stone emerging and hardening, of the *thara* growing and spreading. They absorb the pace before they understand the content.

When a child first holds a breath long enough to swim from one place to another without being carried — this is a moment. It is not marked with ceremony, because ceremony requires a formality that this culture does not produce. It is marked with *attention*. The people who see it happen stop what they are doing and watch, and the child swims through the green water, and the people watch, and the child arrives, and the people return to their work. That is the ceremony: being seen. Being attended to. The community's silent acknowledgment that a new person has entered the working body of the settlement.

Children play, but their play is not separate from the life of the place. They play in the gardens — chasing the small pale fish that shelter in the canopy, hiding among the *thara* stalks, swimming through the gaps in the dwellings' walls. Their play is physical, tactile, exploratory. They learn the geometry of the settlement by moving through it — where the capture chambers are, where the soft stone is, which gardens are dense and which are thin, where the edge of the plateau is and why you do not go there. They learn by touch and by movement, not by instruction. Instruction comes later, when the aptitude appears, when the role begins to take shape, when the child starts to become a tender or a gardener or a builder. Before that, they learn by *being*, by inhabiting the place the way a fish inhabits a reef — not as a set of rules to follow but as a set of conditions to live within.

## Old Age and Dying

People live shorter lives here than on the surface. Not dramatically shorter — but the thermal demand is constant, the mineral air is not kind to lungs over decades, and the water takes its toll on joints and skin and the slow patience of the body. A person who lives to fifty is old. A person who lives to sixty is a wonder.

Old people do not retire, because there is no *tirement* to *re* from. There is only the work, and the work changes as the body changes. An old soft-ground tender, whose hands have lost the sensitivity to read emerging stone, becomes a keeper of the capture chambers — tending the gas flow, maintaining the seals, ensuring that the air is there when the younger people need it. An old gardener, whose eyes can no longer read the fine details of a *thara* frond's health, becomes a planner — sitting in the still water between the stalks, thinking about where the next garden should be planted, where the next vent might open, where the settlement will need to grow in ten years or twenty. They think in the long time, the time beyond their own bodies, the time of the stone and the *thara*.

Old people are valued for their memory. There is no writing. There is no record beyond the stone itself. What is known is what is remembered, and what is remembered is what the old people carry. They remember where vents used to be, and where they might open next, because the pattern of the fissures is a pattern that repeats over generations, and the old have seen it repeat. They remember which *thara* plantings succeeded and which failed, and why. They remember the shapes of dwellings that no longer exist, whose stone has hardened and whose gardens have died, and they remember the people who built them, and the way those people held their hands when they shaped the clay. They are the settlement's library, and like all libraries, they are irreplaceable, and like all libraries, they are slowly being lost.

When an old person dies, the settlement loses something it cannot rebuild. The people around the death gather. Not in a formal way — they simply come, as they come to anything, drawn by the absence that the body's stillness creates in the water. They hold the body. They bring it to a place where the *thara* has died and the stone is hard and old, and they lay it on the stone, and they place stones on it, slowly, over hours, over a day, building a cairn in the dim green water. The body becomes part of the plateau. It does not decay in the way a body decays on the surface — the water is warm, and the microbial ecology is sparse, and the decomposition is slow. Over months, over years, the body is incorporated into the sediment, into the stone, into the place. Nothing leaves. Everything stays.

The gesture for a person's death is the same gesture used for a vent that has gone quiet: the slow closing of the hand. The earth has stopped breathing here. The stone has hardened. What was soft has set. What was becoming has become.

## What They Are

They are a people made by a place that is still being made.

That is the sentence that contains them, but it does not exhaust them, because no sentence can. They are four hundred people, give or take, living in a town that moves, on a plateau that grows, under water that dims, beside vents that murmur, among plants that glow. They breathe stolen air. They shape soft ground. They plant for grandchildren. They die and become stone. They have no word for progress because progress implies a destination, and they have no destination. They have only the becoming — the stone becoming hard, the *thara* becoming wood, the vent becoming quiet, the child becoming a tender, the tender becoming old, the old becoming the ground.

They are not unhappy. They are not happy. These are surface words, words that belong to a world of light and air and the sharp distinctions that light and air make possible. Here, in the green dim, in the warm water, in the slow time, the words do not apply. They are *attending*. They are *present*. They are *becoming*, with the stone and the *thara* and the vent and the breath, and the becoming is not good or bad, not progress or decline. It is simply what is happening, here, where the earth is still young, where the ground is still soft, where the light is a rumor, where the air is a gift, and where the people tend what grows and what grows tends them, and the tending is the life, and the life is the tending, and it goes on.
