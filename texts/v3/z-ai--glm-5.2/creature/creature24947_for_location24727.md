# What Lives There

The first thing that lives there is the place itself, and this must be said plainly even if it cannot be understood plainly: the Verdant Mass is alive. Not alive the way a forest is alive — a collection of living things standing on dead ground — but alive the way a body is alive, the way a reef is alive, the way a thing that has no single head and no single heart and no single boundary between self and not-self can be alive. The substrate breathes. The substrate metabolizes. The substrate grows upward while it decays downward, and the boundary between those two processes is not a line but a zone, a thick middle country where decomposition and generation happen in the same cell at the same time, and no one has ever determined which is winning because winning may not be the right word for what is happening there.

So. The Mass lives. And within it, everything else.

---

## The Filamentous Layer

Begin at the surface. Begin with the fuzz.

The top centimetres of the Mass are a meadow of filaments — hair-thin, colourless or faintly green, each one grooved along its length with microscopic channels that wick water by capillary action. They are not roots. They are not stems. They are something closer to cilia — sensory, absorptive, responsive. Touch them and they flatten. Wet them and they swell and stand erect. They cover everything, every column, every plate, every dead stem and living rhizome, a universal pelt that makes the whole surface of the continent soft to the touch and slightly damp and faintly warm, like the skin of something sleeping.

Within this layer lives an ecosystem so dense and so fine that it has no analogue. Each handful of filamentous growth contains, by the estimates of the few naturalists who have spent time on the Mass's edge, more individual organisms than there are people in any city on Earth. Most are not visible to the unaided eye. They are mites — but not mites as we know them. They are threadworms — but their threads are not for locomotion. They are rotifers — but they have been recast by ten million years of evolution in a medium that never dries, never freezes, and is always, always warm. They have the look of things that have been left in a warm, wet place for a very long time and have forgotten every adaptation that was not needed. Many have no eyes. Several lineages have no mouths — they absorb nutrients directly through the cuticle from the living substrate itself, making them not parasites but embedments, organisms that live inside a living thing the way marrow lives inside bone.

There are predators in this microscopic world. They are not dramatic. They are ciliates with inflated oral cavities that engulf other ciliates. They are tardigrade-like things with stylet mouthparts that pierce the filaments and drink the cell contents, which are not sap but something closer to lymph — nutrient-rich, faintly alkaline, carrying signalling compounds whose function is not understood. There is a whole food web here, a whole trophic architecture, and it operates at a scale where a centimetre is a territory and a millimetre is a home range and a raindrop is a flood.

The people of Keswer do not know about this world. Or rather — they know about it the way you know about the bacteria in your gut. They know the surface is alive. They know it moves. They know that if you leave a piece of dried curtain-material on the ground overnight, it will be soft again by morning and beginning to put out new growth. They do not need to see what makes it work. They know it works.

## The Plate-Growers

The lily-pad forms — the flat, spreading plates that sit on the surface — are not one species. They are dozens, perhaps hundreds, and they are engaged in a competition so slow it makes glaciation look hasty. Each plate is a colonial organism, a disc of fused tissue that grows outward from a central attachment point, spreading across the filamentous layer, pressing down, weighting the substrate beneath it, digesting what it covers and incorporating the nutrients into its own expanding edge. Where two plates meet, they do not merge. They ridge. The boundary zone rises — a wall of compressed, hardened tissue, sometimes a centimetre high, sometimes ten, a miniature mountain range formed by two colonies pushing against each other with the hydraulic patience of things that have nowhere else to go.

Some plates are pale — a whitish green, almost luminous in the early light, and these are the ones the browsers prefer. They crop the edges where the tissue is youngest and thinnest, their mandibles shearing through the soft growth with that sound — that scissors-sound, that endless fabric-cutting that carries across the open ground between the columns. Other plates are dark — almost black, dense, fibrous, and these are the ones the people of Keswer harvest. They cut slabs from the dark plates, dry them on the cliff-edge in the sun and wind, and the resulting material is hard as wood, light, easily worked. Most of Keswer's structures are built from it. The floors. The walls. The handles of their tools. The plates they eat from. Everything in Keswer that is not stone or cordage is dried plate-growth, and the people who live there have the texture of it in their hands from birth.

There are things that live on the plates. Small things. Flat things. The kind of life that lives on a surface and nowhere else — scale-insects, if they were insects, which they are not, but the analogy holds. They are pale, sessile, attached along the ridges where the plates meet, and they feed on whatever moves through the boundary tissue — water, nutrients, signalling compounds. They are not numerous enough to damage the plates. They are numerous enough to be a food source, and the things that eat them are themselves eaten by larger things, and so on, up and up, the trophic ladder climbing toward the browsers and the predators and whatever else the Mass supports that no one has catalogued because the Mass does not welcome cataloguers.

## The Columns

The columns are alive. This is obvious. But the way they are alive is not obvious, and it matters.

Each column is a bundle — dozens, sometimes hundreds of individual stems fused along their length, sharing resources through their fused vascular tissue, growing together, expanding together, contracting together. They are not one organism. They are a committee. And the committee has been meeting for a very long time — the oldest columns, in the deep interior, are estimated at four to six thousand years, based on nothing more reliable than the rate of growth visible at the edges and the assumption that the interior is older, which it must be. The columns do not have rings. They do not have any internal marker of age. They have layers, but the layers are not annual — they are deposited in response to growth conditions, and growth conditions on the Mass are determined by the Mass itself, which means the layers are a record of the Mass's own metabolic history, a history that no one can read because it was not written for anyone to read.

Within the columns, in the chambers hollowed by decay and lined with secretion, live the predators. There are also other things. The chambers are not empty between hunts — they are occupied continuously, by a succession of organisms that use them the way animals use burrows. There are things that live in the column walls themselves — borers, not insects, something else, something with a mandible-equivalent and a body like a soft cylinder and no eyes at all, tunnelling through the living wood, their tunnels later colonized by fungi-analogues that digest the waste, the waste then absorbed by the column's own vascular tissue, the nutrients returned. The columns are not just structures. They are ecosystems. Each one is a vertical reef, a column of habitat from the ground to the crown, and the predators that live in the lower chambers are only the largest and most visible of the things that call a single column home.

There are things that live in the upper columns that no one from Keswer has ever seen. The people know this because the spore-curtains, twice a year, deposit material on the cliff-edge that includes fragments of organisms not found anywhere on the ground — wings, not insect-wings but something larger, membranous, veined; pieces of carapace; unidentified structures that might be limbs or might be mouthparts or might be something for which no word exists. These fragments drift down from the upper reaches of the columns, forty metres up, where the air is thinner and drier and the growth is different — sparser, harder, adapted to a regime of direct sun and wind and the constant stress of being the highest point on a continent that is itself the highest thing for a thousand kilometres in any direction. There is a canopy ecosystem up there. The people of Keswer know this the way they know the Mass is deciding something — not by evidence that would satisfy a naturalist, but by the accumulated knowledge of generations who have lived at the edge of something and have learned to read its fragments the way a coastal people reads driftwood.

## The Browsers

Call them what they are. They are the cattle of the Mass.

They are large — four hundred to six hundred kilograms, standing two and a half metres at the highest point of their long, narrow bodies, their six legs lifting them clear of the substrate like stilts. They move in herds, and the herds move along paths, and the paths are worn into the substrate by the passage of uncounted generations, the same routes followed for centuries or millennia, the ground between certain columns compressed and hardened by traffic until it is almost firm.

They are not mammals. They are not insects. They are something that found a body plan that works on a plant-continent and refined it until it could not be improved. Their legs are jointed, but the joints are not hinge-joints — they are pivot-joints, allowing lateral motion, which means a browser can walk sideways as easily as forward, and often does, cropping a wide swath of plate-growth without turning its body. Their mandibles are paired, opposed, and they move in a scissoring motion that produces — yes — that sound, the sound of the Mass at midday, the sound of ten thousand shears working at once.

They have no eyes. They do not need eyes. They navigate by the chemical gradients of the substrate — the plates they prefer emit volatile compounds that are detectable at distance, and a herd of browsers can locate a productive feeding-ground from kilometres away by following the scent the way a moth follows a pheromone trail. They also sense vibration. The substrate transmits vibration with the fidelity of a drum-head, and a browser standing on the surface can feel the footsteps of other browsers a kilometre away, can feel the clicking of columns, can feel — perhaps — the deep subsonic tones that rise from the Mass's interior at dusk, the ones that have no explanation.

They are not stupid. This needs saying, because the absence of eyes invites the assumption of absence of mind. But the browsers exhibit behaviours that are not instinctive, not reflexive, not explained by simple stimulus-response. They mourn. When a browser is taken by a predator, the individual nearest to it — not a relative, not a calf, just the nearest individual — will stand over the place where the browser was dragged down, and will remain there for an hour or more, mandibles still, legs locked, not feeding. The herd moves on. The mourner catches up later. No one from Keswer has ever seen two browsers mourn the same loss. It is always one. Always the nearest.

The people of Keswer hunt the browsers. They dig pitfalls at the edges of established paths, lining them with sharpened plate-wood stakes, and they wait. When a browser falls, they kill it quickly — a stake to the joint where the neck meets the body, which is the only place the carapace is thin enough to penetrate with hand tools. They butcher it on the substrate, and the Mass receives the blood and the offal and the indigestible parts, and within a day there is nothing left. The people take the meat, the sinew, the chitin. They use everything. They have been doing this for as long as anyone can remember, and the browsers have not changed their paths, have not learned to avoid the pitfalls, have not — as far as anyone can tell — adjusted their behaviour at all. This might mean the browsers do not learn. This might mean the losses are acceptable. This might mean something else.

## The Predators

They come out at dusk. The light changes — the amber going to copper going to the unnamed colour — and they emerge from the column-base chambers, and they are silent in a way that nothing else on the Mass is silent, because everything else on the Mass is making noise all the time, and the predators are the exception.

They are flat. This is the first thing you notice if you see one, which you probably will not, because they are the colour of the substrate and they move with a fluidity that suggests they have been practising this specific skill for a very long time. Their bodies are wide — two metres across, sometimes more — and low, no more than half a metre at the highest point, and their legs are not legs but flanges, extensions of the body wall that grip the substrate by adhesion or by some mechanical means, and they flow across the ground rather than walking.

They take browsers by the leg. They do not go for the throat. They do not go for the body. They take a leg — one leg, a hind leg, usually the third pair — and they grip it and they pull, and the browser goes down, and the substrate opens under it, and the browser is gone. It happens in seconds. The other browsers do not run. They do not even stop chewing. The scissors-sound continues without interruption, and where there were sixty browsers there are now fifty-nine, and the predator is already in its chamber or moving to the next ambush position, and the Mourner — the nearest individual — is standing still on the place where the ground closed.

The predators are not numerous. They cannot be. A predator that takes a browser every few days, that lives in a column, that has no competition in its territory — a predator like this needs space, and the Mass provides space, the enormous negative space between the columns, the cathedral-aisle corridors that are not navigable by anything that cannot process the scale of the architecture. There are perhaps a thousand predators on the entire Mass. Perhaps fewer. They are the rarest large things on the continent, and they are the most feared by outsiders, and the people of Keswer do not fear them at all.

The people of Keswer say the predators are part of the Mass. Not in a metaphorical sense. In the sense that the Mass grew them, the way it grew the columns and the plates and the curtains. The predators keep the browsers moving. The browsers keep the plates cropped. The cropped plates do not overgrow the filamentous layer. The filamentous layer harvests the fog. Without the predators, the browsers would concentrate, would overgraze, would destroy the surface, and the surface is the thing that drinks, and the thing that drinks is the thing that lives. The Mass needs its predators the way a body needs its immune cells — not constantly, not in large numbers, but specifically, precisely, at the right time and place.

This is what the people of Keswer believe. They may be right.

## The Things in the Substrate

Here is where certainty ends.

The substrate is not solid. It is layered, porous, shot through with tunnels and chambers and rhizome-channels and the spaces left by decayed tissue. It is, in a real sense, a three-dimensional landscape — a world with depth, with geography, with weather (water percolates downward; heat rises upward; gases move laterally along pressure gradients). And things live in it.

Some of these things are known. The rhizome-network — the vascular system of the Mass itself — is inhabited by organisms that live nowhere else: worm-shaped things that move through the fluid in the rhizomes the way fish move through rivers, feeding on the dissolved nutrients, in some cases the same nutrients that the Mass is transporting to its own growing tissues. They are not parasites, exactly. They are commuters. They ride the Mass's own circulatory system, and the Mass does not expel them, which means either it cannot or it does not benefit from doing so, and the distinction between those two possibilities is the distinction between a body and a home, and no one knows which the Mass is.

There are larger things. The deep tones — the subsonic pulses that rise at dusk, that you feel in your teeth — are not the Mass resonating. Or they might be. But the people of Keswer have heard them change. Not in pitch, not in rhythm, but in location. A tone that comes from the southeast one season comes from the northwest the next. The Mass's own resonance should not move. The Mass is the Mass. It does not have a direction. But something in it does.

The people who walk into the Mass each summer, who press their hands to the columns and listen — they are listening for this. They are listening for whatever moves in the deep substrate, whatever it is that produces the tones, whatever it is that is large enough and deep enough and enough its own thing to have a location that changes. They do not say this. They say the Mass is deciding something. But the columns do not decide. The columns grow. The plates do not decide. The plates spread. What decides is something that has a position, that moves, that is in the Mass but perhaps not entirely of the Mass, and the people of Keswer have been pressing their hands to the columns and listening for it for longer than they have been cutting plate-wood and hunting browsers and grinding spores into flour.

## The People

They are not many. Four settlements, perhaps more, a few thousand individuals in total, living on the rim where the living ground meets dead stone. They have no name for themselves collectively. They have names for their settlements — Keswer, and the others, whose names the document does not record because the document was written from outside and the outsiders did not go there.

They are human. This matters, because everything else on the Mass is not, and the people are the exception, the migrants, the latecomers, the ones who arrived after the Mass was already growing and found the edge of it and stayed. They are not adapted to the Mass. They do not have the mottled colouring or the vibration-sense or the chemical navigation. They are ordinary people — two legs, two arms, eyes that see in the visible spectrum, ears that hear in the audible range, bodies that are too warm for the Mass's heat and too cold for its nights without clothing. They do not belong there. They know this. They stay anyway.

They have adapted in the ways that humans adapt — with culture, with practice, with the accumulation of knowledge that is passed not through genes but through words and habits and the things that are done without being said. They close their shutters at dusk. They stay on the paths. They do not go into the interior except at the appointed time and the appointed place. They do not ask what the Mass is. They ask what the Mass is doing, which is a different question, and the answer is always the same: it is growing.

They eat browser-meat and spore-flour and the oils pressed from seed-pods. They drink water collected from the fog that condenses on the cliff-face — not the fog that the Mass drinks, which is the same fog, which means the people and the Mass are drinking from the same cup, which is something the people do not think about because thinking about it does not help. They build with dried plate-wood and dead substrate. They cord fibres from the filamentous layer. They make adhesives from column-resin. They have everything they need because the Mass provides everything and asks nothing, except that the people do not go too deep, and do not stay too long, and do not ask too much, and come back when they are finished.

They are not unhappy. This should be said. They are not suffering. They are not trapped. They live at the edge of something vast and alive and strange, and they have made a life there, and the life is small and hard and warm and full of the sound of the Mass, which is the sound of everything on it, which is the sound of a continent breathing and clicking and chewing and growing, and they have lived with that sound for so long that silence would be the thing that frightened them.

Their children play at the edge of the living ground. They dare each other to step on it. The ground is warm. The ground is soft. The ground gives underfoot, and the children laugh, and the Mass does not notice, and the Mass grows, and the children will grow too, and some of them will become the ones who walk in, once a year, and press their hands to a column, and listen, and hear whatever it is that is heard when you press your hands to something alive and ask it, without words, what it is deciding.

---

And underneath all of this — underneath the filaments and the plates and the columns and the browsers and the predators and the people and the things in the deep substrate that move and make tones — underneath all of it, the Mass itself, which is not a thing but a process, not an organism but an event, not a continent but a continuous happening that has been happening for longer than anything on it has been alive, and that will continue to happen, growing upward and decaying downward, drinking the fog and exhaling the steam and clicking in the heat and groaning in the cold, for longer than anything on it will continue to be.

What lives there is the Mass. Everything else is a guest.
