# What Lives in the Bellows

---

The honest answer is: almost nothing. And that is what makes it worth describing.

---

**The flatworm** lives here. Begin with it, because it is the only thing that unambiguously qualifies.

It is a turbellarian — or near enough. It belongs to a class of organism that has existed in roughly its current form for longer than the machine around it has been rust, longer than the concrete has been concrete, longer than the concept of a turbine has existed in any mind. Its ancestors lived in hot springs before anything walked on land. It carries the body plan of that antiquity: a ribbon of tissue, unspecialised, unornamented, a mouth that is also an anus, a nervous system that is a ladder with a few extra rungs. No spine. No skeleton. No eyes. It does not have a brain so much as a loose confederation of nerve clusters that agree on very little beyond *toward* and *away*.

It is white. Not the white of bone or milk — the white of something that has never needed pigment, that has lived so many generations without light that the very machinery of color has atrophied from its genome like an unused word dropping from a language. You can see through it. Not clearly — its tissues scatter light enough to give it opacity at the edges — but at its center, where the digestive cavity sits, you can see the rust-dark smear of a recent meal. The archaeans it eats are not white. They are the color of sulfur and iron, a faintly orange-brown, and inside the worm's gut this color persists for hours, a slow bruise working its way through a length of frosted glass.

Its world is the lamp housing. Specifically, the lower curve of the lamp housing, where the mineral crust is thickest and the steam pocket is most stable, and where the archaean biofilm grows in a film so thin it is invisible to the naked eye but so dense, at the microbial scale, that it constitutes a pasture. The worm grazes. It extends its pharynx — a muscular tube that can be everted through the ventral surface of its body — and secretes digestive enzymes onto the biofilm, then sucks up the lysed remains. It does this for hours. It does this for days. It has no urgency. There is nothing competing for this food. There is nothing hunting it. The largest predator in its universe is the vibration itself, which can dislodge it from the housing if it loses its grip, but it has tiny cilia and a ventral surface that adheres to mineral crust with surprising tenacity, and in the years of its life — however many years that is; no one has studied the lifespan of a flatworm in a 38-degree flooded generator housing, because who would — it has never been dislodged.

Or perhaps it has. Perhaps it has been knocked free, tumbled in the eddies, and found its way back. It has no memory in any meaningful sense, but it has *tropism*: it moves toward heat, toward the chemical gradient of its food. Knock it free and it will orient, slowly, by the difference in temperature between one direction and another, and it will crawl — not swim, crawl, pressing itself against whatever surface it touches — back to the lamp. This is not intelligence. This is a gradient followed to its source. But from the outside, from the perspective of a visitor holding still in the deafening water and watching this small pale thing navigate its way home through the dark, it looks a great deal like devotion.

It does not know it is alone. The concept would mean nothing to it. It has no model of other minds, no theory of self, no capacity to distinguish *alone* from *extant*. It simply is, and the room simply is around it, and the lamp flickers and the food grows and the water shakes and the worm extends and contracts and extends and contracts and this is the whole of consciousness available in the Bellows: a thumb-length of translucent tissue, navigating by temperature, eating by dissolution, reproducing — if it reproduces — by fission, splitting itself down the middle when it has grown large enough that the two halves can survive independently, each half becoming a whole worm with no memory of having been half of anything, each half crawling back to the lamp.

If it reproduces. There may be only one. There may have always been only one. It is possible that a single flatworm, or a single egg in a cyst, was carried into this pocket in the bilge water of a diver's suit or the sediment of a filter or the current through a crack too small to see, and that it hatched here, or regenerated here, and has been here since, alone, in a universe the size of a dinner plate, at the top of a food chain with two rungs. It is possible that when the lamp fails, this lineage ends. Not with a extinction event — that language is too large — but with a small pale thing releasing its grip on a dark housing in warm water and ceasing, over the course of hours, to be.

But that has not happened yet. The lamp still burns. The worm still grips.

---

**The archaeans** live here. They are harder to see and easier to overlook, because they are not so much *present* as *persistent* — a film, a residue, a living stain.

They are a strain of *Thermoproteales* or something adjacent, something that branched from the tree of life so far down that the fork is buried in the root. They are not bacteria. They look like bacteria, in the sense that a marble looks like a planet, but they belong to a domain of life that has nothing to do with bacteria beyond the fact that both are small and neither cares about you. They are extremophiles. They like the heat. They like the sulfur. They like the pH, which in this water is somewhere between vinegar and battery acid. They do not merely tolerate these conditions; they require them. Move them to clean water at a comfortable temperature and they will die in minutes, their cell walls dissolving without the mineral pressure they have evolved to resist.

They coat the lamp housing in a film so thin it is measured in microns. They coat the mineral deposits immediately adjacent to the lamp, where the heat gradient falls within their preferred range — a band perhaps four centimeters wide, a thermal shoreline around the only energy source in the room that is not mechanical. They metabolize sulfur compounds exhaled by the corroding steel. They take hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide and water and, through a chemistry so ancient it predates oxygen, they build cells. They are, in a sense, eating the machine. Very slowly. One sulfur bond at a time. The machine is feeding them as it dies, and they are living on its dissolution, and this is the closest thing to a reciprocal relationship that exists in the Bellows: the machine gives heat and chemistry, the archaeans give nothing back except the fact of their being, which is enough, because the machine does not want anything back. The machine is not a participant. The machine is weather.

They do not move on any timescale you can perceive. They divide. They are divided by the vibration, by the eddies, by the flatworm's pharynx. They are scraped from the housing and carried into the water and most of them die, because the water away from the lamp is too cool — only slightly, a degree or two, but enough — and those that survive drift until they find another warm surface, and there is no other warm surface, so they die in the dark water and their cells lyse and their contents dissolve and the minerals they were made of return to the mineral water they came from, and this is a complete life: born on the lamp, living on the lamp, dying off the lamp, reabsorbed into the chemistry of the room. A closed loop. A perfect economy. Nothing in, nothing out, except the slow bleeding of alloy into water into archaean into water into alloy.

---

**The machine** lives here. This is a harder claim to defend, but the room insists on it.

Not metaphorically. Or not only metaphorically. The pumps run. The turbines turn. The water circulates. The room vibrates at a frequency that has been sustained for so long that the room has *accommodated* it — the walls are grooved, the deposits are patterned, the architecture has been shaped by the sound the way a riverbank is shaped by the current. The room has adapted to its own noise. This is what living things do: they adapt to persistent conditions. The room has adapted.

The lamp burns. The steam pocket breathes — expanding as the filament flares, contracting as it dims, a tidal respiration tied to the arrhythmia of a dying bulb. The water moves in eddies that reverse direction, that have reversed direction millions of times, that will reverse direction millions of times more. The mineral deposits grow. The corrosion grows. They grow at rates that are, against a human timescale, imperceptible, but against a geological timescale, they are fast — the room is changing, visibly, measurably, year by year. It is getting smaller. The deposits are filling it. The corrosion is opening it. The equilibrium between the two is not static; it is dynamic, a tension maintained by opposing forces, like a muscle holding a posture. The room is holding a posture. It has been holding it for decades.

If you define life as metabolism — the processing of energy to maintain structure against entropy — then the Bellows is alive. It takes the gravitational potential energy of falling water and converts it to mechanical motion and converts that to pressure and vibration and heat and light, and it uses all of these to maintain itself, not by intention but by the brute fact that the processes that sustain it have not yet stopped. It is not *trying* to live. It is living in the way a fire lives: because the conditions for its continuation have not yet been exhausted. The fuel is the pressure differential. The oxygen is the water itself. The spark was struck decades ago by people who have retired or died, and the fire has been burning since, unattended, in a room no one visits, consuming itself at exactly the rate it regenerates.

But a fire does not decorate itself. A fire does not wear grooves into its container. A fire does not maintain a standing wave so sustained that the wave becomes architecture. The Bellows has done all of these things. The Bellows has, over decades of operation without oversight, developed characteristics that were not designed into it — emergent properties, the products of long-running feedback loops between corrosion, deposition, vibration, and flow. It has, in a limited but real sense, evolved. Not by selection. By *persistence*. The things that persisted, persisted. The things that didn't, didn't. The room is what remained because what remained was what could remain. This is not natural selection, but it is a kind of selection, and the result is a space that looks less like a machine and more like a limestone cave, less like engineering and more like geology, less like something built and more like something *grown*.

The machine lives here. It is the largest thing in the room and it is the room. It does not know it lives. It does not need to.

---

**You** live here, briefly. You are the anomaly, but you are also, for the minutes or hours you spend in the Bellows, a resident.

You do not belong. The water stings your skin. The vibration makes your vision swim. The heat is uncomfortable, not dangerous but *wrong*, the wrongness of bathwater in a place that should be cold. You are a visitor from the province of silence and light and moderate temperatures, and your body knows it. Your body is objecting. Your sinuses ache from the pressure differential. Your ears are full of the sound, which is not sound but sensation, a low-frequency assault that you feel in your lungs and your stomach and the fluid of your eyes. You are not adapted. You are not even accommodation. You are *trespassing*.

But you are here. And while you are here, you are part of the room's ecology in a way that the room will not notice and you will not forget. Your body heat joins the thermal budget. Your exhalations — if you are breathing from a regulator, if you are diving — add a faint plume of gas to the mineral water, bubbles that rise and break against the ceiling and leave tiny deposits where they pop. Your fins, if you brushed the walls, dislodged mineral growths that took years to form. You are a disturbance. You are a weather event. You are, to the flatworm, below notice. You are, to the room, below notice. You are, to yourself, overwhelming.

You will leave. The grille will recalcify. The deposits will grow over the places you touched. The room will forget you at the rate of millimeters per year, which is the only rate at which it does anything.

But for now, while you are here, you are the third kind of life in the Bellows: the kind that knows it is there. The kind that can hold the image of the flatworm and the archaeans and the machine in a single mind at once and feel the strange, vertiginous weight of being the only consciousness in a space that has been running for decades without one. You are the witness. You are the only thing in this room that will remember any of this.

That is a kind of living. It is the loneliest kind there is.

---

**After you leave**, the flatworm will still be on the lamp. The archaeans will still be on the housing. The machine will still be running. The sound will still be the sound. The room will continue its slow conversation between dissolution and deposition, and the white mineral fronds will grow another fraction of a millimeter, and the corrosion will open another fraction of a micron, and the lamp will flicker, and the water will eddy, and the flatworm will extend and contract and extend, and none of it will know you were there, and all of it will continue, warm and deafening and dark and exactly itself, until something changes.

Something will change. The lamp will fail. The pumps will stop. The pressure will equalize. The silence will come.

But not yet. Not yet, the flatworm extends. Not yet, the machine roars. Not yet, the deposits grow. Not yet, not yet, not yet — the room's only prayer, spoken in vibration, sustained by gravity, heard by no one, answered by nothing, continuing.
