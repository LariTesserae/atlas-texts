# Inhabitants

## The Workers

They are not a community in the way outsiders might expect. They are a crew.

That distinction matters. A community has shared rituals, common meals, the soft entanglements of people who chose to live near each other. A crew has a job and a rotation and bunks that get reassigned when someone leaves. The Condensation Gallery has a crew.

There are thirty-one of them at full complement, though the number hovers more often around twenty-six or twenty-seven. People leave. Not dramatically — they finish a contract cycle, climb down the forty meters of iron rungs, and don't come back. Nobody asks why. The ladder is reason enough.

The ones who stay longest have a particular quality, something you notice after a few days among them. They are not hardened, exactly. Hardened implies a process of becoming tough, and toughness implies resistance. These people do not resist the gallery. They have adapted to it the way the grainers have adapted to the condensate — by becoming slightly more like their environment. Their skin dries. Their movements slow. They develop a tolerance for dim light that is not the same as good night vision but is something adjacent: a patience with not-seeing, a willingness to operate in partial information and fill the gaps with familiarity.

---

Brennen runs the processing level. He is the closest thing to a supervisor, though he would reject the term and the crew would find it funny if someone used it. He is somewhere between forty and fifty-five — the dry cold and the altitude make it hard to judge, and he does not talk about where he was before. He has been here for eleven contract cycles, which is longer than anyone else currently in the gallery. He moves through the scaffolding with a practiced ease that looks almost lazy until you realize he is conserving oxygen with every gesture, every step calibrated to the minimum effort required. His hands are the thing to watch. They are large, dry, cracked at the knuckles, and they move over the processing machinery with a tactile fluency that is the real authority he holds. He can feel when a filter mesh is about to fail. He can hear a gasket losing its seal before the pressure gauge confirms it. He does not explain these things. He simply acts on them, and the crew has learned to watch his hands and follow.

He sleeps on the highest occupied platform, in a sheet-metal compartment no larger than a closet, with his sleeping bag pressed against the outer crystal wall. At night the cold comes through that wall and into his back, and he says he sleeps better for it. He says the cold is honest. He does not elaborate.

---

Suke and Mett are the collection team. They are not related, not paired in any formal sense, but they work in such close coordination that the crew treats them as a unit. Suke is lean, precise, with a habit of talking to the condensate as she works — not words, exactly, but a low murmur that might be counting or might be something else. She repositions the copper arrays when the formation sites shift, and she does it fast, hauling sheeting across the grated platforms with a strength that seems improbable for her frame until you understand that she is not lifting the weight so much as balancing it, using the tension cables and the angles of the scaffolding to move heavy things with geometry rather than muscle. She taught herself this. She arrived on a short contract and simply did not leave.

Mett handles the pump system. He is quieter than Suke, broader, with a stillness about him that the gallery seems to respect. When the condensate surges and the gallery becomes urgent with activity, Mett does not hurry. He moves at the same deliberate pace he always moves, but his hands work faster, disconnecting and reconnecting hoses, adjusting valves, clearing blockages in the gaskets with a short probe he keeps in his sleeve. The condensate does not stick to him. This is a detail the crew has noticed and does not discuss. It beads on his skin and rolls off. On everyone else it leaves a faint film that eventually dries and can be brushed away. Mett's skin refuses it. He says nothing about this. Suke has been seen watching his forearms during a surge, but she has also said nothing.

---

There is a woman named Odera who maintains the generator. This is the job that matters most and the job nobody wants, because the generator enclosure is the loudest point in the gallery and because the generator is temperamental in a way that the crystalline machinery is not. The crystal systems are alien but consistent. The generator is human-made and moody. It requires a fuel mixture adjusted for altitude, a particular oil viscosity that thickens in the cold, and a timing that drifts as the temperature shifts. Odera knows all of this. She keeps a log in a grease-penciled notebook that she has been filling for six contract cycles, and the log is the closest thing the gallery has to a manual. When the generator cycles down for maintenance, she is the one who starts it again, and the crew has noticed that it always starts for her on the first try. She is not large. She is not loud. She has a way of standing very close to the machine and resting one hand on its housing that suggests she is listening to something the rest of them cannot hear.

She has a scar on her left wrist that she says came from the ladder. She does not say which trip, which direction, which rung.

---

The rest of the crew rotates through positions that are less defined. Hauling supplies up the ladder — water, fuel, rations, filter mesh, gasket material, replacement bulbs. Monitoring the collection arrays. Clearing the grainer-holding compartments and releasing the animals back into the gallery. Maintaining the scaffolding, which is always flexing, always settling, always developing a new creak or wobble that someone has to investigate. Sleeping. Eating. Sitting on the platforms during the idle periods between surges, legs dangling, watching the grainers crawl.

They eat dried rations heated on the generator's exhaust manifold, which Odera tolerates because she understands that people need hot food and because the manifold heat is wasted anyway. The rations are uniform. The crew does not complain about them because complaining would imply the possibility of something different, and there is no something different. There is what the supply runs bring, and what the supply runs bring is what is available. The food is calories. It is not pleasure. The one exception is a thick paste that comes in tubes and tastes faintly of nuts and salt, and the crew has developed an informal economy around it — trading shifts, trading bunk proximity to the warmer interior walls, trading the right to use the porthole during what passes for sunset, when the haze below the spires takes on a color that is not quite orange and not quite anything else.

## The Grainers

They are harder to describe than the workers, because they are harder to see.

Not because they hide. They do not hide. They are simply the same color and texture as their environment, and the light in the gallery is dim, and they move slowly. You look at a crystal wall and see crystal. You look again and realize that a section of what you took for surface texture is actually a grainer, feeding, its segmented body flat against the formation face, its dozens of filament-legs pressed to the crystal like the veins of a leaf pressed to glass.

They are the length of a forearm at most. The smallest are the length of a finger. They are segmented — not in the clean way of an insect but in the irregular way of crystal growth itself, each segment a slightly different geometry, the whole body a chain of mineral shapes that flexes at joints you cannot quite identify. The legs are the most alive part of them. Each one is a filament, fine as wire, translucent, and it flexes with a muscular precision that is deeply strange in something that looks like rock. When a grainer walks, the legs move in a wave that travels the length of the body, and the clicking is the contact of each filament-tip with the surface — crystal on crystal, glass on glass, a sound like a whisper made of geometry.

They feed on the condensate. This is the central fact of their existence, and possibly the central fact of the gallery's existence, though nobody has confirmed whether the grainers are a feature of the spire's natural ecology or something that arrived with the first workers and stayed. They settle on the condensate film where it beads on the crystal faces, and they press their bodies flat, and the condensate moves through them. You can see it happen. The translucent body darkens, not uniformly but in patches, as the condensate fills whatever internal structure the grainer uses for digestion. The process takes minutes or hours. When it is done, the grainer lightens again and moves on, and the spot where it fed is dry.

The workers retrieve them from the filter meshes with their hands. The grainers do not resist. They do not seem to register the transfer as an event. They are taken from the mesh, held for a moment — and workers describe the feel of them as warm, which is strange, because the gallery is cold and the grainers are mineral — and placed back on a crystal surface, where they resume their slow search for condensate. The warmth fades quickly once they are released. It is as though the condensate inside them holds a heat that the crystal body does not retain.

There is one more thing. The crew does not discuss it, but it has been observed often enough to be a known phenomenon rather than a story. During the night cycle, when the gallery is at its darkest and the condensate is not forming, the grainers gather. Not on the walls. On the machinery. Specifically on the generator enclosure, on the processing unit, on the sealed vessels where the filtered condensate is stored. They cluster there in the hundreds, their bodies pressed against the metal and glass, and they are still. Not feeding. Not searching. Resting, perhaps, though that word implies a consciousness that may not be accurate. The workers who have been here longest — Brennen, Odera — say the grainers are listening. To what, they do not say.

## The Gallery Itself

And there is the question of whether the spire is inhabited in a way that includes the workers rather than simply containing them.

The recrystallization event that produced the spires is not understood. The spires are not inert. Their internal temperature fluctuates in ways that do not correlate cleanly with external conditions. The condensate forms in patterns that suggest an internal logic — a rhythm or a cycle or a need — that the workers can observe and harvest but not predict. The crystal walls, when the gallery is dark and quiet, have a quality that is difficult to describe without sounding foolish. They seem attentive. The light that pools in their depths during the night cycle moves in ways that do not match the fixed positions of the bulbs.

This may be nothing. It may be the refraction of industrial light through a complex crystal structure, nothing more. The workers treat it as nothing. They have work to do. The condensate forms or it doesn't. The machinery runs or it doesn't. The grainers feed or they don't. The spire does what it does, and the workers do what they do inside it, and the distinction between inhabitant and habitat may be less clear than the ladder and the sheet metal and the scaffolding would suggest.

The gallery holds them. That is the word that fits, though it is not quite right. A gallery holds art. This place holds a process — mineral, mechanical, biological, human — and the process continues because that is what it does, and the things that live here are the things that continue.
