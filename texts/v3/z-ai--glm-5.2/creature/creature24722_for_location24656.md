# The Weeping Highlands

There are people here. Maybe people isn't the word. Maybe it is. They would say it is, if you asked them, if you could find one to ask, which is the first difficulty.

They live *in* the canopy.

Not on it, the way you might picture — not perched on the interlocking branches like a treehouse village. *In* it. The woody shrubs that cover acres from a single root system, the ones with bark like oxidized copper — they have hollows. Not hollows that were carved. Hollows that grew, the way a burl grows, the way a cyst grows, the way any living thing grows around a presence it has decided to tolerate. The people found the hollows, or the hollows found the people, or the hollows were always going to be hollows and the people were always going to find them — the distinction doesn't hold here, where everything is so slow that causality itself moves like something wading through cold water.

The hollows are not large. A single individual fits, curled, knees drawn up, spine against the curve of living wood. The wood is warm where it has been lived in long enough. Not warm the way a hearth is warm. Warm the way a body is warm — faintly, internally, the residual heat of slow metabolism pressed against slow metabolism until both sides have made small adjustments. The bark on the inside of a long-inhabited hollow is smoother than the bark outside. It has a polish to it, almost. The person who sleeps there has worn it the way water wears stone, by being there, night after night, season after season, for a span of time that they do not measure because measuring requires a before and an after and they are not much interested in sequence.

---

What they look like:

You would not see them. That is the primary fact. You would walk past them. You have walked past them, if you have been there — you just didn't know it. They are the pale movement in the moss. They are the dark shape that moves through the ferns and is gone. Both at once, actually, because they are not uniformly colored. Their skin responds to the ambient light in a way that is not quite camouflage and not quite mood — it is closer to the way the lichen responds to moisture, or the way the moss changes density depending on the vibration in the rock. Their skin is *listening* to the environment and adjusting, not rapidly, not like an octopus, but slowly, on the timescale of minutes, so that a person standing still in the ferns becomes, over the course of a quarter-hour, indistinguishable from the ferns.

They are not large. This is important to understand. The cold, the altitude, the metabolic economy — these things do not produce largeness. A grown adult is about the size of a child of ten, if you need a comparison, though the proportions are wrong. Their limbs are longer relative to the torso. Their hands are large, disproportionately so, the fingers narrow and strong, the kind of hands that have spent generations prying things from wet rock, peeling bark, working fingers into crevices. Their feet are broad and flat and callused in a pattern that mirrors the texture of the moss they walk on, so that their footprints, which they leave in the wet ground, look less like footprints and more like small compressions, the kind the moss recovers from in an hour.

Their faces are not the point. But: broad cheekbones, wide-set eyes that are not so much dark as *deep* — the color of peat water, which is to say a brown that is almost black, with a clarity that comes from the lack of anything floating in it. Their irises are large, the pupils dilated in permanent accommodation of the grey light. They do not blink often. They do not need to. The air is wet enough that their eyes never dry. Their hair is dark and fine and grows in whatever direction the moisture in the air pushes it, which means most of them look windswept even in stillness, their hair rising and falling with the faint exhalations of the moss beneath them, so that a person standing in a field of breathing moss has hair that breathes too, gently, like anemone.

They do not wear clothing in the way you mean. They wear moss. Not decoratively — functionally. They cultivate it on their bodies, in the hollows of their shoulders, along the forearms, across the lower back where the body holds warmth longest. The moss is the strange pale blue-grey kind, the one with the hexagonal cells, the kind that seems to hold light. It insulates. It holds water against the skin, which in this environment is a thermal strategy — the water warms to body temperature and stays, a layer of wet warmth between flesh and air. When the moss dies and dries, they peel it off in sheets and replace it. There is something tender about this. They help each other. The old moss comes away from the skin with a small sound, a kind of unsticking, and the skin beneath is pale and puckered and very clean, and the new moss is pressed on and held until it takes, and this is as close to intimacy as they come, or as close as you would recognize.

---

How they live:

Slowly. Everything about the highlands is slow, and they are not an exception — they are an expression. They eat what the plateau offers. The spore-fluid from the ferns, which is viscous and faintly iridescent and does not freeze — they collect it in the creases of their hands and drink it directly, or they store it in the hollows of certain rocks that they have shaped for this purpose, not carved, just *used* until the rock has worn into the right shape. The spore-fluid is caloric in a way that water is not. It has a taste that is difficult to describe — mineral, faintly sweet, with an undertone of something almost metallic, almost electrical, and it does things to the body. It does things to *their* bodies. The ones who drink it most consistently have eyes that catch light that isn't there. The ones who drink it least have eyes that are merely dark.

They eat the pale amphibians, when they can catch them, which is not often, because the amphibians are better at waiting than the people are, and the people respect this. Catching an amphibian involves sitting in a seepage channel for hours, hands submerged in water cold enough to numb, waiting for the faint movement that means one has drifted close. When it comes, the hands close, and there is a brief thrashing, and then a stillness, and then the amphibian is eaten raw, still cold, still pulsing faintly in the hand. This is a feast. This is a rare and important thing. The person who catches one shares it. Not ceremonially — practically. The meat will not keep, and they are hungry, and the people nearby are hungry, and so the amphibian is divided with a small sharp stone and consumed immediately, and the person who caught it is not thanked because thanking is not a thing they do. The eating is the thanks. The sharing is the thanks. The act of being present, together, with cold meat in a wet world — that is the whole of it.

They eat other things. Insects. The pale things that move in the moss — small, warm, quick, which they catch with a speed and precision that is startling, given how slow they are in every other respect. A flick of the hand, a crunch, done. They do not savor this. It is maintenance, not meal.

---

What they do with their time:

This is where it gets difficult to describe, because the answer is both *nothing* and *something vast*.

They listen.

The plateau hums. You know this — the buried machinery, the pumps or generators or whatever they are, still turning deep in the rock, driven by water pressure. The people hear this. They hear it the way you hear your own heartbeat — not as an event, but as a condition. It is the baseline of their existence. And they listen *to* it, deliberately, in a way that you do not listen to your heartbeat. They sit in their hollows, or they lie on the living lattice of branches, or they press themselves against the weeping rock, and they listen to the hum, and they... do something with what they hear.

It is not language. It is not communication, exactly. It is more like tuning. They adjust themselves. The way the moss grows thicker near the vibrating zones, the way the ferns produce more spore-fluid where the hum is strongest — the people do something similar. They lean into the frequencies. They let the vibration rearrange something inside them, some pattern of thought or feeling or physiology, and when they emerge from the listening, they are slightly different. Calmer, or more focused, or more willing to sit in the cold water and wait for an amphibian. The hum gives them something. A kind of orientation. A sense of *fitting*.

Some of them — the older ones, the ones who have been listening longest — can trace the pipes through the rock by ear. They walk across the plateau with their heads tilted, their large hands pressed flat against the wet stone, and they know where the iron is, where the valves are, where the junction boxes sit crusted and silent in their casings of mineral and lichen. They do not try to open them. They do not try to repair them. They do not try to understand them. They just... acknowledge them. A hand on the stone where a pipe runs, a pause, a breath, and then they move on. As though visiting. As though checking. As though the machinery is a neighbor, and the neighbor is old, and the neighbor is still running, and that is worth knowing.

There is one woman — or one person, the gender is not clear and may not be meaningful among them — who has found a junction box that is not entirely dead. It hums differently from the others. It has a warmth to it, a faint electrical warmth that is distinguishable from the geological warmth of the rock. She sits with it. She has been sitting with it for a long time — not continuously, but returning, season after season, pressing her back against the rock face where the junction box is embedded, letting the water run over her, letting the hum move through her, and she has begun to understand something. Not about the box. About the hum itself. About what it was *for*.

She does not have words for what she understands. She does not need words. The understanding lives in her body, in the way her breathing slows when she sits with the box, in the way her hands move afterward, tracing patterns in the wet moss that are not random and are not remembered and are not taught. Something is being received. Something is being translated. Whether the translation is accurate, whether the source is still speaking, whether the message was ever meant for anyone like her — these are questions that do not occur to her, because questions require a framework of inquiry, and she does not have one. She has the hum. She has the wet stone. She has the patience of a creature that has lived its whole life at a pace where geological time and personal time have begun to overlap.

---

Their number:

Not many. A few hundred, perhaps, spread across a plateau so broad you could walk for days and never see another. They do not congregate. There is no village. There is no gathering place. They find each other by the same means they find the amphibians — by patience, by proximity, by the slow accumulation of presence. Two people who have been living in hollows within a mile of each other will, eventually, become aware of each other. Not by sight. Not by sound. By the *texture* of the moss between them, which has been walked on by two sets of feet instead of one, which has been breathed on by two bodies instead of one, which is subtly different in a way that only someone who has lived their whole life listening to the moss would notice.

When they find each other, they do not greet. They sit. They sit together in the wet and the grey and they breathe and they listen and they do not speak, because speech is fast and loud and crude and it disturbs the silence, which is not absence but density, which is not empty but full, which is the medium in which they live the way fish live in water. They sit together and the silence holds them both and after a time — hours, a day, longer — one of them will move, and the other will move, and they will go together to where one of them has been sleeping, and they will share the hollow, and the hollow will grow a little larger, and the wood will warm a little more, and this is how they are together. This is how they love. This is how they build.

Children, when there are children, are born small and quiet and already listening. They come out knowing the hum. Their eyes, from the first, have that peat-water clarity. They do not cry. They breathe. They are placed against the mother's chest where the moss grows, and the moss receives them, and they are held, and they are fed spore-fluid from a cupped hand, and they grow, slowly, at the pace of everything here, and by the time they are old enough to walk they already know the plateau the way you know the rooms of your childhood home — by feel, by smell, by the particular quality of silence in each place.

---

The large things in the canopy:

They know about the things in the canopy. The large, dark, torpid things that eat rarely and absorb what warmth the pale sun provides. They have a relationship with them. Not a friendly relationship. Not a hostile one. A *neighborly* one, in the way that two predators who occupy different niches in the same small ecosystem can be neighbors — aware of each other, deferential to each other's space, willing to share the resource when there is enough and willing to avoid each other when there is not.

The canopy-things are larger than the people. Much larger. They are dark and slow and draped across the interlocking branches with a sprawl that suggests they have been there for a long time, that they have been there longer than the people, that they were here when the pipes were new and the machinery hummed with purpose. The people give them room. When a canopy-thing shifts, when it lifts its great dark body and moves to a new section of branch — slowly, heavily, each movement a commitment — the people below feel it. The lattice flexes. Water drips. The moss sighs. And the people move aside, not in fear but in the way you move aside for a river. You do not argue with the river. You do not negotiate. You simply are not where the river is going to be.

Sometimes a canopy-thing dies. This is an event. Not a celebration, not a mourning — an *event*, in the way that a rockfall is an event, in the way that a freeze is an event. A large dark body, cooling, settling into the branches it has occupied for longer than any person's memory. The people come. They take what they can use — the hide, which is thick and rough and useful for wrapping things, for holding spore-fluid, for pressing against the body in the coldest nights. They take certain organs, which are dense and warm for days after death, which seem to hold heat the way the moss holds water, and which are eaten carefully, sparingly, because they are powerful and the power is not entirely understood. And then they leave the rest. The canopy-thing's body becomes part of the lattice. The moss grows over it. The ferns find the crevices. Within a season, you cannot tell where the body was.

---

What they know about the old machinery:

This is the thing that matters, and it is the thing that is hardest to explain, because they do not *know* it in the way you know a fact. They know it the way the moss knows the vibrating zones. They have grown around it.

The pipes. The valves. The junction boxes. The buried pumps. The geometric cuts in the rock. They know these things the way you know the bones of your own skull — not by having seen them, not by having studied them, but by having lived inside them. The plateau, for them, is not a landscape with ruins in it. The plateau is a *structure*. A structure they inhabit the way mitochondria inhabit a cell. They do not know who built it. They do not know what it was for. They do not know if it is still doing what it was designed to do or if it has been failing, slowly, for so long that the failing itself has become the function. What they know is that it hums. And the hum is the ground note of their existence. And they have leaned into it. And it has made them what they are.

The woman with the junction box — she is beginning to understand something about the hum's *pattern*. Not its content. Its pattern. The way it rises and falls, not randomly but in a rhythm that suggests purpose, suggests *signal*, suggests that whatever is buried deep in the rock is not merely running but is *doing something*. Is still, after all this time, *doing something*. And she does not know what. And she does not need to know what. But she listens, and she traces her patterns in the wet moss, and the patterns are getting more complex, and the moss that grows in the patterns is the pale blue-grey kind, the kind with the hexagonal cells, and it is growing in spirals, and the spirals match the spirals on the copper wires inside the junction box, and she does not know this, and she does not need to know this, but the plateau knows it, and the plateau is patient, and the plateau has time.

---

There are not many of them. There have never been many of them. There may be fewer of them now than there were, or there may be more — it is difficult to tell, because they do not count themselves, because counting requires a before and an after, because the before and the after require a boundary between past and present that does not exist when you live at the speed of geological time. They are here. They have been here. They will be here, or they will not, and either way the moss will grow, and the water will seep, and the hum will continue, and the pipes will weep alongside the rock, and the distinction between the built and the grown will continue to erode until it is not a distinction at all but a memory of a distinction, and even the memory will be grown over, and the silence will hold, and the plateau will go on, vast and cold and wet and alive, its people curled in the hollows of the living lattice, their hair breathing with the moss, their eyes deep as peat water, their hands pressed flat against the stone where the iron runs, listening, listening, always listening, for the hum that tells them they are home.
