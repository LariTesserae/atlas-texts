# What Lives in Kell-Vorn

---

Let me start with the beetles, because they are the ones you notice last, and because by the time you notice them you have already been standing in their territory for longer than you meant to.

They are small. You would call them insignificant if you did not watch them long enough. Black, smooth-backed, the size of the first joint of your smallest finger. They move in lines — true lines, geometrically honest lines, the kind of lines a draftsman would need a straightedge to produce — and the lines run across the stone faces of the houses at roughly the height of a man's knee. Not every house has them. Not every wall. But where they are, they are constant. The columns are perhaps three beetles wide and uncountably long, disappearing around corners and into crevices and emerging again on the other side as though the wall were a membrane they had learned to pass through.

They do not forage. This is the thing that stays with you. Ants forage. Ants swarm and scatter and converge and carry things back and forth in purposeful chaos. These beetles do not carry anything. They walk their line, and the line goes somewhere, and you cannot follow it to either end because either it turns into a crack too narrow for your eyes or it simply continues past the point where you lose interest in tracking it. If you put your hand across the line, they walk over your hand. The legs are cold and dry and very precise. There is the faintest sensation of each footfall — not a tickle, exactly, but a sequence of small, deliberate pressures, like someone tapping out a message on your skin in a language you don't know.

The people of Kell-Vorn do not disturb the beetles. The beetles eat something — a mite, a fungus, a growth too small to see — that lives in the thatch and in the joints of the stone walls. Without the beetles, the houses would come apart faster than the people could repair them. The people know this. They have always known this. It is not a discovery they made; it is a condition they were born into, like the damp, like the dim light, like the slow bruise-colored dawns.

I mention the beetles first because of all the living things in Kell-Vorn, they are the ones that most make you feel that you are inside a system. A process. Not a village, not a settlement, but a node in a larger network of exchange that extends into the stone and the moss and the cloud bank and the mineral streaks in the tuff and, perhaps, into places you cannot see. The beetles walk their lines, and the lines are straight, and the straightness is not a thing you can explain by anything you know about beetles.

---

The people, then. The hundred or so humans who live in the tier houses.

They are small-bodied. Not stunted — there is no sign of deprivation in their proportions — but compact, the way people are compact who have grown up in a place where calories are gathered rather than cultivated, where the protein comes in pulses and the lean seasons are lean enough to leave their signature on a body over generations. The adults stand perhaps a head shorter than you would expect. Their frames are narrow. Their hands are disproportionately large — the fingers long, the knuckles pronounced, the skin across the backs roughened by years of working tuff and bone and fiber. You notice the hands before you notice the faces.

The faces are wide across the cheekbones, narrow at the jaw. The eyes are dark — not just brown but genuinely dark, the iris nearly indistinguishable from the pupil in the dim light of the shelf — and they do not blink often. This is a thing that takes time to notice and longer to become comfortable with. It is not a staring. It is an absence of the reflexive, restless eye-movement that people in brighter, more open landscapes seem to need. These are eyes that have grown up in a world where the light is always partial, where the air is always damp, where the visual field is always a composition of greys and greens and mineral blues. They do not dart because there is little to dart toward. They rest. They watch. They take in what is there without insisting on it.

Their skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness or confinement — the pallor of climate, of cloud-filtered light, of a life lived in the shade of a ridge and under a perpetual cloud bank. It has a quality of translucence, particularly at the temples and the wrists, where you can see the blue trace of veins beneath. Against the dark stone and the green moss, the paleness makes them look almost mineral, as though they too had been cut from the tuff and left to weather.

They dress in layers. The innermost layer is moth-silk — woven tight, pale, almost the color of the moths themselves, which produces a strange effect when someone moves past a wall where the moths are resting and their clothing and the insects are the same shade. Over the silk, they wear garments of woven black moss fiber, loose and rough, and over these, in the coldest seasons, hide — not a recognizable pelt from a recognizable animal, but a cured, stiff, grey-brown material that comes from the amphibians when one dies of natural causes or is taken for food. The hide is decorated sometimes with small stones pressed into the surface while it cures, or with lines of beetle-backs — the dark elytra of dead beetles, sewn in rows — which catch the fungus-light at night and shine like wet seeds.

They are quiet. Not silent — they speak, and their speech has a music to it, low and tonal, more breath than voice, carrying in the damp air with a clarity that seems impossible given the softness. But they do not speak much. You hear fragments as you move between the tiers: a word passed from one courtyard to the next, a call between houses that is more exhalation than shout, a child's voice rising and falling in something between song and counting. They do not narrate their actions. They do not explain themselves to each other. The knowledge of the settlement is held in common and does not require repetition. When an elder shows a younger person how to chip a tuff blade, there is very little talking. The elder works. The younger watches. Then the younger works, and the elder watches. The corrections are made by gesture — a tilt of the head, a movement of the hand, a tap on the stone where the angle is wrong.

They have a concept of time, but it is not yours. The cloud bank makes the usual markers unreliable — the sun is a brightening, not a position, and the stars are a rumor. Instead, they track time by the cycles of the fauna. The moth season. The crustacean boom. The amphibian mating, which happens when the condensation reaches a certain intensity and the puddles in the courtyards become deeper than the width of a hand. The beetle-lines shift — not their direction, but their position on the walls, moving higher in the cold, lower in the relative warmth. These shifts are the calendar. A child in Kell-Vorn learns to read the calendar by watching before it learns to speak, because the watching is more important than the speaking, and because the calendar does not wait for language.

There is something else about the people that you notice only after several days among them. They do not startle. You will be standing in a courtyard, watching a flat amphibian eat a moth, and a child will walk past you, close enough to touch, and you will not have heard the child approach. This is not because the child was stealthy in the way that a hunter is stealthy. It is because the child has never learned to move loudly. The stone underfoot is worn smooth enough to be quiet, and the people have grown up on it, and their feet find the quiet places by instinct. They do not announce themselves by walking. They are simply there, and then they were there, and then they are somewhere else.

---

The moths deserve more attention than they received in passing, because they are, in a real sense, the center of the settlement's economy and its primary nervous anxiety.

They are large. Handspan-wide. Their wings are the grey-white of old bone, or of the tuff itself after rain, and they are furred — not dusted with the fine scales of lowland moths, but genuinely furred, with a pelt as thick and soft as a small mammal's. The fur is the same pale color as the wings. When you touch one — and you will touch one, because they are everywhere and because they do not fly from a gentle touch — the fur is warm. Not warm like a body, but warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, warmer than anything else on the shelf. The moths generate heat. How, and why, is not clear. There is no obvious mechanism. But the warmth is real, and the people use it: in the coldest part of the deep-night, when the cloud bank sits heaviest and the condensation freezes on the upper walls, the people of Kell-Vorn allow the moths to gather inside the houses, on the inner walls, and the collective warmth of a thousand moth-bodies raises the interior temperature by a degree or two. Enough. The people sleep in their moth-silk and their moss-fiber and the modest warmth of the moths, and it is enough.

The eyes. The description mentioned them, but it is worth returning to, because the eyes do not resolve into familiarity no matter how long you look. They are not compound. A moth should have compound eyes — that is the contract, that is the structure, that is what the word *moth* implies. These eyes are single. A dark circle, wet-looking, with no visible faceting, no ommatidial grid. They look, in shape and sheen, like the eyes of a vertebrate. Like the eyes of something that sees one image, not a mosaic. You try not to think about what a moth with vertebrate eyes might see. You try not to think about what it sees when it hangs on the upper wall of your house at night, trembling, its face toward you, its single dark eye open and wet and still.

The moths have a life cycle that the people track with precision. The pupae are the primary food source — gathered from the upper walls in season, pale and soft, the size of a finger-joint, tasting of fat and something metallic. The people harvest them with their large, rough hands, plucking them from the stone with a motion that is quick but not cruel. They take perhaps half. They leave the rest. This is not sentiment. This is the arithmetic of survival. Take too many and the moth population collapses and the warmth goes out of the houses and the silk goes out of the looms and the food goes out of the stores. Take too few and the moths overwhelm the settlement, eating through grain and fiber and the binding of the walls. Half. Always half. The proportion is not written anywhere. It is not taught as a rule. It is simply known, the way the angle of a tuff blade is known, the way the weight of a thatch-stone is known, the way the position of the beetle-lines is known — by the body, by the hands, by the accumulated experience of four generations on this shelf.

---

The flat amphibians. The ones with the forward-facing eyes.

There are perhaps thirty of them in the settlement at any given time, though the number fluctuates. They are the size of a dinner plate, flat as a leaf, the color of the tuff — ash-white with mineral-blue mottling that makes them nearly invisible against the stone courtyards where they sit. You will stand in a courtyard and not see one and then your eye will adjust and you will realize there are two within arm's reach, and they have been there the entire time, and they are watching you.

The forward-facing eyes are the thing. Amphibians do not have forward-facing eyes. Prey animals do not have forward-facing eyes. Forward-facing eyes are for predators — for animals that need to triangulate distance, that need to calculate an attack. These amphibians are predators. They eat the moths. They eat the beetles, sometimes, when a beetle-line crosses the ground in front of them. They eat the arboreal crustaceans, on the occasions when one falls from a tree. They do not eat each other. You know this because their numbers remain stable, and because you have watched them in the courtyards at dusk, sitting within inches of each other, utterly indifferent to each other's presence, like stones that happened to have eyes.

Their tongues are pale, long, and exact. The strike is faster than you can follow. A moth is drifting past, bone-white wings catching the fungus-light, and then the tongue is out and the tongue is back and the moth is gone and the amphibian is settling again, its flat body resuming its perfect stillness, its forward-facing eyes resuming their perfect, patient watch. There is no chewing. The moth is swallowed whole, and a small swell travels down the amphibian's flat body like a ripple across a pond, and then there is nothing.

The people do not hunt the amphibians. Sometimes an amphibian dies — of cold, of age, of some amphibian ailment you cannot diagnose — and the people collect the body and cure the hide and eat the meat. But they do not pursue them. The amphibians are too valuable alive. They are the moth-control. Without them, the moths would take the settlement. The people understand this. The amphibians understand nothing, probably. But the arrangement works, and has worked for four generations, and neither party has shown interest in renegotiating.

---

The arboreal crustaceans. The ones in the fern-trees.

They are the most alien of the fauna, and the most difficult to account for, because their behavior does not map onto any category you have. They are crustaceans — you can see the jointed legs, the segmented body, the pale chitinous shell — but they live in trees, breathe air, and move with a mechanical precision that suggests something more than instinct. Each step is placed. Each joint is articulated independently. When they move, they move like a machine designed by someone who valued accuracy above speed, above grace, above any quality you would normally associate with a living thing.

They breathe through membranes along their flanks — thin, translucent, pulsing faintly as air is drawn in and pushed out. This is the breathing you heard when you first arrived. Not the people, not the amphibians, not the moths. The crustaceans. Thousands of them, in the trees, on the undersides of the leaves, each one drawing air through its flank-membranes in a rhythm that is nearly synchronized, and the synchronization produces the sound — the held note, the sub-threshold hum, the texture in the air that you feel in your teeth.

They eat leaves. The copper-colored leaves of the fern-trees, chewed into holes, the canopy becoming a sheet of punctured darkness through which the dim light passes in small, scattered points. The damage is extensive. Some trees are nearly defoliated. But the trees do not die. They produce new leaves constantly, and the crustaceans eat the new leaves, and the cycle continues — a slow, patient consumption that never quite reaches the point of destruction, that hovers at the edge of lethal, that is, perhaps, another negotiation, another set of terms that the ecosystem has reached without anyone's consent or awareness.

The people eat the crustaceans. They pluck them from the trees — the crustaceans do not flee, do not resist, do not seem to register the removal as a threat — and roast them on flat stones over small fires. The fires are nearly invisible: moss and dried fern-frond, too damp to flame, producing a low, slow heat and almost no smoke. The crustaceans roast in their shells, and the shells turn from pale to a deep, mineral blue — the same blue as the streaks in the tuff, the same blue as the fungus-light — and the meat inside is white and dense and tastes of the forest, of copper and wet fern and something faintly sweet.

The shells are not discarded. They are ground and mixed with tuff-dust to make a paste that the people use to repair the walls. The blue mineral streaks in the new mortar fade over time, but you can see, in the older houses, the places where repairs were made — a slightly different texture, a faintly different shade of white, a ghost of blue that the weather hasn't yet erased.

---

The birds. Or the things above the ridge that fly like birds.

You will not get a clear look at them. No one does. They are shapes against the cloud cover, circling in patterns that overlap without intersecting, which is either a coincidence of geometry or a discipline of behavior. They do not call. They do not land. They descend occasionally — passing through a gap between houses, close enough to feel the displacement of air, far too fast to study — and what you see in that half-second is wrong. The wings are too long. The body is too narrow. The motion is not flight but something closer to swimming, a undulation through the air that suggests a medium thicker than air, or a body adapted for a different medium entirely.

The people do not speak of them. This is notable, because the people speak of everything else — the moths, the beetles, the amphibians, the crustaceans, the ferns, the moss, the cloud bank, the stone. The things in the air above are not part of the settlement's economy. They are not part of its ecology, as far as anyone can determine. They do not interact with the shelf. They do not come to ground. They circle, and they descend, and they rise again, and they are there, and the people of Kell-Vorn do not look up at them.

You will ask. The question will form itself in your mind over days — what are those, up there, above the ridge — and you will ask, and the person you ask will pause, and their dark, unblinking eyes will rest on you for a moment longer than is comfortable, and then they will say something in their low, tonal, breathy speech that you will not understand, and they will turn back to whatever they were doing, and you will not ask again.

---

The ferns. The moss. The trees. The fungus.

I want to include these because the line between fauna and flora in Kell-Vorn is not a line but a gradient, and the plants here behave in ways that are, if not animal, at least something more than passive.

The ferns uncurl. The description said this, but the pace of the uncurling is what matters. It is not the sudden unfurling of a fern in a lowland forest, triggered by warmth and light. It is a slow, continuous motion, visible if you watch — and you will watch, because there is nothing else to watch, because the settlement is a place that teaches stillness — visible over the course of an hour, the frond extending itself by millimeters, the tip tracing an arc through the dim air, the pale green of the new growth deepening as it matures. The ferns are sensitive to touch. If you brush a frond, it contracts — not instantly, but over several seconds, pulling back like a slow finger, and then, after a longer pause, it resumes. This is not unusual for ferns in general. But the slowness of it, the deliberateness, the way the contraction seems to involve a choice rather than a reflex — that is unusual.

The moss on the north-facing walls is thick enough to hold water. You can press your hand into it and feel the moisture seep between your fingers, warm from the sunless warmth of the stone. The moss does not grow on the south-facing walls. It grows on the north, and the line where it stops is sharp — a boundary as clean as if it had been drawn. The people know this boundary and build accordingly: the north walls are moss-walls, left unpatched, allowed to thicken, and the moss holds the stones together and insulates the houses and provides, in certain seasons, a second food source — a dense, slightly bitter green paste that the people spread on roasted crustacean meat.

The copper-leafed trees. Their roots are prying the settlement apart. This was mentioned. What was not mentioned is that the people are aware of this and have made a decision about it. The lower walls, the ones the roots have begun to disassemble, are not reinforced. They are not repaired. They are allowed to fail. When a section of wall gives way, the people remove the fallen stones and build a new wall behind the old one, a few feet further into the shelf, and the old wall becomes a planter, a terrace, a root-run for the tree. The settlement is not being destroyed. It is being *moved*. Slowly, over generations, the tier houses are retreating up the shelf as the trees advance, and the people are following the retreat, and the houses they leave behind become part of the forest. You can see this in the upper tiers: ruins that are not ruins but transitions, houses that have become trellises, walls that have become root-systems, doorways that are still doorways but lead from one courtyard into a thicket of copper leaves and pale ferns and the quiet, mechanical precision of crustaceans stepping along branches.

The fungus. The blue light. The pulse.

It grows on the lower walls of every house, in the gaps between stones where the moisture gathers thickest. It is not a mushroom — no cap, no stem, no visible fruiting body. It is a film, a coating, a presence that is more texture than shape. It is the color of the mineral streaks in the tuff — cold blue, a blue that has no warmth in it, a blue that seems to absorb rather than emit. And it pulses.

Thirty seconds. Maybe thirty-five. The cycle is not exact — it varies by a second or two, which makes it impossible to predict, which makes it feel organic, which makes it feel like breathing. Bright enough to see by. Dim enough that the seeing is more feeling than seeing, more intuition than image. At full pulse, you can make out the shapes of houses, the lines of the steps, the movement of a person crossing a courtyard. Between pulses, you can make out nothing, and the darkness is complete, and the only light is the drifting pale of moths moving through the gaps, and the dark circles of their wet eyes catching nothing at all.

The people navigate by this. They have no other light at night. Fire is forbidden — not by law, by consequence; everyone knows what fire does to the moths. And so they move through their settlement in the blue pulse, and they are patient with the darkness between pulses, and they wait, and the light comes back, and they move, and the light goes, and they wait again. It makes their nights slow. It makes their nights attentive. You cannot move quickly through a darkness that pulses. You learn to move at the speed of the light.

---

The elders.

There are five of them. You know they are elders because the younger people defer to them in small, specific ways — bringing them food first, stepping aside on the narrow stairs, turning to face them when they enter a courtyard. But there is no ceremony. No markers of rank. The elders do not sit in a special place. They do not wear different clothing. They are identifiable only by their faces, which are more lined than the others, and by their hands, which are more worn, and by their eyes, which are the same dark, unblinking, patient eyes but set deeper, framed by skin that has been damp for so long it has taken on the texture of the moss on the north walls.

They remember. This is their function. Not in the way that all old people remember, but in a specific, structured way: they hold the memory of the cycles. The moth-cycle, the crustacean-cycle, the amphibian-cycle. The dry season, the wet season, the season that comes once in a lifetime. They remember the time, told to them by their grandparents, when the moths didn't come. When the amphibians died. When the beetle-lines spiraled. When the fungus-light went out.

They do not speak of this often. But when the cloud bank changes — when the condensation thickens or thins beyond what the usual cycle would predict — the elders gather in the upper courtyard, the one nearest the cairn where four generations are buried, and they stand together in silence, and they watch the air, and you can see in their faces that they are checking what they see against what they remember, and that the comparison is not always reassuring.

---

And there is one more thing. One more inhabitant of Kell-Vorn that is not a person or an animal or a plant but that lives there nonetheless.

The silence.

Not the absence of sound — the text said this at the beginning and it is true. The silence of Kell-Vorn is a presence. It is the sub-threshold hum of ten thousand crustacean bodies breathing through their flank-membranes. It is the soft condensation forming on every surface. It is the beetle-lines moving across stone without whisper. It is the amphibians sitting in their courtyards without shifting. It is the moths resting on the walls without trembling — except at dusk, when they tremble, when they listen, when whatever they are listening for might be the same thing the elders watch for when the cloud bank changes, and might be the same thing the things above the ridge are circling, and might be the same thing that the beetle-lines are walking toward, and might be nothing at all.

The silence lives in Kell-Vorn the way the moss lives on the north walls — thick, damp, patient, holding everything together. It is the medium in which the settlement exists. It is the air of the air, the texture of the texture, the thing that the people breathe when they breathe, and they are breathing it always, and it is breathing them, and the exchange is so equal and so continuous that you cannot tell, standing in the narrow gap between two houses with the ferns uncurling above you and the beetles walking their lines below, whether the silence belongs to the people or the people belong to the silence.

And then a moth drifts past your face, close enough to feel the warmth of its fur, and its single dark eye is open, and it is looking at you, and then it is gone, and the silence closes behind it like water closing behind a hand.

The houses stand. The stone is sharp. The shelf is narrow. The cloud bank sits. The moisture condenses. The light pulses. The beetles walk. The people watch. The things above the ridge circle, and circle, and do not come down.
