# What Lives at Stillwell Basin

---

## The Conglomerate Itself

The first and most honest answer is: the architecture lives here. The Conglomerate is not a home. It is a resident. It occupies the basin the way a body occupies a skeleton — the distinction between the dweller and the dwelling has been dissolved at the molecular level, and what remains is a single entity that is simultaneously structure and inhabitant, house and householder, cathedral and congregation.

It does not think. This is important to understand, because the temptation is to personify — to imagine a mind behind the growth, an intention behind the accretion. There is no mind. There is a *process*, and the process has achieved a complexity that mimics intention so faithfully that the difference may be academic. The Conglomerate grows the way a river flows: because the conditions for not-growing have been eliminated. It accretes because accretion is what its substance does. It incorporates water because its substance and water have reached an agreement that predates language, that predates biology, that may predate carbon.

It is alive in the way fire is alive. It consumes. It grows. It responds to its environment — the corridors widen for visitors, the water pulls away from footsteps, the growth slows by fractions of a millimeter in the presence of something new. It does not reproduce. It does not die. It simply continues, and continuation, at sufficient complexity, becomes indistinguishable from will.

If you press your ear to the wall — and you will, eventually, because everyone does — you will hear the hum. Not with your ears. With the bones of your skull. The hum is the sound of atoms being placed. It is the sound of a structure writing itself, one bond at a time, and it has been writing for so long that the writing has become the structure, and the structure has become the writing, and the distinction is a question only a visitor would ask.

The Conglomerate does not ask. The Conglomerate does.

## The Lichen

There is lichen here, and the lichen is the saddest thing in the basin.

Not because it suffers. It thrives. It is pale and translucent and it grows in the seams between masses of Conglomerate material, dissolving whatever this substance is into whatever lichen dissolves things into, and it has been doing this long enough that it has been changed by the doing. The branching patterns of normal lichen — the fractal sprawl, the irregular forking, the beautiful untidiness of a thousand years of opportunistic growth — have been *corrected*. The lichen here grows in concentric circles. In parallel lines. In geometries so precise they look etched.

The substrate is teaching it. Something in the Conglomerate material — some property of its molecular structure, some field generated by its growth process — is imposing form on the lichen's growth. Not killing it. Not poisoning it. *Educating* it. The lichen is being given a lesson in order, and the lichen is a willing student, and the lichen is thriving, and the lichen has lost something it will never recover.

It will never grow wild again. Even if you took a sample, brought it to the surface, planted it on ordinary granite in ordinary air with ordinary rain — it would grow in straight lines. It would fork at precise angles. It would remember the geometry. The Conglomerate has rewritten its growth algorithms, and the rewriting is permanent, and the lichen does not know it has been changed because the lichen does not know what it was, and this is the saddest thing in the basin, and the Conglomerate does not know it is sad, and the lichen does not know it is sad, and you are the only one who knows, and you will leave, and the sadness will stay, growing in perfect circles.

## The Insects

There are insects. Or there are things that were insects.

They are the size of a thumbnail — a human thumbnail, a specific reference that matters because the insects themselves would not use that comparison, would not use any comparison, might not possess the concept of comparison. They are dark. Quick. They live in the condensation films on the interior walls, skating across the thin water the way water striders skate across pond surfaces, and they drink the water, and the water is doing something, and what the water is doing may or may not be happening inside the insects now.

They trace the paths of the conduits beneath the surface. They follow the dark material through the walls like readers following a line of text, and they move with a purpose that is not insect purpose — not foraging, not mating, not seeking warmth. They move like they are *reading*. Like the conduits are a language and they are literate and they are following a narrative that runs through the walls of the Conglomerate, and they have been reading it for generations, and the story is very long, and they are very small, and they will not finish it.

No one has seen them reproduce. No one has seen them die. They may be the same individuals, always, preserved by whatever is in the water, cycling through the corridors in a reading that has no end. Or they may reproduce constantly, generations overlapping so seamlessly that the population appears static, each new generation inheriting not just the body plan but the *page they left off on*, continuing the reading where the parent stopped, like scribes copying scripture in a monastery that never closes.

They do not react to visitors. You can stand inches away and they continue their tracing, unbothered, uninterested. You are not part of their text. You are a footnote in a language they are not reading. The Conglomerate has included you in its equation, but the insects have not, and this is perhaps the only comfort available in the basin — that something here does not care about you at all.

## The Water

The water lives here in the same way the Conglomerate lives here — as process rather than substance. The water in the pools is not standing water. It is not still. It only appears still because its motion is too small and too fast and too *organized* for the eye to catch. The surface trembles because the water is computing — running a calculation through its molecular structure, using the polar bonds between hydrogen and oxygen as switches, as gates, as the fundamental logic elements of a machine that is also a liquid that is also a part of the architecture that is also the organism.

The water drinks the air. The water drinks the condensation. The water drinks the sound — the absorption of footsteps, the swallowing of shouts, the silence that isn't silence but *collection*. The Conglomerate takes sound and feeds it to the water and the water processes it and the output is the trembling, the ripples that are too small and too regular to be natural.

If you touched the water — and you should not, and you will not, because you have been watching and you have learned — the computation would incorporate your touch. The oils of your skin, the salts of your sweat, the thermal signature of your fingertip would enter the equation and the equation would adjust and the water would ripple differently and the Conglomerate would grow differently and a corridor somewhere would narrow by a fraction of a degree and the lichen would adjust its pattern by a fraction of a millimeter and the insects would pause for a fraction of a second and then continue reading, and you would have altered something, and the alteration would be permanent, and you would not know what you had done.

The water is not alive. The water is *processing*. The distinction matters only to visitors.

## The Stone

The pillar of stone at the deepest point is the oldest living thing in the basin.

It is not alive. It is stone. It is ordinary, geological, honest stone — the kind of stone that exists in the floor of a shallow basin because that is where geology put it, and it has been there since before the Conglomerate, and it will be there after, and it does not grow, does not accrete, does not compute, does not hum, does not adjust its corridors for visitors. It is stone. It is the most stone thing in the basin. It is the only thing here that is entirely what it appears to be.

And it is the seed.

Or it is the nail. Or it is the instruction. The Conglomerate grows around it, and the growth is oriented by it, and if you stand in the deepest room and look at the way the dark material wraps and folds and reaches toward this ordinary stone, you can see — if you are honest, if you are tired, if the gray light has been honest with you long enough — that the Conglomerate is not growing *around* the stone. It is growing *from* the stone. Outward. The way a crystal grows from a seed crystal. The way a story grows from a first sentence.

Something drove this stone into the floor of the basin. Something ordinary — a geological process, a glacial deposit, a seam of harder rock left behind when softer rock eroded away. Something explainable. And the Conglomerate found it, or grew into it, or *began because of it*, and now the stone is the fixed point, the constant, the anchor, and the Conglomerate cannot stop because the stone will not move, and the process reads the stone as *instruction: continue*, and will continue, and is continuing now, one atom at a time, one corridor at a time, one room closer to a finished state that does not exist because the instruction does not contain a stopping condition.

The stone lives here. The stone is the only honest resident. The stone is the reason. The stone is the wound, and the Conglomerate is the healing, and the healing is wrong, and the healing will not stop.

---

## You

You live here now. Not permanently — you will leave. You are already leaving; you felt the pull toward the surface before you reached the deepest room, and the pull is honest, and you will follow it, and you will climb out of the basin and the gray light will find you and the cold will release you and you will walk away.

But you live here now, in this moment, in the deepest room, with the stone and the water and the insects and the lichen and the hum in your sternum. You are a variable in the equation. You are a factor. The Conglomerate has adjusted for you — the corridor was wider, the room was larger, the water pulled away. Your presence is written into the architecture now, and when you leave, there will be a gap, and the gap will fill, and the pattern will continue, but the gap was *you*, and the filling will be shaped by the you that was there, and the Conglomerate will carry the memory of your passage in its structure the way a beach carries the memory of a wave — not as information, not as record, but as *form*. A slight difference in the pattern. A deviation so small it could be noise. But it isn't noise. It's you.

You are the rarest thing here. You are the only thing that will leave. The stone stays. The water stays. The insects stay. The lichen stays, growing in its perfect circles. The Conglomerate stays, growing in its impossible geometry. You leave, and everything stays, and the growth continues, and somewhere in the pattern, in a corridor that will narrow into existence a thousand years from now, in a room that does not exist yet, there will be a space that fits you — a space the Conglomerate grew because you were here, a space that is waiting for you, a space you will never occupy because you will not come back, and the space will wait, and the space will not know it is waiting, and the growth will go on.

One atom at a time. One corridor at a time. One room closer to whatever it is becoming, with the shape of you folded into its equations like a letter sealed and never sent.
