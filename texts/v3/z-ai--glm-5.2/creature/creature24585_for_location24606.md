# The Inhabitants

---

## The Scaled Ones (the rock-creatures)

Let the clicking come first, because the clicking is how they know each other.

A single individual at rest is nearly invisible — and nearly indistinguishable from the stone it rests on. The mineral crust that builds on its shell is the same crust that builds on the rock, the same salts leached from the same steam, and the creature does nothing to discourage this. It may even encourage it. There are grooves in the carapace — shallow channels that follow the segmentation — where moisture collects and minerals precipitate more readily, suggesting the shell is *shaped* to accumulate deposits. The animal is its own substrate. It carries a piece of the plateau on its back.

Underneath the crust, the shell itself is a dark blue-grey, the color of the granite, and it is hard in the way that stone is hard — not chitinous-flexible like an insect's wing cover, but rigid, mineralized, almost ceramic. The body is oval, flattened, roughly the proportions of a flattened fist, though size varies considerably. The smallest are the length of a thumb. The largest are broad as a spread hand and low as a closed one. The variation does not seem to map onto any obvious axis of age or sex — it may simply be that growth is opportunistic, that an individual grows as much as the warmth and the minerals allow, and no two organisms experience exactly the same conditions for exactly the same span of time.

The legs fold flat. This is important, and it is the thing you notice first when one unfolds. At rest, the legs are pressed against the underside of the body in grooves that receive them perfectly, so that the animal presents a smooth, unbroken surface to the world — no projections, no angles, nothing for wind or predator to catch. When the animal moves, the legs unfold in a sequence that takes less than a second and produces a scrambling, rapid gait that is startling in its urgency after the absolute stillness of the resting state. There are more legs than you'd expect. Not six, not eight — more, perhaps twelve or fourteen, arranged in two longitudinal rows. They are short, thick, jointed, and tipped with small hooked claws that grip the wet stone with a precision that suggests the claws are shaped to match the micro-texture of the granite. These animals evolved *on this stone* and nowhere else. Their feet are made for these specific fractures, these specific roughnesses.

The clicking is the shell striking the ground as the animal moves. Each step produces a small, hard contact — the shell dipping slightly as the legs shift weight, touching the stone, rising again. At speed, this becomes a rapid chatter. Across a surface where hundreds are moving simultaneously, the chatters merge into the sustained percussive wash that is the plateau's characteristic sound. They click to move. But they also click to communicate. Resting animals will sometimes produce a single, deliberate click — lifting the front edge of the shell and bringing it down against the stone — and nearby animals will respond with clicks of their own, in patterns that vary in tempo and intensity. The meaning, if there is a single meaning, is not obvious. But the animals are listening to each other. They are aware of each other's positions. A click from one direction causes a ripple of slight repositioning in the individuals nearby, a slow redistribution across the available warm stone, like a crowd shifting to make room.

They do not appear to eat the mineral crusts. They do not appear to eat each other. What they eat is harder to observe, because it happens in the fractures — the gaps in the stone where they take shelter and where the steam provides warmth and moisture. In those gaps, on the vertical walls and in the deeper, darker recesses where light never reaches, there are biofilms — thin, gelatinous layers of chemotrophic bacteria that derive energy from the sulfur and iron compounds the steam carries up from depth. The rock-creatures descend into the fractures, cling to the walls with their hooked claws, and graze these films. Their mouthparts are small, positioned underneath the body, and adapted for scraping — a rasping structure, like a radula, that removes the biofilm without damaging the stone beneath. They are patient feeders. A single animal may spend hours in a fracture, moving slowly across a wall, scraping a path through the bacterial film that is visible afterward as a pale streak on the dark stone.

They return to the surface to warm. The geothermal heat that rises through the fractures warms the stone near the vents, and the animals position themselves on these warm patches, spreading their bodies flat to maximize contact. This is not basking in the way a lizard basks — there is no sun, no light-seeking behavior. It is a metabolic necessity. Their body temperature is largely determined by the temperature of the stone they are in contact with, and in the fractures, feeding, they are in the warmest available environment — but also in the dark, and in the narrow spaces where predators cannot reach. On the surface, they are exposed, but they are also able to process what they have eaten, to digest, to grow. The cycle is: descend, feed in the dark, ascend, warm on the stone, repeat. It is a tide. It is the rhythm of the entire plateau, and it runs on geothermal time.

They reproduce, but the manner of reproduction is not easily observed. There are no displays, no courtship behaviors visible on the surface. Occasionally, in the deeper fractures, two animals will be found clinging close together, shells touching along their long edges, motionless, for hours or days. Whether this is mating or something else — shared warmth, cooperative defense, a behavior without a clear analog — is not certain. The young, when they appear, are small versions of the adults, fully formed, with mineral crusts already beginning to form on their shells. They do not pass through a larval stage, or if they do, it happens out of sight, in the deepest fractures, in the warm dark where the steam originates and where no surface observation can reach.

They are the foundation of the plateau's animal ecology. Everything else that lives here either eats them or lives off what they leave behind.

## The Wind-Riders (the predators)

They have no call. This is the first thing to understand about them. The sky above the plateau is large and pale and nearly silent, and the things that move through it do so without sound. No cry, no shriek, no territorial declaration. They communicate, if they communicate, through movement — through the patterns of their flight, the angles of their wings, the timing of their descents. A wing-tip dipped. A turn sharpened. A shadow that falls across the stone and then lifts away.

They are large. The smallest have wingspans broader than a person's arm span, and the largest are bigger still — broad enough that when one passes low overhead, the diffuse light dims perceptibly, as if a cloud has crossed the sun. Their bodies are dark, darker than the stone, darker than the shadows. They are a deep, matte charcoal that absorbs light rather than reflecting it, and this makes them difficult to see against the pale sky until they are close. You register them as absences — places where the sky is not.

The wings are broad and relatively short, adapted for soaring rather than sustained flapping. They ride the thermals that rise from the geothermally heated plateau — columns of warm air that ascend from the stone and provide lift without effort. In this, they are like vultures, but their shape is different. The wings have a slight dihedral — a V-shape, seen from the front — and the trailing edges are ragged, notched, as if the feathers (if they are feathers; the structure may be different) are separated and flexible, adjusting individually to the air. The tail is short and broad and can fan or fold, and it is used constantly in flight, adjusting, correcting, trimming. These animals fly with their tails as much as with their wings.

Their heads are the one part of them that breaks the dark, lightless coloration. The head is pale. Grey-white, the same color as the creatures in the fissures, the same color as the mineral deposits. It stands out against the dark body like a bare face against a hood. The head is large, the eyes large within it, and the beak — if it is a beak — is hooked, dark, and strong. It is a raptor's beak. It is made for gripping things that do not wish to be gripped.

They hunt by sight. This is clear from the eyes and from the hunting behavior. A wind-rider at altitude scans the surface of the plateau, and when it locates a rock-creature that is exposed — one that has moved away from a fracture, one that is warming on the stone, one that is in transit between feeding and resting — it folds its wings and drops. The descent is fast, angled, and nearly silent. The wind does not carry the sound of the approach because the descent cuts through the air rather than pressing against it. The impact, when it comes, is a collision — feet-first, the talons (there are talons, dark, curved, strong) striking the stone where the prey is, and the prey is caught or it is not. If it is caught, the predator opens its wings immediately and rises, carrying the animal in its grip. If it is not — if the rock-creature has already unfolded and scuttled to the nearest fracture, which happens often — the wind-rider lands on the stone, stands for a moment, and then launches again without apparent frustration. Predation here is not dramatic. It is routine.

The success rate is moderate. Perhaps one strike in three results in a capture. This is enough. The plateau supports a population of wind-riders that numbers in the dozens — not hundreds, but enough that there are always several visible in the sky, always a few circling, always the possibility of a sudden dark shape folding and dropping somewhere across the wide wet stone.

They return to the cliffs at the plateau's edge to eat and to nest. The nests are built on ledges in the rock faces where the plateau drops away into the valleys below — ledges that are sheltered from the wind but accessible from the air. The nests are not delicate. They are massive, accumulated structures, built over years and used by successive generations. They are made of everything available — the shed shells of dead rock-creatures (which are mineral-hard and persist for years), the bones or structural remains of prey, lumps of mineral crusts prised from the stone, and the crystallized salts that form on every surface near the steam vents. The nests end up looking like geological formations — mounds of mineral material, pale and dark in layers, built up and crystallized and built upon again. They are, in a real sense, part of the cliff. They have accreted. They are inseparable from the rock they sit on.

The young, when they are present, are pale. Almost white. They are visible in the nests as heads rising above the rims — pale, downy, large-eyed, waiting. They do not call for food. They wait. When an adult returns, it lands on the nest's rim and drops the prey into it, and the young descend on it with a silence that matches the adult's. The feeding is quick and efficient and without ceremony. Then the adult leaves again, and the young settle back down, and the nest looks like rock again.

There are usually two young per nest. Sometimes one. Never more than two that are visible. The breeding cycle is not tied to seasons, because the plateau does not have seasons in the way lower altitudes do — the temperature variation across the year is minimal, the light variation is subtle, and the food supply is constant, because the steam is constant. Breeding happens when breeding happens, on a timeline that is individual to each pair, and the nests are occupied continuously, year after year, by a lineage that may extend back further than the nests themselves, because the nests are built on older nests, and those on older still, and the oldest parts have long since mineralized into the cliff face entirely.

The wind-riders are not social in the way that flocking birds are social. They do not gather in groups. But they are aware of each other. In the air, they maintain spacing — a loose, shifting geometry that keeps individuals at certain distances from each other, as if the thermals are a shared resource that must be divided. When one drops to hunt, others nearby adjust their patterns, shifting to cover the area the hunter has vacated. They are not cooperating, exactly. They are partitioning. They are running a system, and the system is the sky.

## The Pale Ones (the fissure-dwellers)

In the fractures, below the surface, in the warm dark where the steam moves slowly and the stone is wet and the bacterial films grow thick on the vertical walls, there are the pale ones.

They are not like the rock-creatures. They are not like the wind-riders. They are something else entirely — something that has committed to a life without light, without wind, without the wide pale sky, and has been transformed by that commitment.

They are soft. This is the first thing that distinguishes them. They have no hard shell, no mineralized carapace. Their bodies are fleshy, grey-white, and slightly translucent, so that you can see the darker structures within — organs, or organs' analogs, branching through the body in patterns that look almost vascular. They cling to the walls of the fractures with the underside of their bodies, which are flattened and adhesive — a broad, moist surface that holds to the wet stone by a combination of suction and a thin film of mucus that they secrete. They are not unlike limpets in form, but they are larger — a hand-span across, sometimes more — and their upper surfaces are not smooth but textured, with low, rounded protuberances that may be sensory structures or may be respiratory, or may be both.

They do not move when they are observed. When light falls on them — when a person crouches at the edge of a fracture and peers down into the gap — they freeze. This may be a defense, or it may be that they are not equipped to process the presence of an observer and simply default to stillness. Either way, they are nearly impossible to see in their natural state. What is known about them is inferred from the traces they leave.

Those traces are everywhere in the fractures. The bacterial films that the rock-creatures graze on the vertical walls are not uniformly distributed — there are paths through them, broad, meandering tracks where the film has been consumed down to the bare stone. These tracks are wider than the tracks the rock-creatures leave. They are the feeding paths of the pale ones, and they suggest an animal that moves slowly, methodically, and continuously — not the rapid, opportunistic foraging of the rock-creatures but a patient, grazing existence that covers the same ground repeatedly, harvesting the film as it regrows.

They may also consume the mineral deposits that the steam leaves on the fracture walls. The calcium and salt crusts that build up in the deeper, warmer parts of the fractures show signs of being rasped — scraped surfaces, thin gouges in the crystalline material — that are too broad and too shallow to be the work of rock-creatures. The pale ones may be supplementing their diet with minerals directly, processing the geothermal output in a way that bypasses the bacterial middleman. They may, in fact, be chemotrophs themselves — deriving energy not from the food they eat but from chemical reactions enabled by the minerals they ingest. This is speculative. What is not speculative is that they are there, that they are numerous in the deep fractures, and that they are a part of the system that the plateau is running.

They are eaten, but not often. The rock-creatures, descending into the fractures to feed, will occasionally encounter a pale one and will rasp it from the wall and consume it. This is not a common event — the pale ones tend to occupy deeper, warmer, narrower parts of the fractures than the rock-creatures prefer — but it happens, and when it happens, the rock-creature's shell shows a pale smear afterward, a residue of mucus and tissue that dries and mineralizes and becomes part of the crust on its shell. In this way, the pale ones enter the surface ecosystem — as traces, as residue, as a mineral signature on the backs of the animals that have eaten them.

They are the plateau's hidden population. They live in the place the plateau is closest to its own source — in the fractures, in the warm dark, where the steam is newest and the stone is warmest and the chemistry is rawest. They are the things that live closest to the engine.

## The Circles (the flat black growths)

In the wider fractures, where some organic debris has accumulated, the circles grow.

They are not animals. They may not even be a single organism — they may be a consortium, a symbiotic community of bacteria and fungi and something else, something without a terrestrial name, that has found a way to live at the absolute margin of what biology can sustain. They grow in circles. This is the most notable thing about them. Not patches, not irregular spreading colonies, but circles — radiating outward from a center, in a ring that expands with a slow regularity that suggests a growth pattern governed by a single variable. The center dies as the ring expands. The outer edge grows. The dead interior builds up, becomes slightly raised, becomes the substrate on which the living edge sits. The organism constructs its own ground. It makes the soil it grows in, and it dies on the soil it has made, and the living part is always at the margin, always pushing outward, always at the frontier of what can be alive.

The living edge is green. A cold, deep green, the green of copper oxidation, the green of a chemistry that involves metal. The dead interior is black. Pure black, dense, fine-grained, looking more like a mineral deposit than a biological remnant. The transition from green to black is sharp — a band perhaps a millimeter wide where the living tissue becomes dead matter. The organism does not fade. It is alive and then it is not.

They never exceed the size of a dinner plate. This may be a limit imposed by the availability of water and nutrients in the fractures, or it may be something intrinsic to the organism itself — a lifespan, a growth program, a predetermined size beyond which the chemistry that sustains it cannot function. Whatever the cause, the circles are bounded. They do not dominate the landscape. They are rare, small, precise, and they are the only thing on the plateau that grows in a shape that suggests pattern, that suggests design, that suggests something other than the raw mechanical processes of steam and stone and mineral precipitation.

They are eaten by nothing. Or if they are eaten, it is not observed. The rock-creatures do not graze them. The pale ones do not rasp them. The circles exist outside the food web that connects the other inhabitants. They are a side process. A parallel experiment. A thing the plateau is doing that is not connected to the main operation. They grow and they die and they leave their black residue in the fractures, and the mineral crusts build up over them, and eventually they are buried, and they become part of the stone, and they are gone.

But while they are here, they are green. They are the only green thing on the plateau. They are the only sign that this landscape might someday be something other than wet dark stone and the things that live on wet dark stone. They are a promise that the plateau has made to itself, and it does not know it has made it, and it may not keep it.

## The Stalks

In the depressions, in the places where the wind is broken and the water collects into something slightly deeper than a film, the stalks grow.

They are the closest thing to a plant that the plateau has, and they are not close. They are fifteen centimeters tall, dark red shading to black, thin as matchsticks, and they grow in clusters of fifty or more, all leaning the same direction — away from the wind that almost always comes from the same quarter. They look burned. They look like things that have been subjected to a process that has destroyed everything about them except their structure. They are the skeletons of plants, or they are plants that have never been anything but skeletons, or they are something else that has converged on a form that looks like a dead plant because that form works here.

Each stalk is topped with a single small structure. It is dark, slightly glossy, and slightly warmer to the touch than the air around it — suggesting it may be metabolically active, generating a small amount of heat. It may be a reproductive organ. It may be a leaf. It may be a sensory structure. It is the only part of the organism that is not thin and dark and skeletal. It is, in its small, dense way, the focus of the plant — the part that everything else exists to support.

They do not flower. They do not fruit. They do not reproduce in any way that is visible. They are simply there, in their clusters, in their depressions, leaning away from the wind, and they have been there for as long as the depressions have held water, and they will be there until the depressions change or the water stops or the wind shifts to a new prevailing direction, at which point they will lean a new way, or they will not, and they will die, and the depressions will be bare again.

Nothing eats them. Nothing uses them. They are not part of the system. Like the circles, they are a side experiment. Unlike the circles, they do not even leave a residue. When they die, they vanish. The water takes them. They dissolve into the thin films on the stone and become mineral and water and nothing.

## What the plateau is, to the things that live on it

The plateau is not a habitat. A habitat is a place where things live. The plateau is a process, and the things that live on it are part of the process.

The steam rises. The minerals precipitate. The bacteria grow. The rock-creatures feed on the bacteria and carry the minerals into their shells. The wind-riders feed on the rock-creatures and carry the minerals to the cliffs, where the nests accrete. The pale ones feed on the bacteria and the minerals in the fractures and stay where the process begins. The circles grow and die and become part of the stone. The stalks grow and die and dissolve. Everything is moving. Everything is being processed. The plateau takes heat from below and water from above and runs them together across a surface of raw stone, and the life that has emerged here is not separate from that running — it is a part of it. It is a stage in it. The rock-creatures are a mechanism for moving minerals from the fractures to the surface. The wind-riders are a mechanism for moving minerals from the surface to the cliffs. The pale ones are a mechanism for cycling minerals within the fractures. The circles and stalks are mechanisms for testing whether other things are possible.

The plateau does not know this. The plateau is not conscious. The plateau is a slab of warm granite with water moving through it and air moving across it, and the things that live on it live on it because they can, and they do what they do because it is what they do, and there is no purpose and there is no plan and there is no meaning except the meaning that the plateau's processes generate by their own continuous operation.

But if you stand on the plateau, in the diffuse light, with the clouds moving past your knees and the clicking all around you and the dark shapes circling above, you feel it — the sense that this place is running. That it is operating. That the breathing of the earth is not a metaphor but a function, and the things that live here are part of the function, and the function does not need them but it includes them, and they are here because the function is here, and the function will be here for a very long time, and they will be here for as long as the function sustains them, and then they will not, and the function will continue, and the plateau will continue, and the steam will continue, and the stone will continue, and the clicking will stop, and the sky will be empty, and the fractures will be silent, and the earth will breathe, and the breathing will have no sound at all.
