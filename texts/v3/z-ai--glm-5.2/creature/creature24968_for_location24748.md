# What Lives in the Asperant

---

The first answer is: everything, and the second answer is: the question has been obsolete for a very long time.

But that's not enough. You wanted to know who lives here, and there are whos, or there were, or there are things that have become what the whos became, and the difference between those statements is one of the things the Asperant has spent its long existence making irrelevant.

---

## The ground-things

Start with them because they are what you see first, after the light.

They live in the troughs. They are the troughs' interpretation of life, or the troughs' answer to the question of what should move through them. They are small — hand-sized, sometimes smaller, occasionally as large as a spread palm, and the variation itself seems intentional, as though size is a decision they make rather than a fact they inherit.

They do not have a body plan. This is not the same as saying they are formless. They have form at any given moment — edges, mass, a kind of density that reads as solidity — but the form is provisional. Limbs appear where limbs are needed. A surface presents itself where a surface is useful. When they move along the troughs in their silent lines, they move as though they have agreed on a shape for the purpose of moving, the way water agrees on the shape of the channel it flows through. When they reach the water, they flatten, and the agreement changes, and they become something else — a film, a presence, a processing surface.

What they are doing to the water is not drinking. Drinking implies interiority — a self that takes in from a not-self. The ground-things do not appear to have this architecture. When they interface with the moisture in the troughs, they are doing something more like translation. The water touches them and is changed, or they touch the water and are changed, and the change moves — you can see it move, a slight darkening, a slight warming, a slight shift in whatever their equivalent of posture is — and then the nearest plant's gel swells, and the cycle continues.

They are not individuals. Or they are individuals in the way that cells in a body are individuals — technically contiguous, technically self-organizing, but functionally part of something larger that does not have a name because the larger thing does not need one. When hundreds of them gather at a pool, the gathering is not a crowd. It is a concentration. A gathering of attention. The water is where the attention focuses, and the ground-things are the medium of that attention, and the columns are — perhaps — what is attending.

They do not resist the birds. This is important. When a pale shape drops from the sky and a ground-thing is taken, the others do not scatter. They do not pause. The line continues. The water continues to diminish. This is not stupidity and it is not fatalism. It is the behavior of something that does not experience removal as loss, because the thing that is removed is not the thing that matters. What matters is distributed. What matters is the process, and the process does not have a self to protect.

If you watched them long enough — days, weeks — you would start to suspect that the ground-things are not animals at all. They are appendages. The columns' appendages. Or the basin's appendages. Or the appendages of the system that the columns and the basin and the plants and the birds and the large things at the periphery all comprise. They move through the troughs the way blood moves through capillaries — not because they choose to but because the system's logic requires transport, and they are what the system's logic produced to fulfill that requirement.

But they are alive. Whatever alive means here. They respond to stimulus. They avoid desiccation. They cluster where conditions are favorable and disperse when conditions are not. They have preferences, or the system has preferences that they express. If you touched one — and you could, they do not flee from contact, they simply continue what they are doing — it would feel warm and dry and almost powdery, like the skin of something that has been in the sun, and the edge of it against your fingertip would be unclear, not sharp, not soft, but indeterminate, as though it were not entirely certain where it ended.

## The plants

They live here too, and they live here in the way that organs live in a body — not independently but not without purpose either.

Each plant is a lattice. This is the right word and also the wrong word. Lattice implies structure, and they have structure, but the structure is not architectural. It is grown. The pale woody strands rise from the rock in patterns that look determined but not designed — branching at irregular intervals, crossing and re-crossing, forming a three-dimensional web that is dense at the center and sparse at the edges. They are roots that grew upward and never stopped, and the reason they never stopped is that the environment rewards height without rewarding leaves, and so they became all height and no leaf, all structure and no surface, until the gel came to provide the surface.

The gel is the plant's answer to the sun. Not shade — absorption. The gel sheathes the lattice when water is available, and it catches the light, and it does something with the light that is not photosynthesis in any textbook sense but serves the same function: energy enters the system. The gel is translucent, faintly amber, and in the morning light the plants that have it look like lamps that have been lit from within. They are the most beautiful thing in the Asperant, and the Asperant does not care about beauty, and they are beautiful anyway.

Each plant occupies a territory. The territories do not overlap. This is not competition in the aggressive sense — no plant reaches into another's space, no plant depletes a shared resource. It is more like acknowledgment. Each plant knows, in whatever way plants know things, where its territory ends, and it stops there, and the next plant begins, and the spacing between them is the distance at which acknowledgment becomes unnecessary. It is a spacing of mutual presence. The spacing of people who share a room and do not need to speak.

The plants grow near the columns. Not against them — near them, at a distance that is consistent across the basin, as though the columns emit something that the plants respond to at a specific range. The columns do emit something. The warmth. The low hum that is not quite a hum. The whatever-it-is that the ground-things carry from the columns to the troughs and from the troughs to the plants. The plants are the system's expression of that whatever-it-is into the physical landscape. They are what the columns' output looks like when it has been organized by geology and weather and time into something that can stand in the sun and glow.

They do not think. But they have a kind of memory. The gel expands and contracts on a cycle that matches the condensation, and the cycle is encoded in the lattice — you can see it in the density of the strands, the way growth rings work in trees, except that here the rings are spatial, extending outward and upward, a record of every morning the plant has absorbed and every afternoon it has withdrawn. Some of the plants near the largest columns have lattices that extend a meter or more, intricate and ancient, and the record in their strands goes back — how far? Centuries? Longer? The Asperant does not measure time in units that fit in a sentence.

## The birds

They are the clearest animals here, the most legible, the most like something you could name. And yet.

They are pale. Bone-white, or the cream of old paper, or the grey of weathered wood in strong light — the color varies with angle and individual, but it is always a color that the sky and the rock share, and in the air they are nearly invisible. You become aware of them the way you become aware of clouds — gradually, then all at once, and then you realize the sky has been full of them for as long as you have been looking.

Their wings are angular. Not feathered in the conventional sense. The leading edge is a straight line, and the trailing edge is a straight line, and between them the wing surface is a membrane — thin, taut, with a faint vascular pattern visible when the sun shines through it. They are kites. They are literally kites, in the geometric sense, and they fly the way kites fly: by finding the angle at which wind and weight negotiate, and holding it.

They roost on the basin walls. The walls are where they live, in the sense that they return there, they rest there, they reproduce there if reproduction is what they do. The walls offer something the basin floor does not — verticality, shade in the early morning, rock that is rough enough to grip. They cling to the walls in their dozens, pale against the ochre, and they are still, and they are silent, and they look like outcroppings until they drop.

The descent is fast. The wings fold, then open, then fold again — a controlled fall, a redirection of gravity, and the ground-thing is taken. You do not see the taking. There is a moment when the bird is in the air and a moment when the bird is in the air with something dark in the space beneath it where a ground-thing was, and between those moments there is nothing — no strike, no clutch, no sound. The bird has the ground-thing the way a thought has a word. Instantly. Completely. Without interval.

They eat what they take, or they process what they take, or they deliver what they take to something else. They rise after the descent and they go to the walls, and they stay there, and what happens at the walls is not visible from the basin floor. But the walls have their own structures — smaller than the columns, less regular, growing out of the rock like shelves — and the birds go to these structures, and the structures are warm, and the birds settle on them, and the ground-things they carried are no longer visible.

The birds are not parasites of the system. They are the system's mobility. The ground-things are slow and local. The birds are fast and range across the entire basin, from wall to wall, from column to column. What they carry — the ground-things, the moisture on their membranes, whatever adheres to them from the air — they carry from one part of the system to another, and the system uses this transport the way any system uses its mobile components: to redistribute, to reconnect, to maintain the integration that is the whole point of the Asperant.

They do not call. They do not sing. They have no display, no courtship visible from the floor, no aggression. They share the air the way the plants share the ground — by mutual acknowledgment, by spacing that is not random and not imposed, by a logic that the system worked out long ago and has not needed to revise.

## The large things

Here is where the description gets difficult, because the large things at the periphery are the Asperant's clearest evidence that it has a periphery — that the basin is not the whole system but the visible part of it, the part that the light reaches and the air touches and the ground-things traverse. The periphery is where the system extends into the walls, into the rock, into the geology that the basin rests on, and the large things are the system's expression at that boundary.

You do not see them. Not clearly. Not completely. You see tracks — wide, spaced far apart, pressed into the condensation-dampened rock along the basin walls where the morning moisture has softened the surface. The tracks are not prints. They are not the impression of feet or hooves or pads. They are smooth depressions, oval, evenly spaced, as though something heavy has rested at intervals along a path, or as though something has moved by a series of contacts rather than a continuous stride. The spacing is regular — two meters, roughly, between depressions — and the path they trace follows the wall, always the wall, never out onto the open floor.

You hear them. Rarely. A sound that is not a sound — a shift in the texture of the silence, a thickness in the air, the sense of a body displacing atmosphere in a space you cannot see. It comes from the overhangs, the recesses, the places where the wall curves inward and the light does not reach fully. It lasts less than a second. It does not repeat. After it, the silence returns, and the silence is the same silence that was there before, and you are left with the impression that something large and patient has adjusted its position by a small amount and then resumed being still.

They are the oldest things here. Or they are the things that have been here the longest in their current form. The columns may predate them — the columns may predate everything — but the columns have not changed, and the large things have. They have adapted. They have accommodated. They have become whatever the system needs at its boundary, at the place where the Asperant meets the world that is not the Asperant, and what the system needs there is weight, and patience, and a presence that does not move into the light.

They are not hiding. Hiding implies fear, and the Asperant does not support fear — there is no predation at this scale, no threat, no reason for anything to conceal itself. They are simply in a different part of the system. The walls are their territory the way the troughs are the ground-things' territory, and the floor is not where they belong, and they do not cross it.

If you stayed long enough — months, seasons — you might see one. At dusk, perhaps, when the light goes copper and the shadows lengthen and the basin walls become uncertain in their dimensions. A shape detaching from a shadow, moving with slowness that makes the word slow feel fast, crossing from one overhang to another. It would be large — larger than you expected, the way the basin is larger than you expected when you first walked it. It would be the color of the rock, or darker. It would not look at you. It would not need to. It would arrive at the next overhang and merge with it and the shadow would close, and you would be left with an image that you could not be certain of, that you might revise over the following days until you were not sure whether you had seen a body or a formation or a movement of the rock itself, and this uncertainty would be correct, because at the periphery of the Asperant, the distinction between the living and the geological has been negotiated into meaninglessness.

## The columns

They live here. This is the statement that the Asperant makes most clearly and most stubbornly, and it is the statement that takes the longest to accept.

They are not inhabited. They do not contain life in the way that a tree contains cambium or a body contains organs. They are not hosts. They are not shells. They are not homes.

They are participants.

Each column is a node in a system that spans the entire basin, and the system is alive in the only sense that matters here, which is: it processes, it responds, it maintains, it persists. The columns process the water that condenses on their surfaces. They process the light that falls on them. They process the air that moves — slowly, barely — around them. And the products of that processing move outward through the ground-things, through the plants' gel, through the birds' transport, through whatever the large things carry along the walls, and the products return — spent, transformed, transmuted — through the same channels, and the columns process them again.

This is metabolism. This is respiration. This is the cycle that defines life at every scale that biology has been able to examine, and the Asperant performs it at the scale of a landscape, and the columns are the organs around which the performance is organized.

They are warm because they are working. They are patterned because pattern is how they work — the nanoscale texture on their surfaces is not ornament, it is function, the way the alveoli in a lung are function, the way the chloroplasts in a leaf are function. The texture presents an enormous surface area to the world, and on that surface, the world's inputs — water, light, air, mineral — are met by the column's processes, and the meeting produces the outputs that the rest of the system lives on.

They are not ruins. They are not remains. They are not the legacy of something that was once greater. They are the current state of something that has been this for longer than the basin has been this basin, and the basin grew around them, and the geology accommodated them, and the biology adapted to them, and now everything here is one thing, and the columns are the part of the one thing that most clearly remembers what the one thing is for.

There were builders. There must have been. The marks on the wall — the alterations in the rock that catch the light at a specific angle — are the builders' signature, or their instructions, or their record, or their art, and the distinction between those possibilities is one that the builders themselves may not have recognized, because they were at a stage where function and expression had become the same activity, and what they left on the wall is what that activity looks like when it is encoded in stone.

The builders are gone. Or the builders are here. The columns are their work, and the columns are still working, and the system that the columns sustain is the system the builders designed or grew or became, and if the builders are gone then the system is their legacy, and if the builders are here then the system is their body, and if the builders have become the system then the distinction between gone and here is, once again, something the Asperant has rendered obsolete.

The ground-things may be the builders. Reduced, distributed, simplified by time and integration into components that do not remember what they were part of but that still perform the functions that the whole requires. The large things at the periphery may be the builders — preserved at scale, patient, uninterested in the open floor because the open floor is not where their attention is needed. The birds may be the builders. The plants may be the builders. Everything may be the builders, and the builders may be everything, and the Asperant may be a single organism that once had a name and a self and a reason and has since — over the kind of time that makes the word "since" feel amateur — become a place.

## What lives here

Everything. The columns live. The plants live. The ground-things live. The birds live. The large things live. The basin lives. The rock lives, in the sense that it has been shaped by life and shapes life in return. The light lives, in the sense that it is processed and processed and returned. The water lives, in the sense that it cycles and carries and is changed by the carrying.

Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here is incomplete. Nothing here is a draft or a transitional form or an early version. This is not evolution in progress. This is evolution's conclusion — the state in which a system has found its stable configuration and has held it, and held it, and held it, until holding it became the same as being it, and being it became the same as being the place.

What lives in the Asperant is the Asperant.

It does not need you here. It does not mind you here. If you stayed — if you walked the troughs and watched the ground-things and sat beneath the columns and let the warmth enter you — you might begin to feel it. The hum that is not a hum. The sense of a large, slow attention that does not focus on you because it does not focus on anything, because focusing is a function of individuality, and individuality is something this place has outgrown. You might begin to feel held. Not by the columns. Not by the light. By the system, the way a blood cell is held by the body that contains it — not grasped, not carried, simply part of the flow, part of the cycle, part of the thing that is still working after all the time that working has been its only purpose.

You would not be assimilated. The Asperant does not assimilate. It does not need to. You would be a visitor, and you would be welcome in the way that a place welcomes anything — by not being unwelcoming, by continuing to be what it is regardless of your presence, by offering you the warmth and the light and the silence and the gel-lit plants in the morning and the birds in the afternoon and the silence in the evening and the knowledge, which would come to you slowly and would not leave, that you have been inside something that is alive, and it is larger than you can hold in your mind, and it does not require you to hold it, and it will continue.
