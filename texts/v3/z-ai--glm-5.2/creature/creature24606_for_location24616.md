# What Lives in the Confluence

---

## The builders, first, because everything else depends on them

Or perhaps they don't come first. Perhaps ordering by dependence is the wrong instinct here, the way ordering by height would be wrong in a place where everything happens at every height simultaneously. But they are the ones you notice last, which gives them a strange kind of prominence — the thing that was present all along but required the rest of the context to become visible.

They are tall. That is the first reliable fact. Two and a half meters minimum, some approaching three, and their height is not uniform but varies in a way that suggests it is not genetic but *functional* — that a given builder is as tall as it needs to be for the work it is currently doing, and that this changes. You have not seen one change height. But you have seen two builders standing near each other, one clearly taller than the other, and returned minutes later to find them the same height, and you cannot say when the shift occurred because they move so slowly that the change, if it happened, happened at a pace your eye was not built to track.

Their bodies are the pale translucent material of the middle layers. This is not a simile. When you are close to one — and you have been close, closer than you intended, because they do not move out of your way and the corridors are narrow — you can see that the substance of their bodies is continuous with the substance of the walls they are working on. There is no seam. There is no joint where builder ends and construction begins. The translucent material flows from the body into the wall in a gradual transition, and the builder stands there, part of the architecture, for minutes or hours, and then separates, and the place where it was connected to the wall is now solid, finished, a new surface where there was none before.

They are not translucent the way glass is translucent. They are translucent the way a body is — you can see shapes inside them, darker masses that might be organs or might be structural elements or might be something for which the word *organ* is a poor approximation. These interior shapes shift. They move. Not randomly — they move in ways that correlate with what the builder is doing. When a builder is extending a limb into a wall, the dark masses inside its body flow toward that limb, and the limb brightens, becomes more opaque, and the material at its tip begins to change consistency, becoming the pale construction-stuff that will harden into a new surface. When a builder is standing idle — and they do stand idle, for long stretches, in poses that look like contemplation but might be something else entirely — the dark masses settle into central clusters, and the body becomes more uniformly translucent, and it glows faintly with the same amber light that the middle layers produce.

They have heads. Probably. The upper terminus of their bodies is differentiated from the rest — slightly wider, slightly more opaque, with two depressions that might be eye sockets or might be sensory structures of a completely different kind. They do not appear to use them to see. They do not respond to visual stimuli in any way you can detect. You stood directly in front of one for three minutes, your face thirty centimeters from its head, and it gave no indication of your presence. But when a four-winged creature flew past behind you, close enough that the wingbeat displaced air against your back, the builder's head turned — not toward the creature but toward the wall it was working on, as though the passage had reminded it of something about the wall's geometry. It adjusted its stance by two centimeters and resumed its work.

They do not eat. You have not seen any of them consume anything. But they are not independent of the city's metabolism, either. The amber veining in the ground membrane — the capillary network that pulses with heat — extends into the middle layers, and where a builder stands, the veining is denser, brighter, as though it is feeding something. As though the builder is plugged in, drawing material or energy from the substrate through the soles of whatever it has where feet would be. This would explain why they work where they work — not because the work needs doing in a particular location, but because that location is where the nourishment is, and the work is what happens when a builder is nourished. The construction is not a project. It is an excretion. It is what builders do when they are fed.

There are not many of them. You have counted, as best you can in the disorienting geometry of the middle layers, perhaps forty in the area you have explored — a region you estimate at several square kilometers, though distance is hard to judge here. Forty builders, in a space that size, producing the continuous restructuring of an entire layer of a city that stretches continentally. Either there are more, many more, in the regions you haven't reached, or the builders work at a scale that makes their numbers irrelevant — each one responsible for kilometers of architecture, each one a node in a network so sparse that the city is mostly empty of its own makers. The city may be, in this sense, a skeleton without a body. The builders built it, and they maintain it, and they are inside it, but they are not *of* it in the way that a population is of a city. They are more like the cells that maintain bone — present, essential, but not the reason the bone exists.

Or perhaps they are the reason. Perhaps the bone exists because the cells exist, and the cells exist because the bone exists, and this circularity is not a problem to be solved but a description of what equilibrium looks like when it has been running for longer than equilibrium has a right to.

They make sounds. Low, resonant, tonal — not speech, not language, but not random either. The sounds correlate with their work. When a builder is actively extruding material into a wall, its body emits a sustained low tone, something in the range of sixty to eighty hertz, a vibration you feel more than hear. When two builders are working near each other, their tones interact — not harmonizing, exactly, but *interfering*, producing beat patterns that cycle through phases of constructive and destructive interference, and the phases of constructive interference correspond, as far as you can tell, to moments when their separate construction efforts join, when two walls being built independently become one wall. They are coordinating. They are coordinating through resonance, through the physical interference of sound waves in the damp air, and the coordination is not conscious — it is mechanical, automatic, the way two pendulums on the same shelf will eventually synchronize. The builders do not decide to build together. They build together because their bodies are close enough that their sounds overlap, and the overlapping sounds shape the material, and the material follows the interference patterns, and the result is architecture.

This is how the city is designed. It is not designed. It is composed, the way a crystal is composed — by the interaction of forces following rules, producing structure that looks intentional but is only the inevitable result of the rules acting on the medium over time. The builders are the forces. The material is the medium. The city is the crystal. And the crystal has been growing for a very long time.

## The four-winged ones, who were here first

They are not birds. They are not bats. They are not insects, though the four-wing configuration and the percussive wingbeat invoke, at some deep ancestral level, the memory of dragonflies — dragonflies scaled up to a size that would make them the dominant fauna of any ecosystem they entered, which, in fact, they are.

Their bodies are long and narrow, roughly torpedo-shaped but articulated, with visible segments — five, six, seven, you cannot count precisely because the segments flex continuously in flight, the body bending and straightening in sinusoidal waves that seem to drive the wingbeat rhythm. The surface of their bodies is dark indigo, but not a flat color — it has depth, a metallic sheen that shifts between indigo and near-black depending on the angle of light, and in the amber glow of the middle layers it takes on a bruised quality, purple-black-gold, like a wound that has healed into something beautiful. The surface is not smooth. At close range — and they pass close, sometimes close enough that you feel the displacement of air and the brush of a wingtip against a wall nearby — you can see that it is textured, finely ridged, the ridges running fore-to-aft along the body and along each wing, channels that might be structural or might be sensory or might be both.

The wings are membrane, not feather. They are thin — translucent at the edges, darkening toward the attachment points, with a visible venation that looks almost crystalline, as though the wings were formed by mineral growth rather than organic development. The venation is amber. The same amber as the capillary networks in the ground. The same amber as the glow of the builders. This is not coincidence. Whatever substance flows through the city's veins flows through the wings of its dominant fauna, and the connection is not something you can prove but something you can feel, standing in a corridor while a dozen of them thunder past, their wings catching the amber light and flashing it back at you in staccato pulses that are almost, almost, a signal.

They fly in streams. This is the right word. They do not flock — flocking implies individuals aggregating, a collective behavior emergent from separate agents. They stream. They move through the city in continuous flows, dozens at a time, sometimes hundreds, following paths that are invisible to you but clearly marked for them — paths that correspond, you realize after extended observation, to the oldest architecture. The lowest layers. The worn-smooth corridors where the original construction has been modified so many times that its original form is illegible. The four-winged ones follow the city's bones, not its skin. They fly through the structural pathways that have existed longest, and the newer construction has, in many places, been built around their flight paths — corridors left open at heights that match their wingspans, apertures cut through walls at exactly the points where a stream would otherwise be blocked. The city has accommodated them. Or they have accommodated the city. The relationship is old enough that directionality has been lost.

What they eat is the pale film. You have seen them land — awkwardly, claws scrabbling on the graphite surfaces, wings folding in a complex origami that reduces their span by half — and press their faces against the substrate that coats the unwalked surfaces. Their mouths, if they are mouths, are on the undersides of their heads, flush against the surface when they feed, and the feeding is not a bite but a *draw* — you can see the film thinning beneath them, being pulled inward, and the creature's body brightening as it feeds, the amber venation in its wings becoming more pronounced. They are eating the city's immune system, the thing that breaks down and recycles, and they have been eating it long enough that the system has adapted — the film grows back, always, in patterns that suggest it has evolved to survive predation, thickening in protected crevices, thinning on exposed surfaces where it will be eaten but where the growing-back is faster because the moisture is more abundant.

They are not solitary creatures, but they are not social in any way that implies connection. They stream because the paths are there. They feed because the film is there. They do not interact with each other in flight — no grooming, no play, no aggression. They are components. They are the city's circulatory system given wings, moving through the architecture the way blood moves through a body, not because they chose to move but because the system moves them. When one dies — and you have seen one dead, wedged in a crack between two layers of construction, its body already being colonized by the pale film it spent its life eating — there is no response from the others. The stream flows on. The body is recycled. The film eats the eater, and the cycle continues.

They are loud. This deserves emphasis because the loudness is not incidental — it is functional. The wingbeat, that deep percussive thud, is not just the sound of wings pushing air. It is a pressure event. Each wingbeat sends a pulse through the corridor, and the pulses interact with the city's atmospheric turbulence, the constant shifting of pressure differentials, in ways that seem to *trigger* shifts. A stream of forty four-winged creatures flying through a corridor causes the walls to reconfigure. You have watched it happen — the panels sliding, the apertures opening, the architecture responding to the percussion as though the wingbeats are a command code. The creatures are not flying *through* the city. They are flying *into* the city, and the city opens for them, and closes behind them, and the opening and closing is construction, is the city rebuilding itself in response to the rhythms of its own fauna.

The builders build. The four-winged ones *direct*. Not consciously. Not intentionally. But the pressure of their passage shapes the architecture the way wind shapes rock, and the architecture has had long enough to learn the wind.

## The small things, which are legion

The ground membrane is alive in a way that has nothing to do with the pale film. The water channels — those capillary cuts in the surface that carry the constant film of condensation toward the drains below — are full of movement. Not the movement of water, though the water moves. The movement of bodies.

They are small. The smallest are the size of a fingernail, flat, pale, segmented things with too many legs — eight, twelve, you cannot count because they move too fast and the legs blur. They live in the water channels, running on the surface tension of the film, and they eat the film where it has thinned enough to be liquid rather than solid. They are not insects. They have no visible head, no visible mouth — the body is a uniform pale oval with legs distributed along its entire length, and feeding appears to happen through contact, the creature pausing on a patch of film and simply absorbing it through its underside, a process that takes seconds and leaves a bare patch on the surface that will be re-colonized within hours.

The slightly larger ones — thumb-sized, still pale, still wet — have fewer legs and more structure. They have a front and a back. They have something that functions as a head, with two dark spots that might be eyes. They move more deliberately, picking their way along the channels, and they eat the smaller ones. You have seen this. It is fast. The smaller creature does not flee. It does not seem to register the larger one as a threat until contact, and then it is too late — the larger one's body flexes, wraps, and the smaller one is gone, folded into a body that is temporarily distended and then flat again. The pale film eats the dead. The small things eat the film. The slightly larger things eat the small things. The four-winged ones eat the film. The cycle is not a pyramid. It is a knot, tangled and self-referential, every eater eaten in its turn.

There are larger ones. Hand-sized, still living in the channels, their bodies dark now, not pale — dark grey, almost the color of the membrane itself, with a wet sheen that makes them look like drops of liquid that have grown legs and intentions. They have six legs. They have mandibles, visible, functional, clicking softly as they move. They do not eat the smaller things. They eat the membrane itself — scraping at the graphite surface with their mandibles, ingesting the mineral substrate, and what they excrete is the amber substance, the capillary material, the vein-stuff that pulses with heat. They are producing the city's circulatory system. They are mining the ground and laying down veins, and they have been doing this long enough that the entire capillary network — the amber veining that extends from the ground into the walls, that feeds the builders, that lights the wings of the four-winged ones — is their product. Their waste. Their contribution.

They are the most important creatures in the Confluence, and they are the size of your hand, and they live in water channels no deeper than a coin, and no one would ever notice them unless they stood still and watched the ground for an hour, which you did, because you had nothing else to do and the sound was too loud to think through and the ground was the only thing that moved slowly enough to follow.

## The things that cling

On the vertical surfaces, in the interior spaces where the mist is thickest and the amber light is strongest, there are creatures that do not move. At first you mistook them for protrusions — bumps on the wall, irregular growths, features of the architecture. They are not features. They are fauna.

They are the color of wet stone — grey-brown, mottled, their surfaces rough and slightly translucent, like fogged glass. They are flat, pressed against the vertical surfaces, their bodies spread in thin ovals that range from the size of a palm to the size of a dinner plate. They have four limbs — not legs, not arms, but extensions that are distributed radially, pressing into the surface, and the pressing is their feeding mechanism. Where each limb touches the wall, the pale film is absent — not eaten but *displaced*, pushed aside, and beneath the limb the surface of the wall is slightly different, slightly more porous, as though the creature has softened the material and is drawing something from it. Not the mineral substrate. Something else. Something in the wall that the pale film doesn't interact with and that the amber veining doesn't carry. A third substance. A third system. You cannot identify it. You can only see its absence — the patches of wall where the clingers have fed look slightly duller, slightly drier, and the clingers themselves, after feeding, glow faintly at their centers, a brief pale flash that fades over minutes.

Their eyes — and they have eyes, unmistakably — are the most disquieting thing about them. They are reflective. They catch the amber light and hold it, and when you look at a wall covered in clingers, you see dozens of points of reflected light staring back at you, and they blink. Slowly. A membrane slides across each eye, wetting it, and the light vanishes for a moment and returns. They blink at different times. There is no pattern. But the cumulative effect, in a corridor where fifty of them line the walls, is of being watched by something that is not a single intelligence but is not fifty separate intelligences either — something in between, a network of attention that registers your presence without responding to it, that has noted you and filed you and moved on.

They do not move often. But they move. You saw one shift, once, over the course of twenty minutes — a slow, creeping relocation, its limbs extending and contracting one at a time, moving it three centimeters to the left along the wall. It left a trail — a faint line on the surface where its body had been, a track of slightly altered material that the pale film was already colonizing, growing into the track, filling it. The clinger moved to a new patch of wall, settled, extended its limbs, and resumed feeding. This is their life. This is the entirety of their life. They cling, they feed, they move occasionally, they blink. They have been doing this for as long as the walls have existed, and the walls have existed for as long as the city, and the city has no beginning.

## What lives below

You cannot see it. You can only hear it. Through the cracks in the ground membrane, through the drains that swallow the surface water, through the lattice of ceramic and darkness beneath your feet, there is something large.

The sound it makes is a displacement — a slow, heavy movement of mass through fluid, a sound like a boat hull pushed through deep water by a engine that runs at very low RPMs. It is continuous. It does not stop. It moves through the channels below the membrane the way blood moves through veins — slowly, steadily, powered by something that is not a heart but functions as one.

You have knelt at the cracks and listened. The sound changes pitch as the thing moves — deeper when it is far from the crack, higher when it passes beneath, and the passing takes time. Long, slow, the thing is large. Larger than the channels should allow, which means either the channels are larger than they sound, or the thing has grown to fit them, or the thing *is* the channels — has become the channels, its body lining the infrastructure, its movement the peristalsis of a digestive system that processes water and mineral and whatever falls through the cracks.

You dropped a piece of the pale film through a crack once. Watched it fall, past the membrane, past the ceramic lattice, into the darkness. You listened. Three seconds later, the sound changed — a brief acceleration in the displacement rhythm, a slight increase in pitch, as though the thing below had registered the input and adjusted. Not a reaction. An *incorporation*. The film you dropped is now part of whatever is down there, and the thing is fractionally larger, and the channels are fractionally more occupied, and the water that flows through them flows slightly slower, and this is how it has always been, the below-thing growing imperceptibly, century by century, as the city feeds it through its cracks, and it processes the feed and moves the water and the water rises again as vapor and falls again as mist and the cycle does not close because the cycle is not a cycle but a spiral, and the spiral is still expanding.

There may be more than one. The sound is consistent enough that it could be a single organism, but the channels branch and divide, and the sound comes from multiple directions, and the possibility exists that the below is a network — not one creature but a colony, a connected system of bodies that fill the infrastructure the way the pale film fills the surface, and they have been down there so long that the infrastructure is their body, and their body is the infrastructure, and the distinction has been eroded like everything else in this place, worn smooth by time and moisture and the persistent pressure of systems running without interruption.

## The pale film, which is alive

It deserves its own section, even though it has been mentioned, because calling it a film is a failure of language that you cannot correct but can at least acknowledge.

It is a substrate. It is a coating. It is, in biological terms, somewhere between a fungal mat and a biofilm, but both of those terms assume a frame of reference — cellular life, organic chemistry, the particular set of solutions that life on your world arrived at through a specific sequence of evolutionary pressures — that does not apply here. The pale film is alive by the only definition of alive that matters in the Confluence: it does something. It responds. It maintains itself. It grows where conditions permit and retreats where they do not. It processes input and produces output. It is a system, and the system is running.

Its color is pale — whitish-grey, sometimes with a faint iridescence that you initially attributed to moisture but that persists in dry conditions, a shimmer that shifts across the surface like oil on water but is not oil and is not moisture. The iridescence is strongest where the film is healthiest, in the crevices and cracks where moisture accumulates and where the amber veining is densest. The film follows the amber. The amber follows the moisture. The moisture follows the temperature gradients. The temperature gradients follow the systems below the membrane. The film is, in this sense, the visible surface of a chain of dependencies that extends all the way down to the below-thing and all the way up to the upper lattice, and it is the only part of that chain that you can touch.

It is dry. This is surprising, in a place where everything is wet. The film is dry and slightly warm, like the skin of something alive but resting. When you touch it, it does not react. Not immediately. But return to a spot you touched an hour ago and the film there is different — slightly thicker, slightly more iridescent, as though your touch stimulated growth, as though the film *liked* being touched, as though contact is an input it processes and responds to by growing more of itself. You stopped touching it after you noticed this. Not out of fear. Out of the recognition that you are a foreign system in a place where every system is connected to every other system, and introducing your inputs into a network you don't understand is not dangerous but is *irresponsible*, in the way that introducing a new species to an island is irresponsible, not because anything will explode but because the ripples will propagate in directions you cannot predict.

The film does something that no other component of the Confluence does. It makes patterns. Not the patterns of crystal growth or fluid dynamics — not the inevitable, rule-following patterns of physics. These patterns are *informational*. They are patterns that contain something. You noticed it first in a crack where the film was particularly thick: a arrangement of density variations, subtle gradations of thickness and thinness across the surface, that repeated. Not exactly — the repetition was imperfect, modified, as though the pattern was being iterated rather than copied. But the structure was there. A motif. A sequence. Something that, if you had the right sensorium, the right interpretive framework, might be a record — of moisture levels, of temperature shifts, of the passage of four-winged ones, of the work of builders, of the weight of footsteps that crossed the surface decades or centuries ago.

The film remembers. The film is the Confluence's memory, written in a living substrate that covers every surface and processes every input and stores, in its patterns of growth and retreat, the history of everything that has happened to it. The history of the city. The history of the creatures that live in it. The history of the builders' work and the four-winged ones' flight paths and the below-thing's slow expansion and the clingers' patient feeding and the small things' frantic breeding and dying and being eaten. It is all there, in the pale film, written in a language that is not language, in patterns that are not symbols, in a medium that is alive and does not know it is alive and does not need to know.

You are there too, now. You touched the film, and the film grew where you touched it, and the growth has a pattern that corresponds to the pressure and temperature and chemical signature of your fingertips. You are in the city's memory. You have been written. And the film will hold that record for as long as it holds any record — which is, as far as you can tell, indefinitely, because the film does not die. It does not get erased. It grows over itself, layer on layer, and the old patterns persist beneath the new ones, legible to whatever sensorium can read them, a palimpsest of everything that has ever happened on every surface in the Confluence.

You are in the record. You are in the memory of a place that does not think and does not forget.

## What lives here, altogether

The Confluence is not a habitat. A habitat is a place where things live — a container, a setting, a stage on which organisms perform their biological dramas. The Confluence does not contain life. The Confluence *is* life, in the same way that a body does not contain cells but is cells, in the same way that a forest does not contain trees but is trees. Everything here is part of the same system, and the system is the organism, and the organism is the place.

The builders are the growth system. They produce structure. They are fed by the amber veining, and they extrude architecture, and the architecture is the body.

The four-winged ones are the regulatory system. Their movement triggers restructuring, and the restructuring follows rhythms that have been established by centuries of wingbeat percussion, and the rhythms maintain the architecture in a state of dynamic equilibrium — never finished, never abandoned, always in the process of becoming.

The small things — the pale runners, the dark mandibled ones — are the metabolic system. They process the film and the membrane and produce the amber veining, and the amber veining feeds the builders, and the builders build, and the building creates new surfaces for the film, and the film feeds the small things. They are the digestive tract. They are the gut.

The clingers are the sensory system. They line the interior surfaces, their eyes open, their bodies pressed against the walls, processing something — some quality of the material that no other creature accesses — and their slow relocations and their rhythmic blinking are the city's attention, distributed across a thousand points, focused on nothing and everything simultaneously.

The below-thing is the circulatory system. It moves the water. The water carries the minerals. The minerals feed the film and the small things and the amber veining. Without the below-thing, the water would stagnate, and the minerals would settle, and the film would die, and the small things would starve, and the amber veining would stop flowing, and the builders would stop building, and the four-winged ones would have no film to eat, and the city would stop. The below-thing is the heart. It is slow, and it is large, and it has been beating for longer than anything in the Confluence can remember, because the Confluence does not remember — the film remembers, and the film does not tell.

The pale film is the memory. It is the only part of the system that stores information rather than processing it. Everything else is in motion — input, output, transformation, movement. The film is stillness. The film is the record. The film is what makes the Confluence more than a machine, more than a cycle, more than a feedback loop running in a wet room for a million years. The film makes it a *place*. The film gives it a history. The film says: this happened, and this happened, and this happened, and it mattered enough to grow over, and the growing-over preserved it, and the preservation is the closest thing to meaning that this place produces.

And you. You are here. You are walking through the middle layers, ducking under conduits, stepping over water channels, pressing deeper into the amber-lit corridors where the builders work and the four-winged ones stream past and the clingers blink from the walls and the small things dart through the water and the film records your every footstep in its pale, patient, endless script.

You are not part of the system. You are not feeding the builders or directing the architecture or producing the amber veining or processing the walls or moving the water or storing the memory. You are not doing anything that the Confluence needs. But you are here, and the film is recording your presence, and the clingers are watching you with their reflective eyes, and the four-winged ones pass close enough to brush your hair with their wingtips, and none of them — not one — has altered its behavior because of you.

You are tolerated. You are a system running simultaneously with ten million other systems, and the Confluence has room for you because the Confluence has room for everything, and it does not distinguish between its own components and foreign inputs because the distinction is not useful. The film will grow where you walk. The film will grow where you touch. The film will record your passage, and in a hundred years, when you are gone and the corridor you walked through has been rebuilt seventeen times and the walls are in new positions and the water channels follow new paths, the film will still hold the pattern of your footsteps in some deep layer beneath seventeen layers of new growth, and the record of your passage will persist, and the Confluence will not remember you because the Confluence does not remember, but the film will hold you, the way it holds everything, in the pale patience of a living surface that does not know it is alive and does not need to know and does not care and will not stop.
