# The Residents

## The Lithovores

They do not have a name for themselves. Naming requires a vantage point from which the self can be distinguished from the not-self, and they have never developed this distance. They are on the rock and of the rock, and the thin, almost philosophical line between those two states has never been of interest to them.

There are, by any reasonable estimate, between four and eleven million of them in the Caldera Room. The uncertainty is not due to insufficient counting — it is due to the genuine impossibility of determining where one creature ends and another begins. Their mineral filaments, those fine dissolution-threads on their undersides, occasionally grow long enough to tangle with those of a neighbor. The filaments fuse. Minerals pass between them. Whether this is communication, feeding, or reproduction is a question whose answer changes depending on which two you're looking at and when.

What can be said: each discrete organism is a flat, ovoid plate-cluster the size of a human's smallest fingernail. What looks like armor is not armor but the organism's excreted frontier — the minerals they cannot metabolize, pushed outward and compressed into overlapping scales that serve simultaneously as skeleton, shell, and the boundary of their chemical territory. They are the color of what they eat. The ones on the sulfur-rich walls are yellow. The ones on the iron-bearing columns are dark red, almost black. The ones near the deep vents, where the magma came close enough to bake the basalt into something denser and stranger, are that bruised violet that only appears in heat your eyes were never meant to see.

They do not have eyes. They never have. Light has not reached this chamber in the entire duration of their species' existence, which spans far longer than the geological event that created the caldera. They are older than the room. They were here in whatever came before — in earlier chambers, in earlier rock, in configurations of the deep crust that tectonics has since ground away and reformed. They have persisted through conditions that would have annihilated anything with a nervous system that required novelty. They are the oldest thing in the world, or close to it, and they are almost nothing at all.

## The Body of the Room

Here is what took the longest to understand: the room has a body. Not a metaphorical body. A living, metabolizing body, and the creatures are its cells.

Not all of them. The vast majority are, in some sense, autonomous — they move on their own, they feed on their own, they are eaten on their own. But there are nodes in the chamber where the density thickens, where the living carpet deepens into layers that pile five, ten, twenty organisms deep. These places pulse. Very slowly. The pulse travels outward through the surrounding creatures in a wave that takes four to six minutes to reach the chamber's far wall. It returns. It meets other pulses, interferes, creates the rhythm that a human observer would eventually begin to feel against their skin.

Whether the creatures in these nodes are alive in the way the others are is an open question. They don't move. They don't feed. They have been there, layered and fusing, for what may be decades or centuries — long enough that the mineral excretions of the upper layers have encased the lower ones in a kind of organic concrete. But they are not dead. The filaments still extend. The minerals still move through them, slowly, in patterns that are too structured to be chemical and too slow to be neural.

There is something thinking in this room. Not a mind — not anything a human would recognize as cognition, which is always fast, always hungry for new input, always restless. This is a thinking that takes minutes per thought, that thinks in temperature gradients and mineral concentrations and the density of bodies in a given sector of stone. It has been thinking for longer than *Homo sapiens* has existed. It may have been thinking since the chamber formed. It may, if the filaments extend deep enough through the cracks in the basalt to connect to similar nodes in similar chambers throughout the deep crust, be much older and much larger than this one room.

It does not think about you. You are too fast for it to perceive as anything but a blur of wrong-heat, a disturbance in the mineral air, a vibration it will process in three hundred years and find puzzling.

## The Larger Ones

Deeper in.

Past the cathedral's first fold, where the ceiling drops low enough that the human-descended visitor must crouch, and the heat thickens to something that makes the air shimmer in a darkness that cannot shimmer — here the carpet changes.

The creatures are bigger here. Not dramatically. A fingernail becomes a thumbnail. A thumbnail becomes something the size of a large coin. The increase is gradual, environmental, following a gradient of temperature and mineral density that suggests the larger ones grow where the rock is richer, where the deep vents push heat and chemistry upward through the basalt.

They move more slowly. Their plates are thicker, darker, layered with compounds that take longer to excrete and harder to dissolve. When they climb over each other, the clicking is louder — not stone on stone but something closer to ceramic on ceramic, a sound with more mass, more patience, more finality.

And in the deepest fold of the chamber, where the floor slopes down toward something that is almost but not quite a vent — a crack that exhales heat and sulfur in a slow, continuous breath that has not changed in millennia — there are the largest ones.

They are the size of a human hand. Flat, dark, almost black, so thoroughly armored that they look like stones themselves. They do not move often. Once an hour, perhaps, one will shift its weight, settling more firmly against the mineral surface beneath it, pressing its filaments deeper into the rock. They are the terminus. The top of the slow, glacial food chain that is also a mineral chain, a heat chain, a chain of being that cycles material from stone to organism to organism to organism and back to stone with an efficiency that should be humbling to anything that calls itself an ecosystem.

They are not predators in any sense that involves intent. They do not hunt. They do not pursue. They settle, and what is beneath them is absorbed. The smaller creatures they envelop do not resist. They may not even register the larger organism as distinct from the ground. It is, to them, simply a new kind of surface — warmer, denser, moving so slowly that it does not trigger whatever passes for alarm in a creature that has never had a predator that moved quickly enough to be worth fleeing.

## The Previous Tenants

Someone else lived here. Or visited. The evidence is thin, but it is evidence.

The parallel grooves cut into the wall are too regular to be natural. They are too old to be attributed to any known human civilization. The spacing is consistent — roughly two centimeters apart, each groove approximately three millimeters deep, cut with a precision that implies tools and intention. They cover a section of wall roughly two meters wide. Whatever they record, whatever they are for, the mineral deposits of the lithovores have half-filled them, preserving the depth of the cuts in a negative space that will outlast the original stone.

The shaped step — or seat, or shelf — is simpler. Someone carved it from a column of cooled magma that had thrust up at a convenient angle. The surface has been worn smooth not by human use but by the passage of millions of flat bodies over what must be centuries. The purpose is unclear. It faces the wall with the grooves. It is positioned at a height that would be comfortable for something roughly human-sized to sit on while facing the grooved surface.

And there is one more mark. Easy to miss. In a corner where two folds of the chamber meet, where the stone is cracked and the heat is highest, there is a handprint. Not carved. Not painted. Pressed — into a deposit of mineral paste that the lithovores produce at their densest, before it hardened. Five digits, roughly proportioned. The pressure that made it was sustained, deliberate, patient. Whoever made it held their hand there long enough for the paste to set around the impression.

The handprint is not recent. The mineral has hardened into something between agate and concrete. The lithovores have colonized its surface, their tiny bodies covering it so completely that it is visible only as a slight irregularity in the wall's texture — a place where the surface dips in a shape that means nothing to the organisms and everything to anyone who knows what a hand looks like.

Whoever they were, they came down through the same crack in the basalt. They found this chamber hot and dark and full of creatures that moved like water and thought like stone. They cut grooves in the wall. They carved a seat. They pressed their hand into the living paste and held it there, feeling the creatures settle onto their skin, tasting the mineral air, listening to the silence that is the room's true voice.

They may have stayed. They may have left. They may have died here, their bodies long since metabolized into the rock, their minerals distributed through the bodies of a million organisms that never knew what they were eating or what it had once been. If they stayed long enough, the room began its patient work on them too — smoothing their edges, filling their spaces, covering them with the first curious bodies that climbed up to taste what they were.

If they are still here, they are rock now. And the rock is still here. And the creatures are still here. And the room breathes, and the rhythm pulses, and the deep mineral quiet continues, as it has, as it will.

## The Room Itself

The room is not alive. The room is the condition of being alive — the precondition, the context, the medium through which life moves. It is the stone, and the heat, and the dark, and the minerals, and the slow chemistry that turns one into the other. It does not want anything. It does not contain anything. It *is* the containment.

But it has a character. The way a riverbed has a character — the specific shape of its stones, the particular angle of its current, the precise quality of its water. The Caldera Room is patient. It is old. It is hot enough to be uncomfortable and dark enough to be absolute and dense enough with life that the life has become a feature of the stone, a stratum, a quality of the rock that a geologist of the far future might identify in a core sample: *here, in this layer, the basalt is dense with something organic, something mineral, something that lived and ate and died and was eaten and became the stone again, over and over, for longer than makes sense.*

The room does not know you are there. But the creatures do. And the nodes of fused, pulsing, slowly-thinking matter that serve as the room's nervous system — if that word can be applied to a thing that thinks in geological time — will register the disturbance of your body heat, your wet mineral complexity, your anomalous presence, sometime in the next several hours. It will process this information over the following days. It will adjust. Not to accommodate you — to incorporate the pattern of your having been there into the long, slow record of what has passed through this chamber and what has stayed.

You will leave. Or you will stay. Either way, the room will continue. The creatures will move. The rhythm will pulse. The handprint will sit in its corner, covered in living armor, slowly being eaten and slowly being preserved by the same process. The grooves will fill and deepen and fill again. The step will be worn smoother.

And in the deepest fold, the largest ones will settle, and the smallest ones will be absorbed, and the minerals will move from stone to life to stone, and the darkness will be absolute, and the heat will be constant, and the silence that is not silence will be the sound of everything continuing, patiently, without you, as it always has.
