Something lives here. Not much, and not yet — but something has begun.

The pale filaments are the obvious first answer, and they are not wrong. They are *Ulothrix*, probably, or something close enough — a genus of green algae that specializes in exactly this kind of ambiguity, this not-quite-land-not-quite-water. They are single filaments, unbranched, each one a single line of cells strung end to end like a strand of pale beads, and they have found the seams between the stones where silt has settled and where the rough surface of the masonry gives them something to hold. They are not rooted. They have no roots. They tangle. They drift in the current and catch on the rough edges of the chisel marks, and once caught, they stay — not through tenacity but through the simple physics of entanglement, the way a thread catches on a splinter. They are the first thing. They will not be the last.

But they are not really what lives here. They are what is *trying* to live here. The difference matters.

What actually lives here — what has already established itself, what is not provisional or tentative but fully present — is smaller. It is in the water. You cannot see it with the naked eye, though the turbidity that makes the light beams visible is partly its doing. The warm, faintly cloudy water carries a suspension of bacterial life that has colonized the mineral wash coming through the stone seams. The limestone is dissolving, slowly, and the calcium carbonate that leaches from the seams feeds a thin population of chemoautotrophs — bacteria that do not need light, do not need organic matter, that live on the bare chemical interaction between stone and water. They are the first true residents. They were here before the algae. They were probably here before the flood, living in the damp seams of the dry stone, waiting in the way that bacteria wait — which is to say, not waiting at all, simply persisting in a dormant state that can last decades, centuries, until the conditions change and they become active.

The flood activated them. The warm water, the dissolved minerals, the constant flow that brings fresh substrate and carries away waste — this is their optimum. They are dividing. They are metabolizing. They are, in their way, thriving, though the word feels wrong for something so simple and so patient. They do not thrive. They *occur*. They are a process as much as a presence.

And there is something else. Not an organism — or not exactly. In the warm water, in the moving light, in the mineral-rich warmth, there is a faint biofilm forming on the submerged surfaces. It is barely there. If you reached into the water — and you can, the edges are shallow enough — and ran your fingers along the bench carved into the northern wall, you would feel it. A slickness. A slight yielding. The stone beneath is still hard, still itself, but the surface has begun to soften, and the softness is not abiotic. It is the biofilm — a community of bacteria and their excreted polysaccharides, a living matrix that is neither stone nor water but a third thing, a negotiation between the two. It is this film that the description calls "a faint sliminess where the stone is beginning to yield." The stone is not yielding. It is being *consumed*, though consumed is also not quite right. It is being *inhabited*. The biofilm is the first claim. It says: this surface belongs to the water now, and the water brings life, and the life will eat the stone, and the stone will become silt, and the silt will host the algae, and the algae will be eaten by something else, and so on.

But that is all later. That is next year, next decade, the slow future. Right now, what lives here is:

A thin wash of bacteria in the water. A faint biofilm on the stone, thickest in the seams where the mineral water enters, thinning to nothing on the exposed faces where the current scrubs too hard. A few strands of green algae that have drifted in through the eastern archway and caught on the masonry. Possibly — probably — nematodes. Microscopic roundworms that feed on bacteria and that are present in essentially every body of water on earth. They would have come in with the flood. They would be feeding now, invisibly, on the bacterial population that is feeding on the stone. A single layer of a food web so simple it barely qualifies.

Nothing else. No insects, no fish, no amphibians. The water is too new and too isolated — the inflow comes from somewhere subsurface, through stone, and carries no eggs, no seeds, no colonists larger than a few cells. The eastern archway connects to a passage that may lead to open water or may lead to another chamber, but if there is a connection to a larger ecosystem, it is long and dark and cold, and nothing has come through it yet. The chamber is a colonizer's frontier, and the colonists are all microscopic.

They live in the moving light. The refraction that makes the chamber beautiful to human eyes — the shifting columns of brightness, the amber and green walls, the way the filaments glow — is also, for these organisms, *energy*. The algae use it directly. The bacteria near the surface benefit from the warmth the light creates. Even the biofilm on the stone is shaped by the light: on the south-facing surfaces, where the refracted beams hit strongest, the film is thicker, more established. The light feeds the things that are eating the room.

And so the room is being eaten, slowly, by things that are fed by the room's own beauty. This is not tragic. This is not even ironic. It is simply what happens. A room is built by hands. Water enters. The water brings minerals. The minerals feed bacteria. The bacteria soften the stone. The softened stone collects silt. The silt hosts algae. The algae will host something larger, eventually. The room becomes a room-shaped reef, and the reef becomes silt, and the silt becomes ground, and the ground receives another room.

What lives here now is the first chapter of that process. A chapter written in single cells and faint slime, in water that smells of warm stone, in a room that someone built with hands and tools and left behind. The builders are gone. The builders may be dead — the flooding is recent, but the building is also recent, and the two facts together suggest something happened quickly. Whatever it was, the room doesn't remember. The room isn't built to remember. The bacteria don't remember either. They simply respond — to warmth, to mineral, to light, to current. They are the first tenants of a place that will have many tenants, and they are the smallest, and they are the ones that will still be here when the room itself is gone.
