# What Lives Here

No one thing. That is the first correction to make. You arrive looking for an inhabitant and you will not find one because the category does not apply. What lives here is not *a* resident. It is a consortium.

But the consortium has a center of gravity, and the center is the mycelium.

---

It did not come from outside. That is important to understand. When the mountain cracked and the steam came roaring up through raw granite, the spores were already there — had always been there, locked in microfissures in the stone, dormant in veins of mineral water that had not seen open air since the uplift that made the range itself. They were archaea's distant cousins. Old. Patient in the way that only something without a nervous system can be patient, which is to say: not waiting, but not stopping either. Suspended in the geology like seeds in permafrost. When the crack opened and heat and water and carbon met, they resumed. Mid-sentence. As if nothing had happened.

What grew from that resumption is not a mushroom. Not in the way you mean. The fruiting bodies — the rubbery brackets bolted to the stone, the dense pale shelves you see along the rhizomorphs — are exhaust ports. They are the least interesting part of the organism. They exist to shed spores back into the updraft so that if the chimney closes and the heat dies and the system collapses, the pattern survives somewhere else in the rock, waiting for the next wound. They are letters addressed to a future that may never come. The mycelium does not think about this. It writes them anyway.

The body — the actual body — is everywhere. It is the white threads in the moss. It is the film on the wet stone that you might mistake for mineral deposit. It is the corded cables swinging between the walls, and it is the finer web beneath those cables, and it is the finer web beneath *that*, down to filaments so thin they exist at the scale of water molecules, threading through the film of condensation on the granite itself. It runs along root-hairs of the ferns. It runs through the rotting fronds on the ledges. It runs through the liverwort scales and across the bare patches of stone where nothing else can hold on, mining the rock for the minerals it needs — iron, sulfur, calcium, the trace elements that the steam carries in solution. It is a metabolizing skin. It is the thing the Boiling Cloister is doing with itself.

If you could pull it all out and lay it flat, it would carpet the shaft wall to wall, floor to ceiling, and the carpet would be three-dimensional, layered, meters deep in places where dead growth has compacted into a living loam that the fresh mycelium colonizes in turn. Eating itself. Building from its own ruins. The organism is a city built on the landfill of a previous version of itself, which was built on the landfill of a previous version, which was built on bare stone. The oldest layers at the bottom, near the pool you cannot see, are already being cooked back into mineral form by the rising heat. The mycelium does not mourn them. The minerals return to the stone. The stone will vent them again. The cycle is a circle drawn in heat.

It has no center. It has no edge. It has no opinion about itself.

But it has *shape*, and the shape is worth describing, because the shape is what the Boiling Cloister has made of it. The updraft dictates everything. Spores released from the fruiting bodies rise. They do not fall. The column of hot air carries them upward and they settle on the upper walls, on the ledges, in the crevices where condensation is heaviest, and they germinate there — at the top, where it is coolest, where the water actually persists long enough to be absorbed. So the organism grows downward. It extends filaments from the cool upper reaches toward the heat below, following the moisture gradient, following the mineral-rich condensation, following the rot. It reaches *toward* the thing that would kill it. The leading edge of the mycelial expansion is always at the temperature boundary where the heat becomes too intense and the threads die back, retreating, only to advance again when a new fissure opens and redirects the venting, shifting the thermal map. It is a tide. It comes in and goes out according to the geometry of the mountain's wound, which is always changing.

You could say it is in conversation with the geology. That would be too generous. It is more accurate to say it is a stain spreading across a surface that will not hold still.

---

The ferns are its companions, or its livestock, or its architecture — the relationship is not symmetrical but it is not parasitic either. The mycelium threads through the ferns' root systems and exchanges minerals for carbohydrates the ferns produce through their own pale photosynthesis, feeble as it is in the lightless shaft. They are not using light from the sun. They are not using light at all. The ferns here are running on a different chemistry — chemosynthesis, the deep-biosphere trick, the one that lets life eat the earth directly, pulling energy from the oxidation of minerals in the vent water. They are not plants in the way you learned to identify plants. They are plants the way hydrothermal vent communities are plants: technically, marginally, by the skin of their cell walls. The green is a memory. The chlorophyll is still manufactured because the genetic instructions are still there, but it functions at a fraction of its capacity, doing what little it can with the faint ambient glow of the hot mineral water below — which does, barely, emit light. Not visible to you. Visible to the photoreceptors in the fern fronds, which are tuned to the deep red, the far red, the light that hot water makes. The ferns lean toward it. They lean down. They lean toward the boiling pool you cannot see, their fronds orienting the way sunflowers orient, except downward, except toward heat, except toward the thing that is killing them. They live thirty days. The mycelium lives in their remains for sixty more. Then both are replaced by the next iteration.

The mosses are less dramatic. The mosses do what mosses have always done, which is to hold water and wait. They are the sponge. The mycelium moves through them like roots through soil, using the moss layer as a medium, a substrate, a home. The mosses do not seem to mind. They do not seem to notice. They photosynthesize faintly, they hold water, they grow upward in slow tufts, and when the heat or the falling stone or the shifting vent kills a patch, the patch beside it grows over the scar. The mosses are the closest thing the Cloister has to a permanent resident. They were here, in some form, before the mycelium. They will be here, in some form, after it. They are the patient ones. They are the ones that remember.

The liverworts remember nothing. They are a film. They exist because the stone is wet and they can exist on wet stone. They will be the first to die when the chimney closes.

---

There is a moment — and this is the closest the Boiling Cloister comes to having something you might call an inhabitant, a self, a presence — when the mycelial network, the ferns, the mosses, the condensation cycle, and the heat venting from below achieve a kind of temporary equilibrium. A steady state. It does not last. It cannot last, because the mountain is still adjusting, still cracking, still opening new vents and closing old ones. But for a span of hours, or perhaps days, the thermal map holds, the moisture gradient holds, the growth patterns hold, and the whole system breathes as one thing. The updraft pulses with a regular rhythm set by the pressure cycles below. The fern fronds sway in unison. The mycelial cords across the shaft all sway in the same direction at the same moment. The fruiting bodies release their spores in a synchronized pulse that you can feel — a momentary thickening of the air, a whiteout, a snow globe's worth of pale particulate swirling upward into the dark.

In that moment, if you pressed your hand to the stone and closed your eyes, you would feel something that is not a heartbeat but does the same job. The mountain's wound, cycling. The organisms in the wound, cycling with it. A system that has achieved, briefly, the only kind of identity that something without a brain can achieve: *a pattern that persists.*

It does not know you are there. It does not know it is there. But it is *there* — unmistakably, physically, thermodynamically there — and it is doing the thing that life does, which is to continue, which is to take what is given and metabolize it and grow and die and grow again and never once in all of that require a reason.

The fern above you nods. Its pale frond brushes your knuckles. It is wet. It is cool. It is alive in the only way anything has ever been alive in this place, which is: completely, and without reflection, and for as long as the crack stays open, and not one moment longer.
