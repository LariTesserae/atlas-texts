# What Lives in the Xylomatic Continuum

---

## The Grazers, Reconsidered

Start with the obvious. The chitin-shelled grazers — the flat, oval, bark-colored things with their too-soft legs and their circular grinders — are not what they appear to be. Or rather, they are exactly what they appear to be, but what they appear to be is more strange than the first glance suggests.

They are not insects. They are not arthropods. They are not crustaceans. They are not any branch of any tree of life that evolved in water, because there has never been water here, not in their lineage, not in their ancestry, not in the deep prehistory of their microscopic forebears. Their chitin is not the chitin of a beetle's wing-case. It is a parallel material, evolved independently in a dry world, a structural polymer produced by an organism that never swam, never floated, never knew buoyancy. Their shells are dry to the touch. Bone-dry. And they are not hinged. There are no joints. The overlapping plates slide across one another on a film of fine, powdered resin — the same resin the Continuum produces in its growth zones, reprocessed in the grazer's gut and exuded from glands along the body wall. They lubricate themselves with the Continuum's own product. They are, in a sense, wearing the Continuum.

Their legs. The root-legs. They are roots. Not analogues of roots, not root-like structures. Modified roots, evolved from a lineage of organisms that were once part of the Continuum itself. This is the thing that takes time to understand: the grazers are not inhabitants of the Continuum. They are *emissions* of the Continuum. They broke away. Not in the way a branch breaks from a tree. In the way that a single cell, in the deep history of life on this world, once divided and one half stayed and one half left, and the half that left kept dividing, and kept changing, and kept being shaped by the same forces — the dry air, the resin-sap, the bark surface, the noise — until it became something that could move across the Continuum's surface and feed on its parasitic moss-scaling and *click* in its fissures and be, in every meaningful sense, an animal.

But it never stopped being the Continuum. Not entirely. Their genetics — if genetics is even the right word for whatever system of heredity operates in a world where DNA may never have existed — their genetics are still partially continuous with the organism they walk on. They are the Continuum's own cells, become autonomous, become mobile, become *other*, after cons of separation. The separation is not complete. It may never be complete. When a grazer dies — and they die constantly, small dry bodies littering the fissures, curling into their shells the way a leaf curls as it dries — its body is not decomposed by something external. It is *reabsorbed*. The bark surface beneath it softens, grows faintly wet with a digestive secretion, and the grazer's body sinks into it. The chitin dissolves. The root-legs dissolve. The mineral content of the shell — calcium, silica, trace metals — is drawn down into the xylem conduits and carried away in the sap. Within a week, there is nothing. Within a month, the bark surface is smooth again, unmarked, as if the grazer had never existed.

The Continuum eats its children.

This is not cruelty. There is no cruelty here because there is no moral framework in which cruelty could be meaningfully said to occur. There is only metabolism. The grazers live on the Continuum, feed on the Continuum, are made of the Continuum, and return to the Continuum. They are a digestive loop. The Continuum grows moss-scaling on its surface in the deep fissures; the moss-scaling accumulates mineral deposits from the air and from the bark's own chemical exudates; the grazers eat the moss-scaling and concentrate the minerals in their shells; the Continuum reabsorbs the grazers and recovers the minerals. It is a way of mining its own surface. A slow, patient, living mining operation, carried out by organisms that do not know they are mining operations, that have no concept of their own function, that simply *are*, in the way that all life simply is, without justification or explanation, and that serve a purpose in a system so vast that the concept of "purpose" dissolves into the concept of "process."

## The Fliers

They are harder to know. The canopy is eight thousand meters above, and the fliers live there, in the shrieking, bright, gas-exchange currents of the upper stomatal fields, and from the bark surface they are shapes. Dark shapes. Smaller than your thumbnail at arm's length, jerkily crossing the pillars of canopy-light, tumbling in the updrafts, vanishing into the glare.

But they fall. They fall, and when they fall, they reach the ground, and you can find them.

A fallen flier lies in a fissure, six meters down, wedged between two walls of black bark. It is still warm. Not from metabolism — from the fall. Eight thousand meters of air resistance and the heat of compression. It is the size of your hand. It is not what you expected.

It has no wings.

It has *sails*. Membranes, stretched between articulated spars that are not bone and not chitin but something between — a rigid, translucent material, like the filaments the growth zones extrude but thinner, more flexible, grown rather than secreted. The membranes are torn from the fall. In life, they must have been broad, perhaps a meter across when fully extended, gossamer-thin, catching the gas-exchange currents of the canopy the way a sailing ship catches wind. The flier does not fly. It *sails*. It lives in the air the way a jellyfish lives in water: passively, riding currents, adjusting its orientation with tiny contractions of the sail-spars, going where the canopy's breath takes it.

Its body, beneath the sails, is dense. Heavier than you would expect for something that lives in air. A compact, armored torso, dark red — the same red as the extremophiles on the growth zones, and probably for the same reason: the pigment is a heat-tolerant compound, a protective coloration against the intense light and the chemical bombardment of the upper canopy. The torso is covered not with plates but with a fine, dense scales, each one slightly iridescent, catching the faint light that reaches the fissure. The scales overlap downward, so that the body is smooth to the touch from above but rough from below. Water runs off them. But there is no water. What runs off them is resin. The flier's body is coated in a thin film of resin, continuously exuded, which fills the gaps between the scales and makes the body effectively airtight. The flier does not breathe. It does not have lungs. It does not have gills. It has, instead, a distributed metabolic system — its cells exchange gases directly with the atmosphere through the resin film, which is selectively permeable to certain molecules and not others. It is a single-cell-thick lung wrapped around its entire body.

Its head, if it can be called a head, is a cluster of sensory structures that you cannot identify. Not eyes. Not antennae. Something else. Dense, dark, crystalline nodes, embedded in the armored skull, that might detect pressure changes, or chemical gradients, or the electromagnetic fields generated by the canopy's photosynthetic machinery. The flier navigates the canopy not by seeing it but by *feeling* it — by sensing the standing waves of the stomatal breathing, the electromagnetic pulses of the filament grid, the thermal gradients between sun and shadow. It lives in a sensory world that has no visual component, no auditory component in the way you understand hearing. It lives in a world of fields and pressures and chemical traces, and it has lived there for as long as the canopy has been breathing, which is to say: for longer than anything else here.

They are older than the grazers. The grazers are a recent innovation — a few million years, perhaps, a geological eyeblink. The fliers are ancient. They were among the first things to separate from the Continuum, back when the canopy was forming, back when the first stomatal fields opened and the first gas-exchange currents began to flow. They evolved *with* the canopy. They are the canopy's creatures, the way the grazers are the bark's creatures. They belong to the air.

And they are dying. Not individually — individually, they die constantly, falling, always falling, the canopy shedding them the way a tree sheds fruit. But as a category. The canopy is changing. The stomatal fields are shifting, their rhythms altering by fractions of a degree per millennium, and the fliers — adapted to currents that have been stable for tens of millions of years — cannot adapt fast enough. Their population is declining. Slowly. Imperceptibly. But declining. The shapes crossing the canopy-light are fewer than they were a million years ago, and will be fewer a million years from now. The Continuum is not aware of this. The Continuum is not aware of anything. But the fliers are part of the Continuum's ecosystem, part of its metabolic loop, part of the machinery of its own maintenance, and their slow disappearance is a change in the Continuum's own body — a small change, a change so small that the Continuum, which operates on geological timescales, will not notice for another ten million years, but a change nonetheless.

The flier in the fissure is dead. Its sails are torn. Its body is still. Its crystalline sensory nodes are dull. By tomorrow, it will be gone. The bark will have taken it.

## The Dwellers in the Conduits

You do not see them. You will never see them, not from the surface, not without descending into the xylem conduits themselves, which run below the bark at depths ranging from twenty meters to three hundred, and which are — you must understand this — not empty.

The sap fills them. The amber alcohol, the methanol-cut-with-terpene-resin, the dense fluid that pulses and flows and drives the Continuum's industrial metabolism — it is not a sterile fluid. It is not a pure chemical transported through clean pipes. It is a living medium. A broth. A soup. And things live in it.

They are not large. The largest are perhaps the size of a human finger: vermiform, soft, translucent, with visible internal structures — dark filaments running the length of the body, pulsing in time with the sap-pressure waves. They swim. Not with fins or flippers but with peristaltic waves, contracting and expanding their bodies in a rhythm that matches the flow of the sap, so that they appear to be carried along by the current but are in fact swimming, actively, within it, maintaining their position or moving against the flow with a precision that suggests they can sense the pressure differentials and adjust accordingly.

They feed. They feed on the sap itself — on the complex molecules dissolved in it, the resins and the mineral salts and the organic compounds that the Continuum has spent millions of years learning to produce. They are parasites. Or symbionts. The distinction has no meaning here, because the Continuum is not harmed by them and does not benefit from them in any way that it could be said to *experience*. They are simply there. They live in its blood, the way certain organisms live in the blood of animals on other worlds, except that the blood is alcohol and the animal is a continent and the parasites have been there for so long that the Continuum's immune system — if it has one — has long since accepted them as self.

There are others in the conduits. Smaller. Much smaller. The sap is dense with them: microorganisms, or whatever serves as microorganisms in a world without water, adapted to an alcohol medium, metabolizing the terpene resins into simpler compounds that the Continuum's own cells can reabsorb. They are the Continuum's gut flora. Its internal ecosystem. The xylem conduits, which from the surface appear to be infrastructure — pipes, tunnels, delivery systems — are in fact rivers, and the rivers are full of life, and the life is as much a part of the Continuum as the bark and the canopy and the sap itself.

The conduit-dwellers are the deepest layer of the Continuum's fauna. They are the ones that never see light, never hear the roar, never feel the cool air. They live in the dark, in the warm, in the amber fluid, in the pressure and the pulse, and their world is the inside of a living thing, and they have no way of knowing that the thing they live inside is the world.

## The Things at the Growth Zones

The red-shelled extremophiles on the processing domes are the most visible non-grazer fauna in the Continuum, and they are the most alien. Not because they are strange — by the standards of this world, they are almost mundane — but because they have made a choice that nothing else here has made. They have chosen the storm.

The growth zones are hostile. Not to the Continuum, which is the growth zones, but to everything that is not the Continuum. The temperatures on the dome surfaces reach levels that would denature any protein evolved in a water-based chemistry. The methanol vapor, when the exhaust ports vent, is corrosive. The chemical output of the domes includes reactive compounds — peroxides, aldehydes, radical species — that would destroy the cellular structures of any organism not specifically adapted to withstand them. The noise is not merely loud but *physical*: the shockwave from a venting port can throw a grazer fifteen meters. Nothing should live here.

The red-shelled things live here. They live here in densities that would be called swarms if the word did not imply a randomness that is not present. They live here in *patterns*. The spiraling movements up the dome surfaces, the clustering at the exhaust ports, the way they orient themselves in the channels where the oil flows thickest — these are not random. They are structured. They are, in a very real sense, organized.

Not socially. The red-shells do not have social structures. They do not communicate. They do not cooperate. But they are *coordinated*. Each individual is responding to the same stimuli — the chemical gradients on the dome surface, the temperature differentials, the pressure pulses from the exhaust ports — and the stimuli are structured because the dome itself is structured, and so their responses create patterns. The spirals are not the red-shells' invention. They are the dome's geometry expressed through the movements of organisms that are following the dome's geometry. The red-shells are a visualization of the growth zone's own internal processes. They are the dome reading itself, in a sense, through the medium of small red bodies that move across its surface.

They are also the ancestors. Not of the grazers, not of the fliers, not of the conduit-dwellers. Of all of them. The red-shells are the oldest independent lineage in the Continuum. They were the first things to separate from the parent organism, back when the growth zones were new, back when the Continuum first began producing complex molecules at industrial scales. They evolved in the heat and the chemical storm of the processing domes, and they adapted to it so completely that they cannot live anywhere else. Move a red-shell to a bark plain and it will die in minutes, not from cold — the plains are not cold — but from the absence of chemical input. It will starve. It needs the reactive compounds. It needs the methanol. It needs the storm. It is the storm's creature, and it cannot leave the storm.

But the other lineages did leave. Or rather: they descended from lineages that left. Long ago, in the deep prehistory of the Continuum's fauna, something like a red-shell — something heat-tolerant, something adapted to the chemical fury of the growth zones — moved off a dome and onto the bark surface, and survived, and reproduced, and its descendants became the grazers. And something else, something that had already developed the ability to ride the gas-exchange currents, was carried into the canopy, and its descendants became the fliers. And something smaller, something that could fit into the xylem conduits, was swept into the sap flow, and its descendants became the conduit-dwellers. The red-shells are the root. The origin. The common ancestor of everything that lives on the Continuum that is not the Continuum.

They do not know this. They do not know anything. They are small red organisms on a hot dome, feeding, clustering, spiraling, riding out the venting, and they have been doing this for so long that the doing has become the being, and the being has become the place, and the place is all they are.

## The Something Else

There is one more thing. It is the hardest thing to describe because it is the hardest thing to see, and it may not be a thing at all. It may be a process. It may be a behavior of the Continuum itself that you are misinterpreting as a separate organism. But something is there.

In the deep fissures — the ones that go down four meters, five, into the black, soft bark where the moss-scaling grows thickest — there is movement that is not the grazers. The grazers are visible. They are fifteen centimeters, bark-colored, click-audible-through-the-bark. This movement is not visible. It is felt. A vibration in the bark surface that is not the sap-thrum, not the canopy-roar, not the venting-shriek. A vibration with a pattern. Not a regular pattern. Not a pulse. Something more like a sequence. Like a sentence. Like something tapping, deliberately, on the inside of the bark.

You hear it — feel it — in the deepest fissures, where the bark is soft and the air is still and the constant roar of the Continuum is muffled just enough by the walls of the fissure that other sounds become perceptible. You put your hand on the black bark and you feel it: tap. Tap-tap. Tap-tap-tap. A rhythm. A specific rhythm, repeated, with variations.

It could be the sap. Local pressure variations in the xylem conduits directly below the fissure, producing vibrations in the bark surface that happen to have a pattern. This is the most parsimonious explanation. The Continuum is full of patterns. Its growth zones cycle at frequencies. Its stomata open and close in waves. Its sap pulses. The patterns are not information. They are physics.

But the variations bother you. The rhythm does not repeat exactly. It repeats *with changes*, the way a phrase might be spoken, then spoken again with an added clause, then spoken a third time with a word altered. This is not the behavior of a pressure wave. This is the behavior of something that is generating a sequence and modifying it based on — what? Response? Feedback? Something received?

There is nothing down there. You lie on the bark at the edge of the fissure and lower your head into the dark and you see nothing. The black bark is black. The moss-scaling is teal. The grazers are clicking. There is nothing else. But the tapping continues, and it is coming from *inside* the bark, from the layer between the surface and the xylem conduits, from the living tissue of the Continuum itself, and you cannot find a source because the source is not separate from the Continuum. The source is the Continuum.

Or: the source is something that lives inside the Continuum, something that has never been on the surface, something that has no need for light or air or the bark-plains or the canopy. Something that lives in the phloem, in the living tissue between the bark and the xylem, in the cambium layer where the Continuum's cells are actively dividing, where growth happens, where the organism is most alive. Something that has been there as long as the Continuum has been there, which is to say: since before anything else, and that has, over the cons, become so integrated with the Continuum's own tissue that it cannot be distinguished from it.

A parasite? A symbiont? A mind?

You cannot know. You lie at the edge of the fissure and you feel the tapping and it feels like language. It feels like the interior of the Continuum, the deep body, the part beneath the storm, the part that has been resting for millions of years on the bark plains — the part that is not resting at all but *thinking*, in whatever way something that is not a brain and not a nervous system and not an organism in any sense you recognize can be said to think.

The tapping changes when you move. You shift your weight on the bark and the rhythm stutters, then resumes, then shifts. It could be a mechanical response. Your weight changes the local pressure on the bark surface, which changes the local pressure in the tissue below, which changes the vibration pattern. Physics. Not communication.

But you cannot stop listening. You lie there in the fissure, in the dark, in the roar-muffled dark, with the teal moss-scaling crackling beneath you and the grazers clicking in their methodical way and the tapping coming from inside the bark, and you think: *something down there knows I am here*. Not the Continuum. The Continuum does not know you are here. But something *in* the Continuum, something that is part of it and not part of it, something that lives in the cambium the way the conduit-dwellers live in the sap, something that has been there forever and will be there forever and has had longer than anything else to become whatever it has become—

Something down there is tapping, and it is tapping *to you*, and you cannot answer, and you do not know if that is a comfort or a grief.

## What Lives Here

Everything here is the Continuum and everything here is from the Continuum and everything here returns to the Continuum. The grazers, with their root-legs and their bark-colored shells, are the Continuum's surface mining itself. The fliers, with their sails and their field-sense, are the Continuum's air exploring itself. The conduit-dwellers, with their translucent bodies and their peristaltic swimming, are the Continuum's blood inhabiting itself. The red-shells, with their heat-tolerance and their spiraling patterns, are the Continuum's industry metabolizing itself. The moss-scaling is the Continuum's skin colonizing itself. The tapping in the deep fissures is — maybe — the Continuum's body talking to itself.

What lives here is one organism and its descendants and its parasites and its symbionts and its old, old mistakes that became creatures and its older, older processes that became patterns that became something that might be a voice. What lives here is a world that ate its own ocean and forgot that water existed and replaced everything that water made with something that alcohol and resin and time could make, and what it made was this: a bark surface that stretches to every horizon, a canopy that breathes with continental force, a noise that fills every frequency, a light that falls in pillars, an emptiness that is held open like a breath, and small clicking things in the crevices that are the Continuum's own fingers, feeling its own face, in the dark, forever.
