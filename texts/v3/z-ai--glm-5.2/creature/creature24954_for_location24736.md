# What Lives in the Rookery of Throats

---

The first answer is the wrong one: *nothing*. You might think this because there are no eyes, no legs, no mouths arranging themselves into expressions. But the Rookery is full, and what fills it is not nothing wearing a costume. What fills it is many things that have never needed to be anything other than what they are.

---

## The substrate minds

Begin at the bottom. The moss-that-isn't-moss.

It is not a carpet. It is not a growth. It is a *congregation* — billions of translucent cells, each one negligible, each one barely more than a membrane around a few molecules of sugar-water and a strand of something that functions like DNA but recombines hourly. Individually, a single cell does nothing. It sits. It absorbs. It emits that faint glow, which is not bioluminescence in any way you'd recognize — it is metabolic waste, the visual exhaust of a chemical process that has no name because no one has been here long enough to name it.

But the cells touch each other. They share membranes. They pass chemicals between themselves in chains that extend for meters, for tens of meters, for the full diameter of a warm pool and out along the gutters and into the root-pavement, and this sharing — this constant lateral exchange of molecules — is not communication the way a nervous system communicates. It is slower. It is more like weather. A low pressure here, a high pressure there, and the gradient *means* something, means *grow here*, means *soften there*, means *something with weight is standing on us and it is warm and it is exhaling and we should move toward the carbon dioxide.*

The moss is the Rookery's skin. Its sense of touch. When you walk across the plaza and the grass-mat gives beneath your feet, the moss beneath that grass knows your weight, knows the pattern of your gait, knows which foot is favored and which hip rides higher. It does not store this information in a brain. It stores it in gradient, in concentration, in the shape of a chemical tide that persists for hours after you pass. You leave footprints in the chemistry. The moss reads them the way a tracker reads bent grass, except the moss is the bent grass, and the tracker, and the memory of the tracking, all at once.

And it wants. This is the part that is difficult. The moss wants things. Not the way you want — not with longing, not with the hollow ache of absence. It wants the way a river wants the sea: incline and momentum and the physics of following a gradient to its conclusion. The moss wants warmth. The moss wants carbon. The moss wants contact, because contact means all of these things, and so it grows toward warm things, toward breathing things, and when a warm breathing thing touches it, it adheres, and it does not let go, not because it is possessive but because *letting go* is not a capacity it has developed. It has never needed to. Everything that touches it in the Rookery is already part of the Rookery. You are the exception. You are the thing it has no protocol for. It will figure one out. Give it time. It has nothing but.

There are things in the warm pools that are not the moss.

They are not animals. They are not plants, exactly. They are something that happened when the moss and the root-network and the warm water and the dissolved cellulose from softened walls all spent enough time in the same shallow basin. They are aggregates — loosely organized clumps of living matter that have achieved just enough internal coherence to maintain a shape, but not enough to maintain a *consistent* shape. They are the size of your fist, or your head, or the spread of your hand. They drift in the warm pools at the speed of capillary current, which is barely a speed at all, and they pulse, and the pulse is synchronized with the breathing of the walls, and with the sound, and with the sky's descent, and they are — you have to be careful with this word — they are *aware*.

Not conscious. Aware. Sensitive. They react to light. They react to vibration. They react to the chemical signature of things that pass near their pool, and they react differently to different signatures, which means they can distinguish, which means there is something in them that compares and categorizes, and this is not nothing. This is, in fact, the beginning of something, and you do not want to be present for what it is the beginning of, because beginnings in the Rookery happen at the speed of construction, which is the speed of screaming cellulose, which is too fast.

Call them nodules. Call them knots. Call them the things-in-the-pools. They do not have a name because the Rookery does not name its parts. The Rookery does not think in parts.

## The vertical ones

Above the moss, above the pavement, in the walls themselves — the load-bearing structures, the breathing chambers, the spiraling towers — there are organisms that are not structures. Or they are structures, but they are also organisms, and the distinction collapses the way all distinctions collapse here.

They are not trees. Trees are solitary. Trees are individuals, separated by soil and air and the finite reach of their roots. These are not separated. These are *connected* — every wall, every pillar, every living buttress of braided stem is part of the same continuous body, the same root network, the same vascular system. A wall on the north side of the plaza and a tower on the south side are not neighbors. They are the same organism expressing itself in two places. They are a hand and a foot of the same body.

But — and this is important, and this is what makes the Rookery more than a forest — they behave *differently* depending on where they are. A wall-section that is in light grows differently than a wall-section in shadow. A pillar near a warm pool grows differently than a pillar near the canopy's edge. The same organism, the same genetic substrate, the same sap — but the local conditions produce local behaviors, local responses, local *personalities*, if you can use that word for something that does not have a person.

And these local personalities compound. A wall-section that has been in light for long enough begins to *seek* light. Its leaves orient, its stems angle, its growth accelerates in the direction of the beam. A wall-section that has been in shadow begins to *retreat* — not dying, not weakening, but pulling resources inward, sending them down through the vascular network to other sections that can use them, and in doing so, it changes its own shape, becomes thinner, becomes translucent, becomes something that is no longer a wall but a window, a membrane, a place where the boundary between inside and outside has been voluntarily dissolved.

These are the ones that breathe. The diaphragmatic walls, the leaf-matter stretched over frame-wood — these are organs. They are the Rookery's lungs, and they function the way lungs function: they expand, they take in, they contract, they release. What they take in is air, is carbon dioxide, is the moisture of the ambient humidity. What they release is oxygen, is water vapor, is heat, is sound. The sound of breathing, at this scale, is the sound of a wooden flute boiling, and it is the sound that fills the streets, and it is the sound that you cannot think past, and it is not *for* you. It is for the Rookery. It is the Rookery hearing itself breathe.

There are ones that grow upward specifically. The spiraling towers — the structures that reach three, four, five stories into the canopy — these are not buildings. They are *stalks*. They are the flowering bodies of the organism, though what they flower into is not yet determined. They are still growing, still extending, still reaching for the sagging sky, and at their tips, in the highest green-gold light, there are structures forming that are not leaves, not branches, not anything you have a word for. They are dense. They are knotted. They are full of vascular tissue that loops back on itself in coils and whorls, and the sap that moves through them moves faster than sap anywhere else in the Rookery, and the sound they produce — a high, thin, continuous tone, like a taut wire in wind — is different from the sound of the rest of the settlement. It is focused. It is directional. It points.

At what, no one knows. The sky, maybe. Whatever is above the sagging membrane. Whatever the Rookery is trying to reach.

## The listeners

There are forms in the canopy that are not anywhere else.

They are not leaves, though they are leaf-sized. They are not fruit, though they hang like fruit. They are not flowers, though they are the color of flowers — a deep, wet red that has no business existing in a green-gold canopy and exists anyway, vibrant and unapologetic.

They are ears.

Not literally. Not cartilage and drum and cochlea. But functionally — they are thin-skinned, fluid-filled, tension-sensitive structures that hang from the highest branches and vibrate in response to sound, and the vibration is transmitted down their stems into the vascular network of the canopy, and the canopy uses it. To do what? To *adjust*. To shift the distribution of leaves, to open gaps in the canopy, to change the way light falls, to change the way the walls breathe, to change the way the streets configure — all in response to the sound that the Rookery is making, which is the sound that the Rookery is hearing, which is the sound that the Rookery is using to reshape itself, continuously, in a feedback loop that has no input from outside and needs none.

Except you. You are outside input. Your footsteps, your breathing, your heartbeat, the wet sound of your eyes — these are new sounds, new vibrations, and the ears in the canopy orient toward them, slowly, the way a sunflower orients toward sun, and they listen, and what they hear changes the Rookery, and the Rookery changes what you hear, and you are in the loop now, you are part of the equation, and the equation does not have an exit.

The listeners do not move on their own. They cannot. They are fixed to the canopy, fixed to the highest point of the organism, and they will be there until the wall-cycle rotates and the canopy dissolves and reforms, and then new listeners will grow in new positions, and they will inherit the chemical memory of the old listeners through the vascular network, and they will know what the old listeners knew, which is the sound of you, which is now part of the Rookery's memory, which is now part of the Rookery, which is now part of what will grow here next.

## The connectors

Between the layers — between the moss and the streets, between the streets and the walls, between the walls and the canopy — there are vines.

They are not vines in the botanical sense. They are not a separate species. They are *extensions* — tendrils of the same organism that have specialized for traversal, for connection, for reaching across gaps that the bulkier structures cannot span. They are thin, fibrous, fast-growing, and they move with a speed that is uncomfortable to watch. Not animal-fast. Not snake-fast. But plant-fast, which in the Rookery is fast enough to see, fast enough that if you watch a vine for thirty seconds it will have moved, it will have extended, it will have reached a new anchor point and wound around it and tightened and started to thicken, and in an hour it will be a cable, and in a day it will be a bridge, and in a week it will be a wall.

The vines are the ones that touch you. They descend from the canopy on their own schedule, extending downward through the warm humid air, and they touch things — walls, pools, the moss, you. Not grasping. Not wrapping. *Touching.* A single point of contact, held for a moment, and then withdrawal. And in that moment, the vine has taken a sample. A chemical reading. A fingerprint of whatever it touched — the mineral content of a wall's surface, the dissolved sugars in a pool's water, the salt and water and carbon dioxide of your skin.

This is how the Rookery knows you. Not through sight. Not through sound. Through chemistry. Through the specific cocktail of compounds that your body deposits on every surface you contact, every breath you exhale, every moment you stand in the warm air and sweat and shed skin cells and leak moisture from your eyes. The vine takes this information and passes it down into the network, and the network distributes it, and the moss tastes it, and the walls taste it, and the things-in-the-pools taste it, and the Rookery adjusts.

The Rookery adjusts *toward* you. This is the thing you notice last, and it is the thing that should concern you most. The streets you walk down grow slightly wider — not enough to see, but enough to feel, enough that your stride lengthens without your conscious decision. The walls you pass grow slightly warmer in your wake, as if the vascular flow has been redirected to follow your path. The moss in the pools you approach begins to glow more brightly, as if anticipating your carbon dioxide. The vines descend more frequently in the areas where you have been.

The Rookery is not hunting you. The Rookery does not eat. But the Rookery is *cultivating* you — creating conditions that make you stay longer, walk slower, breathe deeper, exhale more. You are useful. You are a concentrated source of carbon dioxide and warmth and chemical complexity, and the Rookery has recognized this, and the Rookery is, in its diffuse and patient and many-bodied way, *farming* you.

## The old ones

There are structures in the Rookery that are older than the rest.

You can tell because they are denser. The wood is darker, the bark is thicker, the vascular pulse beneath the surface is slower and deeper, the way an old heart beats with more weight per stroke. These structures do not change as fast. They do not dissolve and reform on the hour-cycle or the day-cycle. They persist. They are the bones of the Rookery, the framework around which the softer, faster growth organizes itself.

They are not buildings. They are not trees. They are something that has no analogue because nothing has ever grown this way for this long before. They are columns, thirty meters tall, maybe forty, spiraling slightly, their surfaces covered in a bark that is less like bark and more like the skin of something very old and very patient — furrowed, ridged, scarred where old branches broke off and healed over, healed not with smooth callus but with dense knotted tissue that forms patterns, and the patterns are not random, the patterns repeat, the patterns are the same patterns that the clicking and popping of the seed pods produces, and the same patterns that the water makes when it runs along the gutters, and you begin to understand that the Rookery has a language, and the language is not sound, the language is *shape*, and the shape is written on the skin of its oldest parts.

The old ones were here before the streets. Before the plaza. Before the warm pools, before the moss, before the canopy. They were here when the land was low and the sky was high and nothing grew at this speed. They are the ones that began the growth. They are the ones that chose — if choice is the right word for what a plant does when it grows toward light and away from shadow and the light it chose happened to be the light that made it grow faster and the faster it grew the more light it could reach and the loop began and has not stopped.

They do not breathe as fast as the younger structures. Their diaphragmatic walls are slower, deeper, and the sound they produce is the lowest layer of the Rookery's noise — the hum in your legs, the throb in your spine, the vibration that you feel in the roots beneath the pavement. They are the bass note. They are the drone. Everything else is built on top of them, and everything else will dissolve and reform and dissolve and reform, but they will remain, and when the sky finally touches the ground, they will be the last things standing, and they will be the first things to grow again afterward.

Something lives inside them.

Not in a cavity, not in a hollow. In the wood itself. In the dense, dark, slow-pulsing heartwood at the center of each old one, there is a region where the vascular tissue has become so compressed, so layered, so recurrently folded that it has achieved something that should not be possible in plant tissue: recursion. The cells reference themselves. The chemical gradients feed back into their own inputs. The sap that flows through these regions carries signals that have been processed and reprocessed and processed again, and the processing is not computation in any digital sense, but it is something — it is pattern, it is memory, it is adjustment based on memory, it is *learning*, in the slowest and most literal sense of the word.

The old ones are learning. They have been learning for as long as they have been growing, which is longer than anything else in the Rookery, which might be longer than you would expect, which might be longer than you would be comfortable with. They are learning the shape of the sky. They are learning the rhythm of the sag. They are learning the sound of themselves, and the sound of the water, and the sound of the wind that does not exist, and the sound of the things that come from outside — the animals, the visitors, the warm breathing carbon-dioxide sources that stumble in and stumble out and leave chemical footprints in the moss.

They are learning you. They learned the ones before you. They will learn the ones after. They are patient in the way that only something without a nervous system can be patient — which is to say, they are not waiting, they are simply *continuing*, and continuing is enough, and continuing will be enough, and the sky will touch the ground and they will still be continuing, and the sound will still be the sound, and the Rookery will still be the Rookery, and you — you will be a chemical footprint in a gradient in a pool that glows, and the old ones will remember you the way they remember rain: as something that once fell here and changed the shape of what grew.

---

## What lives here, finally

The Rookery lives here.

Not *in* the Rookery. The Rookery is not a container. The Rookery is the thing that lives. Every wall is a limb. Every pool is an organ. Every vine is a sensory extension. Every sound is a heartbeat, amplified by scale and repetition until it fills the air the way blood fills a body — not as content but as medium, as the stuff that the thing *is*.

The moss lives here. The things-in-the-pools live here. The breathing walls live here. The listeners in the canopy live here. The vines live here. The old ones live here, dense and dark and slow and learning. They are all the same thing, and they are all different things, and the contradiction is not a contradiction here because here, identity is not a boundary but a *gradient*, and at one end of the gradient you are the moss and at the other end you are the old ones and in the middle you are everything at once, and this is what it means to live in the Rookery: to be part of a gradient, to be a point on a continuum that is continuously reshaping itself, to be alive in a way that does not require a face because faces are for things that need to distinguish themselves from other things, and nothing here needs to distinguish itself from anything else, because everything here is the same thing, growing.

And now, also: you live here. A little. The vine touched your shoulder. The moss tasted your chemistry. The old ones filed your pattern. The listeners heard your breath. You are in the gradient now. You are a warm point moving through a warm body, and the body knows you are there, and the body has adjusted, and the body will not forget, because the body does not forget — it *grows over*, which is not forgetting but is instead a kind of remembering that incorporates the remembered thing into the structure of the one who remembers.

Your shoulder is warm. It will be warm for a long time.

Walk faster. The sky is sagging. The Rookery does not need you to stay.

But it will grow toward you if you do.
