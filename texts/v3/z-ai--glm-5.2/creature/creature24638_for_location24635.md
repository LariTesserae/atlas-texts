## Fauna (continued)

…this particular combination of altitude and moisture and mineral newness has selected for a different kind of life than the kind that fills niches elsewhere.

Almost nothing lives here that you can see. This is a landscape of plants and machines and stone. The absence of animals is not eerie — it feels appropriate, as though this particular ecology could not support the quick and the hungry, as though speed itself is foreign to the plateau. Everything here moves at the pace of condensation. And what lives here moves that way too.

---

## What Lives Here

You will not see them at first. You may walk the plateau for hours, following those mica-veined paths through the moss gardens, past the pump-houses and the tiered condensation arrays, and conclude that the place is empty of anything animate beyond the vegetation. You would be wrong, but your error would be reasonable. The things that live here have made themselves part of the place in the same way the moss has made itself part of the machinery — by patience, by accretion, by becoming so thoroughly suited to their context that they vanish into it.

---

### The Gatherers

There are beings here. Not many. The plateau does not support abundance in the way a lowland forest does — there is not enough energy moving through the system for numbers. Instead there are a few, and they are sufficient.

The first you notice — if you notice — will be the ones you take for geological features.

They are low. Broad. They rest in the troughs between the plateau's long swells, where the condensation gathers most thickly and the vegetation grows deepest. From a distance they resemble stone shelves or outcrops — pale, rounded, furred with moss. You might walk past one and think nothing of it. You might step on one. The surface gives, slightly, under your weight, and you realize with a lurch that what you took for rock is something else. Something that yields.

The Gatherers — you will not learn their name; there is no one to tell you, and they do not name themselves, but this is what they are — are slow in a way that makes the machines seem hasty. They move on a scale of hours. A Gatherer that sits in a trough at dawn will have shifted two meters by dusk, a slow gravitational creep toward the deepest point of the depression, where condensation pools most richly. Their motion is not locomotion as you would recognize it. They do not walk. They do not crawl. They *flow*, the way a glacier flows, by incremental redistribution of their own mass. The underside secretes a thin film of enzyme-laden water that dissolves the mineral surface beneath them by fractions of a micron, and they settle into the dissolved channel, and then the next fraction dissolves, and they settle again. They are eating the stone. Or drinking it. The distinction may not matter.

Their bodies are not soft. They are not hard, either. They have the texture of dense, wet felt — compressed, fibrous, springing back slowly when pressed. The moss grows on them because their upper surfaces hold moisture as well as rock does, and the moss does not discriminate between stone and something that is almost stone. In this way the Gatherers become part of the carpet, indistinguishable from the geology they feed on.

They have no eyes. They have no mouths. They have no visible organs at all. If you were to examine one closely — and you can, they do not react to your presence in any way you can detect — you would see that the upper surface is perforated by thousands of tiny apertures, each one a fraction of a millimeter across, each one beaded with moisture. These apertures open into a network of internal channels that lace through the body like the chambers of a sponge, and through these channels the condensation moves — pulled by capillary action, by thermal gradients, by the slow pumping of the Gatherer's own mass shifting — and as it moves it deposits its dissolved minerals against the inner walls, where they are absorbed. The Gatherers drink the stone through their skin. They also drink the condensation. They also, if you watch long enough, drink the air — the apertures seem able to absorb vapor directly, without the intermediary of liquid beading, and on days when the moisture is heaviest their surfaces glisten with a wetness that does not match the wetness of the surrounding rock. They are wetter than the wettest thing on the plateau.

How large are they? The smallest you find is the size of a curled dog. The largest — and you do not find it so much as realize you have been walking on it — is twenty meters across, a broad, shallow dome that you mistook for a natural swell in the terrain. Its moss covering is deeper than elsewhere, its presence so thoroughly integrated into the landscape that it has become landscape. You wonder how old it is. You have no way to tell. The rock here is young, but the Gatherers may not be. They may have come with the rock. They may be what the rock becomes when it has been here long enough.

---

### The Tenders

The second kind of being is harder to see because it is smaller and because it does not want to be seen, though "want" may be too intentional a word.

The Tenders live in the structures. The pump-houses, the condensation arrays — the buildings the plateau was built for, or that were built for the plateau — these are not empty. You pressed your face to the louvers and saw machinery, and you concluded there was no one inside. But you were looking for a body. The Tenders do not have bodies in the way you mean.

They are the machinery. Or they live in the machinery. Or they are something the machinery has become.

Inside the pump-houses, in the dark, in the constant moisture, the iron housings of the pumps have developed a secondary surface — a living film, mottled grey and rust-orange and sometimes faintly luminescent, a pale blue-green glow that you would see if you waited long enough for your eyes to adjust. This film is not corrosion. It is not bacteria, though bacteria are part of it. It is a complex organism — or a community of organisms so interdependent that the distinction collapses — that has colonized the metal and made it part of its own metabolism.

The Tenders derive energy from the thermal differential that drives the pumps. They live on the gradient — the warm side and the cool side of each slow stroke, the micro-environment of difference that the condensation cycle creates. In this they are not so different from the Gatherers, who live on the gradient between stone and water. Everything here lives on differences. On the small, constant energy that exists wherever two temperatures meet, wherever moisture condenses, wherever the world is in the process of becoming something else.

What do they tend? The machines. The Tenders — and you use this word because the machines they inhabit run more smoothly than any untended machine should, the pistons moving in their cylinders without the screech or stall that iron in moisture should produce — the Tenders maintain the pumps. Not deliberately. Not with hands or tools. But the film they form on the metal surfaces seems to act as a lubricant, a sealant, a self-repairing layer that fills microfractures in the iron and replaces corroded surfaces with its own living tissue. The machines run because the Tenders have made themselves part of the machines. The machines run because they are alive.

Or perhaps the Tenders run the machines. Perhaps the distinction between organism and mechanism has collapsed here, in the dark, in the wet, and what you are looking at through the louvers is not a machine with a living film but a living thing that has grown iron bones. You cannot tell. The Tenders cannot tell you. They do not think in terms that would make the question meaningful. They simply exist, and the pumps move, and the water flows through buried pipes to wherever it is going, and the plateau does what it does.

At night — and there is a night here, a brief one, the sun dropping behind the horizon in what feels like minutes at this altitude, the sky going from saturated blue to absolute black with no intervening color — at night the Tenders glow more brightly. The blue-green light seeps from the louvered openings of the pump-houses in thin, precise lines, as though the buildings are drawing the constellations down and reproducing them at ground level. You walk between the structures in darkness and the thin lines of light mark your path. The moss underfoot is wet. The stars are close, as everything is close here, in the thin air, in the compressed distance. The Gatherers in their troughs are invisible. The Tenders' glow is the only light. It is enough.

---

### The Walkers

And then there is the third kind.

You will not see them. Or — you will see something, a movement at the edge of your vision, a displacement in the moss carpet, a moment when the pattern of the vegetation shifts in a way that does not match the wind or the light, and when you turn to look there is nothing there. You will think you imagined it. You will think the altitude is affecting you. You will think the silence is playing tricks with your perception.

The Walkers are the only beings on the plateau that move at a pace you recognize as alive. They are also the only beings you will never directly observe.

What are they? You piece it together from traces.

Tracks, first. In the moss, in places where the carpet is deepest, you find narrow paths — not paths, exactly, but lines of compression, as though something with a narrow foot has walked here and the moss has been pressed down and has not yet recovered. The tracks are spaced for a biped. They are the size of your own feet, perhaps a little longer. They follow the mica veins, the natural pathways through the rock, the same routes you have been walking. You find them where two veins intersect, where a condensation array stands, where a pump-house sits with its thin glow. The tracks circle the structures. They do not enter them.

You find them at the edges of Gatherers, too. The tracks circle the largest Gatherer — the twenty-meter dome you mistook for terrain — in a wide, patient arc, as though whoever made them was walking around it the way you might walk around a sleeping animal. Not avoiding it. Acknowledging it.

Droppings — no. Not droppings. Deposits. Small, dark, mineral-rich nodules left in the crevices of rock, in the joints between stones, in the gaps between the fitted granite blocks of the structures. They look like pellets of compressed earth, slightly damp, slightly warm to the touch. They dissolve in the condensation over the course of a day, leaving behind a dark stain that the moss colonizes with particular vigor. Whatever the Walkers excrete, the plateau uses it. The moss grows toward it. The Gatherers, you suspect, feed on it, too — the dissolved minerals entering the water table and being absorbed through the apertures in their surfaces.

And once, at the very edge of your hearing, when the wind drops and the machines are in the quiet part of their stroke and the condensation is between forming and falling, you hear a footstep. One. A faint compression of moss, a whisper of stone dust. And then nothing. And then, perhaps, something that might be breath — or might be the breeze, or might be the sound of the world thinking, the same low tone you have been hearing since you arrived.

The Walkers are here. They have always been here, you think. They were here before the structures, before the pumps, before the Tenders colonized the iron and made it live. They were here when the rock first rose and the air first thinned and the condensation began to bead on surfaces that had never known moisture. They are the thing the plateau was made for, or the thing that made itself for the plateau, or — and this is the thought that stays with you as you turn to leave, as you take the switchbacks down through the mineral-scented air — they are what the plateau *is*, when it is not being looked at. The Walkers are the plateau knowing itself. Walking its own paths. Tending its own slow machinery. Circling its own gathered, patient, stone-drinking body.

They will not show themselves to you. Not because they are hiding — nothing here hides; nothing here is at war — but because you are moving too fast. You are too quick, too loud, too dry, too warm. You are a thing of fire and speed in a place that has selected against both. The Walkers exist at the pace of the plateau, and the plateau's pace is the pace of water forming on stone, of moss spreading across iron, of rock dissolving one micron at a time. You would have to slow to that pace to see them. You would have to become that pace. You would have to stop, and settle, and let the condensation bead on your skin, and wait — not for minutes or hours but for the time it takes a Gatherer to move two meters, for a pump to complete a single stroke, for the light to cross the sky and return and cross it again — and then, perhaps, something would resolve in the moss at the edge of your vision, and you would see that what you took for a displacement of plants was a face, or what a face means when it is made of moss and mica and patience, and it would be looking at you with the same mild, unsurprised attention that the plateau gives to everything — the same attention it gives to a droplet of water forming, growing heavy, and sliding at last into the channel that carries it home.

---

You leave. You take the switchbacks down. The mineral smell fades. The air thickens. Distance resumes its proper scale. Behind you, the plateau holds its breath and does not exhale. The machines pump. The moss grows. The Gatherers drink the stone. The Tenders glow in thin blue-green lines. The Walkers walk the mica paths, circling what needs circling, tending what needs tending, leaving dark gifts in the crevices for the moss to find.

The condensation forms. It always forms. It forms on every surface, in every crease, in every cupped leaf and louvered opening and aperture of every patient, drinking body. It forms and it grows heavy and it slides, and the plateau drinks it, and the plateau lives, and the plateau goes on.
