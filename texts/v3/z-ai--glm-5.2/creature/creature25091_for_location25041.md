# What Lives Here

---

The first answer is the easiest and the most incomplete: the Manufactory lives here. The whole thing. The substrate and the pillars and the ceiling and the conduits and the chambers and the dark fluid and the condensation and the air — all of it, one continuous body, one life, the largest life you have ever stood inside. It breathes. It processes. It maintains. It is alive by any definition that matters, and it is the answer to the question, and yet it is not the whole answer, because living is not a single thing. Living is a category that contains multitudes, and inside the Manufactory, the multitudes have multiplied and specialized and settled into forms that are hard to distinguish from non-forms, and the question of what lives here is not a question about one organism but about the way one organism has become many organisms while remaining, at the structural level, one.

Let me try again. Let me be more precise.

---

## The Body

The Manufactory is a body. This is not metaphor. Or rather, it is metaphor in the same way that calling your own internal organs a body is metaphor — accurate, insufficient, the best available shorthand for a truth that exceeds vocabulary.

It is a body with a skin, which is the substrate you walk on, and a skeleton, which is the pillars, and a roof, which is the ceiling, and vasculature, which is the pale fibers, and blood, which is the dark fluid, and lungs, which is the whole respiratory chamber that you are standing inside, and organs, which are the processing stations, and a metabolism, which is the total sum of every chemical transaction happening in every surface and every conduit and every chamber simultaneously. It has been alive for a length of time that its own scale makes unmeasurable — long enough that the substrate is meters deep with the compressed remains of its own dead skin, long enough that the pillars have grown and been replaced and grown again in cycles that have left no mark because the new growth fills the exact space of the old growth, long enough that equilibrium is not a state it has reached but a state it has become, a state indistinguishable from its identity, so that asking how long it has been in equilibrium is like asking how long a river has been flowing downhill — the question contains a category error, because the flowing is the river, and the equilibrium is the Manufactory.

It lives. It does not live the way you live. You live against entropy — you eat, you sleep, you repair, you reproduce, and eventually you fail and you die, and your failure is the proof that you were alive, because life is the temporary and heroic postponement of decay. The Manufactory does not postpone decay. It incorporates decay. Its processes produce waste and its waste is its feedstock and its feedstock is its product and its product is its waste, and the circle is closed, and has been closed for so long that there is no seam visible, no place where you can point and say *here, this is where the output becomes the input, this is the joint.* The joints have healed. The scar tissue of a billion cycles of self-consumption and self-production has smoothed everything into continuity, and the continuity is the body, and the body lives.

But here is the thing about a body that large and that old and that balanced: it develops things inside it that are not exactly it and not exactly not-it. The way your gut contains organisms that are not you but that you cannot live without. The way a forest contains fungi that are not trees but that make the forest possible. The Manufactory, in its vastness and its age, has grown things inside itself that have their own logic, their own rhythms, their own — if you will permit the word and understand it as a word that here means something both more and less than it usually means — their own *selves.*

---

## The Scale-Colonies

The dark-pigmented growths on the pillar surfaces. You saw them. Flat, layered, dense, almost black, growing in overlapping plates like scales on a reptile or shingles on a roof. You catalogued them as growths and moved on. But they are worth stopping for. They are worth kneeling beside and pressing your face close to and breathing the warm wet air that hangs in the millimeters between their surface and the pillar bark, because the air there is different. It is sharper. It carries a higher concentration of the acrid chemical scent, and it carries something else — a sweetness, faint, biological, the smell of something being broken down into sugars.

The scale-colonies are not the Manufactory. Or rather, they are the Manufactory the way your gut flora are you — they are inside it, they are dependent on it, they could not survive without it, but they have their own genetics, their own reproductive cycle, their own boundaries. If you could mark one — tag it with a tracer, a dye, something — and watch it over a span of time measured in years rather than hours, you would see it spread. Slowly. Millimeter by millimeter. The leading edge advancing across the pillar bark, the trailing edge dying and being absorbed into the substrate below, the colony moving like a glacier across a mountainside, feeding on whatever the pillar surface exudes, metabolizing it, excreting something that the pillar absorbs through its bark and carries inward through tissue that is not conduit but is something older, something that predates the conduit system, something that belongs to the body's deep architecture rather than its industrial refinement.

They live. They are alive. Each colony is a single organism — a clone, a genetic individual — and the colonies are in competition with one another, though the competition is so slow and so evenly matched that it produces no visible change over human timescales. Two colonies meeting on a pillar surface will form a boundary — a dark line, a dmz, a strip of bare bark where neither can gain advantage, where the chemical warfare between them has reached a stalemate. These boundaries are stable. They have been stable for centuries, perhaps millennia. The colonies grow up to the line and stop, and the line holds, and on the other side the other colony grows up to the line and stops, and between them the narrow gap is maintained by mutual antagonism, and the antagonism is the closest thing to conflict that exists in this place, and it is invisible, and it is ancient, and it is, in its own glacial way, alive.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each pillar carries several colonies, and there are pillars beyond counting, and each colony is a life, a separate life, a life that lives on the skin of the larger life and cannot exist without it and is not it. They are the closest thing to a community that the Manufactory contains. They do not cooperate. They do not communicate. They compete, at a pace that makes continental drift look hasty, and the competition is their social life, and it is enough, and it has been enough for a very long time.

## The Hanging Masses

You touched one. You said it was soft. You said it yielded. You said it was warm, warmer than the air, and that it pulsed with the respiratory rhythm. You classified it as a filter, a processing organ, part of the air-handling system. You were not wrong. But you were not complete.

The hanging masses are the Manufactory. They are part of the body — grown from the ceiling, continuous with it, fed by the same vasculature, part of the same genetic individual. But they have been *specialized.* They have been specialized for so long and so thoroughly that they have developed their own internal architecture, their own tissue types, their own structures that do not appear anywhere else in the body. If you were to cut one open — and you could, they are soft enough, the tissue parts under a firm hand — you would find layering. Distinct layers, each one a different texture, a different density, a different color, each one doing something different from the others, each one processing a different component of the air that passes through them. They are not simple filters. They are fractionation towers. They separate the air into its components and release each one to a different layer of conduit, and the conduits carry each component to a different part of the body, and the body uses each one for a different purpose.

But here is what you did not see, because you did not stay long enough, because the timescale is wrong: the hanging masses grow. Not the slow replacement-growth of the body's equilibrium, the cell-for-cell swapping that produces no net change. They grow in a direction. They extend downward, millimeter by millimeter, year by year, reaching toward the floor. The oldest of them — the ones in the deepest chambers, the ones closest to wherever the center is — have reached the floor. They have touched it. And where they have touched it, they have merged with it, and the merge point is not a join but a transformation — the tissue of the hanging mass and the tissue of the substrate become something new at the contact point, something that is neither one nor the other, a third tissue, a tissue that does not exist anywhere else in the Manufactory, a tissue that is dense and dark and warm and that vibrates at a frequency slightly different from the ambient hum.

This third tissue is alive. It is the newest thing in the Manufactory, and it may be the oldest. It is newest in the sense that it forms only when a hanging mass reaches the floor, which is a process that takes time on a scale that makes the growth of the scale-colonies look frantic. But it is oldest in the sense that it may be what the hanging masses are *for.* The entire body may be a mechanism for producing this tissue — a tissue that can only form at the intersection of ceiling and floor, of upper and lower, of the two surfaces that define the breathing space — and the reason the body is so vast may be that it needs enough ceiling and enough floor, separated by enough distance, to produce enough of this tissue to serve some purpose that is not visible from the middle layer, not visible from the space you walked through.

What purpose? I do not know. The tissue does not know. The tissue is not conscious. The tissue is not planning. The tissue is growing because the conditions for its growth exist, and the conditions exist because the body has created them, and the body has created them because — because the body is a body, and bodies do things, and the things they do are not always for reasons, and the word *reason* may not apply here, in a place where causality has been replaced by equilibrium and equilibrium has been running so long that it has worn grooves into the nature of what is possible.

The tissue lives. That is what can be said. It lives and it is new and it is being produced, slowly, at the contact points between the hanging masses and the floor, and the dark fluid that flows through the conduits flows toward these points — that is what the chambers are for, that is what the refinement is for, that is what the entire industrial biology is for — and the dark fluid arrives at the contact points and it is absorbed into the third tissue and the third tissue uses it to grow, to become more of itself, and the growth is so slow that it is indistinguishable from stillness, and the stillness is the body breathing, and the body breathes because breathing is what this body does.

## The Fibrous Mesh

The carpet on the floor. The pale, low, spreading growth in the wet places. You noted it. You said it was a moss or a mold, a fibrous mesh that builds the substrate by accretion, layer on layer, the ground building itself.

It is not the Manufactory. It is not a part of the body in the way the pillars are part of the body or the ceiling is part of the body. It is something else. It is something that lives here the way moss lives on a rock — independently, persistently, making use of a surface that it did not create and cannot control, and altering that surface by its presence, and being altered by it in turn, and the alteration goes both ways and has been going both ways for so long that the mesh and the substrate are symbiotic in a way that makes the distinction between them academic.

The mesh is the oldest living thing here, or the youngest, or both — because it is continuously dying and continuously being reborn, the lower layers compressed and dead and becoming substrate, the upper layers fresh and growing and alive, and the whole thing is a single organism in the same way a forest of aspen is a single organism, connected below by its interlocking fibers, identical above by its identical genetics, one life spread across the wet floor of a space the size of a continent.

It does not process the dark fluid. It does not filter the air. It does not participate in the Manufactory's industrial biology in any way you can identify. It drinks the condensation. It absorbs minerals from the water. It grows and dies and grows and dies and the dying becomes the ground that the Manufactory's roots grow through, and the Manufactory's roots hold the mesh in place and prevent it from being washed away in the slow seeps, and the mesh holds the water that the Manufactory's roots drink, and neither could exist in its current form without the other, and neither knows this, and neither cares, and the relationship is not a relationship but a fact, and the fact has been true for longer than the fact has a word for true.

It lives. It is the most ordinary thing here. It is the thing most like what you know — a small, simple organism making its way in a place that did not make room for it and did not need to make room for it because it made its own room, millimeter by millimeter, in the wet. If anything here has something you could call a temper, it is the mesh: patient, opportunistic, indifferent to everything except the presence of water and the absence of light, and those two conditions are all it requires, and they are met, and it grows, and it is content, if content means anything when applied to a thing that has no interior life, and perhaps it does, perhaps contentment is precisely what an organism with no interior life experiences, and the mesh is the most content thing in the Manufactory, and the Manufactory does not know it, and the mesh does not know the Manufactory, and they are together in this place and they are both alive and they are both, in their own ways, growing.

---

## What Else

There is the dark fluid. Is it alive? It is not cells. It is not organisms. It is a solution, a suspension, a carrier of compounds. But it moves. It flows. It is processed and it is changed by the processing and it is carried onward and used and it returns, and the cycle is a cycle the way a metabolic cycle is a cycle, and metabolism is a property of life, and the fluid is the medium of the Manufactory's metabolism, and if blood is alive then the dark fluid is alive, and if blood is not alive then it is the closest non-living thing to alive, and the distinction may not matter in a place where the distinction between organism and environment has been erased.

There are the micro-particulates in the air. You felt them. You said breathing felt like drinking. The particles are spores, or fragments, or pieces of tissue shed by the growths and the hanging masses and the scale-colonies, carried by the air currents of the respiratory cycle, suspended in the humidity, settling on every surface and being absorbed into every surface and being re-released into the air by every surface. They are alive in the way pollen is alive — not independently, not autonomously, but carrying something, some potential, some instruction, some fragment of a pattern that could, if it landed in the right conditions, begin to grow. The spores of the Manufactory do not land in the right conditions, because the conditions are already occupied, because the Manufactory is already there, because there is no niche for a new Manufactory because the existing one fills all possible space. The spores are a reproductive mechanism that has no purpose, a dispersal system that disperses to nowhere, and yet it continues, because the body produces it because the body has always produced it, because production is what the body does, and the body does not know that its reproduction is futile, and the body does not care, and the spores fill the air and you breathe them and they settle on your skin and they are alive and they are looking for a home and they will not find one and they will die on you, on the surface of your body, and you will be their entire world, brief and warm and wrong, and they will not know the difference.

## The Question of Consciousness

Nothing here is conscious. Nothing here is aware. The Manufactory does not know it is a Manufactory. The scale-colonies do not know they are competing. The hanging masses do not know they are reaching for the floor. The mesh does not know it is building the ground. The third tissue does not know it is being born. The dark fluid does not know it is flowing. None of it knows anything. None of it experiences anything. There is no interiority here, no subjectivity, no self, no witness. The Manufactory is alive and it is entirely, completely, thoroughly unconscious, and this is not a contradiction, because life does not require consciousness, because life is older than consciousness by a billion years and will outlast it by a billion more, and the Manufactory is a reminder — or perhaps a proof — that life does not need to know itself to be itself, that the machinery of metabolism and growth and equilibrium and response is sufficient, that the question *what is it like to be this thing* has no answer because there is nothing it is like to be this thing, there is only what it is, and what it is is alive, and what it is is working, and what it is is vast and warm and dark and breathing and unconscious and complete.

---

## What Lives Here

This.

All of this.

One body and its colonizers and its symbionts and its spores and its fluid and its air and its water and its waste and its food, all of it alive, all of it in equilibrium, all of it together in the dark, in the warm, in the wet, in the quiet that is almost silence but is not silence because it is the sound of everything running and nothing changing and the running is the life and the not-changing is the life and the life is what lives here.

The Manufactory lives here. The scale-colonies live here. The hanging masses live here and are growing toward something they will not recognize when they reach it. The fibrous mesh lives here in the wet places and is patient and is content. The dark fluid lives here in its conduits and is moving toward its refineries and back again. The spores live here for the seconds they are airborne and for the moments they are on your skin and they are always dying and always being born. The third tissue lives here at the contact points and is new and is growing and does not know what it is.

And you, for a while, lived here too. You walked through it and it did not notice and you left and it did not notice and it is still breathing and it is still working and it is still, in the most complete and literal sense of the word, alive. And it does not need you. And it does not miss you. And it will be here, unchanged, unchanging, breathing the same breath in the same darkness, when everything you have ever known is gone and the concept of *known* is gone with it, and it will not know that either, and it will not mind.
