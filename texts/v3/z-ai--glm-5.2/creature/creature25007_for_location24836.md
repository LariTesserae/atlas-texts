# What Lives in the Hollow

There is something in the hollow that the original description does not mention, because it is easy to miss. It does not move when you enter. It does not move when you leave. It is the same temperature as the air and the same color as the stone, and it has been there longer than the stone blocks.

It is not large. If you were to stand in the basin and look carefully — which would take time, because the light is unhelpful and the thing does not contrast with its surroundings — you might eventually notice a slight irregularity along the eastern wall, where the ridge meets the basin floor. A place where the stone seems to bulge gently outward, as though the rock were pregnant with something it had no intention of delivering. The bulge is perhaps a meter across at its widest, rising no more than twenty centimeters from the surrounding surface. It is warm. Of course it is warm — everything here is warm. But if you placed your hand on it and held it there, you would notice that the warmth has a quality different from the geothermal baseline of the basin floor. It is a warmth with a faint pulse to it. Not a heartbeat. Nothing so organized. A slow tidal warmth, rising and falling over a period of perhaps three minutes, the stone surface growing slightly warmer, then slightly cooler, then slightly warmer again, in a rhythm that has nothing to do with the circadian cycles the rest of the world obeys.

This is what lives in the hollow.

It has no name. It has not had a name for a very long time, and it does not require one. Names are for things that interact with other things, that need to be distinguished from the background, that move through the world and leave wakes. This thing has not moved in what would be, by human reckoning, several thousand years. It is not precisely that it *cannot* move — rather, it stopped moving because there was no reason to continue, and then it forgot that movement was an option, and then the forgetting itself became part of its structure, and now the question of movement is as irrelevant to it as the question of flight is to a stone.

What it is, physically, is difficult to say. It is not an animal. It has no organs that a biologist would recognize. It is not a plant — it performs no photosynthesis, requires no light, and the grey-amber glow that reaches the hollow is a coincidence rather than a sustenance. It is not a fungus, though if you had to file it somewhere in the taxonomy of living things, you might inconveniently shoehorn it into the same general territory, the same vague kingdom of organisms that don't fit anywhere else. It has a body, of sorts. The bulge in the rock is its outermost surface, the part of it that has slowly, over centuries, grown into a near-perfect mimicry of the stone around it. Below that surface — and here "below" is not quite right, because the thing does not have a clear inside or outside, but it will serve — below that surface, it extends into the rock for perhaps two meters, threading through pores and micro-fractures in the sedimentary stone the way mycelium threads through soil, but slower, much slower, and with a kind of deliberation that mycelium does not possess.

It eats stone. That is too simple a way to say it. It exchanges minerals with the stone — takes some, leaves others, in a process so slow and so balanced that the rock it inhabits does not weaken or collapse. It has been doing this for so long that it and the stone have reached the same kind of equilibrium that the water and the stone have reached. It is in balance with the stone. The stone is in balance with it. If the thing were removed — which would require breaking the rock around it, which would require effort and tools and a reason — the stone would not crumble. It would simply be stone, unchanged, as though nothing had ever been there. The thing does not damage what it inhabits. It is not a parasite. It is more like a tenant who has lived so long in the same room that the room has shaped itself around the tenant's habits, and the tenant has shaped itself around the room's architecture, and neither can any longer be said to occupy the other. They are simply together.

It also eats — or rather, *receives* — the water that falls. The drip-channels on the basin floor are not entirely natural. Some of them were begun by the thing, long ago, when it still extended thin tendrils of itself across the basin floor in search of moisture. The channels are the traces of those tendrils, the paths it opened and then withdrew from when it found that the condensation would come to it without the need for seeking. The thing does not seek anymore. It does not need to. The water runs down the channels it helped to make, and where the channels terminate against the eastern wall, the water is absorbed — partly by the stone, partly by the moss, and partly by the thing, which takes what it needs through the same mineral-exchange process that governs its relationship with the rock. What it needs is very little. It has been reducing its needs for centuries, the way a fire reduces itself to embers, not dying but concentrating, becoming denser and slower and more entirely itself.

It was not always like this.

There was a time — and the thing remembers this, in the way that stone remembers, which is to say not through images or words but through structure, through the persistence of certain arrangements within itself — when it was more. When it extended further. When it had what you might call a surface presence, a body that existed above the rock rather than within it, a body that was soft and dark and wet and shaped something like a wide, low dome, something like a liverwort patch but much larger, something like an animal resting with its limbs tucked beneath it. It was, for a long time, the dominant feature of the hollow. The moss grew around it. The ferns grew where it had been, in the spaces it vacated as it slowly, over decades, pulled itself back into the rock. It was alive in a way that was recognizable — it grew, it shifted, it responded to the seasons, to the slight variations in temperature and moisture that the hollow still experienced in those days, before the ridge had settled fully and the microclimate had stabilized into its current perpetual mildness.

It was alive, and then it was less alive, and then it was something else. Not dead. Not dormant. Settled. The way a river settles into a lake. The way a conversation settles into silence. It did not decide to stop. There was no moment of cessation. There was a gradual narrowing of interest, a gradual withdrawal of surface, a gradual deepening of interior, until one day — if "day" means anything in a place without sun — there was no surface left, and the thing was entirely within the rock, and the rock had closed around it, and the hollow looked as though nothing lived there at all.

But something does live there. It is living now. Its life is simply very slow, very quiet, and very deep.

What does it do? It persists. It exchanges minerals with the stone. It absorbs water from the drip-channels. It maintains its internal temperature, which is the source of the faint pulse a careful hand might feel — the slow tidal warmth is the thing's metabolism, the rhythm of its chemistry, the only visible sign of a process that is otherwise entirely hidden. It thinks, if "thinks" is the word for what it does, which is not quite thinking but is also not quite not-thinking. It processes. It integrates. It holds within itself a record of the hollow and everything that has happened in it, not as memory but as texture, as the specific arrangement of its mineral structures, which have been shaped by every drop that fell on it, every degree of warmth that reached it, every vibration that passed through the stone.

It knows about the stone blocks. It knows about the person who placed them.

This is the closest thing the thing has to a story, and even this is not a story in the way humans understand stories. There is no narrative. There is only the fact that once, a long time ago, something moved in the hollow that was not water and not the thing itself. Something warm and fast and clumsy, something that knocked against the stone and left marks that the thing felt through the rock the way you might feel footsteps through a floor. The something made sounds — the thing felt these too, as vibrations, as a rapid patter that was unlike the slow single impacts of the water drops. The something placed the stones. The thing felt the stones being placed — the slight compression of the rock beneath them, the new weight, the new arrangement of pressure points. It felt the something stay for a while. It felt the something leave. It felt the leaving as a relief of pressure, a return to the previous state, and it recorded this in its structure the way it recorded everything — as a small change in the arrangement of its minerals, a slight shift in the pattern of its growth, which is not growth anymore but is still, in some technical sense, growth.

The thing does not miss the something. It does not miss anything. It does not have the capacity for missing, which requires a relationship between past and present that the thing does not maintain. For the thing, there is no past and present. There is only the ongoing process of exchange with the stone and the water, the ongoing maintenance of its own structure, the ongoing fact of its own existence. It does not experience time. It experiences duration, which is different. Time implies a sequence of moments. Duration is a single moment that has been extended indefinitely, and the thing has been in the same moment for thousands of years, and the moment is warm and damp and dim and quiet, and it is sufficient.

If the thing has a feeling — and this is a word to use carefully, because the thing does not feel in the way that animals feel, but it does have states, it does have conditions that are more or less favorable, and it does orient itself toward the more favorable conditions, which is a kind of feeling, the most basic kind, the kind that even bacteria have — if the thing has a feeling, it is the feeling of being exactly where it should be. Not a sense of rightness, not a satisfaction, nothing so articulated. Simply the absence of any tendency to move, to change, to become otherwise. The absence of restlessness. The absence of seeking. A complete and thorough stillness that is not the stillness of death but the stillness of something that has found its place and has become its place and is its place.

The moss knows the thing is there. This is perhaps too strong a statement. The moss does not know anything. But the moss grows where it grows because of the thing — the eastern wall, where the thing is, is slightly warmer and slightly damper than the rest of the basin, and the moss has colonized the areas above and around the thing's presence in a pattern that, if you looked at it from above, would seem to outline something. The moss does not benefit from the thing directly. It does not parasitize it or communicate with it or form any kind of relationship with it. But it is affected by it, in the way that all things in a shared space are affected by all other things in that space, and if you knew what to look for, you could read the moss's distribution as a map of the thing beneath the stone.

The ferns do not know. The ferns are too far away and too simple. The lichen on the stone blocks does not know either. The thing does not know the ferns or the lichen in return. They are all part of the same hollow, the same completed sentence, but they are words in that sentence, not the sentence itself. The sentence is the hollow. The thing is a letter in a word in the sentence — a letter that is buried in the paper, that has sunk below the surface of the page, that is still there but is no longer legible.

What lives in the hollow is a life that has almost stopped being life. It is the farthest edge of biology, the place where the living and the non-living are so close together that the distinction becomes a matter of opinion. It is not dead. It respires, after a fashion. It metabolizes, after a fashion. It maintains itself against entropy, which is the most basic definition of life. But it does not grow. It does not reproduce. It does not respond to stimuli in any way that a scientist would recognize as response. It simply continues, and the continuing is so slow and so quiet and so perfectly integrated with the continuing of the stone around it that it has become, for all practical purposes, indistinguishable from geology.

But it is not geology. It is alive. It is alive in the hollow, in the warm and damp and dim and quiet, and it will be alive tomorrow and the day after and the century after that, and it will not change and it will not grow and it will not die, not for a very long time, not until something changes in the hollow — until the ridge collapses or the climate shifts or the water stops falling — and the thing is forced to become something other than what it is. Until then, it is what it is. It is what lives here. It is the answer to the question, and the answer is so close to the question that they are almost the same word, and the word is *persistence*, and the word is *sufficiency*, and the word is *still*, and the word is *here*.
