# What Lives Here

The first answer is the easiest and the hardest: *the place itself lives there*. The Floraderm is not a habitat. It is a resident. It is the resident — the only permanent one, the only one whose tenure is measured in the same units as the architecture. When you ask what lives in a house, you do not usually answer "the house," but here the house is tissue and the tissue is the house and the thing that built the house is the thing that lives in it and there is no separating the verb from the noun. It lives. It is alive. It processes. It grows. It breathes without lungs and pumps without a heart and thinks without — well. That is the question, isn't it. Whether it thinks. Whether the drainage gratings and the column-factories and the fold-seams between districts constitute a form of cognition or merely a form of metabolism so elaborate it has become indistinguishable from industry. You cannot tell from the outside. You may not be able to tell from the inside. The Floraderm does not answer questions because it may not hear them, or it may hear them and not care, or it may hear them and be unable to distinguish them from the thousand other small pressures that arrive at its surface — your footsteps, the grazer's mouth, the slow tidal push of secondary growth — all of it equally minor, equally brief, equally beside the point of whatever it is doing with its deep fluids and its continental processes and its folded space.

But this is unsatisfying. You want to know what *else* lives here. What moves. What eats. What dies in a place that does not want animal life.

## The Grazers

There is more than one kind. You can tell from the marks.

The crescent cuts in the downy secondary growth — those are the most common evidence, and they come in at least three sizes. The smallest is a crescent perhaps two centimeters across, a delicate bite, the edges clean, almost surgical. Whatever made this bite has a mouth that is precise and small and likely toothless — a rasping structure, perhaps, or a hardened plate that shears rather than tears. The grazing marks at this size appear in clusters of four to eight, always in a line, always following the contour of a furrow. The animal — you begin to think of it as something like a caterpillar, though that is an Earth-word and a wrong one — moves along the furrows and clips the secondary growth as it goes, leaving a darkened, oxidized trail behind it. It does not graze randomly. It selects. Some furrows are untouched, others are cropped to the primary surface. There is a preference. The animal can tell the difference between growths that you cannot tell apart, and it eats the ones it prefers and leaves the rest, and this is a kind of intelligence, or at least a kind of discernment, and it is enough to say that the animal has a relationship with the Floraderm that is more than mechanical.

This smallest grazer — call it the *threadclipper*, because that is what it does — is the one you are most likely to see, though you may not recognize it at first. It is the color of the secondary growth it feeds on, which is to say it is white in white districts, amber in amber districts, rose in the rose districts, and it is small and slow and low to the surface and it does not move when you approach because it does not perceive you as a threat, or it does not perceive you at all, or it perceives you and has learned that you are irrelevant. It has no eyes that you can find. It has a body that is segmented, perhaps twelve segments, each one a soft ovoid covered in the same downy filaments as the growth it eats — not camouflage, or not only camouflage; the filaments are the same because the filaments *are* the same, the animal growing on its surface a miniature version of the Floraderm's secondary growth, a kind of portable garden that feeds it and hides it and possibly something else, something symbiotic, the animal carrying the plant and the plant carrying the animal and neither one fully separate from the other. When it feeds, it presses its ventral surface against the growth and rasps — you can hear it if you are close enough and if you hold your breath — a sound like fine sandpaper on soft wood, a dry whispering rasp, and the filaments it clips curl inward and fall and oxidize and within a day they are dark brown and within two days they are gone, absorbed back into the substrate. Nothing is wasted here. The threadclipper's waste, such as it is, goes directly back into the furrows, liquid and dark, and the substrate takes it and the cycle continues.

The medium-sized grazer leaves crescents of four to six centimeters. Deeper bites. These ones take the secondary growth and a layer of the primary surface beneath it — not much, a millimeter, perhaps two — and the cut surface oxidizes to a deeper brown, almost black, and weeps the dark solvent for days. These bites are less frequent and more spatially clustered: you find them in groups of two or three, always near a column, always on the side facing the drainage groove. The animal that makes them is larger, and it is not harmless, and it does not stay on the surface. The burrows — the fist-wide holes punched straight down into the substrate — these are its work. You never see it. You have its burrows, its bites, and one other piece of evidence: a shed covering, found at the lip of a burrow in an amber district, a thin chitinous plate the size of your palm, curved, dark, with a fine ridge pattern on its inner surface that might be gill-structures or might be something else entirely. The plate is dry and rigid and dead, which means the animal shed it, which means the animal grows, which means the animal has a life cycle, which means it has been here long enough to evolve a life cycle, which means it is not a visitor. It is a resident. It belongs here, in the way that anything can belong in a place that does not want it — stubbornly, marginally, at the cost of constant dehydration and the perpetual effort of living in a medium that is not water.

Call it the *deepborer*. It is the closest thing this place has to a vertebrate, though it has no spine that you can verify. It is something between a grub and a lamprey and a creature that has not been named because the category for it has not been invented. It lives in the compressed layers below the surface, in the architecture of old growth, in the dark, in the warm, where the solvent pools and the substrate is soft enough to tunnel through. It comes to the surface to feed — climbing its own burrow, emerging at night (not that there is night, but there are dimmer periods, when the light-field above pulses down by ten or fifteen percent, and the deepborer has learned to read these, or its ancestors learned, and it is nocturnal by the standards of a world that has no sun but has rhythms) — and it clips the growth near the columns because the growth there is richer, fed by the solvent that runs down the column-channels, denser in whatever nutrient the deepborer needs. Then it descends. And you stand at the lip of its burrow and you drop a fragment of surface material and you count to four and you hear it hit and you understand that the world beneath you is deep and dark and inhabited and you have no access to it and the things that live there have no interest in you.

The third grazer — the largest — is the one you find evidence of and do not believe at first. The crescent marks are fifteen centimeters across. They are not in the secondary growth. They are in the primary surface itself — gouges in the woody tissue, deep, a centimeter or more, the edges clean but the wounds significant, the kind of damage that would take weeks of growth to heal. These marks are rare. You find them once every several kilometers of walking, always in transition zones between districts, always near seams. The animal that makes them is large — you can estimate from the bite width — perhaps a meter long, perhaps more, and it feeds on the Floraderm directly, on the structural tissue, on the living wood, which means it has a mouth that can shear lignified cellulose, which means it has jaws or plates of considerable hardness, which means it is not a soft thing, not a threadclipper, not a marginal resident clinging to the edges of an inhospitable world. It is a real animal, adapted fully, and it eats the place it lives in.

You never see it. You find its tracks once — not prints but *compression marks*, shallow depressions in the substrate where something heavy has rested, the size and rough shape of a human torso, the surface furred with secondary growth that has begun to grow back over the impression. It was here. It lay down. It rested. And then it got up and went somewhere, through a seam perhaps, into a district you have not walked, and it is there now, or it is somewhere else, and it is feeding and resting and feeding and it does not know you are on its world and it would not care if it did.

## The Browsers of the Columns

There is a separate category of animal that does not graze the surface at all. You find their evidence on the columns — the living machines, the factory-stations with their flat tops and drainage channels. On the bark of a column in the amber district, at a height of about a meter, you find a series of small circular holes, each the diameter of a pencil, drilled into the woody tissue at regular intervals. They are not random. They follow a spiral pattern up the column, each hole offset from the last by approximately thirty degrees, the spacing between them widening as they ascend. At the top of the column, the holes stop. The bark around the highest holes is dark and wet with solvent, and the holes themselves are filled with a pale, waxy substance that you do not recognize.

The animal that made these holes — call it the *spiralborer* — is a specialist. It does not eat the surface growth. It eats the column. Specifically, it eats the column's processing output — the dark solvent that flows up through the interior of the column and out through the drainage apertures. The holes are taps. The spiral pattern is a drilling strategy that follows the internal vascular channels of the column, each hole hitting a different feed-line, the wax plugs regulating the flow, the animal feeding on the solvent as it seeps out. It is a parasite of the factory system. It has evolved to exploit the infrastructure, and the infrastructure has not evolved to stop it, or has evolved to tolerate it, or has not noticed. The spiralborers are rare — you find evidence on perhaps one column in fifty — but they are present in every district you walk through, and the pattern of their holes is always the same, the same spiral, the same offset, the same widening spacing, which means the behavior is encoded, which means it has been encoded for a long time, which means the spiralborer has been here for generations, for centuries, drilling and tapping and feeding on the output of a factory that was old before the first human foot touched this surface.

You see one, once. On a column at the edge of a transition zone, where the geometry begins to flex. It is small — the size of your thumb — and it is the color of the dark solvent, glossy, almost wet-looking, and it is clinging to the bark with six legs that are not jointed in any way you recognize, each one a tapering fiber that adheres to the surface by some mechanism you cannot see. Its body is elongated, teardrop-shaped, and at its narrow end there is a structure like a drill-bit — a rotating, serrated plate that is currently embedded in the bark, chips of woody tissue falling in a slow fine stream. It is making a new hole. It is working. And the column is working around it, solvent flowing, the faint creak of adjustment, and the spiralborer is part of it now, a feature of the factory, a small machine that grew itself, and the Floraderm does not shake it off, and the spiralborer does not leave, and the arrangement is stable, and it has been stable for longer than your species has been writing.

## The Air-Things

There are things in the light-field. You see them only at the edges of vision, in the flattening wash of sourceless brightness, where the eye loses depth and the mind fills in phantoms. But they are not phantoms. You learn this when one drops.

It falls from the ceiling — or from the light, or from whatever layer up there emits the illumination — and it lands on the surface twenty meters from you with a sound like a damp cloth hitting a table, and it is the size of your hand and it is flat and translucent and it has no features you can identify as a head or a mouth or limbs. It is a membrane. A living membrane, thin enough that you can see the dark solvent moving through it in branching channels — its own vascular system, its own fluid architecture — and it is alive and it is on the ground and it does not move and you watch it for three minutes and then it lifts. It does not fly. It does not have wings. It rises — straight up, slowly, the membrane flexing, undulating, generating lift by some mechanism that has nothing to do with aerodynamics as you understand it — and it returns to the light-field and vanishes into the brightness and you do not see it again.

There are more. You catch them at the periphery — flat shapes, drifting, their translucence making them nearly invisible against the white sky, their presence detectable only as a slight wavering, a ripple in the uniform field. They are not numerous. Perhaps one per square kilometer, perhaps fewer. They live in the light. They live in the light the way the deepborer lives in the dark — adapted, resident, invisible to the surface-dweller. What they eat, what they do, how they reproduce — you do not know. You have one data point: a membrane, fallen, risen. You have a hypothesis: they feed on the light itself, or on the photic field, or on something the field produces — a byproduct, a particle, a nutrient that the ceiling generates as it generates illumination. They are the ceiling's grazers. The threadclippers of the sky. And the Floraderm does not mind them, or does not notice them, or has made a place for them in its design, and they drift, and they feed, and they fall sometimes, and they rise again.

## The Things That Are Not Animals

There is a category of resident here that defies the word *animal* and also defies the word *plant* and also defies the word *machine*, and you find it in the transition zones, in the seams, in the places where the geometry flexes and the physics bends and the rules change.

They are not the Floraderm. They are not columns. They are not secondary growth. They are something the Floraderm has grown that is not part of its own body in the same way the columns are part of its body. They are — you struggle for the word — *organelles*. Commensal structures. Things that live in the Floraderm the way mitochondria live in cells, incorporated, semi-autonomous, possibly once separate and now fused.

You find the first one in a transition zone where the floor is tilting toward the horizontal and the columns are beginning to lean. It is embedded in the wall of a furrow — a nodule, fist-sized, dark, smoother than the surrounding tissue, with a surface that is not woody but leathery, slightly yielding to the touch. It is warm. Warmer than the substrate. And it is vibrating — not with the deep slow pulse of the Floraderm's metabolic rhythm but with a faster frequency, a buzz, a hum that you can feel through your fingertips. You press your ear to it. You hear — and you do not believe this, and you write it down and you do not believe it — you hear *sound*. Not the sounds of the Floraderm. Not the creak of columns or the pulse of deep pumps. You hear structured sound. Patterned sound. A series of tones, short and long, in a sequence that repeats with variations, and the variations are not random, and the sequence is not language, or it is not any language you know, but it is *information*. Something is being said. Something is being transmitted. And the nodule is the speaker, or the nodule is the relay, and the thing speaking is the Floraderm, or the thing speaking is the nodule, or the thing speaking is something else entirely that lives in the Floraderm the way the spiralborer lives in the columns, embedded, resident, older than the oldest furrow.

You find more of them. In every transition zone, embedded in the walls or the floor, sometimes in the columns themselves, always warm, always vibrating, always humming with that patterned sequence of tones. They are networks. They are connected — you can feel it, the way you can feel that the columns are connected, the way you can feel that the drainage systems are connected. The Floraderm has grown a communications system, or a communications system has grown in the Floraderm, and the nodules are its nodes, and the tones are its traffic, and whatever is being communicated is being communicated across the seams, across the folded districts, across the non-Euclidean architecture of a world that uses bent space as an organizational principle.

You do not know what lives in the nodules. You do not know if anything lives in the nodules. You know that they hum and that they are warm and that they are part of the Floraderm and that they are also, somehow, their own thing, and that the distinction between resident and host and parasite and organ has broken down entirely here, and that the word *lives* has become a door that opens onto a room much larger than the one you were standing in.

## What Does Not Live Here

You. You do not live here. You are drying out. Your lips are cracked and your eyes burn and the solvent in the air — the terpenes, the astringent vapor — is accumulating in your respiratory tract in ways that will become medical if you stay much longer. You are the anomaly. You are the thing that does not fit. The threadclipper fits because it carries its own moisture on its back, a garden of filaments that holds the solvent in its tissues and releases it slowly. The deepborer fits because it lives below the surface where the solvent is abundant and the air is saturated with vapor. The spiralborer fits because it drinks from the columns. The membrane-things fit because they live in the light, where the physics of moisture is irrelevant. The nodules fit because they are made of the same thing as the Floraderm and the question of fit does not apply.

You fit nowhere. You are a terrestrial animal in a world that has no terrestrial niche. You are standing on the skin of a thing that does not know you and does not need you and will process your body when you die with the same slow efficiency it processes everything — breaking you down, absorbing your water (and your body is mostly water, and the Floraderm has no use for water, but it will take it anyway, or it will leave it to evaporate in the thin dry air, a small wet stain on a vast dry surface, gone in an hour), incorporating your carbon, your calcium, your iron, into its columns and its furrows and its drainage gratings. You will become part of the factory. You will be processed. And the threadclippers will graze over the place where you fell, and the deepborers will tunnel beneath it, and the nodules will hum, and the light will press down, and nothing will remember that you were here.

This is what lives here: a world that eats its residents and its visitors with equal indifference, and residents that have learned to live inside the mouth.
