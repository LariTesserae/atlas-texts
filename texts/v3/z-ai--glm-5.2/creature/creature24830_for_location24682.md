# The Keepers of the Condensary

## First Observation

You would not notice them immediately.

This is not because they hide. They do not hide. It is because the haze that swallows detail beyond forty meters swallows them too, and because they move at the speed the place moves — which is to say, deliberately, without urgency, in the manner of things that have nowhere else to be. And because, after even a short time in the basin, the eye learns to attend to the engine-pulse and the drip of condensate and the flicker of birds, and the human shape — standing still, grey-brown against grey-brown — becomes simply another post in the landscape.

There are not many of them. Seven, perhaps, or nine. The number varies in the way that the water level in the pools varies — not at all, except when you are not looking, and then you realize it has changed, and you cannot say when.

## Bodies

They are not tall. This is the first thing that becomes clear when you are close enough to see one in full. The men and women — if those words still apply here, and they do, roughly, in the way that a word for a thing in one language gets borrowed into another and slowly comes to mean something slightly different — are short in a way that does not seem genetic but seems *compacted*, as if they have been pressed down by the same forces that pressed up the basin. Not stooped. Not crouching. Just dense, close to the ground, as though they have taken on the quality of the silt itself, which gives underfoot and holds.

Their skin has the color of the place. Not brown, exactly, and not the grey of the silt, but something between — a warm, wet, clay color that in the flat light reads as neither light nor dark. They are not dirty. Dirt implies something foreign adhering to a surface. Their skin has simply arrived at the color the environment requires, the way the pale crustaceans in the pools have arrived at paleness. You would not call it a tan. You would not call it weathering. It is the color a person becomes when they have been in a hot, wet, dim place for a long time and the place has stopped distinguishing between itself and them.

Their hands are what you notice. The hands are large — not long-fingered, but broad, padded, the palms thickened in specific patterns that correspond to specific tasks: the gripping of pipe, the turning of valves, the lifting of brick. These are not calluses in the usual sense — the built-up armor of skin against repeated friction. They are more like the worn places on a stone where water has run for a long time. The hands have been shaped by what they hold. They are, in a sense, still holding it, even when empty.

Their faces are difficult. Not because they are deformed or strange, but because they are *finished* in a way that faces rarely are. Most faces are in process — aging, settling, sagging toward some final shape they will not reach before death. These faces have stopped. The faces you see on the woman lifting a grate near the cistern, on the man standing in the doorway of the engine room with his hand on the frame — these are faces that have arrived. They are not young. They are not old. They have the specific completeness of a face that has been doing the same things, in the same light, in the same air, for a very long time, and that has nothing left to become.

One of them — the one you see most often, the one who seems to be near the engines more than the others — has a mark along the left side of his jaw, a pale seam of scar tissue, thin and precise, as if something hot and narrow was pressed there once and the skin grew back without quite closing. It is not a wound. It is old, fully healed, permanent in the way that everything here is permanent. It does not pain him. It is simply part of his face now, the way the rust on the pipes is part of the pipes.

## Clothing

They wear what the place provides, or what they have made from what the place provides, which is not much. The clothing — you would not call it garments, exactly, and you would not call it rags — is a compromise between the heat, the wet, and the need to carry things.

The base layer, when you can see it clearly, is a kind of wrap — wide fabric, perhaps cotton once, perhaps something coarser, wound around the torso and the lower body in overlapping layers that stay in place not by fastening but by the simple friction of wet cloth against wet cloth. The wraps are the color of the silt. They were not that color originally. They have become that color through the same process that has made the bricks silt-colored and the pipes rust-brown and the water grey — the slow, complete saturation of everything by everything.

Over this, some of them wear pieces of what must have been heavier clothing — a vest, a jacket cut down to the torso, something with pockets — that has been so thoroughly wetted and dried and wetted again that it has taken on a permanent stiffness, a kind of carapace. These pieces do not move the way cloth moves. They move the way leather moves, or bark. They have been shaped by use into specific postures: the left pocket sags, the right collar stands. The clothing remembers what it has been asked to do.

None of them wear shoes. The silt is soft and warm. The ground gives. The water seeps up between the toes. Bare feet have the same quality as bare hands here — shaped by the ground, padded, wide, the toes splayed for purchase in the wet sediment. The footprints they leave in the silt between the buildings are the only tracks you see near the Condensary, aside from the thin furrows of the crustaceans in the pools, and the prints fill with water almost as soon as they are made and lose their edges and become small pools themselves.

## What They Do

They keep the Condensary running.

This is a description that sounds more active than it is. The Condensary runs. It has always run. It will always run. The engines do not need stoking — they are driven by something in the lower levels that no one has adequately explained, some process involving heat exchange and pressure differential and the particular properties of the brackish water that seeps up through the silt. The pipes do not need cleaning — the condensate is pure, or pure enough, and the flow is constant. The cisterns do not need repair — the brick is sound, the mortar is sound, the iron bands that reinforce them are sound.

And yet.

There are things to be done, and they do them, and the things they do are the reason the place is what it is, or are perhaps merely coincident with it. It is impossible to tell.

The woman you have been thinking of as the cistern-keeper (because she is often near the cisterns, and because her hands have the particular roughness that comes from gripping brick) moves through the complex with a purpose that is not urgency but is not leisure either. She checks the collection troughs. She runs her fingers along the iron pipes where they join the cistern walls, feeling — for what? For warmth? For vibration? For the particular frequency of the engine-pulse transmitted through metal and water? She pauses at one junction, presses her palm flat against the pipe, holds it there for the duration of three engine-cycles — twelve seconds — and then moves on. She has not found anything. She was not looking for anything. She is performing the act of attention, and the act of attention is part of the system.

The man near the engines — the one with the scar — does something similar in the basement levels. The engines are below the waterline of the basin, in a chamber that is part cellar and part cave, where the silt gives way to the older substrate beneath, a dark, dense clay that holds its shape. The chamber is louder than the surface — the engine-pulse is not muffled here but raw, a physical concussion that you feel in your chest and your teeth. He moves among the machinery with the same deliberate pace. His hand rests on a housing. His ear — he is listening, clearly, though what he hears apart from the engine's own breath is impossible to say — turns toward a particular component, a compressor, a joint. He adjusts something. The adjustment is minute — a quarter-turn of a valve, a slight shift in the position of a baffle — and it changes nothing that you can perceive. The engine-cycle does not speed up. It does not slow down. The water continues to condense. But he has made the adjustment, and the system continues, and perhaps the adjustment was the reason it continues, or perhaps the adjustment was a ritual, or perhaps there is no difference between a ritual and a maintenance procedure in a place where nothing has changed in living memory.

There are others whose tasks are less clear. A younger one — you think younger, though the evidence is ambiguous — sits on a low ridge of silt between two of the buildings, facing the pools, and watches the shrimp-clouds move. She has a shallow bowl in her lap, and in the bowl is water, and in the water are a handful of the small translucent shrimp, swimming in the confined space with the same patternless urgency they have in the pools. She is not studying them. She is not harvesting them. She is sitting with them. After a time — a long time, measured in engine-cycles, perhaps a hundred of them — she tips the bowl back into the pool and the shrimp rejoin the swarm and she stands and walks back into the complex, and you realize she has been checking something, or calibrating something, or doing something that has no name and needs none.

One of them — the oldest, perhaps, the one whose face has arrived the most completely — does very little. He is often in the doorways. He stands in doorways the way the birds fly circuits: not going anywhere, not resting, but occupying the threshold between inside and outside with a particular quality of attention. He watches the pools. He watches the silt. He watches, sometimes, the younger one with the bowl. When the engine pulses, he does not flinch. When the birds pass overhead, he does not look up. He is the closest thing the Condensary has to a center, and his activity is presence.

## What They Eat

The shrimp. The pale crustaceans. The small dark birds, occasionally, though this seems to be rarer — perhaps because the birds are harder to catch, or because there is a taboo, or because the relationship between the keepers and the birds is not predatory but something else.

There is a room in the complex — you find it eventually, a small room off the main corridor, with a brick floor stained dark and a low table and the remains of a fire, or rather of heat — no smoke, no ash, just a circle of charred brick where something very hot was placed, repeatedly, for a very long time. This is where they cook, or prepare, or process. There are shallow clay bowls — the same kind the younger one used for the shrimp — stacked in corners. There are shells. The shells of the pale crustaceans, cleaned and dried and stacked in a neat pile against one wall, as if they might be useful, or as if the act of stacking them is itself the use.

The food is simple in the way the place is simple — not impoverished, not bare, but reduced to its elements. Protein from the pools. Water from the condensers. Nothing else. Nothing has been traded for, or grown, or imported. The diet is as closed as the ecosystem, as the engine cycle, as the light.

They eat when they eat. There is no mealtime. There is no communal gathering around the charred-brick circle. Each of them eats when the task they are doing brings them near the room, or when the body signals — in whatever language the body speaks here, in a place without diurnal rhythm — that it is time. They eat alone, or in pairs, standing, or sitting on the silt. The eating is not sad. It is not joyous. It is eating. It is the input side of the equilibrium.

## How They Speak

They speak to each other, but you will not hear it from more than a few meters away.

Their voices are low — not quiet, but low, pitched in the bass register, close to the engine's frequency, as if they have learned to speak *under* the machine rather than over it. The words are indistinct. Not because the language is unfamiliar — though it may be — but because the consonants are softened by the humidity and the vowels are rounded by the heat, and the whole of speech here has the quality of the water: beaded, trickling, finding its level.

They do not speak much. Not from silence or reticence in the way you might expect — not the grim quiet of people enduring something — but because speech, in a place where nothing changes and nothing needs to be negotiated, has been reduced to its function. They speak to indicate. To point. To name a thing that needs attention in the next moment. A word between the cistern-keeper and the engine-man: a single syllable, accompanied by a gesture toward a pipe. A response: a nod. The conversation is complete. It lasted two seconds. It carried as much meaning as it needed to carry.

There are longer conversations. You witness one, or part of one, between the oldest one and the one who watches the shrimp. They sit in the doorway of the brick building that faces the largest pool. They speak in low tones for what might be a hundred engine-cycles — four hundred seconds, six and a half minutes. You cannot make out the words. Their faces do not change expression. Their hands do not gesture. When it is over, they both stand and go to their tasks, and nothing has changed, and you cannot tell what was said, and you have the sense that whatever was said was not about information but about something else — confirmation, perhaps, or the simple act of two people occupying the same air and producing the same sounds and hearing each other, which is, when you think about it, what the engine does with the pipes and the pipes do with the water and the water does with the air.

It is the place's version of conversation.

## What They Are

Here is the difficulty.

You want to call them workers. They work. They maintain the Condensary. They eat the shrimp. They live in the buildings — there are rooms with sleeping-mats, low pallets of compressed silt-brick covered in the same wrapping-fabric, positioned near the warmest walls, near the pipes, where the condensation keeps the air closest to breathing. They sleep. They wake. They do their tasks. They eat. They sleep.

But workers implies a wage, or an obligation, or a purpose outside the work itself, and they do not seem to have any of these. They do not keep the Condensary running for someone else. They do not keep it running for themselves. They keep it running the way the heart keeps beating — because it is what they do, and the doing is the reason, and the reason is the doing.

You want to call them inhabitants. But inhabitant implies arrival, settlement, the occupation of a place that was here before them. And it is not clear that the place was here before them. The basin surfaced less than a generation ago. The Condensary was built — by whom? — of brick fired from local silt. The shrimp were here when the sea floor surfaced. The crustaceans were here. The birds came after, or were already here, in the air above the sea. But the Condensary — the thing that defines the basin, that gives it its name and its pulse and its purpose — the Condensary was built, and someone built it, and the someone who built it, or the someone who came after, is the someone you are looking at now, or is their parent, or is them, in a time when the silt was softer and the brick was wetter and the engine had just begun to turn.

You want to call them natives. But native implies a people shaped by a place over generations, and this place has not had generations. This place has had *a* generation — one — and the people in it are of that generation, or the edge of it, and they are as complete as the place is complete, as finished, as arrived. They did not grow into this. They did not adapt. They were always this. Or the place was always them. The distinction between the keeper and the kept has dissolved in the heat and the wet and the time that does not pass.

The oldest one — the one in the doorways — if you asked him, if you could ask him, if there were a language in common and a reason to ask and a moment in the engine-cycle when the question would not be swallowed by the pulse — he might say: *We are here because the place is here.* Or he might say: *The place is here because we are here.* Or he might not answer at all, because the question assumes a separation that does not exist, a before and an after, a cause and an effect, and the Condensary does not deal in causes and effects. It deals in water. It deals in heat. It deals in the constant, circular, closed exchange of one for the other.

## The Youngest

There is one who is younger than the rest, and this is the strangest thing about them, because strangeness requires contrast, and the Condensary does not deal in contrast.

She — you think she, though the distinctions of body are soft here, and the clothing does not help — she moves differently. Not faster. The pace is the same. But her attention is *outward*, toward the edge of the basin, toward the haze, in a way the others' attention is not. The others attend to the Condensary: to the pipes, the cisterns, the pools, the engines. Their attention is inward, circular, closed. Hers is not.

She goes to the edge. Not often — not in the way of restlessness or dissatisfaction — but in the way of a task that is also a need. She walks to where the silt begins to rise toward the world beyond the basin, where the engine-pulse is still a throb in the ground but the haze begins, almost, to thin. She stands there. She looks outward. She does not go further. The ground changes at the edge — the silt gives way to older substrate, harder, drier, and she does not step onto it. She stands with her bare feet in the last of the silt, where it is still warm and still wet and still the Condensary's, and she looks at the world that is not the Condensary, and then she turns and walks back.

The others do not watch her go. They do not watch her return. This is not disapproval. It is not indifference. It is the recognition that what she does is part of the system — that her looking outward is as necessary, in its way, as the cistern-keeper's hand on the pipe, or the engine-man's adjustment of the valve. She is the Condensary's relation to what is outside it. She is the edge of the closed system, the place where it touches, or nearly touches, or fails to touch, the rest of the world.

If the Condensary has a future — if the bell ever stops ringing, if the top ever wobbles, if the equilibrium ever breaks — it will begin with her. Not because she will leave. She will not leave. She has the same completeness as the others, the same arrived-ness, the same color, the same hands. But her looking is the crack through which something could enter. The something has not entered. It may never enter. But she looks, and the looking is there, and it is the only thing in the basin that is not perfectly, perfectly closed.

## What They Are, Restated

They are the Condensary's way of being aware of itself.

Not its operators. Not its inhabitants. Not its prisoners, though a less generous reading might use that word, and it would not be entirely wrong — the place holds them, and they do not leave, and the leaving is not a possibility that has occurred to them, because possibility requires a sense of things being other than they are, and nothing here has ever been other than it is.

But if the engine is the heart, and the pipes are the veins, and the cisterns are the organs of storage and the pools are the organs of exchange and the shrimp and the crustaceans and the birds and the dark shape in the deep water are the living tissue of the periphery — then the people are the nerves. The awareness. The thing that feels the pipe and knows it is warm. The thing that watches the shrimp and knows they are there. The thing that stands in the doorway and knows that the basin is the basin, and that it is complete, and that it is good, or if not good then at least *finished*, which in a place without growth or decay or future or past is the same thing.

They are not many. Seven, or nine. They do not reproduce. There is no child in the basin except the youngest, and she is not a child — she is simply the one who looks outward, the newest nerve, the last tendril of awareness reaching toward the edge.

When the youngest is no longer the youngest — when another arrives, or when the looking outward becomes something else, something that steps onto the older substrate and walks — then the system will have changed, and the bell will have stopped ringing, and the Condensary will be something other than what it is.

But that has not happened. The engine pulses. The water beads on the pipes. The shrimp swarm and flinch and swarm. The birds fly their circuits. The dark shape moves in the deep pools. The cistern-keeper puts her hand on the pipe. The engine-man adjusts the valve. The youngest stands at the edge and looks outward and turns and walks back. The oldest stands in the doorway. The air is warm. The light is dim. The ground gives underfoot.

Four seconds. Four seconds. Four seconds.
