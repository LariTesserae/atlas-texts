# What Lives at Mossing

---

The first answer is the obvious one, and it's wrong, or at least it's incomplete: nothing lives here. Nothing that moves, nothing that breathes in the way you'd notice, nothing that startles or flees or announces itself. If you come looking for inhabitants you'll find only the systems — the vine tightening on the gear, the drip finding its channel, the moss compressing and releasing. You'll say: this place is empty. You'll be wrong, but you'll say it, because the word *empty* is what your body will tell you before your mind has time to correct it.

So let's start with what your body notices and work inward.

---

## The Beetles

There is a beetle. One species, as far as anyone has determined — though no one has determined very far, because no one has come here with specimen jars and a taxonomy and the patience required to sit in the damp for hours cataloguing the insects of a place most people avoid. But there is a beetle, and it is small, and it is black, and it lives in the moss-cushions.

It is not a remarkable beetle. If you held it up to the light you'd see a matte black shell, segmented legs that fold neatly against the body, antennae that are perhaps slightly longer than you'd expect for its size. A quarter-inch long, maybe less. It moves slowly, even for a beetle. You might see one on the surface of a moss-hummock in the late morning, after the condensation has burned off, tracing a wandering path across the green that leaves no visible trail. It will disappear into the moss within a minute, and you will not see where it went. The moss does not part for it the way water parts for a fish. The moss simply contains it, the way soil contains an earthworm — the beetle is within the structure, not on it.

What it does, as far as a careful observer could determine over many visits, is eat dead vine-material. The inner bark of the binding-vine, once it has decomposed past a certain point, softens into a fibrous pulp that the beetles can pull apart and process. They lay their eggs in the softer dead wood, in the gaps where a vine has died back and left a hollow. The larvae are pale, almost translucent, and they spend what appears to be a very long time — weeks, possibly months — in the wood, processing it, breaking it down further, leaving behind a fine dark material that is indistinguishable from the root-mat substrate. They are, in their small way, part of the mechanism. The decomposition cycle of the Works runs on fungal action and bacterial action primarily, but the beetles do something neither of those do — they create channels. The tunnels left by the larvae become the pathways through which water moves through dead wood, and the water carries the fungal spores deeper into the material, and the fungi follow, and the decomposition accelerates, and the cycle tightens.

There are not many of them. You could sit in the Works for an hour and see perhaps a dozen, if you were looking. They do not swarm. They do not congregate. Each one seems to exist in its own small territory, its own section of moss-cushion or dead vine, and they encounter each other rarely and without drama. They are the only creatures here that you could describe as a *population*. Everything else is a presence, or an individual, or an absence that used to be a presence.

If the Works had a consciousness — and they don't, but if they did — the beetles would be what its immune system looks like from the outside. Not defending against invasion but managing decay. The beetles decide how fast the dead parts return to the living parts. They set the pace. And the pace is slow.

## The Gnats

In the dampest parts of the Works, in the chambers where the condensation is heaviest and the root-mat is closest to standing water, there are gnats. They are nearly invisible — a drifting, barely-there cloud that you notice the way you notice dust motes in a sunbeam. They exist at the edge of perception. You feel them before you see them: a faint awareness of movement near your face, a barely audible hum that is more vibration than sound.

They don't bite. They don't land on you. They seem to have no interest in anything warm-blooded. They circle in the damp air, and their life cycle — which no one has observed in detail — appears to be tied to the standing water that collects in the concave sections of the corrugated metal overhead, the broken flags of the old roof. The water pools there, dark and still, and the gnats must breed in it, because when a pool dries up — which happens rarely, but it happens — the gnats in that chamber disappear within a day.

They serve no function that anyone can identify. They are not pollinators — the flowers here are wind-pollinated or self-fertile. They are not food for anything — there is nothing here that eats them. They are, as far as the system is concerned, unnecessary. And yet they persist. They are the one part of the Works that doesn't seem to belong to the loop, that doesn't seem to participate in the beautiful closed economy of growth and decay and regrowth.

This makes them, in a way, the most alive thing here. Everything else in the Works is function. The gnats are just gnats. They live because the water is there and they were born in it and they will die in it and the water will be there after them. They are the only inhabitants of Mossing that exist for no reason beyond their own existence. There is something almost moving about that, if you are the kind of person who is moved by small purposeless lives in large purposeful systems.

## The Spider

There is, usually, a spider.

Not always the same spider — or perhaps it is, and spiders live longer than you think, or perhaps the spider is a succession of spiders and you cannot tell them apart. But there is, almost always, in one particular chamber, a pale spider in a web.

The chamber is near the center of the Works, where the vine-lattice is thickest overhead and the light is greenest. The web is strung between a bracket fungus the size of a dinner plate and a vine-tendril the width of a forearm, and it is not a geometric web, not an orb web, but a sheet web — a irregular hammock of silk that sags in the middle and catches the condensation that drips from above. The spider sits at the edge, on the fungus, and it is almost the same color as the fungus, which is almost the same color as the wood it grows on, which is almost the same color as the shadow. You would not see it if you didn't know to look.

The spider is slow. Not the torpid slowness of cold, but the patience of something that has very few predators and very few prey and has calibrated its expectations accordingly. It catches the gnats, when it catches anything — the occasional gnat that drifts too close to the web and is taken with the mechanical indifference of a system that does not celebrate or mourn its catches. You might see this happen once, if you visit many times. The gnat is caught. The spider moves across the web with a deliberation that seems almost ceremonial. The gnat is wrapped. The spider returns to the fungus. The web shimmers, wet, and the condensation continues to bead on it, and the day continues.

The spider is the only predator in the Works. It is the apex. It has no competition and no threats and very little prey, and it survives on the margin between enough and not enough, which is where many things survive, though most things don't realize it. The spider realizes it, or behaves as if it does. It is the closest thing Mossing has to a resident who is aware of its own residence.

When a spider dies — and they do, though no one has witnessed the moment — the web persists for a while, catching condensation, looking like something is still maintaining it. Then it falls, or sags beyond recovery, and there is a gap in the chamber where it used to be. And then, after a time, a new web appears. This is either the same spider, or a new spider, or the Works growing a spider the way it grows a vine — because the space requires one, because the geometry of fungus and tendril demands to be connected by silk, because the chamber is not complete without the web.

You can see why people find this place unsettling.

## The Worn Paths

There are paths through the Works, as the description notes. Worn into the root-mat. Compressed by feet. The feet were human — the compression is the right spacing, the right depth, the right pattern for bipedal movement over time. Someone has walked here, repeatedly, enough to leave permanent tracks in a living floor.

How many? How often? The paths suggest a single walker, or at most two. There is one primary path that loops through the interior, connecting the larger chambers, passing by the most elaborate mechanisms, circling back on itself in a way that suggests either familiarity or ritual. There are smaller paths that branch off it — shorter, less defined, leading to specific spots: a place where the vine-lattice opens to the sky, a place where a particularly large gear still turns, a place near the spider's chamber where someone has clearly sat, many times, on a particular moss-cushion that is compressed into a shape that is not quite a seat but is no longer a hummock.

The paths are not new. They are not old, either. They are maintained — not by design but by use, the way a deer trail is maintained, by the simple physics of repeated passage. The root-mat grows up and the feet press it down and it grows up and the feet press it down and the equilibrium is a path, a permanent path, one that would disappear within a season if the walking stopped and would reappear within a season if the walking resumed.

So someone lives here, or near enough. Someone who walks the paths and sits on the moss and knows the spider's web and knows which mechanisms creak at which hour of the morning. Someone who has made this place, if not a home, then at least a habit.

## The Person

You would not call them a keeper. They don't keep anything. The Works don't need keeping. They would not call themselves a keeper, or a warden, or a guardian, or any of the words that imply a human standing between a place and its deterioration, because there is no deterioration here. The Works are fine. The Works have been fine for longer than there have been people to worry about fine.

What there is — what there seems to be, if you spend enough time in the nearest settlement asking the right questions in the right tone, which is the tone of someone who is curious but not alarmed, who wants to know but does not want to interfere — is a person who comes here.

A woman, probably, or an older person whose gender has become ambiguous in the way that some people's genders become ambiguous when they have lived past the point where the body's performance of itself is a priority. The descriptions from the settlement are not specific. They say: there's someone who goes to the hollow. They say: she's been doing it for years. They say: I think she lives out that way, but not in the hollow, not in it. Near it. They say: she's strange but not dangerous. They say: she doesn't talk much. They say: I haven't seen her in a while.

What she does, as near as anyone can piece together — and this is piecing, this is reconstruction from fragments and from the evidence in the Works themselves — is walk. She walks the paths. She sits in the places where the moss is compressed. She watches the mechanisms. She listens to the dripping and the creaking and the slow compression of the system as it cycles through its endless afternoon.

She does not maintain the Works. She does not prune the vines or clear the channels or adjust the mechanisms. She does not garden. She does not steward. She is not a caretaker in any sense that implies responsibility for the place's continued function. The Works would continue without her. The Works did continue without her, for however many centuries or millennia before she began coming.

What she does is witness.

She comes, and she sits, and she is present in the space, and the space is present with her, and there is a relationship between them that is not ownership and not stewardship and not worship but something else — something that has no clean word in the language of the settlement. The closest word might be *attention*. She pays attention to the Works. She pays attention the way you pay attention to a person who is talking slowly and doesn't need you to respond but needs you to hear them. The Works are not talking. The Works are not aware. But the Works are *occurring*, constantly, at a pace that requires a specific kind of patience to perceive, and she has that patience, and she gives it.

She has been coming for — the settlement says years, but the compressed moss suggests longer. Decades, maybe. The kind of time that changes the shape of a body, that settles someone into the particular posture of someone who has sat in one place very often. She is not young. She may not be old, exactly, but she is not young. She has the patient thinness of someone who walks long distances alone and eats what is available and has stopped expecting comfort to be a feature of her days.

If you found her — and you might, if you came at the right time, early in the morning when the condensation is heaviest and the mechanisms are most active — you would find her sitting on the moss-cushion near the spider's chamber, wrapped in something dark and damp, watching the gear turn. One tooth. Two. The afternoon passing. The light moving through the lattice and shifting across the floor and the vine growing and the water dripping and the beetle in the moss somewhere beneath her, doing its work, and the spider on its web, doing its work, and the gnats in the air, doing nothing, and the Works, the whole vast slow Works, doing what they do, which is continuing.

She would look at you. She would not be surprised. She would not be alarmed. She would assess you with the same slow attention she gives the mechanisms — not judging, not evaluating, just registering your presence in the space, noting how you displace the air, how your warmth changes the local temperature gradient, how your feet compress the root-mat. She would see you the way the Works see you, if the Works could see: as a transient. A fast-moving, fast-breathing, fast-burning thing that will be gone before the gear turns another tooth.

She would not speak, or she would speak very little. What she said would not be welcome or warning or explanation. It would be something small and observational. *The left channel is running heavier this morning.* Or: *The spider rebuilt.* Or nothing at all, just a slight shift of her body to make room, not because you're expected but because the space is large enough for two and she has the habit of not occupying more than she needs.

And you would sit, and you would listen, and you would feel what she feels, which is the Works operating around you and through you and despite you, and you would understand why she comes, which is that the Works are the only place she has ever been where nothing needs her and nothing wants her and nothing is changed by her presence, and that this is — for some people, for a very few people — not loneliness but its opposite. The opposite of loneliness is not company. It is the experience of being in a system that does not require you to be anything at all. The Works do not require her. They do not notice her. They accommodate her the way they accommodate a shift in temperature or a fallen branch — by incorporating her into their indifferent, beautiful, ongoing function.

She is not the soul of the place. The place has no soul. She is not its keeper. The place keeps itself. She is a person who has found the one place in the world where she is exactly as significant as she wants to be, which is not at all, and she stays near it, and she comes to it, and she sits, and the gear turns, and the water drips, and the spider rebuilds, and the Works continue.

## What Lives Here, Truly

The binding-vine lives here. The mosses live here. The bracket fungi and the liverworts and the lichens and the pale bell-flowers and the stamen-heavy blooms and the root-mat and the beetles and the gnats and the spider and the mechanisms that no one built and no one maintains and the woman who walks the paths and sits on the compressed moss.

And something else lives here, too, if *lives* is the word — something that is not any of these things but is all of them together, the emergent thing, the system-as-organism, the thing that is born of the interaction of vine and metal and water and time and that has no name and no boundary and no awareness but that *is*, in the way that a river is a thing though it is made only of moving water, in the way that a fire is a thing though it is made only of burning.

The Works at Mossing live here. The whole thing. The organism that is the place. The slow, patient, self-sustaining, self-reinforcing, undesigned, unimproved, unkillable loop of growth and mechanism and decay and regrowth and mechanism again. It does not think. It does not feel. It does not want. It does not need.

But it continues. And continuation, in the absence of everything else, might be what living is.

The gear turns. The water falls. The vine grows into the machine and the machine moves the vine and the vine grows again. The woman sits. The spider waits. The beetles process the dead. The gnats drift in the green light. The condensation forms and evaporates and forms again, every morning, every morning, every morning.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is going anywhere. The Works at Mossing are not a destination or a waypoint or a ruin or a wonder. They are a place that is complete, that has been complete for a long time, that will be complete for a long time to come. They are what you get when you leave things alone long enough for the leaving-alone to become the thing that shapes them.

They are home to themselves. And to the woman who comes. And to the beetles and the gnats and the spider and the moss and the vine and the water and the slow, slow turning of a gear that no one built for this purpose but that turns nonetheless, one tooth per hour, into a future that looks exactly like the present, which looked exactly like the past, which is enough. Which is more than enough. Which is everything.
