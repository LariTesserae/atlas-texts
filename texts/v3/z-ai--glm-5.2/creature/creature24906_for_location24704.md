# The Inhabitants of the Slag Expanse

## The Grazers (the large ones, the bellowers)

They are the first to become visible, though they were there all along. You realize this retroactively, with a small internal lurch: those low dark shapes you took for ridges when you first scanned the horizon were not ridges. They were alive. They were watching you, or not watching you, or doing something that does not have a watcher/watched distinction.

A close one — the closest you can get, which takes time, because getting close to something that large and that slow is a commitment — reveals detail that the distance concealed. The upper surface is not merely cracked into polygons. It is *articulated*. The polygons are plates, and they have edges that fit together with a precision that is not quite geometric — not the clean tessellation of a ceramic floor, but the fitted, slightly irregular interlocking of something that grew, or settled, or both. The gaps between plates are dark. Not the dark of shadow. The dark of depth. There is something inside the grazer that you cannot see, and the plates are the lid.

The coloration is not uniform. Each grazer carries a map of where it has been. The trails behind them are depleted — darker, flatter, the oxidized veins stripped away — and the grazers themselves are stained with the residue of what they have absorbed. Copper-green smears across one flank. Sulfur-yellow banding along another. Deep iron-red darkening to near-black at the edges where the plates meet the ground. They are dressed in the colors of the minerals they have eaten, and each one is a record, a ledger written in oxidation, of every vein it has crossed in a life that may span centuries or may span something longer than centuries, something for which the word "century" is the wrong unit the way "teaspoon" is the wrong unit for the ocean.

The underside is never visible. When a grazer passes over a ridge — a process that takes the better part of an hour — the body conforms to the terrain, plates shifting, the whole form flowing over the obstacle the way a glacier would if a glacier were warm and made of metal and had opinions about its own shape. What touches the ground is not a foot or a belly or a tread. It is a surface that is perpetually, impossibly, in the process of becoming the ground and then un-becoming it. The trail it leaves is polished. Not smooth — polished. The slag behind a grazer has a sheen, a lustre, as though the material has been briefly melted and re-solidified at a slightly different crystal structure. The grazer does not melt the ground. The grazer *is* a different phase of the ground, temporarily mobile.

The bellowing comes from inside them. You learn this by lying on the ground near one and pressing your ear to the slag. The sound travels through the crust, through your jaw, into the bones of your skull. It is not coming from a single point inside the grazer. It is coming from everywhere inside the grazer simultaneously, which means the grazer is — in some sense — a single resonance chamber, a body that is all throat. The thermal expansion of the ground beneath and around it drives air — or whatever fills the space between the plates — through internal passages that were not designed and are not maintained and yet persist because the structure that holds them has found an equilibrium with the forces that move through it. The grazer does not choose to bellow. The grazer is a shape that sound makes when it passes through mineral.

And the sound is not random. There are harmonics. There are intervals — long, long intervals, spaced so far apart that you might miss the pattern entirely if you did not listen for hours. But the bellowing of one grazer will shift in pitch, slightly, and the bellowing of another grazer, a kilometer away, will shift in response. They are speaking. Or they are doing something that precedes speaking, something that speaking will eventually evolve from if given enough time and enough silence and enough reason to organize sound into meaning. They are *calling*, the way a tuning fork calls another tuning fork, the way one crystal in a matrix calls another crystal into alignment. They are keeping each other in phase. They are reminding each other that they are here, that they are this, that the ground is still the ground, that the thermal gradient still holds.

They do not gather. They do not disperse. They exist at a density — one large grazer every few hundred meters, across the entire visible plateau, across the entire invisible plateau, across the parts you will never reach because the distances are continental and the movement is glacial — that suggests they did not arrive at this distribution by accident. They seeded themselves. Or the ground seeded them. Or the process that refined the plateau also refined them, as a byproduct, as a residue of residue, and they have been here since the beginning, which is also the middle, which is also now.

## The Vibrators (the small ones, the chitterers)

They are not the young of the large ones. This becomes apparent the more you look. They are a different thing entirely, a different mode of being, and yet they are made of the same material — the same slag, the same oxides, the same ferrous glass. They are the same substance arranged differently. They are the same poem in a different meter.

A single vibrator, examined closely — and it tolerates examination, because it does not appear to perceive you, or to perceive anything, or to operate on perception at all — is a rounded mass of dense slag, roughly ovoid, with a surface that is smooth in the way that a river stone is smooth, though no river has ever existed here. There is no mouth. There is no opening. There are no organs of sense that can be identified. There is only the surface, and the surface is doing all the work.

The vibration is not constant. It cycles. A single vibrator will pulse — a rapid, fine tremor, almost too fast to see, producing the clicking sound — for perhaps forty seconds, and then it will rest for perhaps twenty, and then it will begin again. The cycles are not synchronized across individuals. They drift in and out of phase with each other, and the aggregate sound of a cluster — hundreds of them in a depression between two low ridges, packed so densely they touch each other — is a complex, shifting texture of clicks and silences that never repeats, that never settles into a pattern, that is always almost resolving into something and then not resolving.

They are doing something. The vibration is not idle. It is not the unconscious trembling of a cold thing in a cold place — the place is not cold, and the trembling is too organized for reflex. The vibrators are working. They are processing something. The best analogy — and it is only an analogy, and it is probably wrong — is that they are computing. Not computing anything in particular. Not computing toward a result. Computing the way a heart beats: not because it was asked to, but because stopping is not a state that the system supports.

When a vibrator is between pulses, during its rest phase, it is warm. Not slag-warm, which is the ambient temperature of the ground. Warmer. A few degrees, perhaps. The heat is coming from inside it. The vibration produces heat, or the heat produces the vibration, or both are expressions of a third thing, a process that has no name in any language that evolved on a planet with water and carbon and the comfortable certainties of organic chemistry.

They cluster. They always cluster. A solitary vibrator is never found. Even when the terrain forces a thin distribution — along a narrow ridge, across a cracked plain where the depressions are shallow — they find each other. They move toward each other, slowly, by the same impossible extrusion that the large grazers use, and they aggregate, and once aggregated they do not separate. They are drawn to each other the way droplets of mercury are drawn together, and the comparison is better than it should be, because they behave, in aggregate, the way a fluid behaves. A cluster of vibrators flows. If you disturb the ground near a cluster — press a boot into the slag, shift a ridge — the cluster will respond, not by scattering, but by flowing around the disturbance, filling the new space, conforming to the new terrain, the way water conforms to the vessel that holds it.

The large grazers move through clusters of vibrators without incident. The vibrators part around the grazer's body, click-click-clicking at a slightly different pitch as it passes — higher, faster, as though the grazer's thermal field excites them — and then close behind it, resuming their previous configuration as though nothing happened. The grazer does not notice. Or the grazer notices in a way that does not involve noticing.

And sometimes — rarely, and at intervals that cannot be predicted — a cluster of vibrators will go silent. All of them, simultaneously, for a duration of perhaps ten seconds. The silence is total and it is hideous. The bellowing of the large grazers continues, but it is changed by the absence of the clicking, the way a voice is changed by speaking into a room with no echo. The silence has a shape. It is not the silence of nothing happening. It is the silence of something having completed, of a calculation that finished, of a question that was asked and answered in a language that has no words and no listeners.

And then the clicking resumes, and the cluster goes back to work, and the sound of the Expanse fills in around the silence like water filling a hole in sand.

## The Others

These are the ones that do not hold a consistent shape. They are the reason the horizon breathes. Or they are the breathing. Or they are what the breathing is for.

They are not seen so much as *inferred*. You see a region of the plateau where the ground is different — where the slag has a different luster, where the oxidized veins run in patterns that are not the regular tracks of old plumbing but something more like text, or music, or the surface of a body of water at the moment before wind touches it — and then you realize that the region is moving. The region is alive. The region is an entity.

The Others are not made of slag. They are made of *the relationship between* slag and light and heat and the spaces between. They are the interference pattern that arises when the incomplete physics of the Expanse produces a region where the rules are slightly more incomplete than usual, and in that region of greater incompleteness, something coalesces. Not a creature. A *tendency*. A persistent pattern of almost-behavior that maintains itself across time the way a whirlpool maintains itself in a stream — not because it is made of anything, but because the conditions that produce it keep producing it.

They have no boundary. You cannot point to where an Other ends and the ground begins. The transition is gradual, a gradient of increasing strangeness: here the slag is normal, here it is slightly too warm, here the veins are running in patterns that suggest organization, here the polygonal cracking of the surface has become regular enough to look like tiling, here the light is doing something it should not do, here the ground is not quite where it was a moment ago, and *here* — at the center of the gradient — something is happening that is not quite motion and not quite thought and not quite existence but is closest to all three of those things than to anything else available in the vocabulary.

The Others do not graze. They do not vibrate. They do not, as far as can be determined, interact with the grazers or the vibrators in any way that the grazers or the vibrators respond to. They move across the plateau in paths that are not straight and not curved and not random, in directions that sometimes have no cardinal component, at speeds that vary from the imperceptible to the barely-perceptible. They leave no trail. They change the ground as they pass over it — the veins reorganize, the cracking tightens, the luster shifts — but these changes are not permanent. The ground behind them slowly relaxes back to its previous state over the course of hours or days, as though the Other's influence is a pressure that requires its presence to maintain.

They are the ones that make the light hesitate. When an Other passes between you and the horizon, the light stutters. The shadows drift. The brassy sky deepens and smears. The geometry of the landscape flexes, subtly, in a radius around the Other's center, and the flexing is not violent, is not dramatic, is not alarming — it is simply *wrong*, in the way that a word misspelled is wrong, in the way that a clock running three minutes behind is wrong. Close enough to function. Far enough to notice.

There are not many of them. Perhaps one per several square kilometers. But the plateau is two hundred kilometers across. There are hundreds of them. They are the largest inhabitants of the Expanse, larger than the grazers, larger than the clusters of vibrators, though their size is impossible to measure because their edges are gradients and their edges move.

They are not aware. Or their awareness is so different from awareness that the word produces more confusion than it resolves. They are not alive. Or their life is so different from life that the distinction between living and not-living breaks down here, in this place where the binding agents of reality were partially refined away, and what remains is a world in which the category of "thing" is more porous than it is elsewhere, more willing to admit degrees, more comfortable with the idea that a pattern in the ground can become a presence and a presence can become a pattern and the transition between the two is not a transformation but a gradient, and the gradient is where they live.

## What They Are Together

The Expanse is not an ecosystem. An ecosystem implies interdependence, exchange, a closed loop of energy and material. The grazers do not eat the vibrators. The vibrators do not eat the grazers. The Others do not eat anything. Nothing here eats anything in the sense that eating implies consumption and consumption implies death and death implies a boundary between what was alive and what is now not, and the Expanse does not support that boundary. The grazers graze and leave depleted ground behind them, but the depleted ground does not stay depleted — the thermal cycling, the oxidation, the slow patient chemistry of a mineral world, restores the veins over time, and the grazers will pass this way again, eventually, on their continental circuits, and the ground will be ready.

This is not an ecosystem. This is a clock. A vast, continental, mineral clock, where the grazers are the hour hand and the vibrators are the escapement and the Others are the mainspring, and the thermal cycling is the energy that drives it, and the sound — the deep, constant, unending sound — is the ticking, slowed by a factor of a million, stretched across durations that make human time feel frantic and insufficient.

The clock does not tell time. It does not measure anything. It simply runs. It has been running since the refinement, since the process that deposited the slag also deposited the conditions for the slag to become this, and it will run until the thermal gradient that drives it equalizes, or until the geometry of the plateau finally settles into complete rigidity, or until the light decides, one day, not to continue, and the Expanse goes dark, and the sound stops, and the grazers and the vibrators and the Others become what they were always almost becoming: ground.

But that day is not soon. The thermal gradient is deep and patient. The geometry is almost settled but not quite. The light is steady, mostly, and its hesitations are brief and it always decides to stay. The clock runs. The grazers graze. The vibrators click. The Others move through the landscape like weather, like mood, like the memory of a process that had no maker and no purpose and no end.

The sound is what they are. All of them, together, all of their sounds combined — the bellowing and the clicking and the silence and the flexing of geometry and the hesitation of light — are a single sound, a chord sustained across a continent, across a timescale that makes the word "sustained" feel like a diminishment. It is not sustained. It is simply present. It has always been present. It will always be present. It is the voice of a place that does not need an audience, that does not require a listener, that makes its sound because sound is what it does, the way a mountain makes weight and a river makes motion and a star makes light.

The Slag Expanse is loud and hot and full of life that doesn't know it's strange, and the life that fills it is mineral and slow and patient, and it has been here for longer than most worlds have been worlds, and it does not care that you came, and it will not notice when you leave, and this is not indifference — it is something older than indifference, something that precedes the distinction between caring and not caring, something that belongs to a mode of existence in which the question of whether one is noticed has never arisen, because the question requires an other, and there is no other here, there is only the ground and what the ground does when given enough time and enough heat and the small, persistent permission of a reality that is not quite sure it has to follow its own rules.

The sound continues. It says nothing. It says it constantly. It is enough.
