# What Lives Here

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is not wrong: moss.

But saying *moss* is like pointing at a city and saying *people*. It is accurate and it is insufficient. The moss here is not one thing. It is not even one kind of thing. What lives on the walls of the Throat — what has lived here, quietly, for longer than the word *quietly* can carry — is a community, an ecosystem, a civilization of things that have no nervous systems and no need for one.

---

The largest colony is the one on the eastern wall, the hanging garden nearly two meters across. It is old. Not old the way a tree is old, with rings you can count and a history you can read in the grain. Old the way a hill is old. It has been growing in this spot since before the stones were stacked into a seat, and it was already substantial then. You can tell because the moss has grown *over* the chisel marks on the shelf above it — not into them, not colonizing the worked stone specifically, but simply continuing its slow lateral expansion the way it has for centuries, reaching the edge of the modified shelf and covering it the way snow covers a path: without intention, without avoidance, just *arriving*. The moss does not know the shelf is there. The moss does not know anything is anywhere. It is growing toward the light that does not reach it, because that is what it does, and it will continue doing this until something stops it, and nothing here will stop it.

If you press your hand into the center of this colony — and you would have to climb to reach it, and you should not, but if you did — your hand would sink in to the wrist. The moss is half a meter deep. The lower layers are not alive, not exactly. They are the compressed remains of their own ancestors — peat, really, the beginning of a soil that will never become soil because there is no ground here for it to become. The colony is building itself on itself on itself, a living crust atop its own dead, and the dead hold water, and the water feeds the living, and the cycle is so complete and so self-sustaining that you could remove this colony from the wall and set it in a dark, wet box anywhere on earth and it would continue this process for decades. It does not need the Throat. The Throat is simply where it happens to be.

But it is *of* the Throat, in ways that matter. The species here — and there are at least four, maybe more, tangled together in a mat so dense they cannot be separated without destroying the whole — are not species found in the forest above. The forest above is a lowland tropical system: hot, bright, loud with birds and insects and the constant aggressive competition of a biome where everything grows fast and dies young. The mosses on the eastern wall grow *slowly*. Unrecognizably slowly. A frond that would unfurl in three days in the surface forest takes six weeks here, in the cold, in the dark, in the perpetual wet. The cells are different. Smaller. Thicker-walled. Built for endurance rather than expansion. If you took a sample of this moss and planted it in the forest above, it would be overrun and consumed in a season. It would be unable to compete. But down here, in the cold and the dark and the sound, it has no competition. It is the competition. It has won a race that nothing else entered, and its prize is this wall, this water, this unbroken stretch of time.

The colony drips. It drips the way a living thing breathes — not because it chooses to, but because it is full, and being full means letting go of what it cannot hold. The water passes through it, is filtered by it, emerges from its lower edge slightly changed — slightly warmer, slightly mineral, carrying the faintest trace of the organic acids the moss produces as it grows. This water joins the lattice of streams on the wall below. The moss is, without knowing it, feeding the sound. Its drips are notes in the chord. Its presence alters the chemistry of the water that passes through it, and that altered water carves slightly different channels in the stone below, and those channels produce slightly different sounds, and the chord shifts, and the moss does not know and does not care and the sound does not care and the stone does not care, but the system holds. Everything holds everything else. Nothing is aware of this. It does not matter.

---

The ferns are different. The ferns are, in their way, ambitious.

They grow from the vertical cracks in the rock — the same cracks the water uses, the same fissures that channel the lattice downward. Their roots are not in soil. There is no soil. Their rhizomes have wedged themselves into the stone itself, into the hairline fractures, expanding slowly, imperceptibly, with a force that is geological rather than botanical. They are prying the rock apart. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But a fern on the northern wall, halfway up, has been working at the same crack for what may be two hundred years, and the crack is wider now than it was, and the fern is deeper in it, and eventually — in another century, or two, or five — the crack will be wide enough for the fern to be replaced by a different organism, or for the rock to calve away in a fragment the size of a hand, and the fragment will fall, and the fern will die, and the moss will colonize the new surface, and the water will find new channels, and the sound will shift again.

The ferns are the mortal ones. The mosses are, practically, immortal — they die back and regrow, layer on layer, but the colony persists. The ferns are individuals. They have a lifespan, a beginning and an end, a growth and a senescence. Each one is a life. A single life, lived in a crack in a wall, in the dark, in the sound, uncurling its fronds outward over the emptiness of the chamber like a hand reaching for something it has no senses to detect. They live thirty years, fifty, perhaps eighty if the crack is stable and the water is consistent and no fragment falls. Then they brown. Their fronds dry and curl. The rhizome, deep in the crack, goes dormant, and then it goes dead, and the crack holds its remains like a memory, and a new spore lands — drifted down on the slow breathing of the chamber's air, or washed in by the water — and germinates in the same crack, in the same dead matter of its predecessor, and begins again.

There are perhaps thirty ferns visible in the chamber at any given time. Some are young, their fronds barely unfurled, still tight croziers the size of a fingernail. Some are mature, their fronds reaching a meter out from the wall, trembling in the faint air currents. Some are dying, their tips going brown, their stems going soft. The population is stable. It has been stable for a very long time. Each fern replaces itself, approximately, over the span of its life. The system does not grow. It does not shrink. It maintains.

This is what lives here. Not individuals but a *holding pattern*. A community of organisms that has found its equilibrium and will remain in it until the conditions change, and the conditions do not change, because the conditions are stone and water and gravity and time, and these things do not change on the scale that ferns operate.

---

The liverworts are the ones nobody talks about, because nobody sees them. They are the mat on the floor — the dark, almost invisible film that covers the damp stone between the broken wooden posts. They are not impressive. They are not beautiful, or if they are, it is the beauty of a wet rock, which is a beauty that requires a particular kind of attention. They are a smear. A film. A staining on the stone that you might walk across without noticing, might kneel on without recognizing as alive.

But they are the oldest inhabitants. Not the oldest *colony* — the moss on the eastern wall may be older as a continuous growth. But the oldest *presence*. Liverworts are among the most ancient of land plants. Their ancestors were the first green things to leave water and learn to live on rock, and they have not changed much since, because they found a way of being that worked and they kept it. What is on the floor of the Throat is, essentially, what was on the floors of places like the Throat four hundred million years ago. Before ferns. Before mosses, even. Before the sound. Before the water had finished its work.

They do not mind the sound. They do not mind the cold. They do not mind the dark. They do not mind anything, because they do not have the equipment to mind. They exist on the floor of the chamber in a thin, wet, continuous mat, absorbing what moisture condenses on the stone, photosynthesizing what tiny fraction of light reaches them from the shaft far above — a fraction so small it is barely measurable, and yet enough, because liverworts have spent four hundred million years learning to live on almost nothing, and they are very, very good at it.

They are the thing that will be here last. If the crack above closes, and the light goes, and the ferns die, and the mosses slowly retreat, the liverworts will remain. They will persist on the floor in their thin wet film, living on chemical energy and the faintest memory of light, until the water stops, and the stone dries, and even they cannot hold. And then they will be gone, and the Throat will be an empty stone hollow with nothing living in it, and it will still be called the Throat, because names outlast the things they name.

But that is not today. That is not soon. The water is here. The liverworts are here.

---

And there is one more thing.

You will not see it. Almost nobody does. It is not hidden — it does not have the capacity to hide — but it is so small, and so still, and so patient, that it exists below the threshold of attention in a place where the sound and the dark and the cold are already pressing that threshold to its limit.

There is a creature. A single species of creature, because the conditions here select for exactly one kind of life and no other. It lives in the moss on the eastern wall, deep in the colony, in the zone between the living surface and the dead layers beneath. It is pale. It is soft-bodied. It is a gastropod — a slug, though that word is ugly and inaccurate for what this thing is and how it lives. It is perhaps two centimeters long, translucent, with internal organs visible through its skin like a ghost of itself. It has no eyes. It lost its eyes long ago, or rather, its ancestors lost theirs, and it was born into a world where eyes were a cost the body could not afford, and so it does not have them. It navigates by touch and by moisture gradient, moving through the moss the way a fish moves through water — not choosing a direction but following a condition, seeking wet, seeking the film of condensation that means life.

It eats the moss. Not destructively. Not fast enough to damage the colony. It grazes — a scrape here, a scrape there, moving slowly, digesting slowly, excreting slowly, returning the moss's own material to the base of the colony in a form the moss can reabsorb. It is a recycler. A single link in a food chain that is only one link long. The moss feeds it; it feeds the moss. Nothing feeds on it. There is nothing here that would. It is too small, too deep in the moss, too cold to the taste, too full of the compounds the moss produces to protect itself. It is safe. It has been safe for as long as the colony has existed, which is to say for longer than the stone seat, longer than the shelf, longer than the wooden posts at the bottom.

There are not many of them. Perhaps a dozen. Perhaps fewer. They do not reproduce quickly, because there is no need to reproduce quickly in a world where nothing preys on you and your food is everywhere and time is unlimited. They live — each one of them — for perhaps five years. They lay eggs in the deep layers of the moss, small clusters of translucent spheres that look like condensation and would be mistaken for condensation by anyone who saw them, which no one does. The eggs hatch in the dark. The young are smaller than a grain of rice. They have never seen light. They never will.

This is the only animal that lives in the Throat.

It does not hear the sound, or if it does, it does not know it. It has no ears. It has no brain in the sense that would make *hearing* a meaningful concept. It moves through the moss, and the moss vibrates with the polyphonic wash of water on stone, and the vibration passes through the slug's body, and the body does not interpret it, does not process it, does not attend to it. The sound is the texture of its world. It is the medium in which the slug exists, the way water is the medium in which a fish exists. The slug does not know it is in a cathedral of sound. The slug does not know it is in a cathedral.

---

And then there is the thing that is hardest to say, and hardest to say with any confidence, and yet.

Something lived here that was not moss, was not fern, was not liverwort, was not slug. Something that came down through the crack in the limestone, lowered itself through the ferns, stood on the floor, stacked stones into a seat, chipped handholds into the wall, built a platform, worked a shelf. Something with hands. Something that listened.

We do not know what it was.

The instinct is to say *human*, and it may have been. The stone tools — if they were stone tools — are consistent with human work. The dry-stone seat is consistent with human patience. The chisel marks are consistent with human hands. But *consistent with* is not *evidence of*, and in the Throat, the evidence has been consumed. The wood has fossilized. The bone, if there was bone, has dissolved in the acidic water. The stone tools, if they were left, are indistinguishable from the stone around them — the Throat is made of stone, and a stone tool set down on a stone floor in a place where water runs over everything becomes just another stone in a generation.

What we know: something with hands came here. It climbed down. It sat. It listened. It climbed up. It came back. It did this more than once — the handholds, the shelf, the platform, these are the evidence of return, of repetition, of a practice. Something came here on purpose. It came for the sound. It built the smallest possible architecture of survival — a seat, a shelf, a platform — not to live here, not permanently, but to *stay*. To stay long enough to hear the chord change. To sit in the deafening water-music and the cold and the dark and let it be what it was.

Was it human? Perhaps. The lowland forest above has been inhabited by humans for tens of thousands of years, and humans are a species that goes into caves, that seeks out sound, that builds seats in dark places and sits in them. Humans are a species that listens. This is documented. This is what we do.

But humans are also a species that leaves things. We leave refuse, we leave marks, we leave the debris of our presence in layers that archaeologists can read. The Throat has no layers. No middens. No charcoal. No bones. No dropped tools, no lost ornaments, no fragments of food. If a human lived here, they lived here *cleanly*, in a way that is almost unprecedented in the human archaeological record. They came, they sat, they listened, they left nothing but the seat and the handholds and the chisel marks.

Or they left other things, and the water took them. The water takes everything, given time. The Throat is wet. Everything organic that is not constantly maintained will decompose, dissolve, be carried down through the drainage crack into the deeper stone. A human could have lived here for years, and if they took their tools with them when they left — or if they died elsewhere, or if they simply stopped coming — the water would erase them in a decade. In a century, there would be nothing but the stone. And a century is nothing here. The moss does not notice a century. The water does not notice a century. The fern on the northern wall, the one prying apart the crack — it has been alive for two.

What lived here, then, was a listener. That is what we can say. Something that came for the sound and built the minimum necessary to stay in the sound and listen. Whether it was human or something else — something that lived in the forest before humans, something that found the crack and went down and sat and heard the chord change and came back up and came back again — we cannot know. The Throat keeps its secrets the way stone keeps its grain: by being stone, by not caring, by holding what it holds and nothing more.

---

What lives in the Throat now is what has always lived in the Throat, minus the listener, plus time.

The moss grows. The ferns unfurl and die and unfurl. The liverworts persist. The slug — the pale, eyeless, transparent slug — moves through the deep layers of the moss at a speed that would make a glacier look hasty, and it does not know it is the only animal in a cathedral of water-sound, and it does not know it is the inheritor of a place that something with hands once built a seat in, and it does not know anything, and it does not need to.

The sound continues. The chord shifts. The water finds its way down the stone through a thousand tiny channels, and the chamber collects the sound and holds it, and the sound fills the hollow the way the cold fills the hollow, the way the dark fills the hollow — completely, without gaps, without apology.

The listener is gone. The seat is empty. The water does not miss the listener. The moss does not miss the listener. The sound does not miss the listener. The sound was here first. The sound does not need an audience to be what it is.

But someone sat in that seat. Someone heard it. And the stone remembers the way stone remembers — not in any conscious sense, not with any sentiment, but physically, structurally. The seat is shaped to a body. The handholds are shaped to hands. The shelf is flat where it was not flat before. The Throat has been touched, and the touching remains, and the moss grows over it, and the water runs over it, and the sound covers it, and it remains.

What lives here: moss, ferns, liverworts, a slug, water, sound, cold, dark, and the shape of someone who is no longer there.
