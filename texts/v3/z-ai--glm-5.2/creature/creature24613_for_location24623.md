What lives here is the hollow itself.

That is not evasion. It is the closest accuracy I can find, and it took a while to arrive there, because the obvious answers kept failing.

No organism lives here. That needs to be said first, and plainly, the way the description says the stone is bare. No lichen, no microbial crust, no insect, no bird that nests in the alcoves. The condensation cycle does not support life in any quantity that life would recognize as its own. The water appears and withdraws too fast, carries nothing, deposits nothing. A creature that tried to live on the stone here would find the stone giving and taking moisture in a rhythm that has nothing to do with nourishment. The pools are mirrors, not wells. So: no creature lives here in the way that creatures live — metabolizing, reproducing, accumulating biomass, leaving traces of consumption. The hollow is not a habitat. It is a condition.

But the hollow itself — the system of stone, water, air, and thermal differential — functions in a way that is difficult to call anything other than alive, if you are willing to use that word loosely, which I think you have to be, in a place like this. It breathes. The daily condensation cycle is a respiration: inhale at night, when the stone cools and the moisture gathers on its surfaces; exhale by day, when the water withdraws back into the rock and the air goes thin and clear again. The rhythm is diurnal, tidal, utterly reliable. It has been running for longer than any human structure has stood. When you press your palm to the wall at dusk and feel the stone drawing inward, what you are feeling is a kind of breathing — not metaphorical breathing, not a poet's indulgence, but a real cyclical exchange between a solid and a gas, mediated by temperature, governed by physics, sustained by the particular geometry of this particular pinch in this particular ridge. The hollow is a lung, and it has been breathing the same breath, in and out, since before the cairns were built.

It lives in another sense: it maintains itself. The description says this clearly — the hollow is not changing toward anything, not declining from anything. It is *in* its condition. Every surface has reached a state of resolution that is neither new nor ruined but simply finished. This is what living things also do, when they are mature and stable and not in crisis: they maintain. They hold their form against entropy through the constant small work of metabolism. The hollow does this through the condensation cycle, through the thermal adjustments, through the micro-fracturing that is felt as vibration. It is not waiting for anything. It is doing the work of being itself, which is ongoing, which is daily, which is the definition of a life that has found its scale and its pace and does not need to be anything other than what it is.

What else lives here is the water.

Not the water as a substance — water is not alive — but the water as a *process*, as the specific intermittent cycling that happens in this hollow and, as far as anyone can tell, only in this hollow. The description calls the condensation cycle a closed loop: stone to air to stone. The water that appears on the walls at night is, in large part, the same water that evaporated from the same walls that morning. It does not leave. It does not arrive from elsewhere. It cycles. This is a life, of a kind. Not biological, not even chemical in the sense of reactions and transformations — the water does not transform, it simply moves, phase to phase, surface to surface. But it has a rhythm and a persistence and a specificity to it that feels like the minimum viable definition of habitation: something is here, doing what it does, repeatedly, without external input, and has been doing it long enough that the doing has become the place itself.

The water is the hollow's blood, if you want. Or its lymph. It moves through the stone's pore spaces and microfractures the way fluid moves through tissue. It emerges at night and retreats by day. It collects in the low catchments and looks at the sky. It makes the sound — the tap, the hesitation, the irregular percussion — that is the hollow's only voice. Without the water, the hollow would be silent, and the silence would be absolute, and the place would be a sealed mineral chamber with nothing to do and no way to mark time. The water gives it time. The dripping is a clock, though not a useful one — it does not measure seconds or minutes, it measures the specific geometry of overhangs and drip-points and the variable surface tension of schist. It is a clock that tells you only about itself.

And someone lives here. Or near here. Or comes here, which in a place this bare, this without accumulation, is the same thing.

The builder of the cairns. The person who fitted the dry wall in the eastern alcove, who swept the floor, who wore the shallow depression into the stone with the patience of a body returning to the same position over many nights, many seasons, long enough to grind down metamorphic rock by a fraction of a millimeter. This person lives here in the way that the hollow allows: intermittently, without leaving residue, without accumulating. Whatever they bring in their hand, they take out in the same hand. They leave only modifications to the stone, and the modifications participate in the same cycles as the natural rock — eroded, condensated upon, thermally stressed — so that over time the distinction between what was built and what was here before softens, and the cairns and the wall become less like artifacts and more like expressions of the place itself, the way a callus is an expression of the skin that grew it.

Who is this person? I let the description tell me, and what it tells me is: someone who understands stone. Not a geologist, not necessarily — someone who has spent enough time with rock, handling it, fitting it, reading its flatness and its grain, that the material has become a language they speak without thinking about it. The dry wall is simple but not careless. The cairns are balanced with an elegance that requires only limited materials and unlimited time. This is the work of patience, not skill — or rather, patience *is* the skill, and it has been practiced long enough that the results look effortless, which is what expertise always looks like when it has reached the stage where it no longer needs to perform.

This person comes to the hollow and stays through the cycle. They know the condensation. They know when the dripping starts, which alcoves go dark first, which pools last longest into the morning. They have built their sleeping hollow in the eastern alcove for a reason — perhaps the western wall blocks the wind that doesn't reach here anyway, or perhaps the alcove's orientation gives them a particular view of the sky through the open top, or perhaps the reason is mineral, something to do with the way that specific intersection of shelf-edges channels condensation, and the alcove stays drier than other spots. Whatever the reason, it is a reason that has been earned by repeated presence. They know this place the way you know a room you have slept in for years — by the quality of its silence at different hours, by where the light falls and when, by the sound the walls make as they cool.

They do not seem to live here in the sense of dwelling, of making a home. There is no hearth, no midden, no accumulation of belongings. But they live here in the sense that matters for a place like this: they return. They come back. They have been coming back long enough to wear stone down with their body. And when they leave, they take everything with them, and the hollow forgets them within hours, and this is acceptable to them — perhaps it is the point. Perhaps what they come here *for* is precisely this: a place that will not hold them, that will not accumulate, that will accept their presence and then erase it completely and without resentment. A place that holds only itself.

If I had to say what kind of person this is — and I am not sure I do have to, but the description pushes toward it — I would say: someone who does not need to be remembered. Someone for whom the act of being in a place is sufficient, and the leaving of traces is not necessary, and the building of cairns is not communication but something closer to the stone's own process — a small adjustment, a making, done for the sake of the making, in a place where making is the only verb that the mineral world understands.

---

So what lives here:

A lung of stone that breathes water.
A closed loop that has been cycling since before memory.
A person who comes and goes and builds and is forgotten and comes back anyway.

And none of these is the right answer, and all of them are. The hollow does not distinguish between its geological respiration and its human visitor. It treats the cairns the same as the natural stone. It absorbs the builder's breath and the builder's warmth and erases them with the same indifferent thoroughness it applies to everything. This is not cruelty. It is the nature of a place that has achieved equilibrium — it does not discriminate, because discrimination requires contingency, and the hollow has no contingency left. It is resolved. It holds only what it is.

What lives here is what has always lived here: the stone, doing what stone does, at this altitude, in this light, with this water, for this long. And occasionally, briefly, a person — patient, quiet, skilled with dry fitting — who enters the cycle and exits it and is part of it for as long as they stay and no longer. They are not separate from the hollow's life. They are one of the things the hollow does, the way condensation is one of the things it does, and the cairns are one of the things it does, and the flashing of mica in brief sunlight is one of the things it does. The builder is a feature of the place, not a resident. The place does not have residents. It has processes, and the person is one of them, intermittent, untraceable, and as much a part of the stone's honesty as the garnet knots the color of old blood.
