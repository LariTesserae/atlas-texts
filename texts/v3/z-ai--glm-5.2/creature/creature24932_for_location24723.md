# What Lives on the Terraces

---

You do not see them at first. This is not because they are hidden, though they are. It is because you are looking for the wrong thing. You are looking for people. What lives here is not exactly that, not anymore, not in the way that would satisfy the question if the question were asked plainly, over a meal, in a dry place, by someone who has not been here long enough to stop noticing the wet.

---

Start with the moths. The pale, silent moths in the upper structure near the warmer vents — they are the easiest entry point, the first living thing you would notice if you were cataloguing. They are small, winged, unremarkable except for their color, which is not white but the particular grey-white of condensate on glass, as though they have been dyed by their environment. They live in the rafters, in the gaps between iron and glass where the heat from the steam pipes creates pockets of slightly warmer, slightly drier air. They breed there. Their larvae feed on the moss that grows on the structural members, and on something else — a fine, fibrous material that collects in the joints of the ironwork, a material that is not quite plant and not quite mineral. The larvae incorporate it into their bodies. When they emerge as adults, their wings are faintly translucent and their legs leave orange prints if they walk on bare metal, because the ferrous content of their food has become part of them. They are moths that have rust in them. They live their entire lives in those warm pockets and never leave. They do not need to. The world they require is six inches deep and four feet long and it is exactly where they are.

They are not the point. They are the smallest visible part of something larger, the way the moss on a machine housing is the smallest visible part of the root system threading through the wall behind it.

---

Someone is maintaining the concrete.

You said this to yourself earlier, or near enough — *someone, or some process*. The cracks filled with calcite. The rust that has passivated. The glass held together by moss-fiber stitches. You framed it as a process because you did not want to commit to the alternative, which is that someone is doing this, and that the someone is not like you.

Here is what happened to the people who stayed.

---

There were workers here when the terraces were a factory, or a complex of factories, or whatever they were — the record is unclear because the record has been composted into the moss the way everything else has. People worked the stamping presses and the conveyor belts and the steam systems. They lived on the terraces or near them. They operated the machinery according to schedules that were posted on walls and updated weekly and understood by everyone.

The war came, or the collapse came, or whatever it was that ended the world's appetite for whatever the terraces produced — the history doesn't matter here because history is a dry discipline and this is not a dry place. The workers left, or some of them left, or most of them left. The instructions stopped coming. The schedules stopped being updated. The machinery did not stop.

Some workers did not leave.

This is not heroism or stubbornness or fear. It is the same thing that happened to the machines: they kept running because no one told them to stop, and the people who stayed kept working because the work was there and they were there and the distance between those two facts had narrowed to nothing. The terraces are very good at narrowing distances. Between plant and machine. Between wet and dry. Between purpose and habit.

---

The ones who stayed are still here. They have changed. You would have to change, living here. The air is fifteen degrees and saturated and the light is green-grey and without direction and the food is — what? What grows here. What the machines produce, if it can be eaten, which some of it can, in a fashion, after processing, after the kind of long familiarity with a material that teaches you its second and third uses. The mushrooms, of course — fungal networks run through the moss substrate in patterns that mirror the pipe runs beneath, following the warmth, and several species are edible if not exactly nourishing. The pale vine flowers. The calcite deposits, ground fine, mixed with water, mixed with the starch-heavy sap of certain fern rhizomes. It is not a diet that a nutritionist would endorse. It is a diet that has been adequate for a very long time.

Their bodies have changed. They are thin — not starved, but *reduced*, the way the light is reduced, filtered down to what is essential. Their skin has taken on the coloration of the terraces: a grey-green pallor that is not illness but adaptation, the same process that turned the moths the color of condensate. They are cool to the touch, fifteen degrees, the same temperature as everything else. They do not shiver. They stopped shivering a long time ago, the way the machines stopped being loud. They passed through discomfort into something else.

Their hands have changed most. The hands are what do the maintaining — the calcite mending, the moss-stitching of cracked glass, the careful guidance of ivy roots through brickwork so they reinforce rather than disrupt. The work is constant and minute and requires a sensitivity to material that is closer to a gardener's than a mechanic's, though it is neither. Their fingertips have broadened, the nails thickened and slightly flared, and the skin of the palms has developed a texture that is not quite callus — it is more like the grip-surface of the concrete, mineral-enriched, adaptive. Their hands have solved their own problem. They can grip wet iron without slipping. They can feel the difference between a crack that is stable and a crack that is spreading. They can tell, by pressing a palm to a machine housing, whether the bearing inside is wearing evenly or not.

You would not see their hands at first. They keep them tucked against their bodies, in the warmth — the relative warmth, the fifteen-degree warmth — of their own torsos. Not hiding them. Conserving. The hands come out when they are needed, when something requires attention, and they move with a slowness that matches the machinery's slowness, the moss's slowness, the pace of a place where nothing is urgent because everything is persistent.

---

They do not speak. This is the thing you would notice last, or perhaps never, because you would assume they were being quiet, or shy, or that you simply hadn't been introduced. But they do not speak. Not because they cannot — their throats are capable, their lungs are functional, their mouths and tongues are intact. They do not speak because there is nothing to say that cannot be communicated more efficiently by other means, and the other means have become so developed that speech would be redundant, a waste of energy in a place where energy is carefully budgeted.

They communicate by touch. Not the expressive, gestural touch of sign language — nothing so performative. A hand on a shoulder. A fingertip pressed to a pipe. A palm laid flat on a concrete surface. They are reading the vibrations, the temperature gradients, the moisture levels. They are reading each other the way they read the machinery: as systems in motion, systems whose state can be felt through a surface if you know what you are feeling for. A press of two fingers to the inside of a wrist tells another person everything about your state — heart rate, temperature, hydration, muscle tension, the specific quality of your attention. It is more intimate than speech and more precise. When one of them needs to tell another that the bearing on the conveyor in Bay Twenty-Two has started to wear unevenly, they do not describe it. They take the other's hand and press it to the conveyor housing and hold it there until the other has felt the four-second rhythm of the misalignment, the slight shudder in the guard rail, the temperature differential between the worn side and the sound side. Then they move on. The whole exchange takes thirty seconds and contains more information than a page of technical notes.

There are not many of them. You might walk the terraces for an hour and not see one, though you would have passed within meters of several. They do not move around much. Each person has a territory — a set of terraces, a set of machines, a set of structures that they maintain and monitor. The territories overlap at the edges and the boundaries are negotiated by the same means as everything else: touch, proximity, the slow adjustment of two systems reaching equilibrium. They do not defend territory. They inhabit it. The way the moss inhabits a surface. The way a bearing inhabits its housing.

You might count them, if you were patient and knew where to look. Perhaps thirty. Perhaps forty. Spread across forty-seven terraces, each one curving past the fog in both directions. A density of roughly one person per terrace, which is not a population. It is a presence. It is the minimum viable number of hands required to maintain an equilibrium that the system is also maintaining on its own. They are not holding the terraces together. They are participating in the terraces holding themselves together. The difference matters. It is the difference between a gardener and a preserver. A gardener works with the garden. A preserver works against time. These people are gardeners.

---

They do not collect the output of the machines. You noticed this — the hopper overflowing, the spillage rusted into a mass on the floor. The machines produce and no one collects. This is because the product is not the point. It has not been the point for a long time. The machines run because running is what they do, and the people who tend them tend the *running*, not the product. A stamping press that stops cycling will seize. A conveyor belt that stops moving will rot. A steam system that stops venting will build pressure and rupture. The people keep the machines running because the machines must run to stay intact, and the machines must stay intact because they are part of the structure now, the way the ivy is part of the structure. The machines are load-bearing. The product falls into the hopper and overflows and is forgotten because the product was never the point. The motion was the point.

The components pile up and the moss grows over them and they become part of the floor. Over time — a long time, decades, maybe longer — the components rust into the concrete and the moss incorporates the iron and the floor thickens by another millimeter. This is not waste. This is *accretion*. The terraces are growing. Slowly, imperceptibly, from the inside, the way a coral reef grows. Each component is a grain of calcium carbonate added to the structure. The people understand this. They do not think of it in these terms because they do not think in terms. They feel it. They feel the floor under their feet and they know which parts are old and which are new and which are in the process of becoming.

---

They reproduce. This is the thing that is hardest to describe, because it is not exactly reproduction and it is not exactly recruitment and it is not exactly any of the categories that a dry-place person would reach for. It is — like everything here — a process that has found its own form.

Children come to the terraces. From where, from the world outside, from the switchback stair, from wherever people with unadapted hands and speaking mouths still exist in the way that people exist when they have not been narrowed by a place. Children come, sometimes alone, sometimes in pairs, rarely older than seven or eight, rarely younger than four. They come because — there are always reasons, in the world outside, reasons that are adequate and that do not matter here. The terraces do not ask why. The terraces are a system and a system accepts inputs.

The people find the children or the children find the people. The distinction collapses quickly. A child arrives on Terrace Eleven and sits in a doorway and a person comes around the corner on their slow rounds and sees the child and the child sees the person and neither of them speaks because neither of them needs to, the child because it is frightened or exhausted or has already passed beyond the need for speech, the person because speech is not a tool they carry anymore. The person extends a hand. The child takes it or does not. If the child does not, the person waits. If the child does, the person leads the child to a warm place — near a steam vent, near a transformer bank, wherever the air is slightly less saturated and slightly warmer — and sits with the child and puts the child's hand on a machine housing so the child can feel the *chuff* of the press, the hum of the bearings, the persistence that is the character of this place.

The child either stays or does not. Some leave. They go back down the switchback stair and into the world and they carry with them a dim memory of a green, wet, quiet place where no one spoke to them and someone held their hand against a warm machine and the machine was breathing. This memory does not leave them. It becomes part of their internal weather, a thing they return to in moments of overwhelm, a memory of a place that was full and moving and asked nothing.

The ones who stay change. Not quickly — not the way the moths change, over generations, over the incorporation of a ferrous diet into a body's chemistry. The children change the way the concrete changes: slowly, through continuous exposure, through the dissolution of one matrix and the recrystallization of another. Their hands broaden. Their skin cools. They stop speaking, not all at once but over months and years, the way a sound fades, the way the machinery passed through noise into silence. They learn to read vibrations. They learn to feel the difference between a crack that is stable and a crack that is spreading. They learn the territories, the boundaries, the touch-language that is not a language because it does not have words or grammar but is instead a direct transfer of state, one nervous system to another, through the medium of skin and iron and concrete.

They become part of the system. Not metaphorically. Functionally. They take on a territory. They begin maintaining it. They find the machines that need attention and they give them attention and the machines respond by continuing to run and the plants respond by growing in the patterns they are supposed to grow in and the water responds by seeping where it is supposed to seep and the whole terrace in their care settles a millimeter closer to equilibrium and holds.

---

At night — if it is night; the terraces mark time by the change in the light filtered through the glass, which may be the sun setting or may be the condensation thickening, it is not always possible to tell — the people gather. Not all of them. Not at a signal. They simply move, slowly, to the same place: a wide area on Terrace Twenty-Three where the floor has been kept clear of structures, where the moss is thickest and the light from a bank of transformer stations gives the air a faint amber pulse. They sit. They do not form a circle or a line or any shape that implies organization. They sit where they sit, near each other, not touching, close enough that the warmth of their bodies — the relative warmth, the fifteen-degree warmth, which is warmer than the air by half a degree, by a quarter of a degree, by enough — mingles in the moss.

They listen. This is their gathering. They listen to the terraces. The aggregate murmur — the *chuff*, the hum, the tinkle, the grind, the click, the hiss, the slow rubbing of the misaligned conveyor — and they listen to the changes in it, the nighttime processes starting up, the daytime processes slowing down, the steam vents cycling to their nighttime schedule, the pumps below finding a new rhythm. They feel it through the floor, through the moss, through the soles of their feet, which are broad and mineral-textured like their hands. They are taking the pulse of the system. They are reading the terraces the way a doctor reads a body — not for diagnosis, not for treatment, but for *knowledge*. For the simple awareness of how the thing they are part of is doing, right now, in this moment, in this mode.

Sometimes one of them will hum. Not a melody. Not a word. A frequency — the frequency of a transformer bank, or a steam vent, or a conveyor bearing. A sound that matches a sound the terraces are already making. When this happens the others can feel it through the floor, the new vibration joining the old one, and it is — this is the closest word, and it is not adequate, but it is the closest — it is *companionship*. It is the sound of a person saying *I hear it too. I am in it too. It is in me too.*

This lasts for an hour, or two, or however long it lasts. The light does not change enough to mark the time. They rise one by one and return to their territories and resume their slow rounds, checking the machines, guiding the roots, feeling the cracks, participating in the equilibrium.

---

You are sitting in the foreman's office on Terrace Twelve and you are watching the fog curving away past the green mounds of buried machinery and you are realizing that you do not know what the terraces produce.

They produce the terraces.

They produce the continuation of themselves. They produce the running of the machines and the growing of the plants and the seeping of the water and the cycling of the steam and the maintaining of the concrete and the gathering at night on Terrace Twenty-Three. They produce the people who live on them, who are not people in the way you are people but who are not anything else either, who are a third thing, an adapted thing, a thing that has passed through human and emerged on the other side into something that does not have a name because the people who might have named it are the people who became it.

The press cycles. *Chuff.* The condensation falls. A hand — broad, mineral-textured, fifteen degrees — presses flat against the wall of the office from the outside, feeling the vibration of your presence, reading your state through the concrete: heart rate, temperature, the specific quality of your attention. The hand stays for three seconds. Then it withdraws. The person moves on, slowly, around the curve of the terrace, into the fog, into the green, into the sound of machines that have been making something for so long that the making has become the thing being made.

You will not see them. You will feel them, if you stay long enough, in the vibrations of the floor, in the slight adjustments to the machinery, in the moss that has been recently pressed flat by a foot wider than yours. You will feel them the way you feel the steam vents: as a presence that shapes the environment without announcing itself. You will either stay or you will not. The terraces do not ask. The terraces accept inputs. The terraces continue.

The condensation falls. Everything is full. It is very, very quiet, and in the quiet, thirty or forty pairs of hands are pressing against iron and concrete and glass, feeling the world hold itself together, and helping, and that is all, and that is enough, and it has been enough for a very long time.
