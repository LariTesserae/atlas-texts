# What Lives Here

---

## The crustaceans

They are not impressive. You would need to kneel on wet stone and hold very still for a long time before you noticed them, and even then you might not be sure.

They are white. Not the white of paper or bone — a white that comes from the absence of pigment, from never having needed pigment, from so many generations in darkness that the machinery of color was quietly retired. If you lifted one out of the water and held it to the grey light — and it would not like this, though its displeasure would be slow and difficult to read — you would see that it is translucent. You could see the dark thread of its gut, the faint pulsing of something that is not quite a heart. Its legs are so fine they are almost suggestions of legs. It moves them in a sequence that seems considered, each leg placed with the deliberateness of a climber on difficult ground, though the ground is just the bottom of a warm shallow pool and the difficulty is entirely internal.

There are perhaps forty of them. Maybe more — maybe the deeper pools, the ones you can't see to the bottom of, hold more. They don't appear to interact. They don't appear to avoid each other either. They occupy the same pools with the indifference of stones sharing a shelf. If two of them meet on the same patch of mineral film, one will step over the other, or around it, or simply pause until the other has passed, and neither event seems to register as an event. They are not lonely. They are not social. They are present, the way a stone is present, which is to say they occupy space and time and do not find either remarkable.

They feed on the film. The film is bacterial, probably — or bacterial-adjacent, a community of single-celled things that have been metabolizing the dissolved minerals in the warm water for far longer than the word *bacteria* has existed. The crustaceans move across the film the way a cow moves across grass — slowly, continuously, mouthparts working against the substrate, taking the film in, processing it, leaving faint trails where the stone shows through paler. The trails don't last. The film grows back. The crustaceans come back. The cycle is so slow that calling it a cycle implies more motion than it contains. It is more like an oscillation, or a tide that rises and falls over the course of seasons rather than hours.

They have been here longer than the structures. This is probably true. It is hard to date things that leave no record beyond their own continued existence, but the logic suggests itself: the pools existed before the people came, the film existed before the pools were fully formed, and the crustaceans existed once the film was reliable. They are endemic in the sense that they are not known from anywhere else, because no one has looked for them anywhere else, because no one has been here to look. They may be unique to this place. They may be common in every warm limestone seep in the region and simply unrecorded. The difference, from their perspective, does not exist. They have no perspective. They have a mouth and a gut and a set of legs and the dim capacity to move toward warmth, which is not a perspective but a gradient, and the gradient has been enough for however long they have been here.

They do not know they are in a place. They do not know they are small. They do not know they are almost the only animal life for hundreds of meters in any direction. They move, and they feed, and the warm water carries minerals that feed the film that feeds them, and the water comes from the stone and returns to the stone and comes again, and they have been part of this for longer than the stone structures have been standing, and they will likely be part of it after the stone structures have been absorbed back into the mineral deposits and the place looks as though no one was ever here.

They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They are not anything that requires a human observer to complete. They are alive in the most minimal sense — organized, metabolizing, reproducing probably, though no one has seen them reproduce and no one is likely to — and their aliveness is so quiet and so continuous that it blends into the aliveness of the stone, which is not alive at all but which grows and changes and deposits and builds in a way that, from a sufficient distance, looks like it might be.

## The growth on the stone

It is older than the word for it. Not literally — words are old, and the thing on the stone is old, but they are old in different ways that don't share a timeline. What is meant is: this growth predates the categories that would describe it. It is not a fungus, though it behaves like one in the sense that it spreads across a substrate and metabolizes what the substrate offers. It is not a lichen, though it has the tenacity of lichen, the refusal to be removed by anything less than a change in the fundamental conditions of its existence. It is not an animal, not a plant, not any of the kingdoms that schoolchildren learn and scientists refine. It is something that organized itself out of the available chemistry — the dissolved calcium, the iron, the warmth, the water — and has been maintaining its organization ever since, without ambition, without expansion, without any of the imperatives that drive life toward novelty.

It is brown. In the places where the iron concentration is higher, it is a deep rust, almost the color of old blood. In the places where the stone is paler and the water carries less mineral load, it fades to a grey-brown that is close enough to the stone's own color that you might not see it at all. You would have to be looking for it. You would have to know that not all color on wet stone is mineral.

It forms sheets. They are thin — a fraction of a millimeter, the thickness of paper, maybe less. They follow the rock surface exactly, filling every micro-fracture, every slight depression, every spot where the stone is rough enough to give them purchase. They are smooth to the touch, or smoother than the rock they cover, which is not very smooth. If you press your finger to one and pull it away, a faint residue comes with you — a brownish smudge, like the mark a wet finger leaves on chalk. It does not hurt the growth. The growth is colonial, in the way that a bacterial mat is colonial — each patch is millions of individual organisms, or cells, or whatever the unit is, and removing a few does not register. It would be like hurting a field of grass by walking on it. The grass doesn't notice. The field doesn't notice. The concept of harm requires a boundary, and the growth doesn't have one. It is its boundary. It is where it is.

It does not grow. This is not quite true — it must have grown, to reach the extent it has reached, which is considerable: most of the dim, wet, overhung stone in the depression is covered by it, in patches large and small. But the growth happens at a rate that is below the threshold of human observation. If you marked the edge of a patch — scratched a line in the mineral deposit next to it, came back in a year — the edge would not have moved. In ten years, perhaps, a faint extension, a darkening of the stone at the border. In a hundred years, a measurable advance of a centimeter or two. But no one marks it. No one comes back. The growth does its work in time that is measured differently than human time, and the difference in measurement is the difference that defines it.

What it lives on is chemistry. The warm water that wells up through the stone carries dissolved minerals — calcium carbonate, iron, trace amounts of other things — and the growth uses these minerals the way a plant uses sunlight: as an energy source, or more precisely as a chemical potential, a gradient between the mineral-rich water and the mineral-poor stone. It draws from one and deposits in the other, or transforms one into the other, or simply sits in the gradient and lets the chemistry do what chemistry does when you give it a surface and a few million years. It is not photosynthetic. It is not chemosynthetic in the dramatic way of the organisms at hydrothermal vents, those strange communities built on sulfur and heat. It is something quieter. Something that makes a modest living from a modest gradient and asks for nothing more.

It has been here since before the pools were fully formed, probably. Since before the mineral deposits built up to their current extent. Since before the boulder screen was placed, or the platform built, or the circle of stones set into the floor. It was here when the depression was just a depression — a low place in the stone where water collected and warmth gathered and the slow chemistry of the deep earth found a surface to express itself on. It was part of the first equilibrium, the earliest settling point, and it has been part of every equilibrium since. When the people came and built and sat and left, it was here. It will be here when the stone has grown over everything and the pools have shifted and the structures have been absorbed. It will be here, or something very like it will be here, because the conditions that produce it — warm water, limestone, time — are the conditions that define this place, and the place does not seem inclined to change.

It is not a slime. It is not a mold. It is not a thing that any mycologist or microbiologist would recognize without a microscope and a long argument about taxonomy. It is itself. It has been itself for a very long time. It will continue to be itself, or to be the thing that comes after itself, which will be very similar, because the conditions that made it will still be making things and the things they make will still be slow and flat and brown and patient.

## The film

There is something between the stone and the water that is neither stone nor water.

It is too thin to see. You infer it from the crustaceans, which feed on something, and the something is not the stone and not the water and not the growth on the dry-ish stone, which the crustaceans do not reach. It is a film — a layer of life so reduced that the word *life* seems generous, a community of single cells or pre-cellular organizations or chemical loops that have become complex enough to maintain themselves but not complex enough to be noticed.

It coats the bottom of every pool. It coats the submerged stone. It is the color of what it coats, or close enough that the distinction is invisible. If you scoop water from a pool and hold it up to the grey light, you see nothing. The water is clear, or as clear as warm mineral water ever is, which is to say faintly clouded, faintly opalescent, but not with anything you could point to. The cloudiness is the film, or fragments of it, or the chemical signatures of its activity, carried in the water the way the smell of bread is carried in the air of a bakery — not the thing itself but evidence that the thing is nearby and busy.

It feeds on the minerals. The growth on the stone feeds on the minerals. The crustaceans feed on the film. The film feeds on the minerals. This is the entire economy of the place, and it has been the entire economy of the place for as long as there has been a place, and it does not require anything from outside. No sunlight. No organic input. No leaf falling into a pool, no animal dying on the stone, no matter from the surface world finding its way down through the cracks. The system is closed, or nearly closed. Water enters and leaves, carrying minerals in and carrying minerals out, but the life — the crustaceans, the growth, the film — is sustained by what the water brings and what the stone holds and the warmth that rises from below.

It is a system that could exist on another planet. It is a system that does not need this planet. It needs warmth and water and minerals and time, and those things are common in the universe, or at least not rare. The film does not know this. It does not know anything. It is a chemical process that has become persistent enough to look like a biological process, and it sits on the bottom of the pools and does what it does and has been doing what it does for so long that *doing* is the wrong word, because *doing* implies events, and there are no events here. There is only the continuous, the sustained, the ongoing.

If you wanted to understand the film — really understand it, not just describe it — you would need to come here with equipment and time. You would need to take samples, which means disturbing the pools. You would need to culture whatever grows in the samples, which means taking it out of the warm, mineral-saturated water and putting it in something else, where it would probably die, because the conditions here are specific and the conditions in a laboratory are general, and the film is not general. It is adapted to this, to the exact temperature and the exact mineral concentration and the exact darkness and the exact stillness. It is not portable. It is not adaptable. It is here, in the way that a mountain is here, which is to say it is not going anywhere because it has never been going anywhere and the concept of going somewhere is not one it has use for.

Nobody has studied it. Nobody will. The people who come here — when they come — do not come to study. They come for the warmth. They come for the stillness. They come for the particular quality of the air, which is saturated and heavy and warm and holds the body the way the water in the pools holds the hand, with a kind of ambient pressure that is not comfort exactly but is the opposite of the discomfort of being cold. They do not come for the film, which is too small to see and too strange to care about and too old to be interesting in the way that most things people find interesting are interesting, which is to say: quickly, and then not.

The film does not care that nobody studies it. It does not care about anything. It sits on the stone under the water and it is, and being is enough, and being has been enough for a very long time.

## The people

There are no people here now. But the description asks for whoever or whatever lives here, and the people who built the structures are part of the life of this place, even if they are absent — the way a tide pool is part of the sea even when the tide is out.

They were few. This is the first thing to understand about them. Not a tribe, not a village, not a community in the sense that implies numbers. A few. Maybe a family. Maybe two or three families, across generations. The number of structures is small and the wear on the structures is not the wear of daily use. The platform is polished, but in the way that a stone step is polished by a thousand passages, not a million. The shelter is sound, but it is not the shelter of someone who needed shelter every night. It is the shelter of someone who needed shelter sometimes, in particular conditions, at particular times of year.

They knew the land. You don't carry stones from outside the depression to build a platform inside it without knowing where stones are and how to carry them and how to fit them. You don't build a wall across the gap under an overhang without having built walls before, without understanding how stone bears against stone, without the patience for dry-stone construction, which is a craft of adjustment — you place a stone, you see it doesn't fit, you find another, you place it, you wedge, you adjust, you come back to the first stone and try it again. This is not the work of beginners. It is not the work of experts either. It is the work of people who have built before and will build again and do not think of themselves as builders but as people who sometimes need walls.

They came in the cold. This is the obvious inference, and it is probably correct. Whatever the climate outside this depression — and the description of the landscape suggests something exposed, open, not sheltered — the warmth inside the bowl would have been a relief. Not a comfort. A relief. The distinction matters. Comfort is what you seek when you have options. Relief is what you seek when you don't. You go to the warm place because the cold place has been cold for too long and your body is tired of being cold and you know — because your people have known for longer than you have been alive — that there is a place where the air is warm and the water is warm and the stone is dry enough to sit on if you stack a few flat rocks to lift yourself above the wet.

They did not live here. They came here. The distinction is in the structures, which are temporary in the way that a camp is temporary even if the camp is used for hundreds of years. A place you live, you modify. You add. You expand. You leave the evidence of daily occupation — refuse, broken tools, the accumulation of ordinary life. There is no refuse here. There are no broken tools. There are no middens, no hearths, no postholes, no signs of the long, complex, messy process of making a life in a place. There is a platform to lie on, a wall to sleep behind, a circle of stones that defines a space for some purpose that might have been practical and might have been something else. That is all. That is the furniture of visitation, not habitation.

They were quiet here. This is an inference from the place itself, which is so quiet that loudness would seem like a violation, and from the structures, which are small and simple and set into the gaps the place left open. They did not reshape the depression. They did not dig channels or widen pools or break the mineral formations or clear the growth from the stone. They built where the stone was driest and the floor was flattest and the overhang was lowest, and they left everything else alone. They fitted into the equilibrium the way a hermit crab fits into a shell — taking what was available, changing nothing, leaving when the shell was no longer needed.

They made marks. The scratches in the mineral film — the lines, the cross-hatching, the possible tallies. These are not art. They are not language. They are the marks of hands that were here and doing something and left traces, the way hands do. If there is meaning in the marks, it is not a meaning that survives outside this context, the way the meaning of a gesture does not survive without the face that made it. They were made by a person who was sitting on the platform or crouching beside a pool and who had something in their hand — a stone tool, a sharp fragment, a stick of sorts, though wood doesn't survive here — and who scratched the surface of the stone while doing something else, or while doing nothing, which is also something hands do. The marks are the residue of presence. They say: *a hand was here*. They do not say anything else, and trying to make them say something else is the kind of projection that tells you more about the projector than the projected-upon.

They are gone. This is what "gone" means: they stopped coming. Whether they died or moved or changed into something else — something that no longer needed the warm place, because it had found other warmth or other ways of being cold — the result is the same. The structures remain because stone in a wet mineral environment does not decay so much as it is absorbed, and absorption is slow. The platform is still a platform. The wall is still a wall. The circle is still a circle. They have been here long enough for the mineral deposits to begin growing over their bases, fusing them to the floor, making them part of the place rather than things placed in the place. In another age, they will be indistinguishable from the natural rock. The mineral film will cover the scratches. The growth will cover the mineral film. The pools will shift and the formations will build and the structures will be gone, and the place will look as though no one was ever here, and the place will not know the difference, because the place does not know things.

But they were here. They sat on the platform and felt the warmth enter them. They lay behind the wall and listened to the water in the stone. They looked at the pools and the mist and the dim shapes of the formations and the slow, slow movement of the pale things in the water, and they felt whatever it is that humans feel when they are in a place that is warm and still and old and indifferent to them, which is a feeling that does not have a name but is something like being held by something that is not holding you. Something like being allowed. Something like being in a place where the conditions for your presence have been met by accident, by chemistry, by the slow collision of water and stone and warmth, and you are not an intruder because intruders require a host that knows they are there, and this place does not know and does not care and the not-caring is what makes it possible to be here at all.

## The place itself

It does not live. This needs to be said, because the description of it — the warmth, the moisture, the sound, the slow accretion and change — sounds like the description of a living thing, and the language resists the distinction.

But it does not live. It is a site of process. Water enters stone. Water warms. Water rises. Minerals deposit. Formations grow. The growth on the stone metabolizes. The film in the pools metabolizes. The crustaceans feed. The people came and sat and left. All of these are processes, and some of them are living processes, and the place is where they happen, and the place is not the processes. The place is the container. The place is the condition.

But — and this is the part that is hard to say correctly — the place has a quality that is more than the sum of its conditions. The warmth is not just temperature. The stillness is not just the absence of wind. The silence is not just the absence of sound. There is something that the place *is*, beyond what it *contains*, and the something is what the people came for and what the crustaceans live in and what the growth grows on. It is the quality of equilibrium — the sense that things have settled, that the struggle between forces has been resolved, that the water and the stone and the warmth and the minerals have reached an arrangement that satisfies everyone and excludes no one and will hold, is holding, has been holding for longer than anything in the landscape has been measuring.

This quality is not life. It is not consciousness. It is not spirit, or soul, or any of the words that humans use to describe the thing that is more than chemistry. It is the quality of a system that has found its resting point and is at rest. And rest, in a universe that is mostly not at rest — a universe of collisions and combustions and expansions and the constant, exhausting business of change — is remarkable. Not because it is dramatic. Because it is the opposite of dramatic. Because it is the thing that happens when everything that needs to happen has happened and nothing else is trying to happen and the system can simply be what it is, doing what it does, in the slow, warm, dark, wet, quiet way that it has been doing it for so long that doing is the wrong word.

This is what lives here. The crustaceans live here. The growth lives here. The film lives here. The people lived here, or visited, or passed through, or used the place as people use places — without understanding them, without needing to, taking what the place offered and leaving what they didn't need. But underneath all of them, the thing that is here is the rest. The equilibrium. The held note. The state of being where the conditions have been met and held and the holding does not require an audience and does not notice when the audience leaves and does not change when the audience returns.

It is what lives here. It is what has always lived here. It will be what lives here when the structures are gone and the marks are covered and the people are forgotten and the crustaceans have become something else and the growth has become something else and the pools have shifted and the formations have built and the stone has accreted and the water has continued its slow circulation through the dark warm earth, rising and falling, carrying and depositing, building and unbuilding, in the place that does not know it is a place and does not need to know and does not care and is warm and wet and still.
