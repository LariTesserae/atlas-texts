# The Hollow of Moving Stones

The grazers.

That is the word that settled on you, but it is not quite right. They graze, yes — they settle against walls and the stone beneath them brightens as though freshly quarried, the mineral film lifted from the surface by some process that involves no mouth, no tongue, no mechanism you can identify. But calling them grazers implies a separation between them and what they consume, and down here that separation may not exist.

---

They are the stone.

Not metaphorically. Not the way a mountain goat is the mountain because it lives on the mountain. They are the stone in the way a wave is the ocean — a local expression of a larger body, a momentary shape assumed by something that has no obligation to hold any shape at all. When you watch one long enough — and you have, because there is nothing else to watch, and time here has a weight that pins you in place — you begin to see it. The pale chalky body is not sitting on the limestone floor. It is continuous with it. There is no seam. The underside of the grazer does not meet the stone; it *is* the stone, risen. The rest of the body is just more stone, organized differently, lifted into a shape that permits locomotion, however slow.

This is why they make no sound. Two stones pressed together make sound. A stone pressed against itself makes nothing at all.

When two grazers meet and merge — and you have watched this happen three times now, each time with the same unhurried, dreamlike patience — they are not colliding. They are pooling. The boundary between them dissolves, and for a time there is a single larger body, a brief amalgamation, and then the mass separates again into two individuals. But not the same two. Each has taken something from the other. A slightly different contour. A patch of color, perhaps — one had carried a rust-red vein through its body, and afterward both carry it, faintly, the way a shared memory might distribute itself between two minds. They have exchanged something. Information, maybe. Mineral, certainly. Something else, possibly.

You count the grazers visible from one vantage point and stop at sixty, not because there aren't more but because at a certain distance the grazers become indistinguishable from rubble, and rubble becomes indistinguishable from grazers, and you realize the distinction may be yours alone. The cavern floor is lumpy, textured, full of low rises and shallow depressions, and many of these features are moving. You are standing in a room full of things that might be alive and might be geology and the difference, down here, may not signify.

---

Their sizes vary, but not on a spectrum you understand. There are fist-sized ones — small, dense, almost spherical, their surfaces smooth as river stones. There are dog-sized ones, more irregular, with the provisional appendages you've come to recognize: not legs, not roots, but something between, growths that extend and retract and seem to serve whatever purpose the moment requires. There are horse-sized ones, and these are the most unsettling, because at that scale you can see the internal structure — not anatomy, but texture, the grazer's body revealing itself as compressed limestone layered like shale, the striations visible in its flanks, and you understand that what you are looking at is a cross-section of the cavern floor, standing up and walking away.

And there are larger ones. At the far edge of the plain, where the buildings resume, you saw something that might have been four meters across, flattened against the ground, barely raised, so slow that you only noticed it because you had been sitting for fifteen minutes and the skyline of the distant structures had changed. It was not the buildings that had moved. It was the ground itself, flowing across the plain in a body so large it had become a landscape feature. You watched it for a long time. It did not stop. It did not speed up. It moved with the patience of a thing that has never been in a hurry and has never been given a reason to start.

---

They feed. You are confident of this. The bright patches they leave on walls and floors are patches where the mineral film has been removed, and the grazers' bodies — particularly the smaller ones — are slightly larger after feeding than before. They are incorporating the material. They are eating the cave and adding it to themselves.

But they also deposit. You found this by watching the water basins on the plain. A grazer half-submerged in a basin does not merely drink. Over the course of an hour, the water around it clouds. A sediment forms — fine, pale, mineral. The grazer has grown slightly smaller. It is shedding. It has taken material from one place, carried it, and left it in another. The clouded basins are not contamination. They are construction sites. Something is being built in the water, too fine and too slow to see, but the cloudiness has a shape to it if you look closely — a faint lattice, a crystalline suggestion of structure, like the first strokes of a drawing that has not yet decided what it will be.

The grazers are not consuming the city. They are redistributing it. Moving material from walls to basins, from streets to the spaces between buildings, from one region of the cavern to another, following the slow currents of the ground beneath them. They are the stone's circulatory system. They are how this place metabolizes itself.

---

The builders knew them. You are certain of this now. The wide streets are not accommodations for ground movement alone — they are corridors. The grazers move through the streets the way blood moves through vessels, following the channels cut for them, pooling in the low places, gathering in the intersections. The buildings are not just built to withstand the turbulence of the ground; they are built to be cleaned by it. The grazers remove the mineral film that the condensation deposits, and in doing so they keep the limestone exposed, bright, functional. A wall that was never cleaned would accumulate a thick crust of mineral deposit over the centuries — would, in effect, bury itself. The grazers prevent this. They are the city's maintenance system. They were always the city's maintenance system.

And this raises a question that you cannot answer but that the place seems to be asking: did the builders build the city and the grazers came to tend it, or did the grazers exist first, shaping the stone by their movements, and the builders came later, learning from them, building where the grazers had already carved the channels, working with the maintenance system rather than creating it?

The tools on the shelf in the tall building — the chisel, the hammer, the calcified measuring device — these are human-scale tools. They imply human-scale hands, human-scale intentions, human-scale lifespans. Someone stood here, once, and worked the stone. But the grazers do not have hands. They do not have intentions in any way you can recognize. They work the stone anyway. They shape it, move it, clean it, redistribute it. The difference between the builders and the grazers may be the difference between someone who decides to do a thing and someone who simply does it, as part of what they are.

---

There is a rhythm to the place. You have been here long enough to feel it, though not long enough to measure it. The ground moves in waves — not waves of shape, waves of *activity*. For a span of time, perhaps an hour, perhaps two, the turbulence increases. More cracks open. More mounds rise and fall. The grazers move faster, though faster here is still slow by any standard aboveground. They converge. They merge more frequently, the exchanges becoming denser, more rapid, the pale bodies flowing together and apart in patterns that look, from a height, like the patterns of thought — nodes forming and dissolving, connections made and broken, information routing through a system that has no wires and no neurons and no purpose you can name.

Then the activity subsides. The ground stills. The grazers disperse. A long quiet follows, during which the only movement is the slow individual grazing, the patient cleaning of walls, the gradual accumulation of mineral film in the basins. You sit in the quiet and the damp and the sourceless blue-white light and you listen to your own breathing and you understand that this rhythm — active, quiet, active, quiet — is the heartbeat of the place. Not a metaphor. The cavern has a pulse. The stone has a pulse. The grazers are the blood.

---

You found a grazer near the tall building that is different from the others.

It is small — the size of a closed hand — and it carries a color the others do not: a dark vein of purple-black, the same color as the crystalline deposits in the cavern walls, running through its body like a thread. It moves differently, too. The others drift, settle, feed, drift again. This one moves with direction. It travels along the street outside the tall building, enters the doorway, crosses the floor, climbs the shelf — a process that takes twenty minutes and involves the slow extrusion of a climbing surface, the way a glacier might climb a step if glaciers had reason to — and settles beside the tools.

It does not feed on the tools. It does not clean them. It sits beside them. The purple-black vein in its body pulses — or seems to pulse, though you cannot tell if the pulsing is real or a trick of the light, or a trick of watching one thing for so long that you begin to invent movement to fill the silence.

You watch for a long time. The grazer does not move. The tools do not move. The purple-black vein does not pulse again, if it ever did. And then, without any signal you can detect, the grazer descends from the shelf, crosses the floor, exits the building, and resumes its grazing along the wall.

You do not know what that was. You do not know if it was a visit, or a vigil, or a routine, or nothing at all. But the grazer knew the tools were there. It went to them specifically, directly, in a place where nothing else in the cavern exhibits direction. It sought them out. And then it left them.

---

The grazers do not remember the builders. You think this is probably true. They do not have the architecture for memory — no neurons, no structure that persists, no organ that holds and replays. They are stone, and stone does not remember the way living things remember.

But stone holds shape. Stone holds pattern. The streets are still here, and the grazers still move through them, and the buildings still stand, and the tools are still on the shelf, and the grazer with the purple-black vein still visits them. Something persists. Something that was put into the stone by the builders — a pattern of channels, a layout of spaces, a relationship between structures — has outlasted the builders by an incomprehensible span of time and is still being honored, still being maintained, still being tended by the slow circulatory system of the living floor.

The builders are gone. Their bones, if they had bones, are mineral now, indistinguishable from the limestone, perhaps incorporated into a grazer, perhaps dissolved in a basin, perhaps part of the wall of a building they once built with their own hands. But the pattern they laid down — the streets, the spaces, the shapes — persists in the body of the place itself. The grazers are not remembering the builders. They are *being* the memory. They are the stone's way of holding a shape that was given to it, the way a river holds the shape of its banks after the rain that carved them has stopped.

---

And there are others.

You have not seen them. You have seen what they leave.

In one region of the city — far from the tall building, far from the plain, in a cluster of structures so worn that they are barely walls, suggestions of walls, the ghost of architecture — you found a building whose interior floor was covered in marks. Not carvings. Not inscriptions. Marks. Grooves in the limestone, shallow and fine, made by something that pressed into the stone and dragged. The pattern was not random. The grooves ran in parallel lines, turned at right angles, intersected, formed a grid that covered the entire floor of the building — perhaps four meters by three — and extended out through the doorway and into the street beyond.

The marks were fresh. The limestone was bright where the grooves cut it, unclouded by the mineral film that coats everything else. What made them had passed through recently — within hours, perhaps, or minutes before your arrival. You knelt and touched the grooves and felt the sharp edges of fresh-cut stone and you listened to the silence and you heard nothing. Whatever had made these marks had moved on. Or was nearby and not moving. Or was beneath you, in the stone, part of the ground, invisible because it was everything.

The marks were not language. You are fairly certain of this. They were too regular, too systematic, too much like the plans of a thing rather than the description of a thing. They were a map. They were a diagram. They were the marks of something that was thinking about the city — measuring it, recording it, laying out its pattern in a new form — and they were made by a body you have not seen, in a way you do not understand, for a purpose you can only guess at.

There are grazers, and there is the ground, and there is the stone, and there is the light, and there is the damp, and there is the silence, and there is whatever made these marks.

---

You sit in the doorway of the marked building and you watch the grazers in the street outside and you feel the ground move beneath you and you think about what lives here.

The grazers live here. The stone lives here. The light lives here, in its sourceless, shadowless, patient way. The moisture lives here, cycling between air and surface and basin and grazer. The buildings live here, in the slow geological sense of living — persisting, changing, drifting, settling. And something else lives here, something that marks the stone with fresh diagrams, something that directs itself to tools on a shelf, something that may be the grazers collectively or may be the ground itself or may be something older and stranger that has no name in any language that has ever been spoken aboveground.

You are temporary. They are not. They are the stone's long conversation with itself, conducted in silence, over a span of time that makes the word *ancient* feel like a thing invented yesterday. They were here before you came. They will be here after you leave. They will be here when the entrance you descended through has closed — when the stone has shifted and the passage has sealed and the way in has become the way out has become a wall has become a memory has become stone has become a grazer has become the light, the silence, the damp, the dark, the patience, the pattern, the pulse.

You stand. You leave. The grazers do not notice. The ground does not notice. The marks on the floor do not change. The light stays on. The place breathes.
