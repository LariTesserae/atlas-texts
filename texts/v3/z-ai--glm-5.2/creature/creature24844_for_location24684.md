# What Lives in Kell-Oshar

---

## The Crawlers (for lack of a better word)

Start with the obvious. The things you can see, if you're patient enough, if you stand still long enough for your eyes to commit to the copper murk and learn its limits.

They are not insects. They are not arthropods in any taxonomic sense you'd recognize. The segmentation is wrong — too regular, too geometric, as though each segment were a manufactured unit rather than a body part grown under evolutionary pressure. And the legs: dozens of them, yes, but they move in a pattern that isn't the rippling gait of a centipede or the deliberate steppage of a millipede. It's more like a conveyor. Each leg lifts at exactly the same height and re-plants at exactly the same interval, and the result is that the creature doesn't walk so much as *translate* across a surface, maintaining the same altitude and speed regardless of whether the surface is horizontal, vertical, or inverted. They move the same way on a ceiling as on a floor. Gravity does not appear to be a consideration.

There is a word for what they do, and the word is *commute*. They commute. They leave one junction and travel to another junction, pause, and travel back, or travel on to a third. The routes are fixed. You can map them by the wear patterns in the pipes — grooves polished into the metal (or whatever it is) by what must be uncountable repetitions of the same path. Some of the grooves are deeper than others. The deepest ones correspond to the widest pipes, and the widest pipes connect to the largest machines, and the largest machines sit in the largest squares, and the largest squares are nearest the center of the grid.

If there is a hierarchy here, it is geographical, not social. The crawlers near the center are not different from the ones near the periphery — same size, same build, same matte-black finish, same indifference to your existence. But they travel shorter distances and pause longer. The peripheral ones cover more ground and pause less. It is the inverse of what you'd expect from a settlement pattern, where central individuals tend to move more and peripheral ones less. Here, the center is the destination. Everything else is supply line.

What are they supplying? What are they moving?

Watch one long enough and you'll see it pause at a widening in a pipe — those deliberate-looking expansions mentioned earlier — and remain still for some time. Ten minutes. Twenty. An hour. Then it moves on. When it leaves, the widening is empty. When it arrived, the widening contained a small accumulation of the dark powder that coats the pipe interiors. It is not unreasonable to conclude that the creature consumed it. It is also not unreasonable to conclude that the creature *processed* it — took it in, did something to it internally, and deposited it elsewhere, unchanged in composition but perhaps changed in something else. Location. Distribution. Alignment.

They may be gut flora. That is the analogy that fits best, and it is the one that will bother you the most. The city is the body. The machines are the organs. The pipes are the circulatory system. And the crawlers are the bacteria in the gut — not necessary for the basic function of the system, but necessary for its long-term stability, breaking down accumulations, keeping things moving, preventing blockage and stagnation. They are maintenance. They are the reason the pipes have never been cleared and never needed to be cleared, because clearing is what they do, constantly, one grain of powder at a time.

If this is correct, then they were placed here. Designed, manufactured, and installed, the same as the machines and the pipes. They are not fauna that colonized an empty niche. They are infrastructure. And the fact that they are organic — or organic enough to move, to pause, to wear grooves in metal with their feet — is not a contradiction. It is a design choice. Whoever built Kell-Oshar did not distinguish between the living and the mechanical. Or rather, they distinguished, but treated the distinction as a material property, not a categorical one. You use stone for walls. You use metal for pipes. You use these creatures for maintenance. Each material is suited to its task. None is more essential than another.

This is the reading that makes the most sense of what you can observe. It is also the reading that makes the place most difficult to think about, because it implies a mind — or a civilization, or a process — that understood life as a component, a means, a thing to be shaped and installed and left to run. Not cruelty. Not exploitation. Something more alien than either. An engineering tradition in which the line between "organism" and "mechanism" was simply not a line that needed to be drawn.

## What Lives Deeper

The text says: if there is anything else alive in Kell-Oshar, it is deeper than you can go.

This is true, but it requires elaboration. The vault floor is not the bottom. The lift shaft you came through connects to the upper levels of a system that extends downward in ways the visible city does not advertise. There are gratings in some of the squares — set flush with the basalt floor, invisible unless you happen to step on one and feel the faint vibration more strongly there, as though the breathing of the machines is closer and more concentrated beneath the metal. The gratings are fixed. They do not have handles or hinges. They are not meant to be opened. They are there to permit the passage of air — the warm current that rises from below, drifts to the ceiling, cools, and sinks — and nothing else.

But through the gratings, if you lie flat and press your eye to the copper-lit gap between the grating and its frame, you can see down. Not far. The glow of the mineral veins persists below the floor, dimmer here, the veins thinner and more widely spaced, as though the basalt's mineral content is being drawn downward — leached, perhaps, by whatever process the machines are driving. Below the first grating, you can see perhaps two meters. There is another floor. Or a shelf. Or a ceiling for a space below. It is smooth. It is empty. It has the same rectilinear character as the city above — the same aesthetic of functional minimalism, the same absence of decoration or signage. But there are no machines on it. No pipes. No buildings. Just smooth dark stone with faint copper veins, and then darkness.

The second grating you find — in a different square, nearer the periphery — shows something slightly different. Below it, the space is larger. You cannot see the far wall. The floor below is not empty: there are structures, but they are not buildings. They are low, rounded forms, like the backs of animals surfacing from deep water, made of the same basalt, seamlessly continuous with the floor beneath them. They are not carved. They are *grown* — or deposited, or exuded. They have the look of stalagmites, but their placement is too regular, and their surfaces are too smooth, and each one has at its apex a single opening, dark, roughly circular, the diameter of a human arm.

The openings are warm. You can feel the heat rising from them through the grating. This is the source of the current. The entire ventilation system of the city — such as it is — is driven by these vents in the deep floors, these mouths in the low stone mounds, breathing up into the vault and pulling the air in a slow cycle that has been running since before the concept of air had anyone to experience it.

Whether the mounds are alive is a question that the architecture refuses to answer. They are not machines — they have no metal, no pipes, no moving parts. They are not geological — their placement is too deliberate, their form too consistent, their integration with the vent system too functional. They may be the oldest things in Kell-Oshar. They may be the thing that the city was built to serve — the heart of the organism, down where the copper veins thin out and the powder accumulates and the warmth originates.

Something may live in them. Or they may be the living thing, themselves — a kind of organism that expresses itself as architecture, that builds its body out of basalt and heat and time, that has no need for mobility or metabolism in any form you'd recognize because it has solved the problem of existence so thoroughly that it requires nothing but to sit and be warm and breathe.

You cannot reach them. You can only lie on the floor of a dead city in the dark and press your face to a grating and look down at something that might be an animal the size of a building, might be a vent for something deeper, might be nothing at all — and feel its breath on your face and wonder which of those options is the most frightening, and which is the most reassuring, and realize that in Kell-Oshar the two have become the same.

## The Powder

The powder is alive.

Not in any way that violates chemistry — the iron-sulfur complex is stable, inert, non-reactive under any conditions you can reproduce on the surface. It does not metabolize. It does not grow. It does not reproduce. It does not respond to stimuli. By every biological criterion, it is a mineral byproduct.

But it is alive in the way that a city is alive — not in its constituent particles but in its pattern. The powder moves. Not quickly. Not visibly. But over the span of the system's operation, it has flowed through the pipes in a direction — from the periphery inward, from the small machines to the large ones, from the outer buildings to the central squares, accumulating at the center in concentrations that should be physically impossible for a dry powder in a sealed system with no applied force. The powder migrates. It organizes. It fills pipes selectively — thickening in some, thinning in others, maintaining a distribution that is not random and not uniform but structured, as though it has a preferred configuration and is constantly, imperceptibly, adjusting itself toward it.

The crawlers move it. The machines move it. The air current moves it. Each of these forces is individually insufficient to account for the observed distribution. Together, they are still insufficient. There is something else acting on the powder — some force or tendency or property inherent in the crystalline structure itself — that biases its movement. It is not magnetism. It is not gravity. It is not electrostatic. It is something else, something that the crystal lattice of the iron-sulfur complex exhibits only at the concentrations and configurations found inside the pipes of Kell-Oshar, something that would not be observable in a laboratory sample because a laboratory sample would not have the pipes, the machines, the crawlers, the warmth, the time.

The powder has had time. More time than any substance has a right to have and remain itself. And in that time, it has organized. Not consciously — the powder does not think. But organization does not require thought. Organization requires only persistence, repetition, and a stable medium in which patterns can form and be retained. The pipes provide the medium. The machines provide the persistence. The crawlers provide the repetition. And the powder provides the patterns — patterns that have been forming and dissolving and reforming for so long that the current configuration is not random but optimized, not designed but evolved, the end result of an uncountable number of iterations in which each arrangement of powder in pipes was tested by the system's operation and either retained or discarded based on criteria that the system itself defines.

The powder is the city's nervous system. Not its brain — the city does not think — but its neural tissue, its medium of internal communication, the substance through which information passes from one part of the system to another. The machines generate it. The pipes conduct it. The crawlers maintain it. And the powder, in its slow migration toward the center, carries with it the accumulated state of the entire system — every pressure differential, every temperature gradient, every variation in the breath rate of the machines (and there are variations, so slight and so slow that you would need instruments to detect them, but they exist) — encoded not in symbols but in distribution, in the way a grain of powder here rather than there constitutes a bit of information that the system can read and respond to, not consciously but mechanically, the way a thermostat responds to temperature, the way a root grows toward water.

The city is alive. The city has always been alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Functionally. It is a living system in the same way a cell is a living system — not because it thinks or feels or wants, but because it maintains itself, processes information, responds to its environment (such as it is, sealed and static and unvarying), and persists. It persists. That is the core of it. That is what life does. It persists, and it persists by means of a metabolism that converts input to output through a structured, information-mediated process, and Kell-Oshar does this, has been doing this, will continue to do this, and the fact that its metabolism is mineral rather than organic and its information is encoded in powder distribution rather than nucleotide sequences is a difference of chemistry, not of kind.

## Who Lives Here

No one.

No one has ever lived here. Not in the sense that requires a body, a name, a lifespan. The buildings were never occupied. The doorways were never thresholds. The streets were never walked by anything that could be said to *live* in them. The city was not built for inhabitants. It was not built *by* inhabitants, in any way that the word "inhabitants" would normally imply. It was built by something — a civilization, a process, an intelligence — that did not live in the things it made. That made things to function without it. That made things that would continue after it, that would persist in its absence, that would carry on whatever it was that needed carrying on without the inconvenience of having anyone present to watch or guide or care.

Whoever — whatever — built Kell-Oshar is gone. Has been gone for a span of time that the mesa's geology records in the slow drift of mineral veins, the gradual thickening of pipe deposits, the imperceptible wear of crawler-grooves in metal. They left no trace of themselves. Not because the trace has eroded — nothing erodes here — but because they did not leave one. They did not consider themselves relevant. They built the system and started the system and walked away, or died, or transformed into something that no longer has use for cities or machines or powder, and they left the system running because the system was the point, the system was the product, the system was what they had made and what they wanted to endure, and they did not confuse their own endurance with its endurance.

They are not coming back. This is not a false certainty — it is the only reading consistent with the evidence. The city does not contain provisions for return. There are no living quarters, no food stores, no airlocks or decontamination chambers, no spaces designed for the comfort or survival of anything that breathes oxygen and water and needs to eat. The lift shaft is not an entrance. It is an artifact of whatever survey process located the mesa in the first place. It is a hole that was drilled from outside, by people who were not the builders, who found this place after it was already running, who rode down a slow-cranking lift and stood in the copper dark and felt the thirty-second breath in their chests and knew, the way you know, that this was not made for them.

Nothing lives in Kell-Oshar. Everything lives in Kell-Oshar. The distinction does not survive the place. The machines breathe. The crawlers commute. The powder migrates. The mounds exhale. The city metabolizes. The mesa persists. And you, standing in the dark, feeling the breath in your feet, cracking your lips against the dry air, straining your eyes against the copper murk — you are the most alive thing here, and you are also the most irrelevant. The system does not need you. The system has never needed anything. The system is what need looks like when it has been answered so completely that the question has been forgotten.

You are not its inhabitant. You are its witness. And the witness is not part of the design.

## What Remains

When you leave — and you will leave, because you must — what lives here will continue as it has. The machines will breathe. The crawlers will commute. The powder will migrate. The mounds will exhale. The city will metabolize the mesa at a rate that makes mountains look temporary. The grid will align itself toward a reference point that no longer exists, and the alignment will be no less accurate for the absence. The buildings will stand, empty and final, and the pipes will carry their dark freight through walls and under floors, and the squares will hold their machines like altars holding gods that have no worshipers and no need of them.

The lift will carry you up. The shaft will close behind you, or it won't — it doesn't matter, because the seal of the vault is not in the shaft but in the system itself, in the closed loop of air and warmth and powder that has been cycling for so long that the outside is not a place the system remembers or concerns itself with. You will emerge into whatever weather exists above, and the weather will seem wrong — too fast, too variable, too much a thing of change and chance. You will feel the wind on your face and it will feel like noise. You will feel the sun on your skin and it will feel like interference.

The warmth will stay in your bones for days. Longer, maybe. The thirty-second breath will haunt the silence of your sleep. The copper tinge will persist behind your eyelids, fading so slowly that you will not be able to tell when it is gone, and you will not be able to confirm that it is. You will find yourself pausing in the dark, listening, and not knowing what you are listening for. You will find yourself pressing your ear to walls, to pipes, to the metal housings of machines in the world above, and hearing nothing, and feeling the nothing as a loss.

Kell-Oshar will not miss you. It cannot miss you. It does not have the capacity for missing, because missing requires a relation, and Kell-Oshar does not have relations. It has functions. It has processes. It has a metabolism and a circulation and a maintenance schedule and a pattern of information encoded in powder that has been optimizing itself since before the first organism on the surface above learned to die.

But you will miss it. Not the place — the place is not missable. The *state*. The state of being inside something that is complete. The state of being in the presence of a system that has no use for you and therefore no grievance with you and therefore no judgment of you. The state of being irrelevant and knowing it and finding, in the irrelevance, a kind of peace that you did not expect and cannot reproduce and will not find again, because it is not the product of any belief or practice or philosophy. It is the product of Kell-Oshar. It is what Kell-Oshar makes. It is what has always been made here, in the dark, in the warmth, in the thirty-second breath, for no one, for everyone, for the mesa itself, which does not know it is being metabolized and would not mind if it did.

This is what lives here: a process so old it has become a place. A metabolism so patient it has become a city. A life so thorough that it has no need of the living.

And the crawlers, going about their business in the pipes, in the dark, unhurried, unseen, wearing their grooves a micron deeper with every passage, maintaining the system that maintains them, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end and no purpose beyond its own continuation, which is the oldest and most honest purpose there is.
