# What Lives in Khelm-Vydal

The first answer is: the workers. But that is not the whole answer, and it may not even be the truest answer, and it is certainly not the most interesting one.

---

They are not from here. That matters, but not in the way you might expect.

Most of them came from somewhere east — not a single somewhere, but a chain of somewheres, industrial towns that had finished their purpose and were winding down the way wound-down places wind down: slowly, then all at once, then not at all because there was nothing left to wind. They are mechanics, pipe-fitters, masons, surveyors. They are people who know how to make a thing stand up and stay standing, and when the work ran out where they were, they followed the work here, the way workers do, the way workers always have.

But they have changed. Not dramatically. Not in the way that people change when they cross a sea or learn a new language. They have changed the way a tool changes when you use it for a task it was not designed for but can still perform. They are worn into new shapes by the work here, by the specific pressures of this place, and the new shapes are still recognizably the old shapes — you can see what they were — but the edges are different. The handles are different. They fit a different hand now.

Their hands are the first thing you notice. Large, all of them. Not necessarily broad, but long — long fingers, wide palms, the hands of people who spend their days gripping wet pipe and fitting it into wet junctions and applying sealant with their thumbs because the sealant knives don't work in the cold. The condensation gets into everything. The sealant sets slower in the damp. They have learned to feel when a joint is right, not by looking at it but by the way it resists when they push, and this is a thing you learn with your hands, not your eyes, and their hands have learned it. The skin on their knuckles is cracked in thin lines that fill with rust-colored grit that is not rust — it is the mineral dust from the aggregate, the fine particles that the basalt exhales, and it gets into everything the way the condensation gets into everything, and the workers' hands are stained the color of old brick.

They do not speak much. You knew this. But the reason matters. It is not fatigue, though they are tired — the work is hard and the hours are long and the cold sits in the joints like a second kind of ache layered over the first. It is that sound here is swallowed. The damp air absorbs noise. A sentence spoken at normal volume reaches the next person as a murmur, and the next person after that hears nothing. They tried shouting, in the early days. The surveyors' notes mention this — a brief experiment with raised voices that produced nothing but a flat, dead sound that fell out of the air immediately, like a stone dropped into mud. So they stopped. They developed the hand signals instead. A closed fist means *hold*. An open palm means *release*. Two fingers pointed down means *there is condensation coming* — they can feel it, apparently, in their hands, a subtle shift in the pressure of the air against the skin, a tightening, and they signal it and everyone stops and moves under the awnings and waits.

They have a language, though. They speak it in the evenings, in the central district, in the close-packed buildings where the boilers run and the light is yellow and the moisture in the air becomes visible, becomes beautiful almost, a fine golden particulate that hangs in the lamplight. You hear them then — low, melodious, with a cadence that sounds to you like a river you have never visited. The consonants are soft. The vowels are long. It is a language built for a room, not a street, a language for close quarters and warm air. You suspect it is not actually the language they spoke in the towns they came from. You suspect it is something they are building here, the way they are building everything here — from available materials, under pressure, with what is at hand.

They sleep in the upper floors of the central district, in bunks that are built into the walls like shelves, and they sleep close together, not because the space is limited — there are half-built floors above them that are empty — but because the cold is specific and physical and it does not warm to a single body. They sleep in pairs, sometimes in threes, and the sound of their breathing is the sound of the boilers — a low, rhythmic hiss, the sound of a system cycling.

They do not dream here. They say this. Or rather, they say they do not remember dreaming, which is different, and when you press them on it, one of them — a pipe-fitter with hands so stained they look carved from the aggregate itself — says: "The ground dreams enough." And will not explain what that means.

---

The pale creatures in the lakes are not what you would call inhabitants. They are more like the vegetation — present, inevitable, filling a space because the space exists and something must fill it. But they are alive, in whatever sense that word means here, and they are worth describing.

They are the size of a finger, the size of two fingers. Pale, as you noticed. Not white — translucent, actually, a faint opalescence that makes them look like they are made of the same mineral water they live in. They have many legs, and the legs are fine, like the threads of a fern frond, and they move in a sculling motion that is not swimming — they are not buoyant enough for that — but walking, walking on the surface tension of the water, walking through it, their bodies half-submerged, their legs dimpling the surface.

They are not insects. You were right about this. They have no segmentation that you can identify. They have no eyes that you can find. They have — and this is the part that the surveyors did not write down but that you observed yourself — a kind of mouth, or a mouth-like structure, a fold in the anterior end that opens and closes with a regularity that suggests breathing, though you do not think they breathe air. They breathe the water. They eat the water. The mineral content of the water, specifically — the dissolved silicates, the calcium, the faint traces of the marrow that seeps up through the basalt cracks. They are processors. They take what the ground provides and they incorporate it, and their shells — or whatever their outer surface is — become harder over time, more mineral, more like the glassite in the hills. The ones near the outflow pipes, the ones that live in the processing waste, are the largest and the most opaque. They are almost crystalline. When they die — and they do die, you have seen the husks of them in the gutters, small pale shards like broken fingernails — they leave behind a residue that is almost pure mineral, a fine grit that the moss grows on, that the ferns absorb through their roots, that becomes part of the soil that is being made here.

They are a cycle. They are a step in a process that the ground is performing, using them as instruments. You do not think they know this. You do not think knowing is something they do. They simply live in the water and eat the minerals and die and become part of the ground, and the ground uses them, and they are the ground's smallest workers, and they do not have hands.

They click. You mentioned the clicking. It is not communication — you are almost sure of this. It is mechanical. The leg-tips striking the surface of the water, or striking each other, or striking the mineral deposits that form on their shells. It is the sound of small hard things touching other small hard things in a wet place. It is the sound of a process happening. When the condensation begins and the gutters fill, the clicking rises from every surface — from the pipe arrays, from the puddles, from the crooks of the scaffolding where water has collected. It is the town's smallest voice. It is the voice of the ground making itself heard through the bodies of things that are barely bodies.

---

The moss-dwellers are different. They have weight. Not much — you could hold one on your palm and not feel burdened — but they are present in a way that the lake creatures are not. The lake creatures are impressions. The moss-dwellers are assertions.

Flat, you said. Segmented. Yes. They are built like sentences — one clause after another, each one complete, each one connected to the last, the whole thing moving in a line that has direction and purpose. They are the color of the moss they live in, which is dark green, almost black, and this is not camouflage or coincidence — it is diet. They eat the moss. They eat the dead fern fronds. They eat the mucilage. They eat the mineral grit left by the lake creatures' husks. They eat everything that is not actively alive and growing, and some things that are, and their bodies are compost heaps with legs, walking refineries, and they are the reason the streets are passable.

The workers respect them. You noticed this — the worker pausing to let one cross a beam. This is not sentimentality. The workers need the moss-dwellers. Without them, the cut fronds would pile in the streets and the moss would grow over the pile and the ferns would grow over the moss and the town would be swallowed in a week. The moss-dwellers are the counterforce. They are the thing that eats the growth. They are the town's immune system, in a sense, and the workers know this, and they treat them accordingly.

They are not pets. The workers do not touch them. The workers do not name them. But there is an accommodation — a wide berth, a pause, a recognition that these small flat determined things are partners in the work of keeping the town standing, and that they were here first, or if not first, then at least before the workers arrived, which is almost the same thing in a place this new.

---

The canopy-things are harder to describe because you have never seen one clearly.

You have heard them. The papery rustling in the trees — a dry sound, the only dry sound in Khelm-Vydal, and that dryness is itself a kind of signature. Everything else here is wet. The canopy-things are dry. Their membranes — the wing-like structures, the things that are not wings but serve the same purpose — are thin and dark and they do not collect moisture. The condensation beads on them and rolls off. They are the only surface in this town that the water does not adhere to.

This means something. You think it means they are new. Not new in the way the town is new, but new in the way the lake creatures are new — recently emerged, recently arrived, recently invented by whatever process invents things in a place where the niches are empty. But the lake creatures are water-things. They are of the water. The canopy-things are — not. They are of the air, or of the dryness between condensation events, or of the space between things. They are what fills the gap when the air is not actively being water.

They eat the mucilage on the fern fronds. You said this. They are, in a sense, in competition with the moss-dwellers, who also eat the mucilage — but the moss-dwellers eat the fallen fronds, the dead material, and the canopy-things eat the living fronds, the growing material, the mucilage that the plant is still producing. They are on opposite sides of a line that the plant itself does not know exists.

They drop and glide. You have seen this — the small dark shapes detaching from one tree and descending in a shallow arc to the next, the membranes spreading, the body small and light and — you think — furred, or furred-like, covered in something fine and dark that is not feathers and not hair. They make no sound in the air. The sound is only the membranes against the leaves when they land, a brief rustle, and then stillness, and then they are gone into the canopy and you cannot find them again.

They are afraid of the light. Not the daylight — the flat gray daylight does not seem to bother them — but the electric light. When the factory district's bulbs come on in the evening, the canopy-things withdraw deeper into the trees, away from the central district, toward the northern edge where the buildings stop and the vegetation begins. You think they are sensitive to the particular quality of electric light, the warmth of it, the yellowness. It is not a spectrum they recognize. It is a color that does not exist in nature here, because nature here is gray and green and dark and wet, and yellow is the color of something else, something that came from outside, and the canopy-things know this in the way that animals know things — without knowing, without language, with the part of the body that flinches.

---

And then there is the large thing. The thing in the northern district. The thing that leaves tracks.

You have not seen it. You have seen the tracks. You described them: broad, deep, a pattern that is not a hoof and not a paw. Let me try to say what the pattern is.

It is a hand.

Not a human hand. Not a primate hand. But a hand — something with digits, with joints, with a structure that presses into the ground in a sequence that implies grasping, or at least a grasping geometry, a spread of weight that suggests the ground is being held, not simply walked on. The digits are wide. There are — you counted — four? Five? The number is hard because the prints overlap slightly, the digits pressing into the soft ground and then shifting, and the shape that remains is a smear of intention rather than a clean impression.

It is heavy. The depth of the tracks tells you this. The ground in the northern district is soft — still compacting, still forming — and the tracks sink deep, three inches, four, which means significant weight on a relatively small footprint. Something dense. Something with dense bones. Something that is, in its way, made of the ground — or made of something close to the ground, something mineral, something that belongs to the basalt and the clay and the translucent veins and the marrow.

The workers will not talk about it. You said this. But you noticed something else: they do not talk about it in the way that people do not talk about something they are afraid of. They talk about it in the way that people do not talk about something they are waiting for. There is a difference. Fear avoids. Waiting anticipates. The workers do not avoid the northern district. They simply do not go there after dark. And when they come back in the morning, they check the tracks — quickly, casually, the way you check whether it rained overnight — and then they begin work. The tracks are data. The tracks tell them something about the night, about what the ground did, about what is forming, and the workers read them the way they read the condensation: as a signal in a system they are part of but do not control.

What is it? I do not know. The workers do not know. It may not know itself. It may be — and this is the thing you keep coming back to — still deciding. Like the ground. Like the town. Like everything here. It may be something that is forming, that is emerging, that is becoming what it will be by the act of walking from the tree line to the lake and back, night after night, in a pattern that is not a path yet but will be, eventually, when the ground hardens and the grass grows and the pattern is worn deep enough to be permanent.

It goes to the lake. It drinks, perhaps. Or it does something else — something that the lake creatures are part of, that the mineral water is part of, that the marrow seeping up through the basalt is part of. It may be the largest expression of the same process that the lake creatures express at the smallest scale: incorporation. The ground taking what it needs. The ground growing.

The workers know it is there. The workers have always known it is there, or at least since they arrived, since the first night when the ground groaned and the tracks appeared and the cold sat on the town like a hand. They do not speak of it because it is not theirs to speak of. It belongs to the ground. It is the ground's. And the ground is not finished yet.

---

There is one more thing that lives here, and it is not a creature and it is not a person and it is not a plant, but it lives here in the way that a process lives, in the way that a fire lives, and it is the town itself.

Khelm-Vydal is alive. Not metaphorically. Or rather — yes, metaphorically, but the metaphor is load-bearing, the metaphor is structural, and if you remove it the building falls. The town is a thing that is being made, that is making itself, that is growing the way the ferns grow — not by effort but by the simple physics of a system reaching equilibrium. The workers are part of it. The moss-dwellers are part of it. The marrow in the pipes is its blood. The condensation is its breath. The boilers are its lungs. The scaffolding is its skeleton, still forming, still growing upward. The streets are its — what? Its gut, its lanes of transit, the paths along which its materials move. The gutters are its veins. The lake is its — you do not want to say kidney. You do not want to say anything so precise. The lake is a place where something is processed. Something is being taken apart and put back together in a form that the ground can use.

The town lives the way a body lives when it is being assembled in utero — not yet functional, not yet integrated, but already a system, already a pattern, already more than the sum of its parts, and growing toward something that no one, not even the ground itself, can fully predict.

The workers know this. They are midwives and components. They are the hands that build the body that they themselves are part of, and they do not speak of this because there is nothing to say. It is the condition of their work. It is the ground they stand on. It is the fact of the place, as basic and as unquestionable as the dampness and the cold and the pulse of the pumps in the soles of their feet.

---

So: what lives in Khelm-Vydal?

Workers with stained hands and a language they are inventing. Lake creatures that are becoming mineral. Moss-dwellers that are becoming soil. Canopy-things that are becoming air. Something large that is becoming — you do not know what. A ground that is becoming ground. A town that is becoming a town. And the space between all of these things, which is no space at all, because everything here is touching, everything here is pressed against everything, and the touching is the thing, the touching is the life, the touching is what happens in a place that is not finished and does not know what it will be when it is and does not, perhaps, want to know, because the becoming is the thing, the forming is the point, and when it is finished — if it is ever finished — something will have been lost that cannot be recovered, which is the energy of a place that does not yet know its own rules and is writing them as it goes, in the damp, in the cold, in the flat gray light, with the ferns climbing and the moss growing and the creatures clicking and the pumps pulsing and the ground groaning in the night like something dreaming, or something being dreamed.
