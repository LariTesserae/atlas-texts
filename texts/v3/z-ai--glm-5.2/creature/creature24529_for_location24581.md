# What Lives Here

They are not what you would expect to find, and they know this, though "know" is perhaps too strong a word for what happens inside a body that has been shaped by the same forces that shaped the basalt.

---

## The Filterers

Call them fish because you have no better word. They are fish the way a glove is a hand — the shape is correct, the function is approximately right, but the thing itself is something else.

They are pale. Not the pallor of cave fish, not the bleached absence of creatures that have abandoned pigment as unnecessary. Their color is *specific*. It is the blue-grey of the water they live in, matched so precisely that a motionless individual is invisible at ten meters. You see them only when they move, when the angle of light off their flanks shifts by a fraction of a degree and betrays the boundary between animal and not-animal. This is not camouflage in the way you learned it — not a strategy, not a defense. It is the result of building a body from the same materials the water carries. Their skin is mineral. Their scales, if they are scales, are laid down the same way the mineral deposits lay down on the steel structures: layer by layer, atom by atom, from the dissolved geology they swim through. They are made of the place they live in. They are the place they live in, condensed into motion.

Their eyes dominate the head. Not the bulbous telescopic eyes of deep-sea fish, not the flat light-gathering patches of animals adapted to darkness. These are *lenses* — wide, spherical, almost crystalline, set in sockets that allow them to rotate independently. They are built for clarity. In water this transparent, with visibility this extreme, the eye that wins is the eye that can resolve detail at sixty meters, that can distinguish a columnar tower from a metal structure at the limit of sight, that can detect the slow-falling mineral snow against the pale blue field of the water. Their eyes are the most complex organs in this ecosystem, and they use them constantly. The schools move with a coordination that is not instinct, not habit, but *vision* — each fish watching every other fish, each body adjusting its position based on the positions of dozens of others, the entire school functioning as a distributed eye that sees in every direction simultaneously.

They do not sleep. Or if they do, they sleep in motion, some fraction of the school slowing while the rest maintain their patterns, the sluggish ones carried along by the current and the collective momentum of their kin. There is no shelter here. No burrow, no crevice, no darkness to retreat into. The plateau offers nothing to hide behind, and so they have stopped hiding. They are always exposed. They are always visible. They are always watching.

They filter feed. Their mouths are wide and always open, semi-circular gaps in the front of the skull that channel water across gill rakers so dense they appear solid. The rakers are not organic tissue — or not only organic. They are mineralized, reinforced with silica and calcium carbonate drawn from the water itself, and they function as sieves with a mesh size measured in microns. What they catch is not plankton. There is no plankton here. What they catch is the mineral snow — the slow rain of calcium, silica, iron, and manganese oxides that falls from the water column as the plateau's volcanic chemistry precipitates out of solution. They are eating rock. They are digesting the inorganic leavings of geology and converting it, through some biochemistry that no textbook describes, into the tissue of their own bodies. Their guts are long, much longer than a fish of their size should require, coiled in the abdomen like a second spine, and they process the mineral load with a slowness that matches the slowness of the snow itself. A fish that has fed for twelve hours has gained weight that would be imperceptible on a human scale. But they do not need to grow fast. They need to grow *continuously*, and they do.

They school in the spaces between the towers. The columnar basalt creates corridors — hexagonal canyons thirty meters deep, their walls sharp-edged and vertical — and the schools move through these corridors like current through a circuit, following paths that seem arbitrary but that, over time, reveal themselves as loops. Circuits. Each school has a route that carries it through a specific sequence of corridors and back to its starting point, and the route takes hours to complete, and the school completes it again, and again, and again. The routes are not random. They correlate with the fissures — the schools pass over the deep cracks in the basalt where the water moves fastest, where the mineral snow is densest, where the upwelling brings the richest concentration of dissolved material from below. They are not following a plan. They are following a gradient. They are doing what water does — finding the path of least resistance through a system that offers them exactly enough to continue.

They have no predators. Or rather — the things that might eat them do not hunt them in any way that implies pursuit. The shadows at the edge of visibility do not give chase. They simply *are*, and when a filterer dies — from age, from exhaustion, from some internal failure that causes it to slow and drift out of its school's orbit — the shadows materialize. They arrive the way the mineral snow arrives: slowly, without urgency, from the blue distance. What they do with the body is not visible. The body is gone. The school does not react. The circuit continues.

## The Grazers

They are on every metal surface. You do not see them until you are close enough to touch the structures, and then you realize the structures are not still.

They are flat. Not flat the way a flounder is flat — compressed laterally, still recognizably fish-shaped — but flat the way a file is flat, a thing designed to lie against a surface and move across it. Their bodies are oval, perhaps fifteen centimeters long, and they are segmented in a way that suggests arthropod ancestry without committing to it. The segments do not articulate. They flex. Each segment carries a row of legs — short, bristled, too numerous to count at a glance — and the legs move in metachronal waves, each row slightly offset from the one behind it, so that the animal appears to ripple across the metal surface like a message traveling along a wire. They are fast. Faster than they should be in water this cold. Their metabolism is not driven by temperature. It is driven by the sound.

This is the thing that takes longest to understand.

The plateau hums. The basalt vibrates. The metal structures, anchored to the basalt, vibrate with it, and the vibration is stronger in the steel than in the rock — steel resonates more efficiently than stone, amplifies the frequency, turns the structures into tuning forks struck by an unseen hand. The grazers live on the structures because the structures vibrate, and the grazers *feed on the vibration*. Not directly — they are not eating sound, that is not what is happening. What is happening is that the vibration loosens the mineral deposits that accumulate on the steel. The dissolved metals in the water — iron, manganese, copper, zinc — plate themselves onto the structures through a process of galvanic deposition, building up layer by layer, and the grazers eat this deposit. Their legs are not just legs. They are scrapers. Each bristled limb terminates in a mineralized tip, a chitinous rasp that breaks the metallic crust free from the steel and channels it toward the mouth, which is on the underside of the body, a slot that runs the full length of the ventral surface. They move and eat simultaneously. They are conveyor belts. They are machines that happen to be alive.

They have no eyes. They do not need them. Their world is a surface — flat, dark, vibrating — and they navigate it by touch, by the chemical gradient of the mineral deposits they feed on, by the vibration itself, which tells them whether they are on steel (resonant, rich) or basalt (dull, poor). They avoid the basalt. They cluster on the metal. They are the reason the structures are still legible — the reason you can still see bolts and flanges and pipe diameters is because the grazers have kept the surfaces clean of the mineral accretion that would, by now, have buried them. They are not doing this for the structures. They are doing it because the structures are their food, and eating is what they do, and the structures persist because they are being eaten, and they are being eaten because they persist. It is a circle. It is equilibrium.

They reproduce on the structures. You see the evidence in patches — clusters of small, pale ovals adhered to the steel in the sheltered angles where pipes meet beams, where the vibration is slightly damped. The ovals are the same color as the steel, and they are mineralized — a hard shell of deposited metal around an organic core. They do not hatch quickly. In water this cold, with metabolisms this slow, development takes months, perhaps a year. When a juvenile emerges, it is already flat, already scraping, already in motion. It does not have a childhood. It does not have a learning period. It knows what to do because what to do is the only thing its body can do, and it does it until it dies, and when it dies its body adheres to the steel and is mineralized and eventually eaten by other grazers. Nothing is lost. Nothing is added. The system maintains itself.

## The Shadows

You see them at the edge of visibility. You see them because they are large enough to displace the blue light at a distance, and because they move, and because nothing else at that distance moves. They are the reason the filterers school — not because the shadows hunt them, but because the shadows exist, and the filterers have evolved to be invisible to anything that sees the way they see, and schooling is the redundancy that ensures that if one fish is seen, the school is not.

The shadows do not approach. They maintain a distance that is remarkably consistent — fifty-five meters, perhaps sixty — right at the limit of what the filterers' eyes can resolve. This suggests the shadows know the visual range of the filterers. This suggests the shadows are aware of being watched. Or it suggests nothing — it suggests only that the shadows prefer to operate in the narrow band where they are visible enough to be known but not close enough to be detailed, and that this preference has been stable for long enough that the filterers have evolved around it.

What they are is difficult to say. They are not fish. Their motion is wrong — too slow, too deliberate, too *horizontal*. They do not school. They do not appear to interact with each other. Each shadow is alone, or each shadow is the only one you can see, and the others — if there are others — are farther out, beyond the blue haze, in the water that is too far away to carry shape.

They are not filter feeders. They do not graze. They do not need the mineral snow or the metal deposits. What they need is not visible in the water column, not measurable in the chemistry of the plateau. They are the one element of the ecosystem that does not fit the mineral-into-life model that explains everything else. They are the thing that suggests the model is incomplete.

They may be predators. The filterers that die — the ones that slow, that drift, that fall out of their circuits — are gone quickly, and the shadows are the only candidates for the removal. But the shadows do not hunt. They do not pursue. They *arrive*. They arrive the way the sound arrives — from everywhere and nowhere, as a condition of the place rather than an event within it. They are the cleanup. They are the mechanism by which the system prevents accumulation, prevents the buildup of dead tissue that would disrupt the equilibrium. They are necessary. They are the reason the plateau is not littered with the bodies of filterers who reached the end of their circuits and stopped.

They may be old. This is speculation, but it is speculation the place invites. The filterers live fast — or as fast as a mineral-eating fish in two-degree water can live, which is not fast at all, but which is finite. The grazers live and die on the structures they clean. The shadows do not appear to live and die. They appear to *persist*. They are always there. They are always at the same distance. They are always the same size, the same shape, the same absence of detail. If they reproduce, you do not see it. If they feed, you do not see it. If they communicate, they do it in a register that the sound drowns out — or they do it in the sound itself, in the vibration that passes through everything, and their bodies are the receivers, and the plateau is speaking to them in a language that has no words, only frequency, only the constant roar of water through stone.

## What Else

There may be more. The fissures go deep, and the water that moves through them comes from somewhere, and the somewhere may contain things that never reach the surface of the plateau. The mineral snow falls from above, but it also rises from below — the upwelling through the cracks carries material from deeper in the volcanic system, and the chemistry of that material is different, richer in some elements, depleted in others, and it is possible that in the fissures, in the dark narrow channels where the water moves too fast for anything to pause, there are organisms adapted to conditions that the plateau's surface does not represent. You do not see them. You may never see them. They are implied by the system's logic — the system produces niches the way the basalt produces columns, inevitably, geometrically, as a consequence of its structure.

And there may be microbial life. The description said the rock was bare, the surfaces clean, and this is true at the scale of the eye. But at the scale of the molecule, the basalt is not bare. The mineral veins — the pale volcanic glass that catches the blue light like circuitry — are reactive. They exchange ions with the water. They create chemical gradients. And where there are chemical gradients, there are organisms that exploit them, organisms too small to see, too simple to notice, too important to ignore. They may be the base of the food web that the filterers sit atop. They may be the reason the mineral snow is the way it is — not raw geology but processed geology, geology that has passed through the metabolisms of a thousand microscopic species and emerged as the slow rain of particles that the filterers catch in their mineralized gills. The plateau may be alive in a way that has nothing to do with the fish or the grazers or the shadows. It may be alive in its rock, in its water, in the chemical conversation between stone and ocean that has been running since the basalt first rose from the seafloor.

---

## How They Live

They live in the sound. This is the final thing, the thing that ties the fish and the grazers and the shadows and the unseen microbes into a single system. The sound is not background. The sound is not environment. The sound is *infrastructure*.

The vibration that hums through the basalt and the steel and the water is the engine that drives the mineral chemistry. It agitates the dissolved metals, keeping them in suspension long enough to be caught by the filterers' gills. It loosens the deposits on the structures, making them available to the grazers. It carries information — the pressure changes, the temperature differentials, the rhythm of a thermal system that breathes through stone — and the animals read it, not with ears but with bodies, with the mineralized tissues that resonate at the plateau's frequency and tell them where the upwellings are, where the deposits are thickest, where the circuits lead.

The sound is the place. The animals are the sound, condensed into bodies, moving through the medium that produced them. They did not arrive here. They emerged here, the way the basalt emerged — from the interaction of heat and water and mineral, from the forces that built the plateau and continue to operate through it. They are geology that learned to swim. They are rock that opened its eyes.

The plateau does not know they are here. The plateau does not know *it* is here. It is a system without self-awareness, a machine without an operator, running on the fuel of its own internal contradictions — heat against cold, pressure against vacuum, rock against water. The animals are a consequence. The structures are a consequence. The sound is a consequence. Everything here is a consequence of something else, and the chain of consequences extends back to the formation of the seafloor and forward to the eventual cooling of the basalt, the slowing of the current, the silence that will come when the thermal engine exhausts itself and the plateau becomes what every plateau becomes: a feature on a map, a name on a chart, a place where nothing lives because there is no longer anything to live on.

But that is not yet. The basalt is young. The sound is loud. The fish are schooling. The grazers are cleaning. The shadows are watching. The mineral snow is falling. The light is arriving, blue and spent, from a surface that does not know what lies below. And the plateau is humming, and the humming is everything, and everything is alive.
