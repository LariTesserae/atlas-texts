# What Lives in the Vatspring

---

The pale things first, because they are what you would notice if you stayed long enough — and because they are the closest thing to a conventional inhabitant the Vatspring has.

They are not numerous. A careful count, over hours, might yield seven. Perhaps nine. They move between the pools that surface briefly in the root-mat and sink back down, and so they are hard to track — a pool appears, and if you are watching, you may see a translucent shape drift into it from underneath, nosing along the root fibers at its edge. They are the length of a finger or a little longer. They have no legs. They have no eyes that you can distinguish from the general paleness of their bodies. A faint internal structure is visible through the skin — a dark thread running head to tail, which could be a gut or a nerve cord, and two denser spots near the anterior end that could be anything. They drift more than they swim. When the pump thuds and the mat shudders, they contract — a single ripple along the body, a momentary stiffening — and then relax and continue their slow inspection of whatever pool they are in.

They eat, you think, what the root-mat produces: the soft breakdown products of the fermentation you can smell, the fine organic sediment that collects in the pools. They seem to filter it from the water as they move. Their mouths — if they are mouths — are permanent slits, barely visible, held open to the current. They are the only consumers in a system that is otherwise producer and mechanism. They occupy the narrowest possible niche: they take what the mat exudes, and they return it, processed, to the water, which carries it back down to the roots. Whether the system needs them is unclear. They may be a remnant, like the iron grating and the stenciled number — a species that entered when the Vatspring was different and has persisted in this reduced form because nothing has come along to dislodge them. Or they may be integral, a cleanup mechanism so fine-tuned it has become invisible.

They do not react to you. You could put your hand in a pool and one would drift past your fingers without altering its course. They have no predators. They have, perhaps, no reason to fear anything, and so fear has left them, or never developed. They are as placid as the system they live in.

But they are not what truly lives here.

---

What truly lives here is the Vatspring.

This requires some unpacking, because it is not a metaphor. Or rather — it is a metaphor in the same way that calling a coral reef "alive" is a metaphor: it is accurate and it is insufficient. The Vatspring is a single living process in the way that a reef is, and possibly in the way that a forest is, and possibly in a way that neither of those quite captures, because the mechanical components introduce a variable that reefs and forests do not have.

Consider the boundary problem. Where does the plant end and the machine begin? The answer, after enough time in the Vatspring, becomes: the question is wrong. The pipe that carries water through the vine-buttress is not a pipe that a vine has grown around. It is a pipe-vine, a hybrid structure in which the iron provides rigidity and the root tissue provides hydraulic regulation, and neither could maintain its current form without the other. Remove the iron and the vine would collapse under its own weight in the particular arch it has learned. Remove the vine and the iron would corrode through in a season, the water finding new paths through the breaches. They are one thing.

The pump is the clearest case. It sits beneath the root-mat — you cannot see it, but you can feel it, and in one place, where the mat thins over a hard surface, you can kneel and press your palm flat and feel the vibration transmitted through iron and root fiber into your bones. It is a mechanical pump. It has a housing, a flywheel, a chamber. These facts are not in doubt. But the roots have entered it. They have threaded the interior the way they threaded the pipes, and the chamber that the pump acts upon is no longer a pure mechanical space — it is lined with root tissue that flexes and swells with the water pressure, and the pump's cycle is timed not by a cam or a crank but by the elasticity of that tissue, which fills and resists and then yields, and the pump strokes, and the tissue rebounds, and the cycle begins again. Is the pump pumping water through the roots, or are the roots operating the pump? The system has forgotten. The distinction was relevant once and is relevant no longer.

What lives here, then, is an entity — or a process, or an organism, depending on which word you find least misleading — that is composed of iron and vine and root and moss and fungus and water and the slow motion of a pump that has been cycling for longer than the stenciled number on its housing has been legible. It does not think. It does not want. It maintains itself. It responds to perturbation the way a river responds to a boulder: by flowing around it, by eroding it, by incorporating it into its own pattern. The Vatspring has been doing this for so long that the original intent of the human builders — whatever it was, water filtration, irrigation, climate control, something else — has been subsumed into a homeostatic loop that serves only its own continuation. The system does not produce anything. It does not filter water for anyone. It filters water for itself. It pumps water through itself. It grows into its own infrastructure and replaces it as it corrodes. It is, by any functional definition, alive.

Its life is slow. Its life is patient. Its life is so distributed across so many tissues and mechanisms that it has no center, no organ whose removal would kill it — or rather, every organ's removal would kill it, eventually, the way removing enough parts from any organism would. But it has redundancy. The vines could lose a buttress and reroute. The pipes could breach and the roots would find new paths. The pump — the pump is harder. The pump is the heartbeat, and if it stopped, the water would stop cycling, and the mist would stop settling, and the leaves would dry, and the equilibrium would shift. But the pump has not stopped. The pump has, you suspect, not stopped for a very long time. The root tissue that times its cycle is renewed constantly, cell by cell, as old root dies and new root grows in its place, and so the pump is timed by tissue that is always new and always old, always being replaced, always the same. It is the Ship of Theseus, and it is floating.

---

And the fungi.

The fungi deserve their own attention because they are doing something that neither the plants nor the machines are doing, and it might be the most important thing happening in the Vatspring.

The bracket fungi on the walls — the fan-shaped formations, layered like shelving, pale and dark in alternating bands — are not decorative. They are not passive. If you watch one for long enough — and you would have to watch for a long time, hours, a day — you would notice that its edge grows. Slowly. A millimeter a day, perhaps. It grows toward the iron. Where a bracket's expanding edge meets a pipe, it does not grow over the pipe. It grows *into* the pipe — or rather, into the corrosion layer on the pipe's surface. The fungal hyphae penetrate the rust, and they are doing something there. Breaking down the iron oxide. Taking the iron up. Translocating it — where?

Follow the fungal body inward, away from the bracket and into the wall. The mycelium threads through the mesh of moss and vine and root in a network so fine it is invisible to the eye but present to the touch as a faint velvet texture on every surface. And the mycelium connects, eventually, to the roots. To the interior of the roots. The iron that the fungi liberate from the corroding pipes is deposited — in small, molecular amounts — in the root tissue, where it becomes part of the vine's structure, part of its rigidity, part of its ability to hold the arches and buttresses that give the Vatspring its shape.

The fungi are eating the machines and feeding the iron to the plants.

This is not destruction. This is renovation. The machines are failing — slowly, over centuries, but failing — and as they fail, their substance is being incorporated into the biological structure that has already taken over their function. The iron becomes vine-wood. The mechanical becomes organic. The Vatspring is converting itself, piece by piece, from a hybrid system into a pure biological system, and it is using the fungi as its digestive apparatus.

When the conversion is complete — in a hundred years, in five hundred — the machines will be gone. The pump will be a memory in the shape of a root-arch. The pipes will be veins of iron-enriched wood. The Vatspring will continue to cycle water through itself, but it will do so by purely biological means, the way a tree cycles water from root to leaf, and the thudding will stop, replaced by a silence that is not silence but the sound of water moving through living tissue under its own pressure.

The pale things in the pools will still be there, or their descendants will, drifting and filtering, unchanged, because their niche does not depend on the machines. They will be the only witnesses to the transition, and they will not know they are witnessing anything.

---

There is one more thing that lives here, or that lived here, and it is the reason for the bracket and the grating and the number.

Someone maintained the Vatspring, once. Not built it — possibly not even built it — but maintained it. Tended the machines. Replaced parts. Kept the pump cycling when the root tissue had not yet learned its rhythm. Trained the first vines, perhaps, or at least did not tear them out when they began to grow where they were not wanted. This person, or these people, lived in or near the Vatspring for long enough to leave marks — the bracket that held a lamp, perhaps, or a sensor, or a tool. The grating that was a walkway over the wet floor. The stencil that identified the pump for inventory purposes.

They are gone. The Vatspring did not need them to stay, and they did not stay. But the Vatspring did not reject them either. The bracket remains, held by the vine that grew around it. The grating remains, suspended at an angle its makers did not intend. The number remains, illegible, a name fading into its substrate. The Vatspring has incorporated the marks of its keepers the way it has incorporated everything else — not by choice, not by design, but by the simple process of growing around what is present and not growing away from what does not interfere.

The keepers are gone, but their fingerprints are part of the organism now. The iron they bolted to the wall is becoming vine-wood. The grating they walked on is being digested. Even their intent — the purpose they installed the machines for, the function they designed the system to perform — has been metabolized, broken down into raw pattern, and reassembled as something the Vatspring does for itself. The keepers did not create the Vatspring. They created the conditions under which the Vatspring could create itself, and then they left, and the Vatspring continued.

---

So: what lives in the Vatspring?

Seven or nine pale, legless, eyeless things in the root-pools, drifting and filtering, ancient and indifferent.

A mycelial network that is slowly eating the mechanical infrastructure and translocating its iron into the vine-structure, performing a renovation so gradual it is indistinguishable from geology.

A system — an organism, a process — composed of plant and machine and fungus and water, that has been cycling for longer than anyone has been counting, that does not think and does not want and does not need anything external, that is in the process of converting itself from hybrid to pure biological, that will eventually forget it was ever partly iron.

And the ghost of a purpose, embedded in the growth like a fossil in matrix — the intent of people who are gone, still faintly legible in the shape of the arches and the placement of the pipes, still being slowly digested into something the Vatspring can use.
