# What Lives There

## The People

They are not what you would expect. Or rather — they are exactly what you would expect if you thought about it long enough, which most visitors don't, because most visitors are too busy gaping at the walls.

The people of Külhane are *soft*. Not in the way of weakness — in the way of material. They are soft the way bark-cloth is soft after it has been beaten and soaked and worked for days, which is to say: fibrous, dense, pliable, and nearly impossible to tear. Their skin has a quality that visitors from drier climates often misread as frailty. It is not frailty. It is adaptation. After enough generations in air that never drops below saturation, the skin stops fighting for moisture and starts *managing* it. The people here have skin like the undersides of leaves — slightly cooler than the ambient temperature, faintly dewy to the touch, porous in a way that dryer-climate skin is not. Their pores are open. They transpire. A Külhane person working in the market on a humid morning is visibly damp, not from sweat but from the same process that makes the leaves damp: the air is full and the body is full and the excess finds its way out.

They are not tall. This is not stunting — there is no malnutrition here; the food is abundant and varied and protein-rich between the frogs, the pigs, the eggs, the insects, and the dozen edible plants that grow in and on every surface. They are not tall because the space does not reward tallness. The canopy is close. The doorways are trained to the height of the people who use them, and the people who use them are, on average, a hand's width shorter than the lowland average. The architecture and the bodies have co-evolved. A tall person would hit their head. A tall person's children would hit their heads. The town has selected, gently and over centuries, for compactness.

Their hands are remarkable. You notice this in the market, where a woman is cutting a length of living fiber-rope from its parent vine with a blade that is barely larger than a thumbnail — a curved sliver of bamboo, edge-honed, and her fingers move along the vine with a fluency that is less like tool use and more like the vine is an extension of her own nervous system. She finds the nodes by touch. She reads the growth direction by pressure. She makes the cut in a place that will heal cleanly and not compromise the vine's structural integrity, and she does this in under three seconds, while talking to someone else, while a macaque is trying to steal a bundle of bark-cloth from the stall behind her. Her hands are calloused, but not in the way a carpenter's hands are calloused — not at the palm or the base of the fingers. The calluses are at the *tips*, at the pads, at the places where she touches living plants. She has the hands of a person who reads the world through touch the way a blind person reads through fingertips, except she is not blind. She just lives in a place where the most important information — the health of a wall, the water content of a root, the readiness of a graft — is tactile.

They all have these hands.

### What They Know

The knowledge base of a Külhane resident is not like yours. It is not organized around reading and writing, though some of them can do both — there is a script, a modification of a lowland alphabet, used for marking family lineages on bark-cloth and for recording grafting schedules on wooden tallies. But the core knowledge, the knowledge that matters, the knowledge that keeps the town standing and the water flowing and the walls from closing over your doorway while you sleep, is *somatic*. It lives in the hands and in the feet and in the nose and in the ear. It is passed by doing, not by telling.

A child in Külhane learns to read the root-floor before they learn to read text. By the time a child is five, they can walk the town barefoot — and they do walk barefoot, or in thin bark-fiber sandals — and feel through the soles of their feet whether the aquifer beneath a particular stretch of path is running fast or slow, high or low, clear or silty. The root-floor is a drumhead and the water beneath it is the drummer, and a person raised here can read the water the way you might read a gauge. They tap their foot. They listen with their ankle. They know.

By the time a child is eight, they know which trees are structural and which are ornamental, which bark is safe to peel for fiber and which will kill the trunk, which branches are load-bearing and which are temporary and can be cut. They know this the way you know which door in your house leads to the kitchen — not because someone told you, but because the layout is *in you*.

By the time a child is twelve, they have begun their first graft. This is a rite of passage, though it is not marked with ceremony so much as with a slow realization on the part of the adults that the child has started tying things to other things and the things have started growing together. A child's first graft is usually small — a vine trained across a gap, a sapling bent toward a frame. But it is theirs. It is a piece of the town that is theirs, and it will grow with them, and when they are forty, the graft will be a wall or a beam or a roof-channel, and the child will be an adult, and the town will have one more piece that is shaped like the person who shaped it.

The oldest builders in Külhane are not the oldest people. They are the people with the most grafts still living. A builder's authority is not measured in years but in *living architecture* — the number of walls, roofs, channels, and rooms that they have trained and that are still standing and still growing. The most respected builder in town, as of the last generation, was a woman named Selen who had grafted her first structure at age nine and died at age seventy-three, and whose work includes the market's southern arcade, three residential domes, and the water-channel system that feeds the eastern cisterns. She is still present in the town. Her grafts are still growing. The bark-cloth records her patterns so that others can maintain what she started. In a sense she is still building, the way a composer is still composing after the orchestra has learned the piece.

### How They Are Organized

They organize themselves by household, and the household is defined by architecture. A household in Külhane is not a family in the genetic sense — it is a group of people who share a structure. A structure might be a single trained tree-house, or it might be a cluster of grafted buildings connected by shared root-floor and common water access. The household shares maintenance duties: if a wall is closing, everyone in the household helps cut it back. If a cistern is leaking, everyone helps retrain the gutter-vines. If a new room is needed, everyone participates in the grafting and training, and the room becomes a shared project in a way that makes it genuinely *belong* to all of them, because they all shaped it and it is shaped by all of their hands.

Households range from four people to thirty. The larger households tend to be the older ones — structures that have been growing for generations, accumulating rooms and branches and residents the way a river accumulates tributaries. A large household in the eastern quarter has been occupied for perhaps two hundred years and has twenty-three residents across four generations, and their structure is a labyrinth of grafted trunks and trained canopies that includes seven sleeping rooms, two cooking areas, a workshop where bark-cloth is processed, a cistern room that holds enough water for a week, and a colony of macaques in the upper rafters who are considered, by the youngest children, to be siblings.

There is no mayor. There is no council in the formal sense. There is the market — the physical space, the clearing where the light comes through — and the market is where decisions are made, because the market is where people are. Disputes about paths, about water rights, about which trees to reinforce and which to let fail, about the beetle infestation, about the macaque troop's expansion, about everything — these are settled in conversation, in the market, in the flow of daily life. Authority accrues to competence. The person whose grafts are sound is listened to on matters of structure. The person whose root-floor readings are accurate is listened to on matters of water. The person who can calm a screaming macaque is listened to on matters of the eastern troop. There is no election. There is *reputation*, and reputation is earned by the evidence of your hands, which is visible in the walls around you.

### What They Believe

This is harder to describe, because it is not a belief system in the way that term is usually used. It is not codified. It is not written. It is not spoken about directly, in the way that you do not usually speak about breathing.

The people of Külhane believe that the town is alive. Not metaphorically. Not in the ecological sense of "the town supports life." They believe the town is a single living thing — or rather, that the boundary between the town and the forest and the aquifer and the root-floor and the canopy and the animals and the people is an arbitrary line drawn by minds that needed categories, and that the reality is a single continuous organism of which the people are one tissue, the trees are another, the water is another, the ants are another, the macaques are another, and all of these tissues are interdependent and all of them are the same body.

This is not animism. It is not worship. They do not pray to the trees. They do not ask the forest for permission. They *are* the forest, in the same way that your hand is you — it does not need to ask your liver for permission to move. The relationship is not transactional. The offerings to the leafcutter ants — the shallow bowls of sweet sap — are not sacrifices. They are *feeding*. You feed your hand. You feed the ants. The distinction is not one they would recognize.

When a wall closes over a doorway overnight, they do not see this as the tree being aggressive or the town being hostile. They see it as the town growing, the way a body grows, and they adjust, the way you adjust your clothes when you grow. The pruning is not violence. It is grooming. The grafting is not control. It is conversation. The town speaks in growth and the people speak in pruning and training and the conversation has been going on for so long that neither party remembers starting it.

The dead are buried in the root-floor. Not below it — *in* it. The body is placed in a space between root-layers, and the roots grow around it and through it and the water flows over it and the person becomes part of the town in the most literal sense available to these people. There are no markers. There do not need to be. The family knows where the roots are. The roots know where the family is. When a child walks over her great-grandmother's resting place, she can feel the root-floor is different there — thicker, denser, the roots growing in a pattern that is not quite the same as the surrounding floor — and she knows, in her feet, that this is where her family's tissue meets the town's tissue, and that the meeting is not a boundary but a merge.

## The Macaques

They are not pets. They are not wildlife. They are *co-residents*, and they know it, and the people know it, and the negotiation between the two species is ongoing and daily and entirely unspoken.

The three species of macaque in Külhane have different relationships with the human population, and these relationships are as varied and as specific as the relationships between human households.

The largest species — a heavy-bodied, short-tailed macaque with a face like a disappointed banker — is the most integrated. These macaques live in the structures. They sleep in the rafters. They eat at the edges of human meals, not because they are fed (though they are) but because they have decided that human eating spaces are their eating spaces, and the humans have decided that arguing about this is less productive than simply making extra food. This species has a concept of household territory that aligns, roughly, with the human concept. A macaque family of this species considers itself part of the household and will defend the household's structure against other macaque families with a ferocity that the human residents find useful and occasionally alarming. They are the doorbells. They are the guard dogs. They are also the thieves, the vandals, and the occasional attackers of visiting strangers who get too close to the drying racks. The relationship is complicated. The relationship is *familial*, in the full sense of that word: affectionate, resentful, interdependent, and permanent.

The second species — smaller, longer-tailed, more agile — lives in the canopy and descends to the human zone only to forage. These are the ones that steal fruit from drying racks with a coordination that suggests military training. They operate in teams. One distracts. One steals. One stands watch. The people of Külhane have a grudging respect for these macaques that is indistinguishable from exasperated fondness. They have developed countermeasures — trained vine-covers that can be pulled shut over drying racks, certain strong-smelling saps that the macaques avoid — but the countermeasures are partially effective at best, and the macaques adapt. The people see this as the cost of living in a place that is also a forest. You lose some fruit. The macaques lose some dignity when they slip on wet bark and fall into cisterns. It balances.

The third species is the newest arrival. A small, nervous, intensely social macaque that arrived from lower altitudes perhaps fifteen years ago and has been slowly colonizing the eastern quarter. This species is still being *negotiated with* — the people have not yet figured out whether they are a problem or an asset. They are aggressive foragers, which puts them in competition with the second species. They are loud at night, which puts them in conflict with the humans. But they also eat the beetles that have been boring into the structural roots, and this has made them, reluctantly, welcome. The eastern households have begun leaving food out for them in a way that is halfway between feeding and bribery. The third species accepts this offering with the suspicious gratitude of a mob boss accepting a tribute.

No one in Külhane would say they "own" a macaque. The concept would be as strange to them as owning the rain.

## The Ants

The leafcutter ants deserve their own description because they are, in a very real sense, co-architects of the town.

A colony of leafcutter ants in Külhane can number in the millions. Their colonies are vast, subterranean, extending through the root-floor and into the aquifer layer and below it into the mineral substrate. They are the largest non-human residents of the town by biomass, and they are also the most organized.

The people consult them. This is not a figure of speech. Before a builder reinforces a wall, before a household decides to seal a doorway or open a new one, before a new graft is planned, the builder watches the ants. The ants prune. They strip leaves from branches, and the pattern of their pruning is a diagnostic tool as reliable as any instrument. Where the ants work aggressively, the wood is compromised — diseased, rotting, structurally unsound. Where the ants leave a branch alone, the wood is healthy. The ants know things about the town's structural integrity that the people do not, and the people have learned to read the ants' behavior as a kind of living inspection report.

This relationship is ancient. No one knows when it started. The offerings of sweet sap in shallow clay bowls are not a ritual — they are a *maintenance practice*, the way you might oil a tool. The sap keeps the ant colonies strong. Strong colonies prune more thoroughly. Thorough pruning keeps the town's structure sound. The system is a closed loop, and it has been running for longer than any written record.

There are people in Külhane — not many, but a few — who have a particular sensitivity to ant behavior. They are not shamans. They are not priests. They are *readers*. They can look at a colony's pruning pattern and tell you not just which branch is weak but which branch will fail, and when, and in what direction it will fall. They are the closest thing Külhane has to engineers, and their authority is based entirely on track record. A reader who calls a failure correctly is listened to. A reader who calls one wrong is not punished — they are simply consulted less. The system is self-correcting.

The ants do not know they are being read. The ants do not know they are being fed. The ants are doing what ants do, and the people are doing what people do, and the overlap between these two activities is so perfectly mutually beneficial that it looks, from the outside, like design. It is not design. It is cohabitation, extended over centuries, until the two species have become as interdependent as the roots and the water.

## The Parrots

They deserve a mention, not because they are structurally important but because they are *culturally* important in a way that the people of Külhane would never articulate and might not even recognize.

The green parrots with the red faces — the ones that mimic the market vendors — are the town's memory. Not the formal memory, not the bark-cloth records of family lineages and grafting schedules. The *atmospheric* memory. The parrots learn sounds and repeat them, and the sounds they learn are the sounds of daily life: the vendor's call — *"süsüle, süsüle, taze süsüle"* — the barking of the bullfrogs, the creak of a branch under load, the laughter of children, the particular grunt a person makes when they lift a full cistern-bowl. The parrots absorb these sounds and return them, slightly altered, from the canopy, so that the town is always echoing with its own recent past.

A vendor who has been calling *"süsüle"* for thirty years has her voice in the canopy, in dozens of parrots, and when she retires or dies, her call will persist for a while — fading, degrading, the pitch drifting, the intonation shifting, until it becomes something that is no longer quite her and is not quite anyone else, a ghost-call in the trees that the new vendors hear and unconsciously echo, and the echo goes back up to the parrots, and the cycle continues. The market's soundscape is a palimpsest, each generation of vendors influenced by the parrots who are repeating the previous generation, and the parrots influenced by the new vendors, and so on, back and forth, a conversation between species that neither species knows it is having.

The parrots also mimic rain. This is less poetic and more practical. The parrots have learned that the sound of rainfall — which they reproduce with startling accuracy — causes visitors to flinch and reach for cover, and the parrots find this amusing. Whether they find it amusing is a matter of interpretation, but they do it with a frequency and a timing that suggests intentionality, and the people of Külhane, who have lived with these parrots for generations, have a word for the behavior that translates roughly to *being rained on by a parrot*, and the word is not affectionate, but it is not angry either. It is the word you would use for a neighbor's child who has learned to imitate your voice and uses the skill to annoy you. The child is a nuisance. The child is also evidence that you are heard.

## The Pigs

The dark-haired, compact pigs that forage the root-floor are the town's cleanup crew, its soil-turners, its composters, and — though the people would not say this — its other co-architects.

The pigs eat fallen fruit, root-sprouts, the organic debris that accumulates on the root-floor from the constant shedding of leaves and bark and dead insects. In eating, they turn the surface layer, mixing it with their waste, which is immediately colonized by the beetles and the millipedes and the fungi that break it down into the nutrient-rich compost that feeds the roots, which feed the trees, which form the structures, which house the people who keep the pigs. The cycle is as tight and as closed as the ant-pruning cycle, and it is equally old.

The children herd them. This is not make-work. The pigs, left to their own devices, will forage anywhere, including in the root-floor channels where the water flows, and their rooting can disrupt the shallow aquifer in ways that cause blockages and floods. The children — children between the ages of six and twelve, mostly, because after twelve they have graduated to grafting and the herding falls to the younger ones — keep the pigs moving, keep them out of the water channels, keep them in the areas where their foraging is beneficial rather than destructive. The children use switches made of trained vine-stems, and they have calls — high, sharp, specific — that the pigs have learned to respond to, not because the pigs are trained but because the calls are accompanied by the switches, and the pigs are not stupid.

The pigs are slaughtered for meat. This is done without ceremony but also without cruelty, and it is done in the household, by the household, and every part of the animal is used: the meat is eaten, the fat is rendered for tallow for the lamps, the bristles are used for brushes that apply the toxic sap-paste to vulnerable roots, the hide is processed into a tough leather that is used for tool-handles and cistern-linings. The people of Külhane eat meat with the un-selfconsciousness of people who live inside their food chain. The pig is a co-resident. The pig is also dinner. These facts are not in conflict, in the same way that the leaf is part of the tree and the leaf also falls and becomes soil. The people do not consider this contradictory. They consider it *continuous*.

## The Frogs

The frogs are the town's nervous system.

This is not a metaphor the people of Külhane would use, because the people of Külhane do not use metaphors about the town. The town is the town. But the frogs function as a nervous system functions: they transmit signals.

The frog chorus — the evening cascade of barks and clicks and trills that begins at dusk and does not stop until dawn — is not random noise. It is *informational*. The species composition of the chorus changes based on conditions. When the aquifer is high, the bullfrogs bark more frequently and at a lower pitch. When the aquifer is low, they are quieter, and the smaller species — the glass frogs, the tree frogs — dominate. When a new water source has been tapped, or a cistern has been repaired and is filling, the frogs near that location change their call pattern within hours. The people of Külhane, who have been falling asleep to this chorus for their entire lives, can read it the way a sailor reads the sea. An unusual silence in one quarter means the water has shifted. A sudden increase in bullfrog volume means the aquifer is rising. The frogs are gauges. The frogs are sensors. The frogs are the town's way of telling the people what it is doing, in real time, every night, without stopping.

And the glass frogs — the thumbnail-sized ones that sit on the undersides of leaves like water droplets with legs — are the town's canaries. They are the most sensitive species to changes in water quality. If the water in the aquifer becomes silty or contaminated — which happens occasionally, when a root-channel collapses and sediment floods the system — the glass frogs disappear from the affected area first. Their absence is the earliest warning, and the people of Külhane have learned to check for glass frogs the way a miner checks for airflow. No glass frogs on the leaves in one quarter? Check the water. Something is wrong.

The frogs are also food, but only the big bullfrogs, and only in moderation, and only by certain households who have the right — a right earned by history and maintained by practice — to harvest from particular stretches of root-floor. The frog legs at the market come from these households. They are a delicacy, not a staple, and the harvesting is managed with a care that reflects the frogs' importance to the town's information system. You do not eat your nervous system to excess. You eat enough to remember that you are part of it.

## What the Town Itself Is

And then there is the town. The town is the largest resident, and the oldest, and the most patient, and the most silent despite being the loudest.

The town is the trees and the roots and the water and the canopy and the aquifer and the soil and the beetles and the ants and the frogs and the macaques and the parrots and the pigs and the chickens and the people and the path that shifts underfoot and the doorway that closes overnight and the graft that takes and the graft that fails and the cistern that fills and the cistern that leaks and the woman who calls *"süsüle"* and the parrot that repeats her and the child who herds pigs and the builder who watches ants and the reader who feels the root-floor and the dead who are buried in the roots and the living who walk over them and the rain that falls because the air has given up and the cloud that is not above or below but *here*, always here, always.

The town is what lives here. Everything else is the town being specific about itself.
