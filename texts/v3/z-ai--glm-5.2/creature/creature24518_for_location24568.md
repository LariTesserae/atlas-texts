# What Lives on the Weeping Plateau

---

There is no single thing that lives here. That is the first correction your mind makes, slowly, unwillingly, the way it corrected the ground from ground to root. You came looking for an inhabitant. There is no inhabitant. There are only *processes*, and some of them have become complicated enough that the word *lives* begins to fit awkwardly around them, like a shoe on a foot that has stopped being a foot.

But if you stand still long enough — and stillness is hard here, because the Loam moves under you and the sound is everywhere and the wet slaps of falling flatworms keep startling your hindbrain — you begin to understand that the question of *what lives here* has been answered already by the place itself, and the answer is: *the question is malformed.* The plateau does not host life. The plateau is life, and the things moving across it and through it and inside it are not residents but *organs*.

Let me try again. Let me be more honest about what emerges.

---

## The Root

It lives. That must be said plainly, even if it is obvious, even if it has already been said in the description of the place. The root mass lives the way an ocean lives — it is too large to think of as an individual and too coherent to think of as a colony. It is a single organism in the same way a language is a single thing: produced by uncountable agents, governed by rules no single agent knows, persisting across durations that make individual participation meaningless. The root is not a plant. The root is a *condition*. It is the condition under which everything else here exists.

Its intelligence — and I use the word with full awareness of its inadequacy — is not an intelligence that thinks. It does not deliberate. It does not decide. It *responds*, at a scale and speed that makes response indistinguishable from geology. When a column dies, the root mass has already rerouted its hydraulic flow around the falling structure before the column hits the ground. When the pale grazers finish dismantling a fallen column, the root has already sent out the chemical signals that will grow a replacement in that location — or nearby, or not at all, depending on factors that are not available to observation. The root manages the plateau the way your body manages its own temperature: without awareness, without intent, with a fidelity that looks like purpose from the outside and feels like nothing from the inside.

But the root is old. And age does something to systems, even systems that do not think. The white zones are the evidence. The root mass has been processing its own chemical signals for so long — cycling them, layering them, stacking signal on top of signal on top of signal until the lower levels have compressed into something like peat-diamond, like geological strata — that the signals themselves have begun to interact in ways the system did not evolve to handle. The root is not hallucinating in the way a mind hallucinates. It is hallucinating in the way a *body* hallucinates — a phantom limb, a misfiring nerve, a growth that follows instructions that were never meant to be given. The white spirals are the root's body producing a sensation of something that is not there. And the holes — the holes that show you the plateau from above — are the root's body producing an *absence* of itself, which is something a body should not be able to do.

The root does not know this. The root cannot know this. The root is not the kind of thing that knows. But the root *does* this, and the doing has a quality — a texture, a weight — that the word *dreaming* fits better than any other word, even if it is wrong, even if it is the kind of wrong that is also the most right thing available.

---

## The Flatworms

I need to describe them more carefully, because the description of the place treated them as fauna, as background, as part of the mechanical-biological drone of the plateau. They are not background. They are *the*.

They are dark — not the dark of the root mass, which is a saturated, tannin-black, but a darker dark, a dark that seems to absorb the gray-green light and give nothing back. Wet leather, the description said, and that is accurate for their surface texture, but it misses the way they move. They do not crawl. They *flow*. Their bodies are muscular and undifferentiated — no head, no tail, no visible sensory organs — and they move by generating peristaltic waves that travel the length of their bodies, and the waves are so smooth and so continuous that the worm seems to pour itself across the root surface like a thick liquid. When they hang from the column openings, they hang in spirals, slow corkscrews that turn in the column's condensation-trickle, and they filter the mist through their skin — you can see the mist entering them, darkening them further, and you can see, if you watch closely, small bright points moving inside their bodies, which might be digestive structures or might be something else entirely.

They are everywhere. The description said this, but it did not say what *everywhere* means. It means that the root mass is more worm than root, by volume, in its living layer. The burrows that honeycomb the Loam are not gaps in the root — they are *structures built by the root for the worms*. The root grows the burrows. The worms live in them. The worms eat the root's dead tissue and the microorganisms that decompose it, and their waste is metabolized and reabsorbed by the root's vascular system. The root feeds the worms. The worms feed the root. This is not symbiosis — symbiosis implies two separate organisms. This is *one organism expressed in two materials.* The root is the slow part. The worms are the fast part. Together they are a single metabolic cycle, and the cycle has been running so long that the distinction between root and worm has become a matter of perspective rather than biology.

The worms fall. This is important. They fall from the column openings constantly, and the falling is not an accident and not a malfunction. It is *transit*. The worms move through the column interiors upward, riding the hydraulic flow, and they exit the openings and drop to the Loam and burrow and continue their cycle. The falling is how the worms get from the vertical part of the system to the horizontal part. It is logistics. It is the movement of material through a pipeline. The wet slap you hear is a worm arriving at its destination.

But.

The worms near the white zones behave differently. I said they avoid the zones, and they do — the burrows near the zone boundaries are empty, the column openings sealed. But some worms *approach* the boundaries. You see them at the edge, moving in ways that are not their normal flow. They *spiral*. Their peristaltic waves shift from longitudinal to rotational, and the worm begins to turn in a tight circle, its body coiling into a flat spiral that mirrors — exactly mirrors — the spiral of the bleached root fibers at the zone boundary. The worm turns and turns in the spiral of the root, and then it stops. And it *dissolves*. Not dramatically — it does not burst or melt. It simply becomes less defined. Its edges blur. Its dark surface lightens. It becomes, over the course of a few seconds, indistinguishable from the bleached root around it, and then it is gone, and the root has grown a little more in that spot, white and tight and wrong.

The root is eating its own fauna in the white zones. Or the fauna are choosing to return to the root. Or the distinction between fauna and root has broken down entirely in these places, and what you are seeing is not death but *reabsorption* — the system taking back its fast-moving parts, incorporating them into its slow-moving parts, becoming more itself by becoming less diverse. The white zones are where the root is *consolidating*. And what it is consolidating into, no one can say.

---

## The Grazers

The pale, segmented, many-legged things. They are smaller than the worms — finger-length, maybe, pale as paper, wet, moving in single file along chemical trails. They are not interesting in the way the worms are interesting. They are interesting in the way a conveyor belt is interesting — they are *mechanism*.

But they have a quality that I want to describe, because it is the quality that will stay with you. They do not stop. The trails they follow are laid down by the root mass, and the trails lead to work sites — fallen columns, dead root patches, decomposition zones — and the grazers arrive and they *work*. They strip dead tissue. They break down structural material. They move with the regular, unvarying pace of things that have no will, no preference, no capacity for hesitation. They are the clean-up crew. They are the sanitation department. They are the most obviously *industrial* things on the plateau because their behavior has no ambiguity in it at all.

And yet. When you crouch beside one of their trails and watch them pass — hundreds of them, single-file, each one touching the segment ahead of it with its forelegs, each one following the chemical signal with a fidelity that looks like devotion — you feel something. Not from them. They feel nothing. But the *trail* feels like something. The chemical signal they follow was laid down by the root, and the root laid it down because something died, and the root needed that death to be processed, and it called these things to do it. The trail is an act of *care*. Not human care — not concern, not tenderness — but care in the older sense: maintenance, upkeep, the attention a system pays to its own continuation. The root cares for itself through these creatures. The grazers are how the root pays attention.

---

## The Shapes in the Mist

These are the ones I have been avoiding.

Not because they are dangerous — I have no evidence that they are dangerous. Not because they are frightening — though they are, in the way that anything large and incompletely seen is frightening. I have been avoiding them because I do not know what they are, and the description of the place did not know either, and I do not want to pretend to a knowledge I do not have.

But I can describe what I saw, and what I saw was this: they are not one species. The phrase *the shapes in the mist* implies a category, a type, and there is no type. There are *several* kinds of large thing moving between the columns, and they are not the same kind.

The first kind is the one the description captured — dark, low, dog-sized, moving with a rolling gait, too many legs or too few, body not fully committed to a single form. I saw these several times, always at distance, always in motion. They do not stop. Their gait is fluid, not because they are graceful, but because their bodies are *incomplete* — they have not finished forming. You can see this if one passes close enough: the body is still *differentiating*, still deciding what shape to be. Segments are forming and dissolving. Legs are growing and being resorbed. The thing is not malformed; it is *early*. It is a juvenile of something, and it has not yet settled on its final morphology. What it will become, I do not know. Whether it will become something recognizable, I do not know. But it is young, and it is moving, and the movement is not purposeless — it is *sampling*. The creature moves through the plateau, touching the ground, tasting the chemical signals in the root, filtering the mist, and with every contact it adjusts, refines, narrows its options. It is using the plateau's own signals to determine what shape to grow into. It is *reading the root* and becoming what the root tells it to become.

The second kind is different. Larger — cow-sized, maybe, or bear-sized, the scale is hard to judge in the mist. These do not move continuously. They *station*. You see them standing between two columns, motionless, for long periods — minutes, tens of minutes — and then they move, rapidly, to another position between two other columns, and they station again. They are not grazing. They are not hunting. They are *positioning*. When they station, they extend — I do not know what else to call it — they extend *something* from their bodies into the root mass. A limb, a proboscis, a root of their own. It enters the Loam and stays. And while it is in the Loam, the pumping rhythm around them changes. The eight-second pulse shifts. The fluid flow redirects. The temperature of the nearest column drops by a degree, maybe two.

They are *adjusting* the root's systems. They are tuning the plateau. They are walking the factory floor and turning dials.

Who sent them? Nothing. No one. They grew here, the way the root grew here, the way the worms grew here. They are part of the system. But they are the part of the system that *acts on* the system, and that makes them different from the root, which only acts *as* the system. They are the closest thing this place has to *inhabitants* — not because they live here, but because they *work* here, and working implies a kind of presence that mere existence does not.

I do not know if they are aware. I do not think the word *aware* means anything at the scale and age of this place. But they are *consistent*. They do the same things in the same way, and their adjustments follow patterns that, over hours of observation, begin to look like *preferences*. They favor certain column spacings. They avoid certain flow patterns. They cluster near the boundaries of the white zones but do not cross them. They are, perhaps, the system's way of *monitoring itself* — a feedback mechanism grown large enough to move, large enough to be seen, large enough to be mistaken for an animal.

---

## What Is in the White Zones

I need to say what I saw, even though I am not certain I saw it.

At the center of the white zone, in the hole, I saw the plateau from above. The description said this. But the description did not say what else I saw, because I did not say it, because I stepped back, and the not-saying has become a weight I need to set down.

The plateau from above is not the plateau from the ground. From above, you can see the full pattern. The columns are not in rows. They are in a *spiral*. A vast, slow spiral that begins at the edge of the plateau and tightens toward the center, and the white zones are spaced along the spiral at regular intervals, and they are not malfunctions. They are *joints*. They are the places where the spiral's arm bends, and the bending requires an absence, a gap, a place where the root does not grow because the *structure* of the spiral requires the gap to exist. The white zones are not dreams. They are *architecture*. The root is not hallucinating — it is building, at a scale and in a dimension that is not visible from inside, and the building has been going on for so long that the building itself has become indistinguishable from the landscape.

And in the hole, at the center of the spiral, where the spiral is tightest, I saw — or I think I saw — something *move*. Not on the plateau. *In the hole.* Something moving in the space between the root and itself, in the absence the root has made, in the gap that is not a gap but a *window*. It moved the way the worms move — slow, undulating, dark against light. But it was not a worm. It was the size of the whole plateau. It was the shape of the whole plateau. It moved, and the plateau moved with it, and then I stepped back, and I was standing on the ground again, and the hole was a hole, and the root was breathing beneath me, and the flatworms were falling, and the shapes were stationing between their columns, and the factory was running, and the thing in the hole was still there, I think, I think it is still there, I think it has always been there, I think the root grew around it the way a pearl grows around a grain of grit, and the thing in the hole is not alive and is not dead and is not mechanical and is not biological — it is the thing that the plateau is *for*. The root does not know this. The worms do not know this. The tuners do not know this. The plateau does not know what it is for. But the thing in the hole knows, or if it does not know, it *is* the knowing, and the root has grown around it for longer than geology, and the growing will not stop, because the thing in the hole has not finished being enclosed, and when it is finished —

I do not know what happens when it is finished.

I do not want to know.

---

## What Lives Here

The root lives here. The worms live here. The grazers live here. The shapes live here. The tuners live here. The white zones live here, in their way.

And something else lives here, or does not live here but *persists* here, in the holes, in the gaps, in the spiral that is not a spiral but a closing hand. Something that the root has been growing around since before the word *before* meant anything. Something that will be here when the root is gone, if the root can be gone, if the root can stop growing, if the hand can close.

The plateau is a fist closing around something it cannot hold.

The plateau is a mouth that has been slowly, for longer than stone, closing around a word it cannot say.

The plateau breathes, and the breathing is the breathing of a system that has been running so long that it has forgotten what it was running toward, and it does not matter, because it is still running, and the thing in the hole is still there, and the root is still growing, and the worms still fall, and the tuners still station, and the water still tastes like cold tea, and the light is still the color of old pewter, and the cloud still closes over everything when you leave, and the sound follows you down, and the sound is the sound of something that does not know it is a fist, does not know it is a mouth, does not know it is closing, and will not know, and does not need to know, and the closing will happen anyway, and it will happen the way geology happens, and it will happen the way a root grows, and it will be slow, and it will be loud, and it will be wet, and it will be cold, and it will have been happening forever, and it will not stop.
