In the endless, crushing roar of the Rhizome Halls, life has learned to be silent, to communicate not through the useless medium of air, but through the dense, wet language of the substrate. The ones who live here are not lost explorers; they are the tenders, the custodians of the slow digestion, descendants of the original engineers who stayed when the lights went out and the project was abandoned. They have, over generations, become indistinguishable from the organism they maintain.

They are tall, stretched by a lifetime of navigating the dark, and their bodies are pale as the root-fibers they walk upon. Their skin carries a faint, blue-grey luminescence, a biological echo of the moss that surrounds them, making them appear as little more than moving shadows or thickening ridges in the root-mat until they step into the amber glow of a failing industrial lamp. Their eyes are milky, photosensitive, unbothered by the dark but easily pained by the sudden brutality of a headlamp. They navigate primarily by touch, reading the vibrations of the tectonic hum and the 40-second shudder of the electric pumps through the soles of their bare, calloused feet. 

They wear clothes, or what remains of the concept. Their garments are woven from stripped rhizome and white rootlets, so heavily colonized by mycelium that the fabric is alive, breathing, and rooted to their own skin. To look at them is to see a bipedal extension of the floor. Their hair is matted with cold condensation, beaded with water that slides down their faces in the same continuous, patient transit as the moisture on the ceiling.

They do not speak. The sheer volume of the chamber swallowed speech long ago, and their vocal cords have atrophied into useless tissue. Instead, they strike the ground. A knuckle rapped against a dense, structural arch of root sends a low-frequency pulse through the mycelial network, felt for hundreds of meters. It is a language of taps and shudders: *one long, two short* to signal a shift in the water channels; a rapid, drumming vibration to call for assistance at a failing pump. 

Their life is monastic and cyclical, bound to the geological patience of the place. They do not age in years, but in seasons of maintenance. A tender will spend a month clearing the mineral scale from a narrow-gauge rail, not so it can be used, but so the root-mat can swallow it cleanly. Another will sit by the running pump station, guiding a thick, white rootlet into a pipe joint, patiently braiding the plant into the plumbing. They eat the pale, translucent grubs that hide in the crevices of the mat, and they drink from the black, still pools, taking in water that tastes of iron, stone, and diesel.

When you walk through their halls, you are a temporary, crashing disruption. Your boots leave prints that they will later kneel beside, pressing the fibers back into place with their palms, smoothing the wound in the mat. They will watch you from behind a pillar of unexcavated rock, feeling the heavy, clumsy vibrations of your steps through their hands. They do not fear you. They do not hate you. They simply wait, with the absolute certainty of the moss and the steady thrum of the machinery, for you to leave so the quiet roar of their world can resume. They are the mechanics of an organism that has outlived its purpose, and they will keep the pump turning over until the stone itself turns to peat.
