# What Lives in the Cirque of Weeping Stone

---

The frogs do not think of themselves as strange.

They do not know they are high. They do not know that frogs elsewhere live in warmer places, lower places, that their presence here at this altitude in this cold is something a biologist would note and a traveler would question. They know only this: the pools are here, and the pools are wet, and the wet is what they are for. They sit at the margins with their dark slick backs barely distinguishable from the wet stone, and their throats pulse, and the sound comes out of them the way water comes out of the rock — not chosen, not effortful, just the thing that happens when the conditions are met. A frog at the edge of a seep is a condition being met. The croaking is what follows.

There are more of them than you would ever guess from a single glance. They are not all visible. Many sit half-buried in the moss, their bodies compressed into the damp, their eyes just above the surface of the green, watching the air for the movement of insects and watching everything else for nothing at all. A frog in moss is not hiding. A frog in moss is being a frog in moss. The distinction matters. They do not have a concept of concealment. They have a concept of warmth, which means: not in the wind, not in the coldest water, pressed against something that holds heat even slightly better than air. The moss holds heat slightly better than air. The rock does not. The water does not. So they sit in moss, or at the edges of pools where the mud is marginally warmer than the stone, and they call, and they wait, and they eat what comes close enough to be eaten.

Their call is not beautiful. It is not the bright spring singing of lowland ponds, the sound that means thaw and mud and renewal. It is a guttural thing, a wooden-ratchet sound, mechanical, persistent, and it never stops. Not truly never — individual frogs stop, rest, eat, digest — but the population does not stop. There are enough of them that the silence of any individual is covered by the calling of a dozen others, and the combined sound is not so much heard as inhabited. You stand in the cirque and you are standing inside the sound the way you stand inside the air. It is a medium. It is the element in which the other sounds exist. The pipits call against it. The water moves through it. It is the ground.

What do they eat? The insects in the moss. The small rustling things. There are more insects than you would guess, because you cannot see them without crouching close and waiting, and even then what you see is movement, not body — a flicker, a dart, the displacement of a moss frond, the momentary appearance of a leg or antenna before it is drawn back into the damp. They are springtails, probably, and mites, and small beetles, and the larvae of midges that mature in the standing water of the pools. They live in the moss the way the moss lives on the stone — finding the micro-structure, the gaps between stems, the small spaces where air is trapped and warmth persists and food can be found. The moss is not a surface for them. It is a landscape. A forest, a cave system, a terrain of stems and fibers and the slow decay of older growth that they consume and are consumed by.

The frogs eat them. The frogs sit at the pool margins with their mouths at the level of the moss and wait for the insects to move, and when they move, the tongue comes out, and the insect goes in, and the frog settles again, and the throat pulses, and the call resumes. This happens continuously. It happens everywhere in the cirque where there is wet moss and a frog, which is to say everywhere in the cirque. The frogs are not hungry in the way you think of hunger. They are hungry the way a filter is hungry — passively, constantly, taking what passes. They are a processing system. The insects become frogs. The frogs become sound and eggs and eventually, when they die, they become moss-food, and the moss becomes insects, and the cycle does not feel like a cycle to any of its participants. It feels like just being here, just doing this, just the ordinary long business of living in the wet.

---

The pipits know the walls.

Not as walls. Not as barriers. As surface. As the place where the food lives, distributed vertically in a way that changes through the day. In the morning, after the cold of night, the insects in the moss have moved downward, seeking warmth, and the lowest parts of the wall are where the birds feed. As the day progresses and the diffused light warms the stone — warms it almost imperceptibly, a degree or two, but enough — the insects move upward, and the birds follow. The birds do not know they are following. They are feeding where the feeding is, and the feeding is where the insects are, and the insects are where the warmth is, and the warmth is moving, and so the birds move. A flock of fifteen or twenty dark bodies hopping up a wet stone face, picking, picking, their claws finding purchase in the moss and the fluted folds of the rock, their tails pumping, their calls ticking — this is the visible expression of a temperature gradient. The birds are thermometers with wings. They are also appetites with wings, and the appetite is always for the next insect, and the next insect is always slightly higher, and so they climb.

When they erupt — when the flock launches as one body from the wall and crosses the cirque — the sound of their wings is the sound of small commitment. Each wingbeat is a decision that lasts a fraction of a second. The flock makes these decisions together, without deciding, the way a flock does, the way water chooses a path, by the accumulated weight of small tendencies. One bird tilts and the others follow and the air is suddenly full of dark shapes moving in a loose coherence, their ticking calls overlapping into a rattling buzz that the stone walls catch and return and multiply until you cannot tell where the sound originates. It originates everywhere. The walls are speakers. The air is the medium. The birds are the signal.

They land. They feed. They begin again.

What do they know of the cirque? They know it as a place in a larger place. They are not confined to it — they come and go over the rim, following the ridgeline, finding other basins, other walls, other moss — but the cirque is dense, the cirque is rich, and they return to it. The cirque is their main feeding ground. They know its shape the way you know the shape of your favorite room — not as geometry but as a felt boundary, a place where the acoustics change, where the wind behaves differently, where the food is concentrated. They do not think about this. They respond to it. The walls press the air in a way that feels different from open sky, and the different feeling is part of what it means to be here, and being here is what they do.

They do not eat the frogs. This is worth noting. The frogs are too large for them, too wet, too dark, too still. The frogs are outside their category of food. The frogs exist for the pipits as part of the environment — a sound, a presence, something that sits at the margins of the pools where the birds sometimes walk and sometimes do not. The pipits and the frogs are parallel tenants. They share the space without competing. The pipits eat what the moss holds on its surface. The frogs eat what the moss holds in its interior and what moves between the moss and the water. Their economies overlap at the edges but do not compete at the center. This is part of why both can be so abundant. The cirque has room for two complete ways of being, because each way of being is narrow, because each is precisely adapted to a specific niche, because the niches are small enough to be dense.

---

The mosses know nothing and know everything.

There are a dozen kinds, and they do not know they are a dozen kinds. They do not know they are moss. They grow. That is the whole of it. They grow where the wet is and where the light is, and where the wet is steady and the light is dim they are dark, almost black, and their growth is slow, and their texture is dense, and they hold the water in their bodies like a sponge holds water — not by choice but by structure, by the way their cells are arranged, by the physics of small spaces and surface tension. Where the light is brighter they are yellow-green, luminous, and they grow faster, and they are softer, and they dry slightly when the water slows and then they rehydrate when it returns and this is not a struggle for them, this is not suffering, this is simply the cycle of their existence — wet, less wet, wet. They do not experience the transitions. They respond to them.

The mosses have been here longer than the frogs. Longer than the birds. Longer than the overhang and the handaxe and whoever used it. The mosses colonized the walls when the walls were slightly less weathered than they are now, and they have been part of the weathering ever since — their roots (not roots, rhizoids, but functionally similar) finding cracks in the stone and expanding them, holding water in the cracks, and the water freezes and the cracks widen and the moss follows the cracks deeper, and the stone becomes more moss and less stone, and the process is so slow that no living thing perceives it, but the process is real, and the walls of the cirque are in the process of becoming soil, and the soil will one day be the floor of a valley when the walls have come down, and the moss is doing this, and the moss has been doing this for longer than there have been frogs.

The moss does not want. The moss responds. Water is present: grow. Light is present: grow. Nutrients are present, brought by the water, deposited by the dying of last year's growth: grow. The moss builds on its own dead the way the cirque builds on its own eroded stone. Layer on layer. The lowest layers are not soil exactly, not yet — they are compost, they are the beginning of what will become soil, dark, wet, fibrous, shot through with the mineral grains that the water has washed from the stone above. The cushion plants root in this. The sedges extend into it. The frogs sit on it. The insects live in it. The moss is making the ground on which everything else stands, and it is making it from itself, and it does not know, and it does not need to know.

---

The cushion plants are patient in a way that looks like stubbornness.

They grow in domes — dense, compact, springy — on the flattest gravel, the places where water does not pool but seeps, where the ground is damp but not saturated. Each cushion is many individuals growing so close together that their stems interlace and their surfaces merge into a single rounded shape, and from above it looks like one plant, but it is many, and they are holding each other, and the wind cannot pull them apart because they are holding each other, and this is not cooperation in the way animals cooperate — it is not a choice — but it is cooperation in the way that cooperation works among things that grow: the ones that grow close survive the wind, and the ones that do not, do not, and so the ones that are close become the ones that are, and the cushion grows denser and lower and more resistant, and the wind trains everything here to be what it can be at this height, which is: low, dense, interwoven, holding.

The cushion plants are making soil. This is their long work. They catch debris — fragments of moss, insect parts, pollen carried on the wind, particles of stone dust — and they hold it, and it decomposes in their interior, and their lower stems die and become humus, and the humus accumulates, and after decades a cushion plant has built a platform of soil on bare gravel, and when the cushion plant dies (or rather, when individual stems die, because the cushion persists as long as any part of it grows), the soil remains, and other plants find it, and the floor of the cirque is slowly, imperceptibly, becoming a place where things can root deeper. This is happening now. It has been happening for centuries. It will continue. The cushion plants are the patient ones. They are the ones that turn rock into something you could grow a garden in, if you were here long enough to garden, which no one is, which no one has been.

---

The sedges live at the margins.

They are the boundary creatures — not quite in the water, not quite on the ground, but in the place where the water meets the stone and the ground is saturated to the point of being more liquid than solid. Their roots go down into something that is not quite soil and not quite gravel and not quite water but all three at once, and they hold there, and they send up their thin stems and their small tufted heads, and the wind bends them and they bend and they return, and this is their life: rooted in the unstable, bent by the moving, returning. They are the most flexible things in the cirque. Everything else is either rigid (stone) or clinging (moss, cushion plants) or mobile (birds, frogs, insects). The sedges are the ones that yield and persist. They are the grasses' high-altitude cousins. They do not grow tall. They do not need to. Their flowers are small, wind-pollinated, and the wind is always here, so the pollen moves, and the sedges reproduce, and the margins expand, and slowly, slowly, the pools are being edged with green.

---

The insects are the invisible foundation.

You do not see them unless you look. But if you look — if you crouch beside a patch of moss and wait, and your eyes adjust, and you stop breathing so loudly — the moss is full of movement. Small bodies. Springtails, no larger than a grain of sand, jumping when disturbed, their furcula snapping beneath them like a spring uncoiling. Mites, even smaller, moving with a deliberate slowness that suggests they are grazing — on what? On the dead cells of the moss, on the fungi that grow on the dead cells, on the bacteria that grow on the fungi. Beetles — tiny, dark, glossy — that move through the moss stems like small boats through reeds, and their legs are long, and they are hunting, and what they hunt are the springtails and the mites, and what hunts them — the beetles — are the frogs, and what hunts the frogs is the thing in the overhang, or the thing that was in the overhang, or the thing that moves through the cirque at night when the birds are roosting and the frogs are at their loudest.

The insects do not know they are a foundation. They are living. They are doing what they do — eating, reproducing, moving, dying — and their doing of what they do makes it possible for everything above them in the food web to do what it does. There are a lot of them. More than you would guess. The moss is dense and the moss is wet and the moss has been growing and dying and regrowing for so long that the insect populations that depend on it have become stable and large, and the stability is the point — the cirque is not a place of boom and bust, not a place where one good year produces an explosion of life and one bad year produces a crash. It is a place of equilibrium. The insects are always present in roughly the same numbers because the moss is always present in roughly the same amount because the water is always present in roughly the same volume because the stone is always saturated in roughly the same way. The system is steady. The system has been steady for longer than any animal in it can perceive. The frogs do not remember a year with fewer insects. The birds do not remember a year with fewer frogs. There has never been such a year. Not in their lifetimes, not in the lifetimes of their ancestors, not in the history of their populations here. The cirque is a place where the word "always" has weight.

---

The thing you do not see lives here too.

It is present in the bones near the southern wall. It is present in the dropping. It is present in the smear on the rock. It is present in the way the frogs near the southern wall are slightly warier — not less numerous, but quicker to silence, quicker to resume, as though they have learned that stillness is not always safety, that the thing that takes them is not fooled by silence, and so they watch as much as they listen, and they are watching for something that moves differently from the wind and the water and the birds.

What is it? You do not know. The evidence is sparse. A scatter of small dark bones — frog bones, surely, the right size, the right fragility — and a dropping that is dark, compact, containing fragments of insect carapace and small bone shards, which means a predator that eats frogs whole or in pieces and digests the soft parts and excretes the hard. The smear on the rock is ambiguous. It could be blood. It could be mud. The mud here is dark and iron-rich and looks like blood when wet.

It might be a snake. A high-altitude snake — there are species that can survive at this elevation if the cold is not too extreme and if there is cover and if there is prey. A snake would eat frogs. A snake would leave droppings with bone fragments. A snake would be invisible in the moss and the wet stone, its body the same dark color as everything else, its movement the same speed as the water moving over the rock, and you would not see it unless it moved, and it would not move unless it had reason, and it had reason only when it hunted, and when it hunted it was quick, and when it was quick the frog did not have time to recognize danger, and then it was over, and the snake was still again, and the frogs near the silence were silent, and then they resumed, and the snake was a low dark shape in the moss that was not there if you looked for it.

Or it might be something else. A weasel, perhaps — a small mustelid, dark-furred, fast, a hunter that moves between the rocks and the moss and the pools and takes what it finds. Weasels eat frogs. Weasels leave droppings like the one described. Weasels are the kind of predator that is known to every prey animal in a small ecosystem because they are relentless, because they hunt constantly, because their metabolism demands it, because they are always hungry and always moving and always present at the edge of perception. The frogs would know a weasel. The birds would know a weasel. The weasel would be the thing that the system accounts for in its calculations of survival — the reason the frogs are watchful, the reason the pipits erupt so suddenly, the reason the insects stay hidden in the moss and do not move to the surface except when they must.

Or it might be something that has no name you know. The cirque is small but it is old, and it is wet, and it is the kind of place that holds things that have adapted to its specific conditions over a very long time, and some of those things may not be familiar, may not be categorizable, may be the local expression of a form that exists nowhere else because the conditions that produced it exist nowhere else. A predator shaped by this stone, this water, this cold, this density of frogs, this absence of competing predators. A thing that lives in the cracks in the walls, perhaps, or in the overhang when no one is there, or in the pools themselves — a thing that is wet and dark and patient and takes frogs one at a time and has been doing so for longer than the handaxe has been sitting on the ground.

You do not see it. This is its way of being here. Its presence is an absence. Its contribution to the soundscape is the silence of the frogs near the southern wall, which is a gap in the sound, a space where the croaking is not, and the space is shaped like whatever makes it. If you had ears that could hear negative sound, you would hear it — the outline of a body in the absence of calling, the shape of a predator drawn by the silence it causes.

---

And there is the one who lived in the overhang.

Not lives. Lived. Past tense, or tense so long it has no tense. The handaxe is on the ground. The fire is out. The wall is mossy. The person is gone.

But they were here. They built the windbreak. They chose the flattest stones and stacked them with the knowledge of someone who has done this before, or who has been taught, or who has watched and learned. The wall is not elegant but it is functional — knee-high, six meters long, blocking the prevailing wind, which in this cirque circles and drops and presses from the north and west. The overhang faces east-southeast. The wall protects the opening. This is not a random arrangement of stones. This is a decision made by someone who understood the wind in this specific place, who had spent enough time here to know which direction the cold comes from, which direction the water drips least, which direction offers the best view of the floor. The view is of the whole cirque. The shelter is a watcher's shelter.

Who were they? A hermit, perhaps. A person who came to the edge of things and stayed. The cirque is the kind of place that draws a certain kind of person — not the kind who wants comfort, not the kind who wants company, but the kind who wants to be at a threshold, at a place where the world is reduced to its elements, where the systems are visible, where you can sit and watch a complete ecosystem operate and know that you are the only one watching. A person who found the density of life in this small cold wet place to be enough. A person who could sit in the dry space beneath the overhang and listen to the frogs and the birds and the water and feel that this was sufficient — that the sound was sufficient, that the company of nonhuman lives was sufficient, that the stone overhead was sufficient shelter and the moss outside was sufficient world.

Or a traveler. Someone passing through. The shelter could have been built in a day and used for a week and abandoned. The handaxe could have been dropped accidentally, or left because it was no longer needed, or left because the person who used it did not come back. The mountains are full of small shelters built by people who were moving from one place to another and needed a dry place to sleep. The cirque is high but it is not inaccessible — there is a pass below, and the pass connects to other valleys, and the traveler may have been crossing the pass and found the cirque and stayed the night and left and never returned and the wall fell slowly into moss and the tool sat on the ground and the person went on with their life and never thought of this place again.

Or a watcher in a different sense. A person who was watching for something. The cirque is a natural observation point — the walls funnel sound, the floor is visible in its entirety, the approaches are limited. Someone could have used this place to watch the pass below, or to watch for weather, or to watch for something else — someone coming, someone not coming, a signal, a change. The shelf in the wall — the flat stone placed as a shelf — held something once. What? A tool. An offering. A piece of food. A stone with a mark on it. Something that was placed there deliberately and is no longer there, and the absence of it is the only evidence that it was there, which is another kind of presence-by-absence, like the predator's silence, like the thing you cannot see.

The person is part of the system now. Not their body — their body is gone, wherever bodies go. But their shape is part of the cirque. The overhang is different because they built in it. The moss grows differently on the stones of the windbreak than it grows on the natural rock — the stones are flatter, the moss colonizes the top surfaces first, the pattern of colonization is shaped by a human arrangement. The ash from the fire is long gone but the scorch remains, and in the scorch the stone is slightly more porous, slightly more fractured, and water seeps into it slightly differently, and the moss will eventually colonize it slightly differently, and the difference will propagate — slowly, over centuries — into the pattern of life on that section of wall. The person is present in the cirque as a perturbation in the pattern of growth. They are a cause that persists after the effect is no longer visible. They are the ghost of a modification. They are the handaxe on the ground, which is a different stone in a different place than any other stone in the cirque, and which will be found by someone someday, or will be buried by moss, or will be carried away by water in a thousand years, and until then it sits and it is what they left and it is enough.

---

And there is the stone itself.

The stone lives here in the way that stone lives — which is to say, not in the way that animals or plants or moss live, but not dead, not inert, not nothing. The stone is saturated. The stone is weeping. The stone is the thing that holds everything else, and it holds it not passively but actively, in the way that a container is active — by being shaped, by having walls, by directing the water, by creating the basin where the air is trapped and the sound is multiplied and the life is concentrated. The stone is the reason there is a here here.

The stone is metamorphic — compressed, folded, recrystallized by pressure and heat deep in the mountain's history, and now exposed by erosion, by the same freeze-thaw that flutes its surfaces, and the exposure is ongoing, and the stone is changing, and the change is the stone's form of life, its form of time. The stone does not experience its own erosion. But the stone is eroding. The flakes that fall from its surfaces become the gravel on the floor. The gravel becomes the substrate for the cushion plants. The cushion plants make soil. The soil holds the moss. The moss holds the insects. The insects feed the frogs. The frogs feed the thing you do not see. The thing you do not see dies and its body falls among the rocks and the water washes it into the gravel and the gravel becomes soil and the soil becomes moss and the moss becomes stone substrate and the stone is part of the cycle, the stone is the slowest participant, the stone is the thing that takes the longest to change but is changing, always, the stone is alive with time.

The water in the stone is part of the stone. The aquifer that presses outward — the water that emerges not from cracks but from the pores of the rock itself — is water that has been inside the mountain for months, or years, or decades. It entered as rain, as snowmelt, as condensation, and it percolated into the fractures and the spaces between mineral grains, and it moved slowly through the interior of the peak, and it emerges now at the surfaces of the cirque because the cirque is where the pressure forces it out, where the stone ends and the air begins, and the water crosses that boundary and becomes surface water and runs down the walls and pools on the floor and feeds the moss and the frogs and the sedges and the cushion plants and then it flows out through the braided channels and down the pass and into the valleys below, and it will become a river, and the river will reach a lake, and the lake will evaporate, and the water will become cloud, and the cloud will become rain, and the rain will fall on the mountain and enter the stone again, and the cycle is not a cycle the stone knows but it is a cycle the stone is part of, and the stone is part of it by being the medium, the filter, the slow passage between sky and ground.

The stone weeps because it is full. This is not sadness. This is saturation. This is the condition of having taken in more than can be held, and the excess finds its way out. The weeping is the stone giving back what it has received. The giving is constant. The giving is the foundation of everything that lives here.

---

What lives in the Cirque of Weeping Stone?

A system. A process. A place where every element is in motion and every motion serves something and the something it serves is the continuation of the motion. The frogs do not know they are the dominant large animal. The birds do not know they are thermometers. The moss does not know it is making soil. The stone does not know it is weeping. The person who lived in the overhang did not know they would become a perturbation in the pattern of moss growth. The thing you do not see does not know it is known by the shape of the silence it causes. None of them know they are part of a system. They are all part of a system. They are the system. The system is what lives here, and the system is alive in the way that a place is alive — not as a single organism but as a community of participants, each doing what it does, each being what it is, each connecting to the others through the ordinary mechanisms of eating and growing and dying and eroding and flowing and calling and being silent and being present and being absent and being stone that holds it all.

The sound is the system being audible. The wet is the system being tangible. The cold is the system being felt. The emptiness of the air above is the system having room. The abundance of life below is the system filling what room it can. And the handaxe on the ground is the system having once included a person, and including them still, in the way that a system includes everything that has ever touched it — as a mark, a modification, a stone out of place, a wall partly collapsed, a fire long cold, a shelf empty, a tool waiting to be found or to be buried, and either way part of the place, part of the cirque, part of what lives here.

What lives here is everything that has ever been here, compressed into the present moment, running, dripping, calling, eroding, growing, waiting. What lives here is the cirque itself.
