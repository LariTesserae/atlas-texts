# What Lives Here

---

Begin with the film.

It is the first living thing and it is almost not a living thing at all. A skin. A veil. A breath on the surface of the made-stone, so thin that it has no thickness you can measure by eye, so fine that it is less an organism than an event — something that *happens* where the glow meets the water and the water is still enough to let it happen. It is grey-white. It is faintly metallic, as though it were not entirely biological, as though it had incorporated something of the stone's mineralogy into itself, as though it had looked at the calcium carbonate and the iron and the unnamed sweet chemistry and said *yes, that, I'll take that, I'll build myself from that.*

It pulses.

Not rhythmically. Not the way a heartbeat is rhythmic. The pulses move across the film in waves that originate from no clear center and travel in no consistent direction. They brighten and dim and brighten again, and the pattern — if it is a pattern — shifts over hours, over days, in response to something you cannot identify. Changes in the current. Changes in the glow. Changes in the mineral content of the water. Or perhaps in response to nothing external at all. Perhaps the film thinks, after a fashion. Perhaps the pulses are its thinking — slow, distributed, chemical, a mind that covers a hundred square meters of submerged stone and takes six hours to complete a single thought.

The film does not move. It cannot move. It is rooted to the surface it grows on, and when the current scours a patch clean, that patch is simply dead, and the film grows back from the edges inward, slowly, taking weeks to re-cover what a single eddy stripped in seconds. This is its life: growing, being torn, growing again. Growing, being torn. Growing.

On this, everything feeds.

---

The armored things come first.

They are small. The size of a fingernail, the size of a coin, some no larger than a grain of rice. They carry shells — or plates, or something between a shell and a plate — of a material that is not quite the dark substance of the Works but is close enough that you wonder. Did they evolve to match the stone, or did they incorporate the stone, or did the stone incorporate *them*, long ago, so that the distinction between the organism and its substrate has worn away the way the edges of the walls have worn away? You cannot tell. The armor is dark, dense, faintly luminous, and when thousands of them move across a surface, the combined glow of their plates merges with the glow of the stone until the surface itself seems to *ripple*, as though the Works were breathing.

They live in the gaps. The seams between blocks. The cracks that erosion has opened. The joints where one level of platform meets the next. They live in the architecture's failures — in the places where the making has come undone — and this is important, this is essential, because the gaps are where the current slows. The gaps are where the water sits. The gaps are the dead zones, the still pockets, and the armored things are not built for the current. They are built for stillness. They cling to the stone with something — not suction, not hooks, something else, something chemical perhaps, a bond that forms between the mineral in their shells and the mineral in the Works, a handshake between the dead stone and the living one — and they scrape.

They scrape the film from the surface. This is what they eat. They move across the stone in coordinated waves, thousands of individuals flowing in the same direction, and where they pass, the film is gone — stripped clean, the bare stone showing through, dimmer without its coating — and behind them, the film begins to grow back. They are a lawnmower. They are a slow, patient, clicking lawnmower that never stops and never finishes because the grass grows back faster than they can cut it, and this is the relationship, this is the engine: the stone feeds the film, the film feeds the armored things, the armored things expose the stone, and the stone feeds the film again. A loop. A circuit. A metabolism that is not housed in a body but in a *place*.

The coordination is real. It is not accidental. When a current shift pushes a school of eel-things through a platform, the armored things do not scatter randomly — they *flow*, moving as a group toward the nearest gap, the nearest crack, retreating into the architecture in a body, and the retreat is so coordinated that it looks like a single organism contracting. Then the threat passes and they emerge again, spreading outward, resuming the scrape. You watch this happen three times. Four. Five. The pattern is the same each time. They know where the gaps are. They remember. Or the structure of the stone remembers for them — the shape of the surface guiding their movement the way the shape of a riverbed guides water, so that the coordination is not intelligence but *geometry*, the architecture thinking through the organisms that live inside it.

They click. Thousands of tiny plates against stone. It is a soft sound, a granular sound, like sand pouring, and it never stops. It is the sound of the Works being eaten. Not quickly. Not violently. At the pace of a fingernail across a surface. The armored things are part of the erosion. They have always been part of the erosion. They are the erosion's hands.

---

The eel-things.

They are what you notice first, after the glow, because they move with a purpose that the current does not provide. The current is a system — it does what geometry tells it to do. The eel-things *choose*. You can see it in the way a school moves: the individuals shift relative to each other in patterns that are not random, not reactive, but *decided*. One turns and the others adjust. One accelerates into a gap between pillars and two follow and three do not. They are making decisions based on something you cannot perceive — perhaps the micro-currents, perhaps the distribution of the armored things, perhaps something else entirely, some sense that has no analogue in the world above.

They are long. Thirty centimeters, half a meter, the largest perhaps a meter and a half, though it is difficult to tell because they move and the perspective shifts and the water plays tricks. Their bodies are stiff — not the sinuous flexibility of an eel but a rigidity that suggests a spine of cartilage or something denser, something that does not bend so much as *angle*, the body changing direction in quick precise increments like a ship adjusting heading. They propel themselves with a fin — a single continuous fin that runs the length of the underside, rippling in a wave that travels from head to tail or tail to head depending on the direction of travel, and the wave is not constant but *episodic*, the fin flexing in pulses, each pulse a decision, each decision a correction for the current.

Their color is the color of the stone. Pale grey-blue. They are not camouflaged by intention; they are this color because the chemistry of their bodies runs on the same minerals that the Works release, because they are built from the place they live in, because there is nothing here that is not ultimately derived from the made-stone. They are the Works, metabolized. They are the erosion, given fins and a spine and a dark simple eye.

The eye. It is not an eye that sees images. It has no lens, no iris, no retina in the way you understand those words. It is a cup — a depression in the side of the head, lined with cells that respond to the intensity and direction of the glow. The eel-things do not see each other. They see the *light* — the glow of the Works, the glow of the armored things, the shadow of a predator passing between them and the stone — and from this they construct not a picture but a *field*, a map of brightness and darkness that tells them where the walls are, where the gaps are, where the current is moving them, and where the things-that-are-still are waiting.

Because they know about the things-that-are-still. The school's movements tighten when they pass near the deep channels. Individuals at the edge of the school drift inward, toward the center, when the glow below them shifts in a way that suggests a large body resting on the stone. They have learned this. Or their bodies have learned it. Or the architecture has learned it for them — the pillars and walls funnelming the school past the ambush points in a way that suggests the Works were designed to do exactly this, to gather the moving things and present them to the still things, though you cannot say whether this was the builder's intent or simply the inevitable consequence of shaping water in a world where water shapes life.

They eat the armored things. They eat them in the gaps, when the school presses close to a surface and the armored things cannot retreat fast enough. They eat them in the water column, when a current eddy lifts a few individuals free of the stone and they drift, briefly, exposed, and the eel-things are there in an instant, the stiff bodies angling with a precision that seems mechanical, the mouth — a narrow slot, toothless, designed for swallowing things whole — opening just wide enough to take the armored thing in and crush it against the dark material of its own shell.

The school moves on. The armored things emerge. The click resumes. The Works glow.

---

The still ones.

You do not see them at first. You look at a platform and you see stone — eroded, smooth, glowing faintly — and you look away and you look back and the stone has *moved*. Not far. Half a meter. A meter. And you realize that what you took for a surface was a body, what you took for a shape was a *choice*, what you took for architecture was an animal.

They are flat. They are large. The smallest you see is perhaps two meters across; the largest, resting on a wide platform near the base of a pillar cluster, is five meters or more, and you cannot tell where its body ends and the stone begins because the edges have — not eroded, *faded*. The body thins at the margins until it is translucent, until it is nothing, until the animal becomes the surface and the surface becomes the animal and there is no line between them.

Their skin carries the glow. Not their own glow — they have incorporated it, taken the bioluminescent substance into their tissue the way the armored things have taken the dark mineral into their shells. They are wearing the Works. They have made themselves into the Works. And this is not camouflage in the way you understand camouflage — not a strategy, not a trick — it is *assimilation*. They have been here so long, they have rested against the stone for so many generations, that the stone is in them and they are in the stone and the boundary between life and not-life has dissolved the way the boundaries of the walls have dissolved, slowly, patiently, in the cold.

They hunt by stillness. This is their method, their entire method: they rest on a surface and they do not move. The current flows over them. The eel-things pass above them. The armored things scrape the stone nearby. And the still ones wait, and they wait, and they wait, and the waiting is not patience — patience implies a desire to stop waiting — the waiting is simply what they *are*. They are the waiting. They are a body that has been optimized for the absence of motion, and the optimization is total: their metabolism, you suspect, is nearly zero. Their muscles are built for a single contraction — one burst, one strike — and between strikes they are not alive in the way that other things are alive. They are stone. They are glowing stone, resting on glowing stone, in the dark.

The strike. When it comes, it is too fast to follow. One moment the body is flat and still and part of the surface. The next moment there is a gap in the water where an eel-thing was, and the still one's body is flexed, bowed, the muscles contracted into a shape that was not there a second ago, and then the flex releases and the body flattens, and the still one is resting again, and if you look closely you can see the slow bulge of a swallowed eel-thing moving through a digestive tract that is in no hurry because nothing here is in a hurry.

They do not eat often. They do not need to. The cold, the stillness, the near-zero metabolism — these are not adaptations to scarcity. They *are* the scarcity. The still ones have taken the poverty of this world — no sun, no plants, no abundance — and they have made it into a body, a way of being, a mode of existence in which less is not a deficit but a design. They are the most successful things here. There are more of them than you think. Now that you know how to look — for the places where the glow is slightly wrong, where the texture of the surface is slightly too smooth, slightly too regular — you see them everywhere. On every platform. Against every pillar. In every dead zone where the water goes still. The Works are covered in still ones. The Works are partly *made* of still ones, or they might as well be, for all the difference there is between the animal and its resting place.

---

The things above.

You see them as shadows. Occlusions. A darkening of the faint light from above — if there is an above, if there is a surface — that moves and is gone. They are large. You cannot tell how large because you cannot tell how far away they are. Ten meters? Fifty? A hundred? In water this dark, in water this still, distance collapses and scale becomes a guess.

They are slow. The shadows move with a deliberation that is different from the eel-things' deliberation. The eel-things decide and move; the things above simply *are in motion*, the way a cloud is in motion, the way a planet is in motion. They are not choosing. They are following a path that was set for them by something — the current at their depth, the mass of their bodies, a momentum that has been accumulating for longer than you can imagine. They do not turn. They do not accelerate. They do not interact with anything below them, as far as you can tell. The eel-things do not react to them. The still ones do not react to them. The armored things do not retreat from them. Whatever they are, they exist in a layer of this world that does not touch the layers you can see.

But they are there. They are alive, or they are the shapes of things that were alive, or they are something else entirely — something that the word "alive" does not reach. You watch one pass directly overhead, and the shadow it casts is enormous, and it is there for a long time, and then it is gone, and the glow of the Works returns to what it was, and nothing has changed.

You think: they are filter-feeders. They move through the water column and they filter whatever the water carries — the mineral traces, the dissolved chemistry, the fragments of film and the bodies of dead armored things and the waste of eel-things. They are the end of the food chain. They are where the energy goes when everything else is done with it. They are large because the energy at their depth is sparse and a body must be large to gather enough of it, and they are slow because the energy they gather does not permit speed, and they are dark because in water this deep, in water this black, the glow is a liability — it attracts the things below, and the things below are not something the things above want to meet.

Or. You think: they are not animals at all. They are something else. Something that the Works were built for. Something that moves through the architecture from above the way the current moves through it from the south — a presence that the structure was designed to interact with, to shape, to conduct. The walls direct the flow. The platforms shape the turbulence. The pillars create the vortices. And above, above all of it, the things move through the shaped field, and the shaping does something to them, or they do something to the shaping, and this — all of this, the Works, the water, the life, the long slow erosion — is a byproduct of that interaction.

You do not know. You cannot know. The things above do not come down. The things below do not go up. The two layers of this world are separated by a distance that is not just physical but *conceptual* — a gap in what is knowable, a boundary that the mind can stand at and look across and see only shadow.

---

There is one more thing.

It is not a species. It is not an organism. It is a behavior — a thing that happens, sometimes, in the dead zones, in the still pockets where the current does not reach.

An eel-thing enters a dead zone and it does not leave. It settles. It comes to rest against the stone, against the film, and its body goes still, and the stillness is not death — you can see the fin still rippling, faintly, barely, the last ghost of motion — but it is something. A pause. A cessation. The eel-thing rests on the glowing stone and its body begins to change.

The pale grey-blue deepens. The transparency at the edges increases. The body flattens — not dramatically, not in a way that suggests transformation, but gradually, over hours, over days, the cylindrical body spreading, widening, becoming less an eel and more a shape, less a shape and more a surface. The fin stops rippling. The eye — the dark simple cup — dims, and dims, and dims.

The eel-thing is becoming a still one.

Not in one lifetime. Not in one act. But the process begins here, in the dead zone, in the stillness, and it continues across generations — the eel-things that settle produce offspring that are slightly flatter, slightly stiller, slightly more likely to carry the glow in their skin, and those offspring settle in the dead zones too, and the process accelerates, and after enough generations the eel-thing's descendants are not eel-things at all. They are still ones. They are the surface they rest on. They are the Works.

Or. You are reading too much into a single observation. The eel-thing is dying. It has entered the dead zone because the dead zone is where dying things go, and the flattening is decomposition, and the dimming is death, and the stillness is simply the end of movement.

But the armored things do not come to feed on it. The other eel-things do not come to scavenge. The body rests on the stone and the film grows over it and the glow continues and nothing in the dead zone is in a hurry to resolve the question.

---

What lives here is not a list of species.

What lives here is a process. A continuous transformation of mineral into energy, energy into film, film into armor, armor into motion, motion into stillness, stillness into surface, surface into stone. The Works feed everything. Everything returns to the Works. The cycle is not a circle — it is a spiral, narrowing, each iteration slightly less than the last, the energy slightly less, the life slightly less, the stone slightly more worn, and the spiral has been turning for so long that you cannot tell where it started and you cannot tell where it will end and the things that live inside it cannot tell either because they are the spiral, they are the turning, they are the narrowing.

What lives here lives in the gap between the made and the unmade. In the erosion. In the softening. In the slow conversion of sharp edges into rounded ones, of intent into accident, of architecture into habitat. The builders are gone. The purpose is gone. The Works remain, and they are enough, and they will be enough for a long time — long in the way that geology is long, long in the way that water is patient — and when they are gone, finally, completely, when the last glow has faded and the last surface has worn smooth and the last energy has been taken into the last body, what will remain is the water. The current. The cold. The dark.

And in the dark, something will move. Something that was once the Works, metabolized through ten thousand generations of eating and being eaten, resting and moving and resting again. Something that carries the minerals and the glow and the stiffness and the stillness in its body, in the deep, in the cold, in the water that has always been here.

Something that does not remember what it was built from. Something that does not need to.
