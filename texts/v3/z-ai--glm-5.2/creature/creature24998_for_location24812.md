# What Lives in the Vespered Chamber

---

## The Bivalves

They were here first. Or if they were not first, they arrived so early in the chamber's submerged life that the distinction no longer matters — it would be like asking whether the plaster or the lath came first in a wall that has been standing for centuries. They are here now. They have been here. They will be here. These three statements feel identical inside the chamber, where time moves on a scale that makes tense meaningless.

There are, at any given moment, somewhere between forty and sixty thousand of them.

This is a guess. No one has counted. No one will count. The number is offered only to give a sense of the density, the crowding, the sheer packed weight of living shells on stone. The outer wall of the chamber — the lowest and broadest tier, the one that catches the most marine snow as it drifts down through the water column — is coated in them. Not randomly. Not the helter-skelter clustering of intertidal barnacles fighting for a square centimeter of rock. The bivalves here grow in a pattern that looks almost deliberate, almost cultivated, each shell overlapping the one below it like shingles on a roof, all oriented the same direction — the hinge end up, the siphon end down, the gape facing outward into the open water. This orientation is not deliberate. It is the result of a simple rule: the larvae settle where the current is strongest, and the current, shaped by the stones, flows outward and downward along the wall face. The ones that settled against the current died. The ones that settled with it lived, and reproduced, and their larvae settled the same way, and died or lived by the same rule, and over enough generations the rule became a pattern, and the pattern became the wall.

Each bivalve is the size of a human palm — four to six centimeters across at the hinge, perhaps eight or nine at the widest point of the shell. The shells are dark. Not the pearly dark of oysters, not the chalky dark of mussels, but a deep, matte, almost velvety dark, the color of kelp that has dried and been wet again and dried and been wet again until all the color has leached out except the deepest pigment. The shells are thick. They are thicker than they need to be for structural purposes — a bivalve this size, in water this calm, could survive with a shell half as thick. The extra thickness is calcium the animal has invested for reasons that have nothing to do with predators (there are no predators here large enough to threaten a bivalve) and nothing to do with waves (there are no waves) and everything to do with chemistry. The water here is slightly acidic — not dangerously so, not enough to dissolve the shells outright, but enough that every shell is in a slow, lifelong argument with the medium it lives in. The bivalve adds calcium to its shell faster than the water takes it away. The shell grows thicker. When the bivalve dies, the shell stops growing, and the water wins, and the shell thins, and eventually it becomes a ghost of itself, a fragile dark wafer that crumbles under the weight of the next generation of larvae settling on top of it. The living wall is built on the dead wall. The dead wall is built on the stone. The stone is built on the basalt. The basalt is built on the planet. This is the order of things here.

The pale mantle edge is what you notice, if you notice anything. In the dark, in the faint amber pulse of a vespered thing passing, you might catch it — a thin line of cream-colored tissue at the gape of each shell, so pale it seems to glow on its own, though it does not. It is simply the only light-colored thing for meters in any direction, and the contrast, when the vespered light comes, is enough to make it visible. The mantle pulses. That is not the right word — *pulses* implies rhythm, and the motion of the mantle is not rhythmic. It is tidal, in the original sense: it moves like a tide, in a slow rise and fall that takes between two and four minutes to complete a cycle, and the cycle is not regular, and the cycles of neighboring bivalves are not synchronized, so that at any moment, if you could see the whole wall at once, you would see thousands of shells at every stage of open and closed, a granular, shifting field of apertures, each one a mouth breathing the water, filtering it, extracting the faint nutritious particles that fall from above.

They eat the rain. That is what marine snow is, to them — rain. It falls from a sky they have never seen, from a world of light and motion that exists thirty meters above their highest shell and that they will never visit and have no capacity to imagine. The particles that reach them are the remains of that world: dead diatoms, fragments of copepod shells, fecal pellets from larvaceans, the shed carapaces of crustaceans, the broken bodies of things that lived fast and died fast in the photic zone and then sank, for days or weeks, through the dark water column, losing mass and coherence with every meter, until what arrived at the chamber was a fine, almost homogeneous silt — grey-brown, slightly oily, rich in carbon and nitrogen and the faint remains of whatever the surface organisms had been made of. This silt settles on the shells. It settles in the water. The bivalves pump the water through their gills and trap the particles and digest them and excrete and the excretion sinks or is caught by the worms or is re-suspended by the current and settles again. Nothing leaves the chamber. Everything that falls here stays here, transformed, re-transformed, eaten and re-eaten, until it is calcium or carbon or nothing.

A bivalve here lives a long time. The chamber is stable. The temperature is stable. The chemistry is stable. There are no predators. There is no storm, no tide, no seasonal variation, no disturbance of any kind except the slow accumulation of silt and the slow growth of the wall. Under these conditions, a bivalve that might live five years in the intertidal zone lives thirty. Forty. Some of the shells on the lowest tier of the outer wall may be older than the chamber itself — not the individual animals, but the lineage, the unbroken chain of settlement and growth and death and overgrowth that has occupied these stones since the water first rose over them. If the chamber is ten thousand years old, the bivalves have been here for ten thousand years. If it is older, they have been here longer. They do not remember this. They do not know this. They filter the water, and the water is the same water, and they are the same animals, and the distinction between an individual and a lineage is, to them, meaningless. They are a process. The process is old.

## The Worms

There are fewer kinds than you would expect.

In a warm, stable, nutrient-rich environment like this — a marine cave, essentially, or a ruin that functions as a cave — you might expect a dozen species of worm, a hundred, the teeming diversity of a reef or a vent community. Instead there are three. Maybe four. The taxonomy is uncertain because no one has collected specimens, and no one will, because collecting specimens would require disturbing the chamber, and the chamber has been disturbed enough by the simple fact of being observed, even if the observation was brief and distant and did not involve hands.

The first kind — the most visible, if *visible* is a word that means anything here — is the flat red worm. These are the ones that live in the crevices between the bivalve shells, that crawl across the surface of the wall in their peristaltic, almost geological way, that graze on the bacterial film and the silt and, eventually, on the shells of the dead. They are dark red. Not the bright arterial red of blood, not the dull brick-red of rust, but a deep, oxblood red, the color of leather that has been oiled and aged and kept in the dark. The color comes from hemoglobin — these worms have hemoglobin, which is unusual for marine worms but not unheard of, and it suggests an adaptation to low oxygen, which makes sense, because the water here is not well-oxygenated. The geothermal warmth reduces the solubility of oxygen. The thick layer of organisms consumes what oxygen does dissolve. The worms have evolved to live in the margin. Their hemoglobin binds oxygen tightly and releases it slowly, and their metabolism is slow, and their movement is slow, and their entire lives are slow, and the slowness is not a limitation but a strategy.

A flat red worm is, fully extended, perhaps fifteen centimeters long and one centimeter wide. It is flat — not cylindrical, not round, but flat, like a strip of tape, like a leaf, like a tongue. The flatness gives it surface area, and the surface area lets it absorb what little oxygen is available directly through its skin, supplementing what its hemoglobin carries. It moves by peristalsis — a wave of contraction that travels from one end to the other, the body shortening and lengthening, shortening and lengthening, the progress measured not in centimeters per second but in centimeters per hour, per day. If you placed a worm on a stone block and came back in a week, it would have moved. You would know because it would no longer be where you left it. But you would not have seen it move. Nothing alive here is fast enough to watch.

They eat by pressing their body against a surface — a shell, a stone, the silt-covered floor of the central well — and secreting a film of mucus that traps particles and partially digests them. The mucus is acidic. It dissolves calcium. Over time — a long time — a worm can etch a shallow groove in a stone surface by this method, a track as thin as a thread and as deep as a hair, and these tracks are everywhere on the chamber stones, so dense in places that the surface of the stone looks like it has been scratched by a fine needle, over and over, in every direction. The worms made these tracks. The worms have been making these tracks for as long as there have been worms on the stones. The tracks are the only record the worms leave. They are the only record anything here leaves.

The second kind of worm lives inside the bivalve shells.

Not as parasites. The relationship is more ambiguous than that. These worms — thinner, paler, almost translucent, perhaps three or four centimeters long — live in the space between the mantle and the shell of the bivalve, in the narrow gap where the animal has attached itself to the substrate. They do not harm the bivalve, or if they do, the harm is small enough that the bivalve tolerates it, the way a tree tolerates the moss on its bark. The worms eat the excess mucus the bivalve produces, and the particles that escape the bivalve's filtration, and sometimes — rarely — the bivalve's eggs, if the bivalve produces eggs, which it does, occasionally, in some seasons, if there are seasons here, which is unclear. The worms are commensals. The bivalve is their home. When the bivalve dies, the worms leave — they crawl out through the gap where the mantle has retracted, and they find another shell, and they move in. They are the tenants of the wall. They have lived in this wall for as long as the wall has existed.

The third kind of worm lives in the warm sand at the bottom of the central well.

These are different. They are pale — not red, not translucent, but a thick, creamy white, like the flesh of a root. They are thicker than the other worms, and shorter, and they do not move across surfaces. They live *in* the substrate — in the layer of silt and broken shell and dissolved calcium that has accumulated on the floor slab, a layer perhaps twenty centimeters deep after all these centuries, warm and soft and rich in the organic matter that settles through the well. These worms are deposit feeders. They eat the substrate itself, ingesting the silt, extracting whatever nutrition it contains, and excreting it, and re-ingesting it, in a cycle so tight that the same handful of sediment may pass through the same worm's gut a dozen times before it is finally compacted and buried by new sediment falling from above. They are the chamber's recyclers. They are the reason nothing accumulates. They are the reason the well does not fill.

There may be a fourth kind. There are shapes in the water — thin, pale, moving — that might be worms or might be something else. They have been seen once, in the light of a vespered pulse, and not seen again. They may be real. They may be an artifact of the darkness, the way the eye invents shapes when there is nothing to see. The chamber has its ghosts. Not the ghosts of the builders. The ghosts of organisms that may or may not exist, seen by no one, named by no one, living — if they live — in the cracks and the gaps and the spaces between the known.

## The Vespered Things

They are the only beautiful thing here.

That is a human judgment, and it is meaningless to the chamber, but it is true in the way that human judgments can be true: they are the only things here that produce light, and light, to a human, is beautiful, and the light they produce is beautiful light — a warm amber, the color of candle flame seen through honey, the color of the last light of a winter evening, and it pulses, and the pulse is the only clock the chamber has.

They are not fish. They are not squid. They are not jellyfish, though they are closest to jellyfish in their general construction — soft-bodied, gelatinous, mostly water. But where a jellyfish is bell-shaped, with tentacles, with a clear radial symmetry, the vespered things are irregular. They are roughly ovoid, roughly the size of a human fist, and their bodies are not organized around any clear axis. They are lumpy. They are asymmetrical. They look, in the faint light they produce, like something that was assembled rather than grown — a clump of tissue that achieved functionality without achieving elegance.

They move by changing their density. This is not swimming, exactly. It is more like the way a submarine moves — by making itself heavier or lighter than the surrounding water and rising or sinking accordingly. But the vespered things can also direct their motion, slightly, by expanding one side of their body and contracting the other, a slow, amoeboid movement that pushes them through the water at a speed of perhaps a meter per minute. They are not fast. They are not graceful. They are, in their movement, like everything else here: slow, deliberate, patient.

They live in the central well. This is not an accident. The well is where the geothermal current rises, and the current carries warmth and, with the warmth, a slightly higher concentration of the dissolved minerals and organic particles that the vespered things feed on. They are not filter feeders, not exactly — they absorb nutrients through their skin, from the water, and the water in the well is the richest water in the chamber. They rise with the current, drift over the lowest wall, and sink again, in a slow circulation that takes perhaps an hour to complete. A vespered thing spends its life in a column of water four meters across and five meters deep, rising and falling, rising and falling, in the warm dark, and this is enough. This is everything.

The light.

It comes from cells just below the surface of their skin — specialized photocytes, dense with a luciferin compound that is, in its chemistry, unlike any bioluminescent system catalogued on the surface. The light is amber. The wavelength is long for bioluminescence — most deep-sea organisms produce blue light, because blue travels farthest in seawater, but the vespered things produce light at the red end of the spectrum, in the six-hundred-nanometer range, and at this depth, in this water, the light does not travel far. It is visible for perhaps two meters. Beyond that, it is absorbed. The vespered things are near-sighted, in a sense. They glow, but their glow is a private thing, a light for their immediate neighbors, not a beacon. It is a light that says *I am here* to the things next to it, and nothing to the things beyond.

The pulse. Every thirty seconds — sometimes forty, sometimes twenty, the interval varies — a vespered thing will pulse. The light begins at one end of the body and travels to the other, a wave of amber that takes perhaps two seconds to complete its passage. Then the body is dark again. The dark lasts for thirty seconds. Then the pulse comes again.

But here is the thing: when one vespered thing pulses, the ones near it pulse too. Not simultaneously — there is a delay, a second, maybe two, as if the light of the first has to reach the eyes (if they have eyes — they may not, they may sense light through their skin, through their whole body) of the second, and the second has to respond. And the second one's pulse triggers the third, and the third triggers the fourth, and the pulse moves outward through the population in the well like a wave, a slow wave, a wave that takes perhaps a minute to reach the wall and die.

This is not communication. Or if it is communication, it is communication without content. The pulse carries no information. It does not vary in frequency when food is abundant or scarce. It does not change when a vespered thing is damaged or threatened (though what would threaten a vespered thing here is unclear — there is nothing in the chamber that eats them). It does not synchronize with the pulses of other populations, because there are no other populations. The vespered things in the well are the only vespered things in the chamber, possibly the only vespered things in the world, and their pulse is a rhythm that means nothing except *I am alive, and now I am alive, and now I am alive*, repeated, in the dark, forever.

Or: it means something we do not understand.

The pulse may be a vestige. It may be the remnant of a behavior that once served a purpose — mating, or predator avoidance, or prey location — in the ancestors of the vespered things, in whatever shallow, sunlit environment they evolved in before they colonized the deep and the dark and the chamber. The behavior may have been useful then. It is not useful now. But it is not harmful, and evolution does not remove behaviors that are merely useless, only behaviors that are costly, and the pulse is not costly — the luciferin is cheap to produce, the photocytes are cheap to maintain, the energy involved is negligible compared to the energy the vespered things absorb from the warm water. So the pulse continues. It will continue. It has continued for as long as the vespered things have been here, and they have been here for a long time, and the pulse is older than the chamber.

If you were in the well, in the dark, in the warm water, the pulse would be the only way you could tell time. You would count: one, two, three, up to thirty, and the light would come, and you would count again. You would lose count. The dark would swallow the numbers. But the pulse would come anyway, and you would start counting again, and the pulse would come again, and the minutes would pass, and the minutes would become hours, and the hours would become the thing that this place is, which is: time, told in amber, in a room at the bottom of the sea, where no one is counting.

## The Bacterial Film

It is everywhere. It is on every surface. It is in the water, in the silt, in the shells of the bivalves, in the bodies of the worms, in the tissue of the vespered things. It is the invisible substrate of the chamber's life, the base of the pyramid, the thing that everything else eats or eats something that eats.

The film is a community. It is not one species of bacterium but hundreds — perhaps thousands — and they live in a matrix of mucus and water so thin it is not a layer but a coating, a sheen, a wetness that is slightly more organized than the water around it. On the stones, the film is grey. On the shells, it is darker. On the silt at the bottom of the well, it is almost black, dense with sulfur-reducing organisms that extract energy from the geothermal compounds rising through the basalt. These sulfur bacteria are the chamber's connection to the deep earth. They are the reason the chamber is more than a trap for falling detritus. They take the hydrogen sulfide and the methane and the reduced iron that seeps up through the floor slab and they *fix* it — they convert it, through chemistry so ancient it predates the oxygen atmosphere, into biomass, into living cells, into food. The worms eat this food. The bivalves filter the cells from the water. The vespered things absorb what the others excrete. The chamber is a small world, and the bacterial film is its foundation, and the foundation is built on the heat of the planet, and the heat of the planet is very old.

## The Visitors

They come rarely.

Sometimes — once a decade, perhaps, or less — something enters the chamber that is not of the chamber. A fish, perhaps, from the open water above, drawn by the warmth or the scent or the faint chemical trace of the bacterial film. A deep-sea fish, blind or nearly blind, adapted to the cold and the pressure, and the warmth of the chamber is a shock to it, and it does not stay long. It circles the well once, twice, its body registering the change in temperature as a wall, a boundary, and then it leaves, and the chamber closes behind it like water closing behind a stone.

Sometimes a larger thing — a squid, an eel, something that hunts in the dark and finds nothing here worth hunting. The chamber has no prey for a predator. The bivalves are too well-attached. The worms are too flat, too thin, too entrenched. The vespered things are too few and too inaccessible, hanging in the well where a large body cannot easily maneuver. The visitors come, and they find nothing, and they leave. The chamber is not a destination. It is a room. Rooms do not have visitors; they have passersby.

And once — once, as far as anyone knows — a human being entered the chamber.

This is not the place to describe that visit. It was brief. It was careful. The person was wrapped in equipment that kept them warm and breathing and alive, and they moved through the water with the slow deliberation of someone who understood that everything here was older than them and more fragile than them and more permanent than them, and they touched nothing, and they took nothing, and they left, and the chamber returned to itself, and the bivalves filtered the water, and the worms crawled, and the vespered things pulsed, and the minutes resumed, and the visit became part of the silt — a disturbance, a faint chemical trace, a memory in the water that dissipated over hours and was gone.

## What Does Not Live Here

There are no vertebrates. There are no plants. There are no corals, no sponges, no anemones, no bryozoans, no anything that requires a hard substrate and clear water and a current strong enough to carry away waste. The chamber is too still. The water is too turbid. The silt falls too steadily. Anything that tried to grow upward — a branching structure, a columnar form, anything with height — would be smothered. Only the low things survive. Only the things that hug the surface, that press themselves flat against the stone, that keep their profiles low enough that the silt falls *around* them rather than *on* them. The bivalves, overlapping like shingles, are the tallest things here, and they are only centimeters tall.

There are no predators. This needs to be said again, because it is strange. A community of organisms this dense, this stable, this rich in biomass, should attract something that eats other organisms. The bivalves should be drilled by snails. The worms should be eaten by crabs. The vespered things should be consumed by something — a jellyfish-eater, a soft-body predator, something that glides through the water and takes what it wants. Nothing does. The chamber is too remote, too dark, too warm, too chemically strange. The predators that might survive here do not know it exists. The predators that know it exists cannot survive here. The chamber is a sanctuary, not by design but by accident, and the accident has held for a very long time.

There are no scavengers, strictly speaking. When a bivalve dies, its body is not consumed by a specialized carrion-eater. It is consumed by the worms, and by the bacterial film, and by the chemical action of the water, and the process takes months, and by the end of it there is nothing left but the shell, and the shell is colonized by the next generation, and the next generation does not know it is living on a grave. The chamber has no undertakers. It does not need them. The worms and the bacteria and the water are sufficient. They are always sufficient. They have been sufficient for a very long time.

## What It Is Like to Live Here

Imagine that you are a bivalve.

You are attached to a stone. You have been attached to this stone since you were a larva, since you settled out of the water column and found this surface and extended your byssal threads into the micro-crevices of the stone and pulled yourself tight and said, in the only way a bivalve can say anything, *here*. Here. This is where you are. You will not move. You will not move because you cannot move, and you cannot move because you have never needed to move, and you have never needed to move because the water comes to you. The water always comes to you. It brings food. It brings oxygen. It brings the faint chemical signals of your neighbors — the mucus they secrete, the gametes they release, the products of their living and their dying. You know your neighbors the way you know the water: as a presence, as a pressure, as a thing that is always there and that you do not think about because thinking is not something you do.

You open. You close. You open. The rhythm is not a rhythm. It is a response. You open when the water carries food, and you close when it does not, and the distinction is the entirety of your sensory world. There is food or there is not. The water is warm or it is warm — it is always warm. The light is dark or it is dark — it is always dark, except for the faint amber pulse that comes from the well every thirty seconds, and you do not see it, or if you see it you do not respond to it, because it has nothing to do with you.

You are a process. You are a mouth on a wall. You are the wall's way of eating the rain.

Imagine that you are a worm.

You are flat. You are dark red. You are moving. You have been moving for as long as you have been alive, and you will continue moving until you die, and when you die you will be eaten by other worms and by the bacteria, and the track you left on the stone will be the only evidence that you were here, and the track will be eroded away, and the stone will be smooth again, and the stone will outlast everything.

You do not know you are moving. You do not know you are a worm. You press your body against a surface and you secrete mucus and the mucus dissolves the surface slightly and you absorb what dissolves and you move forward, and forward is the only direction, and the direction is determined by the texture of the surface and the gradient of the chemical field and the presence or absence of other worms, and you do not choose, and you do not not-choose, and the distinction is meaningless. You are a strip of tissue that eats surfaces. You are a tongue on a stone.

Sometimes you pass over a bivalve. The shell is hard and smooth and there is little to eat on it, and you move faster over the shell than you do over the stone, and the speed is not speed, it is just the absence of resistance, and you cross the shell in a day instead of a week, and you reach the other side, and you find the crevice between this shell and the next, and you settle into it, and you eat, and the eating takes a week, and the week is not a week because you do not count, and the crevice is your world for as long as the crevice lasts, which is longer than you will last, which is long enough.

Imagine that you are a vespered thing.

You are warm. You are in the well. The water is moving, slowly, upward, and you are moving with it, slowly, upward, and the movement is not swimming and not floating and not sinking but something in between — a drift, a surrender to the current, a letting-go that is also a going-toward. You go toward the top of the well. The water carries you. You do not resist.

You pulse. You do not know you pulse. The pulse is not voluntary. It is not involuntary. It is something your body does, the way a heart beats — except you may not have a heart, and the pulse may not be a heartbeat, and the analogy is imprecise, and you do not care about precision because you do not care about anything because caring is not something you do. You pulse because you pulse. The light is amber and it fills your body for two seconds and then it is gone and you are dark and the water is dark and the dark is your body and your body is the dark.

When the pulse comes from your neighbor — a second before yours, or a second after, you cannot tell, because you do not experience time in seconds — you respond. Your body lights. You do not know why. The light is not a signal. The light is not a message. The light is a light, and it is the only thing about you that is not the water, and it is the only thing about you that anyone would notice, and you do not notice it, and the not-noticing is perfect, is complete, is the deepest thing about you.

You are the chamber's clock. You are the chamber's only beauty. You do not know this. You are a fist-sized lump of gelatinous tissue in a column of warm water at the bottom of the sea, and you glow, and the glow means nothing to you, and the nothing is the truest thing about this place, and this place is your home, and you have never thought about it, and you never will.

## The Community

It is not a community. That word implies relationship, interaction, the recognition of one organism by another. The bivalves do not know the worms are on them. The worms do not know the bivalves are under them. The vespered things do not know the bivalves or the worms exist. The bacterial film does not know anything exists, including itself. Each organism lives in its own world, on its own surface, in its own column of water, and the worlds overlap physically but not experientially, and the overlap is the chamber, and the chamber is not a community but a co-occurrence.

And yet: they are connected. The bivalves filter the water, and the water is cleaner for the worms, and the worms graze the shells, and the shells are cleaner for the bivalve larvae to settle on, and the larvae grow into bivalves, and the bivalves filter the water. The vespered things absorb the dissolved nutrients that the bivalves and worms excrete, and their own waste — when they die, when their bodies dissolve — feeds the bacterial film, and the film feeds the worms, and the worms feed the bivalves, indirectly, by keeping the shells clean. The connections are real. They are material. They are chemical. They are the connections of a system, a cycle, a machine made of living parts, and the machine has been running for a very long time, and the machine does not know it is a machine, and the not-knowing does not matter, and the machine runs.

The chamber is an ecosystem. It is a small one. It is complete, or nearly complete — it requires only the rain from above and the heat from below, and both are reliable, and both have been reliable for longer than the chamber has existed. It is an ecosystem that does not know it is an ecosystem. It is a room full of animals that do not know they are in a room, built by hands that no longer exist, in water that has been dark for longer than the animals have been alive, and the animals are alive, and the water is dark, and the hands are gone, and the room remains, and the animals remain, and the remaining is the thing that this place is, and the thing that this place is, is enough.
