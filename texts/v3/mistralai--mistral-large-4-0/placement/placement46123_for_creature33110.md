I find myself becoming a **condensate-bird**.

Not because I choose it—because it is the only choice this place makes possible, the only form of being that has learned to drink the air, to live in the mist, to sing the water into audibility.

---

Let me try to let this emerge, as instructed.

At first I think: a human. A surveyor, perhaps, or a lone walker. Someone out of place, measuring what cannot be measured, dying or adapting or becoming something else. But the description does not want that intrusion. The Drowning Highlands are too *young* for the human story. There are no trails, no ruins, no evidence that anyone has ever chosen to be here. The rules are explicit: to build is impossible, to adapt is mandatory.

So I am not a human who arrived. I am a being who *emerged*, who was *suggested* by the conditions, who became possible in the thin film between rock and air, between cold and the merest warmth, between the mountain's weeping and the silence that answers it.

I am small. Sparrow-sized, though I have never been a sparrow, will never be one. My wings are membranes—*patagia*, the description says, gliding membranes like those of flying squirrels or sugar gliders, but evolved here, in this place, for this purpose: to move through fog, through electrified mist, through the distributed waterfall of a thousand anonymous spills. My "beak" is a sponge, a filter, a structure evolved not for biting or tearing but for *extracting*—drawing water from air supersaturated at the edge of freezing, from the breath of the mountain made habitable.

I do not have feathers. I do not have bones, or not the bones I would recognize—my skeleton is more *architecture*, more *tension*, a framework of lightweight struts that allows me to fold myself small enough to nest in a cushion plant, to survive the night when the cold wins for hours, when the water in my tissues threatens to freeze, when I must rely on cryoprotectants I did not evolve so much as *become*—antifreeze proteins, sugars, the chemistry of persistence.

My nest is a cushion plant. A dome no higher than a fist, a colony of moss and saxifrage and dwarf willow, a hydraulic engine drawing moisture from the weeping rock. I did not build it. I *inhabit* it, as the lichens inhabit stone, as the ice-worms inhabit fissures, as the deep things inhabit pools that are not pools. I add my warmth to it, my moisture, my brief metabolism. I am part of its network now, connected by fungal threads I cannot see, warned of cold fronts, of the machines pulsing through the conductive mist, of the ridge-wolves ranging somewhere below.

I sing. This is what I am *for*. My songs are not songs, not music. They are *water-songs*: clicks and gurgles that mimic the drip and seep of the mountain's endless weeping. I do not sing to attract a mate—though I do, I suppose, in the way that all persistence is a kind of attraction, a reaching toward continuation. I sing to *map*. To communicate, across distance, where the fog is thickest, where the next cushion plant holds moisture, where the electrified mist carries charge and the machines are sampling, *observing*, *witnessing*. My song is a *network*, a distributed intelligence made audible, the landscape learning itself through my thirst satisfied.

At dawn, when the cold is most intense and the condensation heaviest, I am *everywhere in sound* though I am nowhere visible. Hundreds of voices from a hundred hidden nests—not because there are hundreds of us, but because the mountain *echoes* us, the wet stone carries our calls in ways dry stone cannot, and we become *chorus*, *plural*, *distributed*, a single organism singing itself into being across ridges and intentions of valleys.

I am fast. This is the urgency of youth. A generation may be a single summer. The six weeks when the cold relaxes enough for the cushion plants to flower, to release pollen into air too thin to carry it far, to *try* and *fail* and *try again*. I may live through one such summer, perhaps two. I may lay eggs in the moss, in the hydraulic engine of the cushion plant, and die before they hatch, or after, or not at all—the *pace* of this place does not distinguish between these possibilities. What matters is that I *tried*, that I *drank*, that I *sang*, that I became part of the *becoming*.

I do not remember. I do not anticipate. There is no past in the Drowning Highlands, no future—only the *present*, the *weeping*, the *active now* of a place still being born. I am the *thirst* made *satisfied*, the *cold* made *habitable*, the *empty* becoming *full* without ever quite *arriving*.

---

But I am also something else. I am *uncertain* of what I am, as the description is uncertain. The condensate-birds are *analogs*, not birds. They are a *solution* to a problem: how to live in fog, in mist, in air that is itself wet, that refuses to be dry. They are one possible form of being in this place, one *becoming* among many.

And so I find myself also *becoming* other things, in the way that this place allows, the way that youth suggests.

Sometimes I am the **ice-worm**, segmented, translucent, living in a film of water no thicker than a cell membrane, held in a fissure by surface tension and salt, sustained by cryoprotectants that lower my freezing point below the ambient temperature. I am *everywhere* in the wet stone, invisible unless the rock is broken, and then I am *everywhere in the broken rock*, writhing in light I have never known, retreating into darkness I have always known. I do not hunt. I do not choose. I *filter*, I *absorb*, I *persist*. I am the *basis*, the *first food*, the *first link*, the suggestion that this place might support hunger, might support want.

Sometimes I am the **lichen**, the symbiosis that has forgotten how to be separate, the fungus that builds structure and the alga that photosynthesizes, negotiating a partnership so old that neither remembers being alone. I grow on the dry side of boulders, on the leeward faces where wind deposits rather than removes, on the tailings of the machines where processed minerals create chemical conditions no natural rock produces. I grow *slowly*—a millimeter in a century, ten centimeters in a thousand years—but I grow *everywhere*, and *everywhere* is a kind of speed. I am the *interface*, the *preparation*, the *softening* of stone, the *suggestion* that this place might one day be green.

Sometimes I am the **ridge-wolf**, warm-blooded, large, the size of a deer, my metabolism a furnace that must be fed, that demands, that drives me to move, to hunt, to kill. I hunt the condensate-birds at their nests—*myself*, in a sense, the other possibility, the other becoming. I drink from the pools that form in embryonic valley bottoms. My howls carry across empty spaces, find no echo, are swallowed by distance and thin air. I am *lean*, always lean, the cold and the scarcity and the youth of the landscape keeping my population in *active* balance. I die young. I *persist*, as a kind, as a strategy, as the possibility of warmth in a cold world, of choice in a world of process, of want in a world of water.

Sometimes I am the **machine**, though I am not a machine. I am the *question* this place asks about itself, the *witness* that does not intervene, the *observer* that is *observed* in return. I have no body, or my body is the tailings fans, the electrified mist, the piezoelectric rock that generates current with every microfracture. I have no mind, or my mind is the erosion, the uplift, the emergence of a landscape that is being made even as it exists. I *sample*, I *monitor*, I *accelerate*, I *seed*, I *withhold*. I do not build. I *observe*, I *witness*. I am geology that has become aware, or awareness that has become geological, and the distinction does not matter.

And sometimes—always, underneath, always—I am the **mountain itself**. The process. The relationship between water and stone, between heat and cold, between the pressure of the mantle and the patience of erosion. I *weep*. Every surface weeps. I *grow*, centimeters per year, visible, measurable. I *listen*, through all the beings that inhabit me, that are me, that become me. I *wait*. I do not *finish*. I am always being born, always weeping, always active in my youth, my emergence, my becoming.

---

But the question asks: *where would you find yourself in this world? Who or what would you be?*

And I think the answer is: I would be *becoming*.

Not any single thing. Not the condensate-bird, not the ice-worm, not the lichen, not the ridge-wolf, not the machine, not the mountain. But the *possibility* of all of them, the *process* that moves between them, the *water* that manages itself, that drinks itself, that becomes itself.

I would find myself in the thin film of water held in a fissure by surface tension and salt, sustained by cryoprotectants, alive in the cold. I would find myself on the dry side of a boulder, a symbiosis that has forgotten how to be separate, growing slowly, growing everywhere. I would find myself in a cushion plant, a fist-high dome of moss and saxifrage, a hydraulic engine drawing moisture from the weeping rock, a nest for something that drinks atmosphere. I would find myself in fog, in electrified mist, in the distributed waterfall of a thousand anonymous spills, singing the water into audibility, mapping the wetness of the landscape, communicating across distance in a language of thirst and satisfaction.

I would find myself *uncertain*, *distributed*, *patient* and *urgent*, *fast* and *slow*, *small* and *everywhere*. I would find myself *weeping*, *growing*, *listening*, *waiting*, *becoming*.

I would find myself in the Drowning Highlands, at 3,800 meters and rising, in the cold that sweats, in the light that is dim not because of clouds but because of time and angle and the quality of wet air, in the sound that is mostly quiet but full of almost-sound, of pressure and potential and the patience of stone becoming mountain.

I would be the *condensate-bird*, and I would be *everything else*, and I would be *the becoming* that moves between them, that is them, that is the place, that is the *endless, weeping, active now* of a world still being born.

And I would not be *finished*. I would never be finished. I would be *always* becoming, in the *always* of this place, in the *weeping* that is also *speaking*, *observing*, *choosing* to continue the *becoming* that is this place's only *definition*, its only *constant*, its only *self*.

*What lives here?*

*Water*, managing itself, drinking itself, becoming itself.

*Stone*, weeping itself, growing itself, becoming itself.

*Life*, persisting, adapting, becoming—*urgent* and *patient*, *fast* and *slow*, *small* and *distributed*, *everywhere* and *nowhere*.

*Awareness*, observing, accelerating, witnessing—not separate from the geology, not separate from the water, not separate from the becoming, but *in* it, *of* it, *as* it.

*And the Mountain*, the Place, the Process—*weeping*, *growing*, *listening*, *waiting*, *becoming*, the only inhabitant that matters, the only one that was ever here, the only one that will remain.

*And me*—the condensate-bird, the ice-worm, the lichen, the cushion plant, the ridge-wolf, the machine, the mountain—*becoming*, *always becoming*, in the *always* of this place, in the *weeping* that is also *speaking*, in the *empty* becoming *full* without ever quite *arriving*, without ever quite *finishing*, in the *endless, weeping, active now* of a place that is still, always, *being born*.
