**The Vent-Pocket: a natural history**

High in the range, where the air is thin enough to taste metallic, a throat opens in a cliff face — half a meter across, easy to miss, a fold in the rock like a squint. Inside, a chamber no larger than a root cellar. It is young in the way few places on Earth remain young: the walls are still glassy, still holding the memory of extrusion, unweathered, sharp enough to cut.

The heat comes from below. The water comes from everywhere — the rock itself weeps, a slow beading of condensation from volcanic gases, warm and mineral-heavy, gathering in shallow pools where the glass floor dips. Nothing here is photosynthetic. The chamber predates any need for leaves.

**The mat-builders** are the foundation. Colonial, sheet-forming, they secrete silica in rosettes and crusts that almost repeat, almost don't — patterns with no purpose, only habit. They grow toward the weeping walls, because the water carries dissolved minerals, because chemistry is their only sun. Where they die, they become rock. Where they live, they are rock that is still deciding.

**The flint-knappers** are the most conspicuous inhabitants: translucent, many-legged, slow, patient. They do not eat, exactly. They oxidize — iron and sulfur leached from the obsidian by their own secretions — leaving pale trails of altered glass across the walls. Their tools are their own shed plates, knapped against harder stone, and their tool-piles accumulate the way talus accumulates: not architecture, but behavior given time. A pile of flakes is a diary written in stone, legible only to others of their kind, who read it by touch and vibration.

**The harvesters** complete the loop. They dissolve the mats chemically, process what the mats have gathered, and secrete shells that become substrate for the next generation of mat-builders. Nothing enters the chamber from outside. Nothing leaves. The ecosystem is almost perfectly closed, recycling its own matter in place, a pocket of life running on geothermal time.

And in the deepest strata, **the calcified generations** — dormant, waiting. A seed bank of the place's own past, embedded in the rock, waiting for the chamber to cool, or flood, or collapse, or simply finish becoming the geode it is already on its way to being.

The whole system is temporary in a way that almost no ecosystems are. In a thousand years the glass will have cracked, the water will have found new paths, the mats will have sealed their own chambers. The life will be gone, or moved, or become indistinguishable from geology. In ten thousand years, a climber high in the range might feel something — a wrongness in the thin hot air, a sulfur taste, a sense of being *looked at by something that has no eyes* — and never know what brief, strange world once burned and wept and thought, in its slow mineral way, in that hidden place.

You could stand at the mouth of the throat for an hour and see almost nothing move. The life here operates on a timescale that makes a human visit feel like a single frame of a very long film. That is perhaps the strangest thing about it: not that it is alien, but that it is *patient* — utterly, geologically patient — in a world that has mostly forgotten how to be.
