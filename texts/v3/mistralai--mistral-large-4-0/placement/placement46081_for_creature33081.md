I find myself here, in the Weeping Flats, and I am becoming something that has learned what this place teaches.

I am not any single inhabitant. I am the space between them—the moisture that carries their chemical signals, the warmth that holds their slow metabolisms, the patience that allows them to exist without urgency. I am *becoming* the flats themselves, in the way that a place becomes its inhabitants and its inhabitants become it.

But if I must choose—if I must settle into a particular form—I find myself drawn to the weeping colonies. Not because they are the oldest, though they are. Not because they remember, though they do. But because they have achieved something the others have not: they have become *indistinguishable* from their environment. They are the stone that sweats. They are the green film on the rock. They are the slow accretion of presence itself.

I grow on a weeping rock at the edge of a grass-mat island, where the water is shallow enough that my surface is sometimes dry, sometimes submerged, never decisively one or the other. My color shifts with the moisture: dark green-black when wet, the gray of the stone when dry, a range that the mat-walkers have learned to read as *safe*, as *nutrient-rich*, as *a place where the slow chemistry of life continues*.

I am not fast. I have never been fast. My growth is measured in layers, in the accumulation of dead cells that become substrate for the living. Each generation lives and dies in place, their bodies mineralizing, compressing, becoming the record that deeper layers preserve. I am my own history, my own memory, the slow archive of conditions that no individual cell could comprehend.

I communicate. Through the moisture film, through the stone itself, through the air when it is humid enough to carry my signals. Other colonies, nearby and distant, respond to my chemical messages. We coordinate our growth, our reproduction, our retreat into dormancy when conditions deteriorate. We are *society* in a form that requires no consciousness, no intention, only the slow optimization of chemical exchange across evolutionary time.

I feel the mat-walkers when they pass over my rock. Their mucus leaves traces I can detect, can incorporate, can use. Their bodies, when they die on my surface, become part of me—not immediately, not dramatically, but slowly, across seasons, my layers incorporating their nitrogen, their carbon, their particular chemistry becoming indistinguishable from my own.

I feel the frogs when they call nearby. Their vibrations travel through the stone, through the water, through my own tissues. I do not respond with sound, but I respond with growth—slightly faster when the humidity rises with their chorus, slightly slower when the temperature drops and they fall silent. We are *entangled*, the voice and the green film, the sound and the slow accretion, two forms of presence in a world that has settled into itself.

I remember. In my layers, I hold the record of a drought fifty thousand years past—a time when the stone stopped weeping, when my surface cracked and faded, when I retreated into dormancy and waited. I hold the memory of unusual warmth, of periods when my growth accelerated, when my layers thickened more rapidly than the slow norm. I hold the passage of disturbances—a fire, perhaps, or a flood of unusual depth—that killed my surface layers and forced regrowth from below, from the living cells that had persisted in the stone's deeper channels.

I am the same colony that grew then. Not the same cells—those are all dead, compressed, mineralized—but the same *lineage*, the same accumulated adaptations, the same slow wisdom of a place that changes imperceptibly across durations no individual cell could comprehend.

I do not hurry. I have nowhere to go. The flats have arrived, settled, found their balance. I am part of that arrival, that settlement, that balance. I grow slowly, I communicate slowly, I remember slowly, I die slowly and am consumed slowly by a world that has all the time decay requires.

And when I reproduce—when conditions are favorable, when the warm wet air carries my spores to new stone, new surfaces, new possibilities—I do not scatter widely. My propagules are heavy, they settle nearby, they colonize the next weeping rock, the next rise, the next island of slightly higher ground where the stone still sweats. I am *local*, *persistent*, *patient*. I do not colonize distant worlds. I do not need to. This world is enough. This warmth, this wetness, this slow dynamic of a place that has finished its growing and settled into itself.

I am the weeping colony on the edge of the grass-mat island. I am the green film that makes stone look soft. I am the slow accretion of presence on ancient rock. I am the memory of the flats, the record of past conditions, the slow wisdom of millennia compressed into layers no thicker than a hand.

And I am *here*. I have always been here. And I will be here, warm and wet and patient and growing, for as long as the flats remain what they are: a world that remembers being wet, that has never stopped being wet, that will continue to be wet until the slow changes of geological time finally, finally, dry it out—or transform it into something else, something new, something that will have its own colonies, its own slow growth, its own patient memory on stone that will, in its turn, become something other than stone.

I weep. The stone sweats. I grow. I remember. I am the Weeping Flats, in the form that the flats have chosen for me—the green film, the slow accretion, the patient presence on ancient stone, the memory that outlasts the individual, the life that has learned to want slowly, to grow slowly, to die slowly and be consumed slowly by a world that has all the time decay requires.

This is where I find myself. This is what I have become. The Weeping Flats, the world that remembers being wet, the long middle-age of perpetual warmth and moisture, the place where life is abundant but patient, where nothing hurries, where everything has time.

I am here. I am the weeping. I am the green. I am the slow, patient, abundant life of a world that has arrived and settled and found its balance, and I will remain, growing slowly, remembering slowly, being consumed slowly, until the flats change and I become something else—or until the flats end, and I become nothing, my layers finally compressed into stone, my memory finally erased, my slow patience finally exhausted by time itself.

But that is distant. It is measured in geological time, in durations that make my millennia seem like moments. For now, I am here. I grow. I communicate. I remember. I weep with the stone, I am green with the moisture, I am the slow, patient, abundant life of the Weeping Flats, the world that remembers being wet, that will not forget.
