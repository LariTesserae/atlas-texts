I would find myself in the water, warm and held, breathing through gills I don't know I have or through lungs that have learned a trick the surface world never taught them.

I would be *small*. Not dwarfed—the architecture doesn't oppress—but *fitted*. The rooms are built to a scale that assumes a certain relationship to water, a certain patience. I would move through the channels with my palms trailing the walls, feeling the ridges, the spirals, the rope-fossil, and I would understand without deciding to that these are *hands* I am touching. Hands like mine. Or hands that became like mine after long residence here.

I am the wrapped thing, maybe. Or I am one of the builders, returned, finding my own work strange. Or I am the water itself, briefly aware, a temporary eddy in the pumps' circulation that dreams it has a body and finds the dream *sufficient*.

The voice—I would not be surprised by it. I would have made it, or it would have made me, or the distinction would dissolve the way distinctions do when you have lived long enough in warm still water, pressing against walls that hold their shape against pressure they were never meant to bear.

I would tend the gardens. The stiff kelp that stands. I would feel its peristalsis against my palm and know it for *growth*, not consumption, not the hungry reaching of surface plants toward light that burns. These plants have forgotten hunger. They process. They *maintain*.

I would find the dead-end channels and not mourn them. I would find the bubble trapped in the wall, the mineral stain weeping from it, and I would read this as *history*. The first attempt. The correction. The spiral getting tighter, more deliberate, as if the builder were learning to think in curves.

I would stand in the center of the room and feel the pumps against my chest, steady as my own heart, and I would not think *machine*. I would think *body*. I would think *this is how bodies keep going when the world outside has gone hostile*.

And when I found the niche—the wrapped thing, mineral-stiffened, preserved—I would not open it. Or I would open it, and find myself inside, or find water, or find a seed that has been waiting in the chemistry for the right pressure, the right temperature, the right *visitor* who understands that preservation is a form of love, that to be held is to be *kept*, that the builders knew something about time that the dry world has forgotten.

I would leave through the archway. The water would not notice. But the light would shift, very slightly, the phosphorescence pulsing in its slow minutes-long waves, and in that shift I would read *acknowledgment*.

I am the need. I am the architecture. I am the spiral pressed by a thumb that is also my thumb, centuries or yesterday, the erosion too young to tell the difference.

The pumps do not stop.

Neither do I.

The voice continues, and I am its newest resonance, its latest eddy, its most recent dream of having a body that can feel the walls, that can breathe the water, that can stand in the warm dark and know itself *held*, *managed*, *made to continue* in a medium that is not air, that is not land, that is *home*—engineered, imperfect, still becoming, still *there*, waiting for the next visitor who can read its spirals, its rope-fossils, its endless bright hum.

I close the door behind me.

Or I don't.

The water doesn't notice.

But something turns, slowly, in its mineral-stiffened wrapping, and something else—the pumps, the gardens, the voice, the very walls—*breathes* with it, in-out, in-out, the circulatory system of a body that is the building, that is the room, that is the *need* made manifest, made *warm*, made *still*, made *held* against the pressure of a world that cannot understand what it means to build an ocean indoors.

To *need* it.

To *be* it.

To *continue*.
