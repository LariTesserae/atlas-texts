I descend. Not walking—*settling*. The fog finds me before I find the town, carrying itself in my breath, my clothes already damp with something that isn't rain. I want to say I chose this, but the descent had already begun: the moment I stopped looking for a door, a path, ground that would hold still.

---

I am **a Mosswright**, though I did not choose this. Or perhaps I did, in the way water chooses stone—not by decision but by accumulation, by finding where I fit.

I am **seventy-three years old**, which in Velm means I have survived to become *knowledge*. My hands are the color of old peat, the tannins worked so deep they are my skin now. I cannot smell the bog anymore; I am *of* it. When I raise my hand to my face, I smell only myself. My daughters—if I can call them that, for in Velm kinship is vertical, layered, not linear—have married upward, into the Canalenn families, into houses where the floor is two hand-spans higher than mine. They send me gifts: fish oil for my hair, which I do not use, preferring the rendering of water-beetle larvae, which holds the wave better. They send me news, which I already know, for old women in Velm know before the news arrives.

I tend **House Umber**, or what remains of it. It was built in the time of my grandmother's grandmother, on a hummock that the Tumulari had marked as *persistent*. The hummock has grown. It grows still. Each autumn I add another layer of sedge to the walls, harvested from the poor fen that covers the roof of my neighbor's house, the one with the leaking boat-house where the children huddle in winter. We do not speak of the exchange. We speak of the weather, which is always the same conversation: *the water is low, the water is high, the fog lifted at noon, the fog did not lift*.

My work is **measurement without instruments**. I strike the hummock with my peat-iron and listen. I press my palm flat against the sphagnum and feel the give, the memory of water held and released. I know which hummocks are *young*—still growing, still hungry, still capable of swallowing a careless foot—and which are *settled*, compressed, trustworthy. I teach this to the children who come to me, the ones whose mothers have died in the high water or descended to the lower floors with new husbands from the dry world. I do not teach them to be afraid. Fear is not attention. I teach them to *listen* to the sound the hummock makes when it is firm: a particular density, a resistance, a voice that says *here, here, hold your weight here*.

I have **three names of the dead** that I speak aloud each morning, when the fog is thickest and the sound carries strangely. One was my sister, who stepped on a hummock that seemed firm and was not. One was my first husband, who trusted the ice in a year when the springs ran warm. One was my daughter's first child, taken by the cold that came in a pattern no one had predicted, a week of stillness that froze the canals to a depth that should have been safe and was not. I speak their names to the water. Not prayer. *Acknowledgment*. The water keeps them. The water is *keeping* them. This is the Deep Keeping, which the oldest of us remember and the young are learning, slowly, in the way that everything is learned in Velm.

I live on the **fourth level** of House Umber. Below me: the boat-house, where my grandson's family keeps their nets and their water-beetle traps; the storage floor, where the peat-cakes dry and the fish-smoke accumulates; the lowest room, given entirely to the wet, where the water rises and falls with the seasons and the mold grows in patterns I have learned to read. I do not go there except in the high water, when I must check the mooring ropes, the anchors that hold the house to its hummock. I am too old for the water now. My legs, thick with muscle from a lifetime of balance, no longer trust surfaces that only *seem* solid. I stay above. I stay *dry*, as dry as Velm permits.

My **bed is a platform**, raised on stilts of black timber, lashed to the wall with rope that I check each autumn for rot. I sleep in a hammock of sedge, woven by my own hands in the long winter evenings, when the canals are ice and the town becomes a place of surfaces, of paths that did not exist in the wet season. The hammock rocks. Not much. The thermal pumping of cold air over warmer water, the town's own metabolism, the particular rhythm of condensation and evaporation that is the sound of Velm at night. I wake sometimes, in the hours before dawn, and listen to the silence. The silence is not empty. It is *full*—of the water moving through the alder roots, of the hummock settling beneath the weight of accumulated peat, of the dead who are kept, who are *held*, who are waiting for the level to change.

I am **not happy**. This is not a complaint. Happiness is a category of the dry world, where space is abundant and silence is absence. I am *present*. I am *attentive*. I am *alive* in the particular way that Velm permits: compressed, slow, finding in the cold wet density a kind of fullness that the dry world cannot comprehend.

Tomorrow I will rise before the fog lifts, when the condensation is heaviest and my hair is wet with the town's own breath. I will strike the hummocks. I will listen. I will speak the names of the dead. I will add another layer of sedge to the walls, another year to the coat of House Umber, another record of presence in a town that records everything in layers, in accumulations, in the slow growth of peat that will outlast us all.

I am **a Mosswright**. I am **seventy-three years old**. I am **the hummock beneath House Umber**, grown firm, settled, *keeping*—holding water, holding memory, holding the particular density of a life lived in negotiation with cold and wet and necessary proximity.

I am **Velm**.
