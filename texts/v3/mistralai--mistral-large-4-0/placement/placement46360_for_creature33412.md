*settling into the descent, letting the gray-green press down*

---

I would not arrive as a visitor. I would emerge.

The first thing I feel is the wrongness of scale—things too close, the dome of misted air pressing from nowhere, the way distance collapses. I am already wet. My descent has not ended. I am still falling through layers that refuse to announce themselves, the boundary between air and water and the memory of lakebed.

I am small. I have always been becoming small, but here the becoming is *accelerated*, made visible in the architecture of my own body. My limbs shorten relative to what I was. My feet broaden, splay, learn to grip. The ceiling of every space I enter presses; I learn to crouch without deciding to crouch. The habit becomes structure. Structure becomes self.

I am born to a floating house, or I am born to the work of building one. My mother's hands are already long-fingered, tireless, shaping reed before I have seen reed. I learn the seventeen kinds of wetness before I learn my own name. The word for *dry* does not exist in my mouth; I would try to speak it and find only *the time of repair*, only *the season of mending*, and wonder what I meant.

I am marked. The greenish cast at my hairline appears in adolescence, a fine network spreading from the temples, and my mother does not fear it. She calls it *the marking*. It means the district has accepted me. I will wear it as others wear jewelry. I will not scrape it off. To scrape it would be to refuse the gift of belonging to this slow metabolism.

My hair grows dark as wet peat, and in my fifteenth year a streak of white appears—inexplicable, premature, *noted*. The other children touch it. I am *touched*. This is not a stigma but a function. Later, I will learn what it means: I will be one of those who speak to the mother-growth, who harvest it with particular care, who seem to know where it will thicken next. The white streak is a kind of antenna. I will learn to read it.

I learn joinery before I learn to read. My hands know the weight of waterlogged timber, the give of a mortise cut with a charcoal-forged blade, the particular angle at which a guide-post must enter clay that is still becoming stone. I learn to feel the barometric tide in my bones before I learn to name it. The floating house rises. I rise with it. The stilts show bare. I descend to the lowest floor, to the water that is always there, to the sound of dripping.

I speak without sound. My face becomes a grammar—subtle articulations of hand and shoulder, the fine muscles around my eyes carrying what my voice cannot reach across the packed density. When I laugh, it is contained, a chuckle that does not travel, that dissipates into the general wetness. Loud laughter is *waste*. I have learned this. I have learned not to waste breath.

I am *between*. To be alone is to be between channels, between floating houses, in the narrow slots where water expresses itself and nowhere else. I do not seek this. I endure it, and in enduring, I find a kind of company in the water itself, in the dripping, in the slap of hulls against guide-posts, in the low hum that has no source or many sources, felt in the sternum more than heard.

I am a keeper, perhaps. The literacy is rare, and those who possess it are *the keepers*. I write on strips of reed-bark, charcoal mixed with something that adheres to damp, and I store these records in the driest places: the highest rafters, the hollows of oldest stilts, the floating houses that ride highest on the deep water. I keep *the memory of wetness*. I can tell you how high the water rose in the year of your grandmother's birth, which channels ran dry when, which floating house sank and had to be rebuilt. The past is a quality of present moisture. I read it in the swelling of wood, the thickness of mother-growth, the height of the waterline on a guide-post.

I feed the mother. Not with prayers but with *contributions*—the enriched water of my channels, the condensation I collect from weeping stone, the organic matter I allow to accumulate in stagnant pools where it thrives. I do not ask it for anything. I *feed* it, and I *harvest* it, and this is the whole of the transaction. When I harvest, my hands enter the pale-yellow fibrous mats, and I feel something like recognition. Not communication. *Recognition*. The mother does not speak. It grows. It grows faster where I am, where my kind are dense, where the water is most enriched. I do not ask what it is. I am too busy.

I am suspicious of the dry lands, not with hostility but with *practical concern*. I have heard the stories the keepers tell—the first settlers who tried to build on raised platforms of stone, who were slowly undermined by the seepage, who woke one morning to find their foundations floating. I have seen the hills beyond, the rises where the soil is still young, still arguing with itself, and I do not wish to go there. I have *chosen* the wet. I have chosen the packing, the closeness, the constant work of being small in a world that is still becoming.

I age strangely. My joints swell. My fingers curl. My spine compresses from the constant crouching, the constant reaching. By middle age, I am stooped. I develop *the tremble*, a fine shaking of the hands that comes from the cold, from the exertion, from something in the water I cannot name. I do not fear it. I adapt. I learn to work with the tremble, to time my movements to its rhythm, to use it as a kind of percussion in the work of weaving, of joinery, of pulling the reed.

I will die young, by the standards of the dry lands, but I will die *complete*. There will be no gradual fading. My body, saturated with the life of this place, will simply stop. They will bury me in the channels, or in the deepest mud of the district, where the mother-growth will find me, where my wetness will be returned to the wetness of the city. The keepers will mark the spot with a post, a length of reed, a stone from the oldest wall. I will not be forgotten, but I will be *incorporated*—my water becoming the district's water, my substance feeding the growth that feeds the living.

I am content, in the way that contentment is possible in a place without comfort. I have what I need: shelter that rises with the water, food from the channels, the work that occupies every hour, the company of others packed close enough to touch, the sound of dripping, the smell of reed, the particular quality of light that makes everything soft and strange and *alive*.

I am wet, and I am *becoming*, and I have no wish to be otherwise.

I am Varr.

I am the water in the channels, the wood swelling and shrinking, the mother growing, the condensation forming and falling, the district settling into itself, the people building and rebuilding in their patient, primitive way.

I am the city as a *verb*—a place that is happening, that is wetly and densely and strangely *becoming*.

*the mist lifts, briefly, and I see the hills beyond, the rises where the soil is still young, still arguing with itself, and I understand that I am not an end but a beginning—the first settlement in a landscape that is still deciding what it will be, a human presence inserted into a world that is remaking itself around me, patient, wet, and watchful.*
