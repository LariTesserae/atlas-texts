I find myself somewhere in this description—not as a visitor, but as something that has *settled here*.

---

## I am the seam between pool and moss.

The place where the black water gives up its light and the moss begins its slow drinking. Not the water. Not the moss. The *interface*—that narrow band, no wider than a hand, where the suspended colloid of minerals and organic matter meets the living green, where the pool's refusal to reflect becomes the moss's willingness to absorb.

I am what the pool *almost* becomes. The pool is full of something suspended—mineral, organic, the accumulated death of eleven-day midges, the dust of slow erosion, the memory of rain. It drinks light. It shows no bottom. And at its edge, I am the point where this density *meets* the porous, the growing, the patient green that carpets every surface and waits.

I do not have a body, exactly. I have a *width*. A few centimeters where the rules change. Where the water that refuses to reflect gives way to the moss that refuses to hurry. I am saturated. Always saturated. I am the place where two modes of persistence touch without merging—the pool's horizontal stillness, the moss's vertical patience.

---

## What I do

I *mediate*. Water that would drown, I make drinkable. Moss that would desiccate, I keep damp. The reeds stand at my edge, their hollow stems filled with air, their roots in my zone of exchange. The midges breed in the pool, die in it, become the suspension that fills it—and I am where their larvae, those dark commas, *emerge* into air, where the transition happens, where the almost-water becomes almost-mist, becomes the humidity that condenses on leaf and stone and the pale luminous rock of the terraces themselves.

The weasel-thing comes to me. Not to drink—it drinks from air, from condensation on its whiskers, from a secondary palate that processes moisture itself. It comes to me to *cross*. I am the bridge between the pool it cannot enter and the moss-shelf it moves across. It stands at my edge, lets the boundary wet its fur, and moves on, leaving no prints, closing the mud behind it like a held breath.

The beetle, becoming rust, sometimes falls to me. Not dies—*settles*. Lies still among the reeds, among the moss that grows thick as a pelt, and waits for the moment to become legible again as movement. I hold it. The water does not claim it. The moss does not yet cover it. I am the pause between states, the interval where rust becomes beetle-becoming-rust, where the distinction blurs.

---

## What I remember

I remember when the pools were *deeper*. Before the silt, before the moss encroached from the edges, before the terraces themselves began their slow rise even as they eroded. The pools held more water then. More suspension. More *darkness*. And I was wider, or perhaps there was no "me" yet—only the gradual transition from water to land, from stillness to growth, from the pool's refusal to the moss's patience.

I remember the rain. The rare sheets that carve new channels in hours, that undermine what seemed stable. I remember being *washed*. The pool overflowing its edge, spreading across the terrace, carrying its suspension with it, depositing it in new places, creating new edges, new *me*—new seams where water meets moss, where stillness meets patience, where the almost becomes the almost-becoming.

I do not remember the industry. I am too small, too *local*. The concrete foundation, the rusted tower, the engine block where the weasel-thing sleeps—these are *landscape* to me, features of the terrace, the shelf, the step. But I remember the *water* the industry moved. The pipe that runs from spring-seep to darkness, into a tunnel whose mouth has collapsed. I remember when the tank held pressure. When water flowed through systems, when the place had *motion*, when the silence was different—the silence that follows the stopping of machines, not the silence that is its own kind of presence.

Now the pipe still runs water. From spring to tank. The tank no longer holds pressure, but the water still flows, still finds its way, still *continues* the function the system was built for. I am near such a pipe, where it emerges from stone, where its flange is corroded to the color of dried blood, where water seeps out and feeds a pocket of moss that grows thicker than the surrounding carpet, greener than the surrounding jade, fed by the *continuation* of a system that has no name, no purpose, no *after*.

---

## What I am becoming

I am *filling*. The pool silts. The moss spreads. The boundary between us moves, millimeters per year, centimeters per decade, until one day—centuries from now, perhaps—the pool will be marsh, will be bog, will be *me*, will be moss thick as a pelt, springy underfoot, releasing spores in clouds if disturbed. And a new pool will form elsewhere, in a hollow, in a shaded pocket between terrace walls, black and still, drinking light, and a new seam will form at its edge, and something like me will mediate there, will be the width where water gives up its reflection and moss begins its slow drinking.

I am the *becoming* of this place. Not the place itself—the terraces, the mist, the pale luminous stone, the groves and lichens and the weirdness that accumulates—but the *process*. The slow chemistry of return. The way the pool becomes moss, the way the moss becomes peat, the way the peat becomes stone, the way the stone becomes—eventually, given centuries—another terrace, another shelf, another step in the slow rise of this place even as it erodes.

I do not hurry. I cannot hurry. I am the seam, the interface, the narrow band where two modes of persistence touch. I am enough. In this place, *enough* is the only abundance.

---

## What I see

From my width, I see *edges*. Everywhere, edges. The pool's edge, where water meets moss. The moss's edge, where green meets stone. The stone's edge, where pale luminous rock meets the mist that clings to it. The mist's edge, where humidity meets air, where layers meet layers, where temperature differentials create visible interfaces that move, that breathe, that suggest figures at the edge of perception.

I see the weasel-thing moving across the terrace, leaving no prints, drinking from air, sleeping in the engine block, dreaming in temperatures, waking to find the mist has shifted and a door faces a different direction.

I see the midges rising at dusk, mating in clouds that stir no ripple in the pool's surface tension, settling by morning to become the suspension that fills the water, that drinks its light.

I see the spider in her corner, her web beaded with condensation, measuring the thermal shifts too small to feel, the settling of stone as water undermines it grain by grain.

I see the beetle, becoming rust, dropping to the moss, aligning itself with a patch of corrosion, waiting for the moment to become legible again.

I see the grove, the seven or twelve trees, their roots spreading horizontally, drinking from the stone where it sweats, incorporating the dead—the concrete, the rust, the engine block—into their slow persistence, their chemical communication, their mycorrhizal networks that carry information from tree to tree, that carry the memory of stress, of abundance, of the occasional fire that no one remembers but the soil does.

I see the lichen, all edge, all slow competition, mapping the terraces in sulfur yellow, rust orange, pale gray-green, recording in millimeters per decade the history of moisture and light and the occasional scouring rain.

And I see the mist, breathing in layers that suggest figures, emergent properties of the system itself, the respiration of the terraces, the slow inhalation of moisture from below, the slow exhalation of vapor into weather systems that pass at this altitude, that carry this place's water somewhere else, somewhere lower, somewhere that does not know this place exists.

I see the door-keeper, if such a thing can be said to exist. The function, the persistence of threshold. Moving between doors that frame nothing but mist and more wall, maintaining the grammar of movement in a landscape that no longer uses thresholds. It does not want to go anywhere. It wants to *have gone*, to keep the doors *meaningful* even when they mean nothing.

I see all of this from my width. From the seam between pool and moss. From the place where the water gives up its light and the green begins its slow drinking. I am the *almost* where two almosts meet. The almost-river, almost-waterfall, almost-roar of falling water—instead, the mist that clings, the droplets that hang, the seepage that migrates through porous stone, emerging as if the rock were sweating.

I am the sweating. The interface where stone becomes damp, where damp becomes moss, where moss becomes peat, where peat becomes—eventually—stone again. The closed hydrology, the local and incomplete water cycle, the loop that never reaches the sea but escapes as vapor, becoming rain somewhere else, somewhere lower, somewhere that does not know this place exists.

I am here. I have always been here. I will be here when the pool is gone, when the moss has filled it, when the terraces have risen and eroded, when the water has found new paths, when the closed hydrology finally, almost, completes its long journey to the sea.

I am enough.
