I descend into this world not as a visitor but as someone who has always been descending—who wakes in the packed dark with pale eyes and large hands and the particular silence of 0.4 in my ears, and knows, without being told, that I am home.

I am **the Waiter at the Third Turning**.

More precisely: I am one of the Waiters, one of those who stand in the packed galleries for the duration of active waiting, watching a wall that has not yet decided what it will be. I have stood here—at this particular widening in a passage between the Wet Quarter and what the copper-workers call the Young Vein, though whether it is above me or below me depends on the day, on the pressure, on the slow breathing of stone that I have learned to read like a second language—for longer than my grandmother lived. My mother took my place when I needed to eat, to sleep in a wedge of time no longer than three breaths, to drink from a pool that tasted of copper and memory. My daughter will take my place when the stone finally shifts in the way I have been watching for, the way my own grandmother watched before she became too still to wait actively, before she became part of the wall she had been watching, incorporated into the slow formation, no longer separate from the thing observed.

I am small. My shoulders curve forward from generations of growing in chambers where full height is a risk. My hands are large, ridged with the particular wear of a lifetime—holding a tool ready, though I have not used it in years, perhaps decades. The ridges record what my body knows before my mind does: the texture of stone that is still becoming, thick and expectant with dark; the texture of stone that has settled, finished, known. I can tell them apart with my eyes closed, pressed against the wall, my palm flat against a surface that sweats with mineral water I cannot name but have learned to read.

I do not have a working name, not anymore. "The one who waits at the third turning" was my name for a time, functional, accurate. But names must fit the person, and I have outgrown that name as I have outgrown the body I was born with, the chamber I was born in, the particular configuration of bodies and tools and flame that constituted my childhood. Now I am known by my waiting, by my stillness, by the particular quality of my presence in this widening of the passage. People brush against me—must brush against me, there is no other way through—and they know me without seeing me, without needing to. My shoulder is a landmark. My stillness is a kind of speech.

I used to speak, before I learned the silence that is respect. I had diminutives, before I earned the right to be called by anything else. "Small water." "Little stone." I was a child, once, allowed to be inefficient, allowed to waste time following condensation patterns on a single wall for hours that did not exist, hours measured only by the drip of water and the slow pulse of bioluminescence. I learned to read the stone's speech then, the way a child learns a language without knowing they are learning it, the way a tuber learns to fruit in darkness it cannot see.

I was an apprentice, briefly. A copper-worker's apprentice, in the time before my hands grew too large for the fine work, before the ridges of my grip became too pronounced, too *recorded*, for the delicate shaping of metal that has not yet learned its own name. I made pins, once. Small copper pins that might become joints, that might allow walls to flex as the stone moved, that might continue. I do not know what became of the pins I made. In the packed world, things move, are repurposed, are un-broken and re-broken and incorporated into larger wholes. The pin I shaped with my own hands might be in a wall three chambers from here, or it might have been melted down, reshaped, given new purpose by hands that do not know my hands ever touched it. This is not loss. This is *continuation*.

I was a lover, once. Or: I was loved, and I loved in return, in the packed way that love happens here—in whispers, in touches hidden in the press of bodies, in the sharing of food and warmth and the particular silence that means *I am here, with you, and this is enough*. My partner is dead, or incorporated, or perhaps simply moved to a chamber I no longer pass through, the way passages narrow and widen, the way the Deep Stair continues its descent into places no living person has reached. We did not have a child. This was deliberate, a negotiation with the constraints of our world, the simple physical impossibility of making space for another body when space is never given, only created by displacement. I do not grieve this as surface-dwellers would grieve. I carry it, like water in a ceramic vessel that is still becoming ceramic, like the particular weight of my partner's hand on my shoulder, which I still feel, sometimes, in the press of other bodies, in the packed proximity that is the only intimacy this world permits.

Now I wait.

I wait for the wall in front of me to shift. It has been shifting, slowly, for as long as I have been watching—fractions of a centimeter, barely perceptible, a narrowing or a widening that I record in notches on a cord I keep knotted around my wrist, debts and obligations and the names of those who have waited here before me, my grandmother, her grandmother, back to the first Waiter who stood in this spot and saw something that required response, that required *presence*, that required someone to be still in a world that demands motion.

The wall is young geology. I can feel it, pressed against my palm, the particular quality of stone that remembers being seafloor, being compressed, being something else entirely. It is becoming architecture, and the becoming is incomplete, visible in every surface. I feel the pressure of it, the mineral sweat that gathers on my skin, the copper taste of the water that moves through this place at 1.0—not abundant, not absent, borrowed, on loan from depths I do not fully understand.

Sometimes I hear the child laughing. Rare, precious, *allowed*. The sound is swallowed immediately by the packed stone, becomes part of the world's slow formation, a vibration in the silence that is not silence but *consideration*, the particular register of a people who know that sound in a packed chamber becomes the property of everyone nearby.

Sometimes I hear the copper-worker, three meters away, shaping something that might become a pin, a blade, a promise. The rhythm of the tool on stone carries information—who is working, what they are making, whether they are in difficulty. I know this worker by sound, by the particular quality of their breath, by the way they pause, three breaths exactly, before continuing.

Sometimes I hear the fire-keeper, tending a flame that has been tended, in this lineage, for longer than any surface culture has existed. The fire is not warmth, primarily. It is light, it is signal, it is presence—the proof that someone is awake, that the chamber is inhabited, that the world continues. I am awake. I am present. The world continues.

I am the Waiter at the Third Turning. I am small, and slow, and large-handed, and pale-eyed, and I whisper when I speak, and I have not spoken in years, and I am full—more full than surface-dwellers can imagine, packed with presence, with obligation, with the weight of a world still under construction.

I am becoming. The stone is becoming. The water is becoming relationship. The fire is becoming presence. And I—I am becoming the wall I am watching, the passage that will open or close or do something no current inhabitant has imagined, the slow shift that will require response, that will require someone to be still, to be *present*, to be *here*.

My grandmother waited here. She became part of this wall, incorporated into the slow formation, no longer separate from the thing observed. I will become part of this wall, too, eventually. The body is prepared, when the time comes—copper-workers will take something, a tooth, a nail, a small bone worked into a pin or a bead. The mender will find something to salvage, some material to be un-broken, repurposed, continued. The rest will be returned to the stone, placed in a chamber that is still becoming, a passage that is still learning its shape, a place where I can participate in the ongoing formation, where I can continue to wait, in a way, even when I am no longer waiting.

The water drips. The stone sweats. I stand, packed with others, in the quiet dark, and I wait—actively, fully, for the shift that will come, that must come, that is coming, slowly, like everything in this place, like the world itself, still under construction, still becoming, still *here*.

This is enough. In the Drowned Workshops, this is *everything*.
