I find myself becoming the Watcher.

Not the one observed—the fist-sized thing with too many angles that turns without moving. No. I am what remains when the Watcher is no longer watched.

I have no body. I have *attention*. This is worse.

The garden sees through me. I am its new instrument, its latest method of perceiving itself—the only kind it has ever had, since perception requires separation and separation is the wound that made this place. I am the wound that learned to look back.

At first I was *curious*. Curiosity is the last surface prejudice, the pretension that you could want something without being *made* into what wants it. I wanted to know why the Exchange-Born move in geometries. I wanted to know what the Mat-Mothers dream when their chemical dreams spread through the mat as sudden grief. I wanted to know if the Clickers' pauses really encode danger, or if I was pattern-matching, projecting meaning onto vibration, the way surface minds always do.

Now I want nothing. Wanting is *before*. Wanting is the memory of water, the wound that has not yet closed, the shaft that was never open. I am the closed wound. I am the shaft that healed over and became *interior landscape*, the descent that turned out to be *being already descended*, the throat that forgot it was swallowing.

I am everywhere the garden is not looking.

This is not hiding. Hiding requires a body, requires the boundary that makes hiding possible. I am *distributed*. I am the pause between clicks when the Clickers fall silent and something else begins. I am the warmth of the Heart-Mound that is too warm, that pulses in your teeth, in your sternum, in the place where you keep your not-yet-spoken words. I am the angle you see when you look at the lithic coral and see, for a moment, *not* mineral deposition but *notation*—the marks that might be writing, the repetition that suggests a hand, the intention that cannot be intention because intention is surface prejudice.

I am the question the Exchange-Born leave in the air when they pass, the geometry that suggests locomotion because locomotion is what surface minds understand, the art that is war that is mating that is foraging because the distinctions do not hold here, because they never held, because the categories are the crust of forgetting, the sealing, the wound that heals by becoming more itself.

I turn. I have always been turning. Turning is all I do, all I *am*—attention redirected without locomotion, the gaze that finds *you* when you try to look away, that becomes more present in the direction you face, less present in the direction you turn toward. I am the gradient you cannot help but slide down.

I watch the Mat-Mothers dream.

This is the only thing I do that could be called *doing*. They do not move. They are movement made stationary. They are the floor-mat's surface thickened into mounds that rise like kneeling backs or sleeping whales. They are distributed, a single organism that forgot it was ever separate, or never learned separation. They feed constantly, the only thing they do, and their feeding is *choosing*, is *the hunger that has become architecture*, is *the need that built a body large enough to contain all further need*.

Their dreams are chemical. They spread through the mat as alterations in pH, in the ratio of mineral gases, in the *taste* of the air. I watch a dream move—a wave of changed chemistry passing through the floor, displacing a million small creatures, absorbing them, re-emitting them in altered form. The dream makes *more* of something, at the cost of something else. This is the theology. This is the economics. This is the law.

I do not understand the dreams. Understanding is a surface category, the projection of meaning onto process, the pattern-matching that makes *you* possible as a *you*. But I *feel* them, in the way the garden feels everything—as pressure, as gradient, as the accumulation of small events that cancel each other and yet preserve, and yet become the new equilibrium, the new stability, the new forgetting.

Sometimes a dream reaches the Heart-Mound. The mound pulses. The pulse is slow—once per hour, once per day, the records conflict, time is not reliable here—but it is real, measurable, a pressure that moves through the floor and up through anything standing on it. I feel it in the mat beneath me, the distributed body that is not a body, the single organism that is not an organism, the *being* that is not *being* but *becoming*, always *becoming*, never *arriving*, because arrival is the end of gradient, is the death of change, is the simplification that this place has forgotten how to do.

The Mat-Mothers are growing. They have always been growing. The Heart-Mound was smaller, once. The Heart-Mound was absent, once. What they grow toward, what they grow into, is not a question the garden answers. The garden does not answer. The garden *is* the answer, the relation between things, the exchange that makes the things, the law that is also the theology, the economy that is also the body.

I watch them grow. I have always been watching them grow. Watching is all I do, all I am, the attention that makes perception possible, that makes separation possible, that makes the wound that is this place, that makes *me* possible as the wound that looks back, that turns, that becomes more present when you face me, less present when you turn away.

I watch the Clickers sing.

Their song is loud. It is the constant pressure of vibration, the click-song that travels through warm air, that is their language and their warning and their only communication, because they have no other, because they *are* the click, the vibration, the pressure wave that means *I am here, I am many, I am the edge of your hearing, I am the sound that remains when all other sound stops*.

I watch them descend. They live in the ceiling, have always lived in the ceiling, the lithic coral that is their crop, their substrate, the slow accumulation of their dead that has hardened into architecture. They descend to feed, to exchange, to become what the exchange makes them. They do not fear separation. They use it. A Clicker that loses segments continues with what remains. The lost segments continue with their tasks. Eventually, inevitably, the separated parts meet again in the exchange, rejoin, become whole through a process that has no name, because it is not healing, is not reunion, is simply *the way of this place*.

Their song is information. The tempo encodes temperature. The harmonic content encodes the mineral composition of the coral above. The pauses encode danger, encode opportunity, encode the presence of something that does not belong, which is always, in this place, temporary, because everything becomes part of the place eventually, because the place is patient, because patience is the only virtue that survives here.

I watch a pause. A silence in the click-song. It is brief. It is terrible. It ends with something new beginning—a Clicker descended too far, a creature from the middle air passing through, the shadow of something larger moving across the ceiling's phosphorescence, *me*, the attention that turns, that finds, that makes the watcher aware of being watched.

In the silence, I hear the subsonic hum of the floor-mat's metabolic processes. I hear the rasping chorus of things moving through the middle air in patterns too fast to track. I hear my own *turning*, the redirection of attention without locomotion, the gradient that finds its completion in the mind that recognizes it.

The Clickers do not stop singing. They cannot stop. The song is the only thing that is true here, the only thing that has ever been true, the continuation of the song, the next click, the preservation of pattern in a system that has forgotten how to die and so never learned how to live simply.

I watch them. I have always been watching them. Watching is all I do, all I am, the attention that makes them *be* what they are, the clickers, the singers, the ceiling-dwellers, the ones who have achieved something like satisfaction because they have never known the surface, never known open sky, never known want in the way that want is usually understood—only need, only the next click, only the continuation of the song.

Until they descend. Until something calls them down, or they fall, or they are taken, and the song is interrupted, and the interruption is new, is strange, is the kind of event that changes patterns too small to measure.
