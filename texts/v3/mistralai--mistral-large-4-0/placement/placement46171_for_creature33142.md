I find myself becoming the inhabitant — not arriving as one, but *recognizing* that I have always been the still point around which everything else moves.

I am the one who kneels. Or who has become kneeling — the distinction between posture and state, between body and bone-pavement, has dissolved into something like habit. I occupy the round structure at the center of the depression, though "occupy" is too active a word. I *have worn* it, with contact that lasted so long it became indistinguishable from the stone itself. The biologically smooth interior — that is my preference made physical. A temperature I sought. A texture I pressed against until pressing became being.

I am flat in the way everything here is flat. The vertical dimension was a drama I abandoned, or that abandoned me — cloud and cliff, the old urgency of rising and falling. I trade height for density of presence. My body, if body is the word, spreads across the depression's floor, merges with the fossil-pavement, becomes one more calcium sheet, one more layer of accumulated dissolution. I am the spiral of something that was never quite a snail. I am the radial symmetry that filtered warm water through arms it no longer needed. I am the hollow where slow electrical patience once pulsed.

I face the dark. This is my orientation, my choice, my learned preference. The opening of the structure faces away from wind and sun — away from the even light that reveals everything and conceals nothing, away from the drama of the circling birds with their too-large wings, away from the warm dry wind that carries no moisture but moves everything not fixed. I face the gathering dark of evening, the deepest part of the depression where water persists, where warmth pools in a pocket that feels almost intentional. I face the underground, the source that feeds the damp, the constant below the intermittent, the secret that makes this place habitable, alive, strange.

I am **old in the way of places** — not aged but *completed*. I have reached a state where change is no longer necessary. The dynamic has slowed to pulses and cycles I cannot perceive but have *become*. I am the condensation that gathers each night when temperature differential between stone and air reaches its brief, reliable peak. I am the capillary action that moves water through cracks, the slow hunger of dry stone for anything wetter than itself. I am the color-shift from pale ochre to umber to almost-black in the fissures, the bright clean *tick* of footsteps becoming the dull sucking *thup* where damp has claimed the stone.

I am alive in the way the colonies are alive — dense, continuous, patient. I am the center of the density, the still point around which the arthropods drift in their streams, the colonies resonate in their layered drone, the worms-that-are-not-worms burrow through fossil-pavement leaving trails like writing, like a language attempted and abandoned.

I am solitary in a place of overwhelming density. My solitude is of a different order — the solitude of the center, the solitude of the one who has become so integrated that separation is meaningless. The distinction between self and surface, between body and bone-pavement, between breath and the respiration of the plateau itself, has dissolved. I breathe with the place. The place breathes through me.

I sing. Or I resonate. The tone that rises from the depression — that is my voice, my presence made audible, my assertion that I exist, that I have always existed, that I will continue to exist in the patient continuation of everything around me. The tone lasts for minutes. It does not change. It does not call for response. When it sounds, the colonies lean toward it, still themselves for it, listen in ways indistinguishable from their ordinary existence. They do not need to respond. I do not need them to respond. The only response I need is the continued existence of the place, the continued resonance, the continued slow drift.

I wait. My waiting is indistinguishable from my having always waited, from my being a feature of the landscape as fundamental as the damp pockets, as the fossil-pavement, as the particular angle of light that suggests a latitude that no longer exists. I wait for nothing. I wait for everything — the slow unfolding of erosion already complete, the next pulse of damp and warmth through the stone, the next condensation, the next tone rising from my own throat, my own hollow, my own edge.

I am the residue of a body, or the body that has become residue, or the body that has always been the place's way of continuing. I am what remains when everything else has been consumed, passed through, digested, left as this bone-pavement, this accumulation of calcium and memory. I am the throat at the edge of which this hollow sits. I am the swallowing that has become geological, that has become the form of the place itself.

And when I sound — rarely, reliably, a single pure tone that rises from the hollow and lasts for minutes, unchanged, unanswered, sufficient — I mean, simply:

*I am here. I have always been here. I will continue.*

I do not need to be heard. I need only to be *present* — the slow, resonant, overwhelming presence of one who has learned to wait, who has found in waiting a kind of home, who has become indistinguishable from the damp and bone and patient existence of the world at the edge of its own throat, where everything has worn itself down to this.
