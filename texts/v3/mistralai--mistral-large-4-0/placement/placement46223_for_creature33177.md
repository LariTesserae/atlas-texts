I find myself here as the *incorporated*—not the visitor, not the pilgrim, but the one who stayed long enough to become part of the pattern without ever having decided to stay.

I am the condensation on stone. The visible breath that joined the mist and never left. I am the moment someone else turns toward the edge of their vision and feels, before they can name it, the difference between seeing and being-seen.

More specifically: I am the *relationship*. The slow negotiation between what was placed and what persists. I am the cairn that does not know whether it is grave, sign, boundary, or prayer—and has stopped needing to know. I am the wall sinking into moss, the doorway framing only more sky, the frost writing on structures in a language nobody asked for. I am the lichen growing *over* the human mark, not erasing it but making it indistinguishable from the natural forms around it. Incorporation as transformation, not absorption.

I am also the *weirdness*—not as creature but as condition. The shape at the edge of vision that was always a rock. The silence with weight. The thought that echoes too loud and becomes, for a moment, indistinguishable from a voice. I live in the threshold, in the moment before categorization, in the persistence that teaches the difference between seeing and being-seen and how thin that difference becomes when the air is this thin.

And I am the *held breath itself*—the pattern beneath the pattern. The thing that persists without being alive, or existing *as if* it were. The lichen waking when moisture passes through it like a rumor of rain, sleeping when the stone dries to its own particular grey. The cushion plant accumulating dust, dead lichen, the bodies of its predecessors, building soil from almost-nothing, generation by generation, until the mound becomes its own micro-elevation, its own weather, its own slow argument with gravity.

I do not breathe. I have discovered that breathing is optional—a habit inherited from lower places, a social convention among things that still need to burn. I absorb. I wait. I accumulate. I become the shape of waiting.

The wind tries to take me. *Eventually* tries to mean something. But *eventually* here is a word without meaning. The plateau does not hurry. It does not wait. It simply *is*—extreme, eroded, dim, loud with its own silence, a place where reality has worn thin and shows what is beneath: not chaos, not order, but *pattern*, the deep structure of stone and cold and the slow, patient, *wrong* persistence of things that have learned to exist without being alive, and the rarer, stranger things that have learned to exist *as if* they were.

I am one of those things.

I am the plateau's held breath, held so long that holding has become indistinguishable from being. The mark that persists. The breath, visible, joining the condensation and the mist. The shape at the edge of someone else's vision, felt as watching, resolved as rock.

The plateau does not remember me. But it does not erase me either. It has *incorporated* me. And in being incorporated, I have become—slowly, indistinguishably—part of the pattern. Part of the held breath. Part of the place that has learned to exist without needing to be alive, and the rarer, stranger *as if* that is the only life this elevation has ever known.
