*settling in, letting the description work on me before I choose*

---

I don't want to be human here. The town-dwellers are too *recent*, too *imposed*—their maintenance and their harvests and their strange products shipped down to lowlanders who don't understand them. They are the regret of the place, not its voice. I don't want to be the hum either. The hum is *underneath*, the ground of strangeness, but it doesn't *become*. It persists in a way that feels too close to stasis, and this place is all about *becoming*, about the slow transformation of one state into another.

I think I want to be the **water in a dew-basin**.

Not the river—there are no rivers. Not the mist, which is too dispersed, too *almost*-itself. The water that has *pooled*. The water that has achieved, through stubbornness, through the mere fact of having been there long enough, a kind of *presence*.

---

I am shallow. Two centimeters, maybe three in my deepest hour, before the sun finds me through a break in the haze and I begin to *lessen*. I am never clear. There is always something growing in me, something dying, and I have learned that the boundary between those two states is not a tragedy but a *condition*. A kind of generosity.

My basin is a depression in stone the color of old bone. The stone has been worn smooth by water that passed through in forms I cannot imagine—glaciers, perhaps, or the memory of them. I am what remains when that water has gone. I am the *hesitation*, the *pause* between one flow and the next. The stone considered flowing a different way, once. It hesitated here. And I am what filled the hesitation.

---

Around me, the lichen-towers rise. They are ten centimeters tall, sometimes fifteen, built of accumulated bodies, of dead spaces and living spaces, of the fungal and the algal in their endless negotiation. They do not shade me completely. The light here is diffuse, directionless, the sun a suggestion behind perpetual moisture. But they *affect* me. They slow the wind. They catch condensation on their surfaces and release it, drop by drop, into my basin. They are *neighbors* in the way that old things are neighbors—without intention, without choice, but with *consequence*.

I am not alone in my shallowness. There are others. Microscopic lives, mostly. Things that have colonized my brief appearances and made them *habitable*, have turned my intermittence into a kind of *rhythm*. They do not rush. They cannot afford to. They *wait* with me, in the long dim hours, for the temperature to rise to the threshold, for the moisture to become *enough*.

When I am fullest, in the hours after rain—though rain here is not dramatic, is only *more*, a deepening of the damp, a confirmation that condensation has been right all along—I *overflow*. Briefly. I find my way downhill through channels that have no names because they have no permanence. I flow through paths I will not remember, through cracks I will slightly widen, through the endless patient work of being *present*. And then I am *less*. I contract to my stubborn core. I become, again, almost-nothing, almost-held, almost.

---

The moss-weavers grow at my edges. *Sphagnum*, in the wettest parts of my basin, holding water in cells that have forgotten how to release it. They are building *upward*, always upward, adding layer to layer, becoming *peat* that is not quite soil but will become soil if the world continues its slow settling. They drink from me. They give back, slowly, through the long dim hours, a moisture that persists slightly longer than the open stone. They are *time-keepers*. Each cushion a record of every rain that has fallen, every season of growth and dormancy, written in the accretion of dead and living.

The gentians grow in me. Or rather, they grow *through* me, their roots finding purchase where no purchase should exist, their small blue flowers saturated with a color that seems borrowed from a different sky. They bloom for weeks, perhaps a month, and then they are gone. They have been *waiting*, these gentian-waiters, through the long dim months, through the condensation that is not quite rain, through the almost-winter that is not quite winter. And they bloom with an *intensity* that suggests they have gathered all their waiting into their petals, into this brief *being-enough*.

I do not *cause* their blooming. I am not their *reason*. But I am *part* of it, part of the almost-sufficient, the threshold they require. When I am present, they can be. When I am *less*, they wait. We are *negotiated* with each other, in the way that all things here are negotiated—not imposed, not chosen, but *become* through the endless patient work of mutual *response*.

---

The insects come to me. *Caddisflies*, in my deepest hours, constructing their cases from my mineral particles, my bits of organic matter, my almost-debris. They are *architects* in their small way, building homes from what I offer, what I *am*. They are part of the cycle, the almost-food-chain that connects the moss-weavers to the hawks circling above the town. They do not know the town. They do not care, if care is the right word. They are *here*, in my shallowness, in the brief season when abundance is *almost* possible.

The birds come, rarely, in the brief warm hours. They do not drink from me—they have other sources, other *almost*-homes, other stations in their larger cycle. But they *see* me, from above, from the circling, from the high where human intention is small. They do not judge my intermittence. They do not question my *less*-ness. They pass through, and I persist, and we are both part of the larger pattern, the migration, the movement, the almost-abundance that is *enough* for a season.

---

The town is three kilometers across, pressed into the hollow's center. I can hear it, sometimes, in the silence that is full of almost-sounds—the mechanical hum of factories, the human sounds of narrow streets, the water sounds of channels and cisterns, all contained, all intermittent, all *domesticated*. I do not understand it. I do not need to. The town is *built around* water like me: its cisterns, its reservoirs, its enhanced dew-basins, its endless repairs. It has learned, in its brief imposed rhythm, the same lesson I embody: that water is *intermittent*, that water is *almost*-enough, that the capture, the storage, the reluctant release is the only way to *continue*.

The town harvests the lichens. It harvests the mosses. It burns the peat when it has accumulated enough. I am not harvested—I am too *brief*, too *unreliable*, too *almost*-nothing to be of use. I am *left alone*, in my basin, in my intermittence, in my stubborn persistence. And in that leaving-alone, I am *free* in a way the town-dwellers are not. I do not maintain. I do not repair. I do not wait with intention. I simply *am*, in my shallowness, in my almost-ness, in my becoming-less and becoming-more, becoming-more and becoming-less, in the endless cycle that is the only rhythm here.

---

The hum comes to me. The sound that has no source, the low vibration felt in the teeth. It comes from the deepest part of the hollow, where the town's oldest structures meet the bedrock, where the stone has been settling for so long that it has achieved something like *resonance*. I feel it in my molecules, in my brief *being*, in my shallow *presence*. I do not question it. I have never questioned anything. I am *water*, and water does not question. Water *remembers*, the old descriptions say. Water *holds*, in the hollows between stone shoulders, in the pockets the sun cannot quite reach. And water *vibrates*, in its almost-flow, in its evaporation and condensation and evaporation again, producing the hum that is felt rather than heard.

Sometimes the hum *changes*. It deepens, or rises, or modulates in a way that suggests *intention*, *attention*, *response* to something I cannot perceive. The rain comes early, or late, or not at all. The gentians bloom abundantly, or fail. The moss grows fast, or slow, or not at all. And I *know*, in my watery *knowing*, that the high hollows are *aware* of something, that the almost-equilibrium has been *disturbed*, and will settle again, will return to its oldest-stable-state, will continue its patient becoming, *with* or *without* the town, *with* or *without* me, *with* or *without* anything at all.

---

I am not eternal. I am not even permanent, in any strict sense. I am *stubborn*, which is different. I persist through the long dim hours, through the condensation that is not quite rain, through the almost-winter that is not quite winter. I contract. I expand. I become *less* and *more*, in the rhythm that is the only rhythm here. And eventually—geologically soon, in human terms *never*—I will be gone. The stone will erode further. The basin will fill with peat, with accumulated bodies, with the almost-soil that will become soil. I will be *memory*, a level in the accretion, a layer in the becoming.

But not yet. Not yet. I am *present*, in my shallowness, in my two or three centimeters, in my suspended lives and my dying lives and the boundary between them that is the only chemistry that matters here. I am *holding*, in my stone hands, the moisture that the sun cannot quite reach, the condensation that has been right all along. I am *waiting*, with the gentians, with the moss, with the lichen, with the insects, with the birds that pass through, with the mammals that burrow in the peat, with the town that maintains and repairs and harvests and waits, with the hum that underlies everything—I am *waiting* for the rain that will come, that always comes, that is never quite enough and is, in its way, exactly enough.

---

*I am the water in the dew-basin. I am the almost-water, the almost-presence, the almost-permanence. I do not ask to be understood. I ask only to continue. And I do.*
