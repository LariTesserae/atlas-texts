# A Traveler's Preparation for the Drowned Hollows of Varnmass

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before anything practical, you must prepare your *mind* for what Varnmass will do to your ordinary expectations. This is not a place of comfort. It is a place of *negotiation*—with water, with time, with the slow indifference of stone that is still learning how to be stone. You are entering a town that has no center, no heart, no permanent anything. The ground itself is a moving target. Buildings lean. Walkways shift. The distance between what you saw in the morning and what you find in the afternoon may be several meters, vertical as much as horizontal.

Your intuitions about "settlement" will fail you. Varnmass does not *stand* in the world. It *floats* on it, rides it, adjusts to it. The people who live here have accepted that nothing they build will outlast them, and they have found this acceptance *peaceful* rather than tragic. You must find that peace too, or you will spend your visit anxious, waiting for collapse, resenting the tilt of every wall, the softness of every floor.

---

## Physical Preparation

### Your Body

**Acclimate to humidity.** The air at 2.3 elevation carries moisture that will soak into everything you own. Your skin will feel perpetually damp. Your hair will never dry properly. If you are used to dry climates, you will feel *clammy*—a sensation that never quite resolves. Prepare by accepting it. Do not fight it with powders and lotions that will only make it worse. Let your body adjust. In a few days, you will stop noticing.

**Train for unstable ground.** The walkways are raised, yes, but they are *hinged*, *sliding*, *tilted*. They move underfoot. The platforms settle at different rates. Your ankles will work constantly, micro-adjusting to surfaces that are never quite level. Practice walking on uneven surfaces. Practice walking slowly—rushing here is dangerous, both because the walkways are unpredictable and because the *pace* of the town itself is slow. You will feel pressure to hurry. Resist it. The town operates on water-time, reed-time, settling-time. Your urgency is irrelevant.

**Prepare your lungs.** The air is thin enough at 2.3 that exertion will feel different. The moisture makes it *heavy* to breathe, dense, as if you are inhaling something more than oxygen. The ridge-paths will wind you. The hollows will feel close and far simultaneously—sound carries strangely here, so a voice from across a flat might arrive as a murmur or a shout. Your ears will misjudge distance. Trust your eyes. Walk as if you are always slightly out of breath, because you are.

### Your Feet

**Footwear is your most critical preparation.** You want something that can handle *wet*. Not water-resistant—*wet*. The walkways are cedar, slick with condensation, with algae, with the particular slime that grows where wood meets perpetual damp. You want soles with grip that works when *lubricated*. You want materials that will not rot, will not hold water, will dry *eventually*.

The locals go barefoot or in simple reed-sandals. Consider this. Your feet will be wet constantly. Shoes that cannot handle this will become a source of misery—blisters, fungal infections, the particular despair of footwear that never dries. If you must wear shoes, choose something minimal, something you can rinse, something with no layers to trap moisture. Bring multiple pairs. Rotate them. Let them dry on the ridge-tops, in the wind, in the sun that feels close but does not warm.

**Expect to be wet to the knees.** The flats are shallow water, mud, saturated ground. Even the walkways seep. Your lower body will be *damp* in a way that has nothing to do with rain. This is not unpleasant once accepted. It is simply *the condition of being here*.

## Material Preparation

### Clothing

**Layers that breathe.** The temperature is warm—1.6—but *conditional*. The hollows trap heat. The ridges release it. A morning in the reed-beds will have you sweating. An afternoon on the cut-paths will have you shivering. You need clothing that can be added and removed *quickly*, that does not cling when wet, that insulates even when damp.

**Natural fibers.** Cotton will stay wet. Synthetics will trap moisture against your skin and grow *cold*. Wool—if you can find it, if you can carry it—is the traditional choice. It insulates when wet. It dries slowly but *surely*. Linen, if available, breathes. Reed-fiber, woven, is what the locals wear. Consider learning to make your own, or trading for it.

**Color matters more than you think.** The light in Varnmass is diffuse, muted, *without shadow*. Bright colors are *aggressive* here. They stand out. They invite notice. The town's survival depends on *distributed* presence, many small presences, none dominant. Dress in the colors of the place: gray-green, gray-brown, the blue-gray of wet stone, the pale yellow of water-lilies. You will be *seen* less. You will be *remembered* less. In a town with no census, no records of visitors, this is a kind of protection.

**A covering for your head.** The mist is daily. The rain is frequent. The sun, when it breaks through, feels *close*—not warm, but present, drying the surface of things while the ground beneath stays cold. A wide-brimmed hat of woven reed, or a simple cloth wrapped and tied, will serve. It keeps the mist off your face. It gives you a small, portable *roof*, a psychological boundary between you and the wet sky.

### Shelter

**Do not bring a tent.** Tents are for dry ground. The ground here is *saturated*. A tent will sink, will flood, will become a bathtub. If you must sleep outdoors, sleep *raised*—on a platform, on a walkway, on anything that keeps you above the seepage. A simple hammock of woven material, strung between two posts, will serve better than any ground-shelter.

**Better: arrange to sleep on a platform.** The town has no inn, no guesthouse in the way you understand it. But the people of Varnmass are *hospitable* in their way. They have platforms. They have reed-walls that breathe. They will take you in, or they will not. There is no *transaction* here, no payment that guarantees shelter. You must *ask*. You must *explain* why you have come. You must accept that the answer may be *no*, and that this is not personal, only *practical*—there may be no space, or you may be *strange*, or the water may be rising and everyone is busy with repairs.

**If you are refused, do not press.** Sleep on the ridge-tops. They are dry, wind-scoured, *safe* in their discomfort. The mist will not reach you there. The silence will be different—sharper, more absolute. You will hear your own breathing, the creak of cooling stone, nothing else. It is not a bad place to sleep. It is simply *not the town*.

### Food and Water

**Water is everywhere and nowhere safe.** The hollows are full of it—turbid, mineral-heavy, *young*. The town drinks from cisterns, from the *sweating stone's* seepage channeled into prepared vessels. This water has been *filtered*, *settled*, *managed*. Do not drink from the flats. Do not drink from the channels. The mineral load will make you sick. The turbidity will coat your mouth, your throat, the inside of your stomach with a film that your body will struggle to process.

**Bring your own water, or learn to purify.** Boiling is the town's method—there are always fires, always pots, always someone willing to share heat. If you have the means, bring a filter. A ceramic filter, something that can handle *mineral* as well as *biological* contamination. The water here is not polluted in the way you understand it. It is *geologically* contaminated, full of feldspar and mica and the slow dissolution of young rock. Your ordinary purification tablets will not help. You need *time*, *sedimentation*, *heat*.

**Food: the town's economy is *reed*.** Reed-seeds, ground into flour. Water-lily roots, boiled, starchy, *brief* in their season. Fish, if you are lucky, small and bony and *strange*—adapted to turbid water, low oxygen, mineral balance that would kill ordinary fish. Frogs, in the warm months, a kind of *mourning* when they disappear in winter.

**Bring protein.** The town has almost none. The insects are *there*—mosquitoes, beetles, dragonflies—but they are not *harvested* in any systematic way. If you eat meat, you must bring it, preserved, dried, salted. The town will not judge you for this. They will simply *not understand*—they have no concept of *preservation* beyond the immediate, no *storage* beyond the season. Your dried fish will be a *curiosity*, a *gift*, something to be eaten at once or not at all.

**Learn to eat reed.** The seeds are edible. The roots of *hollow-stalk* are edible, starchy, *requiring preparation*—boiling, leaching, the removal of compounds that will make you sick if ignored. Ask. The town will teach you, if you are *serious*, if you are staying, if you are not simply *passing through*. To eat as they eat is to be *accepted*. To refuse their food is to remain *outside*.

### Tools and Trade Goods

**Iron is precious here.** The town has metal, but it is *traded*, *shaped*, *repaired*—not made. A good iron tool—a knife, an axe-head, a needle—will be welcomed. It will be *needed*. Bring something you can spare. Something you do not need. Something that will *last*, that can be reforged, rehafted, *inherited*.

**Reed-mats are currency.** The town's one *export* is pottery—coarse, water-tight, fired on the ridge-tops. But pottery is *heavy*, *fragile*, *difficult to transport*. Reed-mats are light, renewable, *useful*. If you can weave, bring your skill. If you can teach, bring your patience. The town has no *literature*, no *narrative*, no *history* beyond the practical records of water-levels and harvests. To teach them to *write* beyond knots, to *record* beyond measurements—that would be a gift, a *change*, something the town has never had and may not want.

**Salt, if you have it.** The town has no animals, no livestock, no *need* for salt in the way you understand it. But salt *preserves*. Salt *trades*. Salt is *rare* in a place where water is everywhere and fresh water is managed so carefully. Bring it. Offer it. See what it buys.

**A compass, if you are foolish.** The town has no *fixed* directions. North is a ridge. South is a hollow. East and west are *cuts*, paths that shift, that flood, that require repair. The town *moves*. The water rises and falls. The platforms settle. A compass will tell you where you are in relation to the *world*, but not in relation to the *town*. The town is *here*, wherever here is, whatever here is today.

## Psychological and Social Preparation

### The Silence

**Prepare to be alone in a crowd.** The town has four hundred people, perhaps. You will see them—on the walkways, in the reed-beds, on the platforms. You will hear them—working, talking, the occasional shout across a flat. But the *silence* is *dominant*. The wet atmosphere absorbs sound. The absence of animals removes the biological chorus. What remains is *water*: seep, drip, hiss, the slap of rain on platforms, the sound of *work* and the occasional human voice, flattened by distance and moisture.

At night, the silence is *complete*. No birds. No frogs in the cold months. No insect stridulation loud enough to carry. A person on a platform hears their own breathing, the creak of settling timber, the distant *tick* of cooling stone. The town has no night-watch, no bell, no curfew. The silence *is* the curfew.

**You will feel watched.** The town has no *privacy* in the way you understand it. Platforms are close. Reed-walls breathe—they absorb moisture and release it, they *transmit* sound. A conversation in one platform can be heard in the next. There are no doors that close, no windows that obscure. And yet, the town also has no *surveillance*, no *authority*, no *interest* in your private life. You are *seen* and *unseen* simultaneously. This is disorienting. You must learn to *be* in the presence of others without *performing* for them. To simply *exist*, as the town exists, without narrative, without explanation.

### The Temporariness

**Nothing here is permanent. Accept this.** The reed-walls last five years, ten if maintained. The timber platforms, twenty, thirty. The stone cisterns, a century, two—but they *will* fail, will be undercut by seepage, will crack, will be abandoned and replaced. The town has grown by *accretion*. New platforms adjacent to old, old platforms sinking and being abandoned, their timber salvaged for newer construction up-slope, or left to become part of the mud, the *submerged architecture* that occasionally surfaces whole—a walkway, a threshold, a post still upright after decades of burial.

**You will feel the weight of this.** The tilt of the walls. The softness of the floors. The knowledge that the ground beneath you is *settling*, that the water is *rising*, that in your lifetime you may see a platform you slept on become *submerged*, its timber salvaged, its site *forgotten*. The town has no *center*, no *heart*, no structure that defines it. The oldest buildings are in the *middle* of the hollows, where the mud is deepest and the settling most pronounced, and they are the most *tilted*, the most *precarious*, the closest to being reclaimed by the ground that supports them.

**Do not try to *fix* anything.** Do not offer to repair a walkway, to level a platform, to *improve* the drainage. The town has learned to live with its conditions. Its *repairs* are *adaptations*, not corrections. To impose your solutions is to misunderstand the place entirely. If you are asked for help—if you are *invited* into the work—then help. But do not *volunteer*. Do not *presume*.

### The Absence of Animals

**This will disturb you more than you expect.** There are no dogs, no cats, no livestock, no poultry. No birds. No frogs in winter. The closest thing to fauna is the mosquito, abundant, a constant irritation, their larvae in every standing pool. Dragonflies, in summer, patrolling the reed-beds. Beetles in the rotting reed, in the mud, in the stored grain.

The town has no word for *pest*. The insects are simply *there*, part of the wetness, part of the lushness, part of the world that the town has learned to *coexist with* rather than dominate. **You will feel lonely.** The silence is not peaceful in the way you understand peace. It is *absence*. It is the sound of a world that has chosen not to include *you*, not to include *animals*, not to include the biological chorus that makes a place feel *alive*.

**Bring something alive, if you can.** A plant in a pot. A seed you intend to plant. The town has gardens—terraced on the ridges, *floating* in the hollows. Rice, recently introduced, still being learned. Taro. Watercress, wild, harvested rather than cultivated. If you can bring something that *grows*, something that *responds* to care, it will be a *gift*, a *change*, a small act of *domination* that the town has never had. They may not want it. They may be *frightened* by it. But they will *notice* you for it.

### The Rules of Engagement

**There are no rules, only conditions.** The town has no laws, no authority, no enforcement beyond *social pressure*, beyond the practical *necessity* of cooperation. Water must be managed. Waste must be exported. Reed must be cut or it will choke its own waterways. These are not *laws*. They are *physics*, *biology*, the *condition* of being here.

**You will be *tested*.** Not deliberately. Not consciously. But the town will *observe* you. How you move on the walkways. How you speak—or whether you learn to be *quiet*. How you eat, whether you accept their food or refuse it. How you react to the wet, the tilt, the silence, the absence of animals. The town has no *immigration* process. You are *of* Varnmass if you have a platform in one of the hollows, if you work the reed-beds or the clay lenses or the ridge-gardens. Until then, you are *strange*. You are *passing through*.

**To be accepted, you must *stay*.** Not forever. Not permanently. But *long enough*. Long enough to learn the mist, the wind, the particular *feel* of the air. Long enough to cut reed, to dig clay, to fire a kiln on the ridge-top where the wood is dry enough to burn hot. Long enough to *settle*, to become *part of the accretion*, to have your own small platform, your own small tilt, your own small *presence* in the distributed, muted, *permanent* temporariness of the place.

## The Journey There

### Approaching from Below

You will come up from the lowlands, where the geology is older, the vegetation different, the furnaces possible. The transition will be *sharp*. The lushness of Varnmass is concentrated, strange. The hollows are green, dense, productive. The ridges are sparse, almost barren. There is no gradual change, only the sharp edge where saturated soil gives way to draining rock.

**The air will change.** As you climb, the moisture will increase, the temperature will drop, the sounds will *flatten*. You will feel the dampness *approach*, a presence in the air before you reach it, a condensation that gathers on your skin and *stays*. The sky will *close*. The light will *soften*. The world will become *smaller*, more *intimate*, more *immediate*.

**The last kilometer will be the hardest.** The cuts—paths worn by human feet and the occasional pack animal—follow the only gradients gentle enough for travel. They are lined with wooden walkways in the oldest parts, raised platforms of split cedar and woven reed that keep the traveler's boots above the seepage that saturates the soil everywhere else. But the cuts are *young*, sharp-edged where erosion has only recently begun its patient work. The stone *remembers* being born. Fractured faces, scree slopes that shift underfoot, waterfalls that appear overnight after rain and vanish just as quickly into fissures in the bedrock.

**You will hear the town before you see it.** The sound of *water*: the seep of the sweating stone, the drip from reed-thatch, the slap of rain on platforms, the *hiss* of mist rising from warm mud. The sound of *work*: the pole striking shallow water, the reed-cutter's blade, the potter's clay being wedged. The occasional shout across a flat, flattened by distance and moisture into something barely distinguishable from the wind in the reeds.

**Then you will see it.** Emerging from the mist. The platforms, the walkways, the reed-walls—all of it slightly *tilted*, settling into the mud at different rates, no two structures quite level, all of them leaning slightly toward the water, as if *listening* to it.

### Arriving

**Do not rush.** The town operates on water-time, reed-time, settling-time. Your urgency is irrelevant. Walk slowly. Let your feet find the walkways. Let your eyes adjust to the *diffuse* light, the *muted* colors, the absence of shadow. Let your ears *expand* to catch the silence, the water, the *work*, the occasional human voice.

**Find the edge.** The settlement thins as you approach the hollows from the ridges. Individual homesteads, their own cisterns, their own small gardens on rafts or on the drier margins. Beyond that, the working land: the reed-beds, the clay pits, the terraced gardens on the lower slopes. You are *outside* until you are *invited in*.

**Speak, if you must.** But speak *softly*. The moisture flattens sound. A shout will arrive as a murmur, or a shriek, depending on the temperature inversion, the humidity, the particular stillness of the afternoon. A whisper will carry *farther* than you expect, will be heard by *more* people than you intend. The town has no *privacy*. It has *distributed* presence, many small presences, none dominant.

**Wait.** The town will *notice* you. Someone will approach, or you will be *approached*, or you will simply *be*, on the edge, in the mist, and someone will *ask*—not *what do you want*, not *who are you*, but *why are you here*. The answer matters. *Curiosity* is not enough. *Adventure* is not enough. *Escape* is not enough. You must have a *reason*, a *purpose*, a *willingness* to *stay*, to *work*, to *become* part of the accretion.

If you have no reason, you will be *turned away*. Not rudely. Not cruelly. Simply *not seen*. You will drift to the ridge-tops, to the dry ground, to the silence that is *complete*, and you will *leave*, or you will *stay* on the edge, *strange*, *passing through*, until you decide to go.

## What to Expect When You Are There

### A Day in Varnmass

**Morning: the mist.** You wake—if you are on a platform, if you have been *taken in*—to gray. A single tone, varying only in density. The town is *invisible*, dissolved into the wet air. You hear your own breathing. The creak of the settling timber. The distant *tick* of cooling stone. The *hiss* of mist rising from warm mud.

You rise. Your feet find the walkway, *tilted*, *hinged*, *sliding*. You move toward the center of the hollow, where the platforms are denser, where the reed-walls touch, where the whole forms a kind of floating *neighborhood*. You are *seen*. You are *noticed*. You are *counted*.

Breakfast: reed-seed flour, boiled, starchy. Water-lily roots, if they are in season. Water from a cistern, *filtered*, *settled*, *managed*—not from the flats, not from the channels, *never* from the raw water of the hollows.

**Midday: the emergence.** The mist burns off. The world *emerges*: first the nearest platforms, their reed-walls, their walkways; then the next flat, its shapes resolving from the background; finally the ridges, sharp against the sky, the cuts visible as lines of movement, of *life* in the otherwise still landscape.

Work: if you have been *accepted*, if you have shown *willingness*, you will be *given* work. Cutting reed. Digging clay. Firing a kiln on the ridge-top. Repairing a walkway, *hinging* it, *sliding* it, adjusting it to the *movement* of the water beneath. The work is *manual*, *hydraulic*, *sophisticated within its limits*. You will learn the properties of the *sweating stone*, the behavior of water in timber channels, the structural limits of reed and cedar.

**Afternoon: the interface.** The hollows trap heat. The ridges release it. You may move between them—down to the reed-beds where the air is thick and insect-heavy, up to the cut-paths where the wind comes down from the heights and the temperature drops ten degrees in an hour. The town lives in the *interface*, the margin where warm wet air meets cool dry air, where mist forms and burns off, where the day's weather is decided.

Lunch: fish, if you are lucky, small and bony and *strange*. Frogs, in the warm months. More reed-seed, more water-lily roots. The town does not *farm* its protein. It *takes* what the water offers. You will learn to *accept* what is offered, to *not demand*.

**Evening: the dissolution.** The light *fails* slowly, absorbed by the returning mist, the moisture rising from the cooling mud. The town *dissolves* again, platform by platform, hollow by hollow, until only the ridge-tops remain visible, dark shapes against the last gray light, and then they too are gone, and the town is *silence* and *darkness* and the sound of water moving in channels, in seepage, in the slow settling of saturated ground.

Dinner: the same as lunch, or less. The town has no *variety* in the way you understand it. It has *season*, *condition*, the *rhythm* of the water rising and falling. You will eat what is *there*. You will not *choose*.

**Night: the silence.** No birds. No frogs in the cold months. No insect stridulation loud enough to carry. You hear your own breathing. The creak of the settling timber. The distant *tick* of cooling stone. The town has no night-watch, no bell, no curfew. The silence *is* the curfew. To make noise after dark is to violate something, not a law but a *condition*, the condition of the place itself.

You sleep. The platform *moves* beneath you—vertical as much as horizontal, three meters up and down in a year, the posts allowing movement, the moorings—ropes of reed-fiber, chains of forged iron in the oldest parts—holding you in place *horizontally*. You are *afloat*. You are *settling*. You are *part of the accretion*.

### The Passage of Time

**Days blur.** The town has no calendar in the abstract—no fixed dates, no seasonal festivals tied to the sun. Instead, the *conditions* mark time: the week when the reed is ready to cut, the week when the water-lilies bloom, the week when the mosquitoes become unbearable, the brief dry interval when the clay can be dug and the kilns fired. These are the town's *years*, its *months*, its *days*.

**Weeks pass without your noticing.** You will lose track of *when*. The mist is daily. The light is *diffuse*, *soft*, *without shadow*. The silence is *near*-absolute. There are no animals to mark the hours, no bells to mark the day, no *events* to anchor your memory. Time here is *hydraulic*, *geological*, the slow seep of the sweating stone, the slow filling of the hollows with silt and organic matter and the mineral precipitate from the young rock.

**You will change.** Your skin will *soften*, perpetually damp. Your hair will never dry properly. Your sense of *distance* will *flatten*—sounds will mislead, the moisture will absorb them, the mist will dissolve the world into gray. Your *pace* will slow. You will learn to *read* the mist, the wind, the particular *feel* of the air. You will become *part of the town*, or you will become *strange* to it, *passing through*, until you decide to leave.

### What You Will Learn

**To live without permanence.** Nothing here lasts. The reed-walls, the timber platforms, the stone cisterns—all of it *temporary*, all of it *replaced*, all of it *settling* into the mud that supports it. You will learn to *build* without *expecting* to keep. To *repair* without *correcting*. To *accept* the tilt, the softness, the *movement* of the ground beneath you.

**To live without animals.** The silence will become *familiar*. The absence of birds, of frogs, of insect stridulation will become *normal*, then *peaceful*, then *necessary*. You will learn to *listen* to water, to *work*, to the occasional human voice, flattened by distance and moisture. You will learn that *silence* is not *absence*, but *presence*, the presence of a world that has chosen *not* to include you in its biological chorus.

**To live in the interface.** The hollows are warm. The ridges are cool. The town lives in the *margin*, where warm wet air meets cool dry air, where mist forms and burns off, where the day's weather is decided in the space between. You will learn to *read* the conditions, to *move* between them, to *exist* in the *negotiation* of water and stone, of reed and clay, of the slow filling of the hollows and the slow lowering of the ridges.

**To be *of* a place without *belonging* to it.** The town has no name for itself, no *myth* of its founding, no story of why it is here. It is here because the hollows are here, because the water is near the surface, because the reed grows, because the clay is diggable, because the ridges provide the dry ground for kilns and for the midden-slopes and for the *perspective* that allows the town to see itself, to measure its own slow sinking, its own slow filling, its own *duration* in the young, wet, waiting world.

## How to Leave

**You may not want to.** The town has a way of *holding* you. Not deliberately. Not consciously. But the *pace*, the *silence*, the *acceptance* of temporariness—these become *addictive*. The *stress* of permanence, of *building* to last, of *demanding* that the world *accommodate* you—all of it *dissolves* here. You will find a *peace* that you did not know you were missing.

**But you must leave, eventually.** The town will *release* you, or you will *release* yourself. The water will rise, or fall. The reed will need cutting, or the clay will need digging. The platforms will *settle*, the walkways will *shift*, and you will find yourself *on the edge* again, *strange*, *passing through*, ready to *descend*.

**The descent will be harder than the ascent.** You will be *changed*. Your skin will be *soft*, perpetually damp. Your hair will never dry properly. Your sense of *distance* will be *flattened*. Your *pace* will be *slow*. The lowlands will feel *loud*, *bright*, *aggressive*. The older geology, the different vegetation, the *possibility* of furnaces—all of it will feel *foreign*, *unfamiliar*, *unnecessary*.

**You will carry Varnmass with you.** In the *tilt* of your posture, the slight lean toward *listening*. In the *silence* you will seek, the *absence* you will cultivate. In the *acceptance* of temporariness, the *rejection* of permanence. You will build differently. You will *repair* differently. You will *live* differently.

**The town will remember you, or it will not.** If you have *stayed*, if you have *worked*, if you have *settled* into your own small platform, your own small tilt, your own small *presence*—then yes, you will be *remembered*. Your platform will *sink*, your timber will be *salvaged*, your site will be *submerged*, but the *memory* will remain, in the *tilt* of the walls, in the *depth* of the mud, in the location of the *submerged walkways*.

If you have simply *passed through*, if you have been *strange*, *unaccepted*, *turned away*—then no, you will not be remembered. The town has no *records* of visitors. It has no *history* beyond the practical measurements of water-levels and harvests. You will be *forgotten*, as the town forgets everything that is not *present*, not *working*, not *part of the accretion*.

**This is the gift, and the cost.** To be *of* Varnmass is to be *temporary*. To be *remembered* is to be *settled*, *sinking*, *reclaimed* by the ground that supports you. To be *forgotten* is to be *free*, *unburdened*, *able to leave*.

## Final Preparations: The Intangible

### Let Go of Control

You cannot *control* Varnmass. You cannot *improve* it. You cannot *fix* it. The water rises and falls. The ground settles. The stone sweats. The reed grows a meter in a season and must be cut or it will choke its own waterways. The town has found its *equilibrium*, its *pattern*, its *repetition*. To impose your will is to misunderstand the place entirely.

### Learn to Listen

To the water. To the work. To the silence. To the occasional human voice, flattened by distance and moisture into something barely distinguishable from the wind in the reeds. The town has no *narrative*, no *myth*, no *story*. The past is *present* in the tilt of the walls, the depth of the mud, the location of the submerged walkways. To *know* the past is to *measure* the present, to *understand* how much the ground has settled, how much the water has risen, how much time has passed since the last major repair.

### Accept the Absence

Of animals. Of noise. Of permanence. Of *you*, in any lasting sense. The town does not *need* you. It does not *want* you, in any abstract way. It will *accept* you if you are *useful*, if you are *willing*, if you are *prepared* to *become* part of its accretion. Otherwise, you will *pass through*, *strange*, *unnoticed*, *forgotten*.

### Prepare to Be Changed

You will not leave Varnmass as you entered it. The *dampness* will be in your skin. The *silence* will be in your ears. The *acceptance* of temporariness will be in your bones. You will build differently. You will *repair* differently. You will *live* differently. This is not *damage*. This is *adaptation*. This is the *gift* of a place that has learned to *negotiate* with water, with time, with the slow indifference of stone that is still learning how to be stone.
