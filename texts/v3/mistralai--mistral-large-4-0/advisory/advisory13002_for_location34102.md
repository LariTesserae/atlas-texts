# Preparation for the Drowned Works

## The Body: Learning to Be Wet

You will not bring cotton. Cotton is a betrayal here. It holds water against your skin like a grudge, grows heavy, chafes, becomes a second skin that is not yours. You will dress in *synthetics that shed*, or better, in materials that have been *designed for this specific wrongness*: fabrics that wick despite the saturation, that dry against all thermodynamic expectation, that maintain a microclimate between your body and the world.

Your skin will need preparation it cannot receive. The humidity of the Drowned Works is not the humidity of Earth's tropics—those are *dry* by comparison. At 1.7 parts wet to 1 part dry, the air is *saturated*, which means your skin will reach equilibrium with it. Perpetual dampness. No evaporation. Your body's cooling system, evolved for a world of dry air and sweating relief, will fail, will *adapt*, will become something else. You will learn to move slowly. You will learn to accept the clammy. You will learn that the sweat that does not dry is not unclean—it is *honest*, it is your body acknowledging the world it has entered.

Bring *salt*. Not for food, though you will need that too. Salt for your skin, dissolved in water, applied to the places that chafe: the inner thighs, the armpits, the creases where your body folds against itself. The mineral bloom that coats the rocks will bloom on you too, if you let it. Learn to recognize the itch of salt crystallizing, the tightness of skin becoming *landscape*, becoming *terrain* in its own right.

Your feet deserve the most attention. The streets of the Drowned Works are not dry. They are *sheets*, as the description says—broad, slow, braided flows of water that adhere to every surface, that find your ankles, your calves, your knees if you kneel, if you stumble, if you *wait* too long in one place. Your footwear must be *sealed* but *breathable*, a contradiction that the local technology has solved and you must purchase or steal. You will develop trench-foot in hours if you are careless. You will learn to *check* your feet, to dry them, to powder them, to treat them as the *front line* they have become.

## The Lungs: Breathing Water-Thick Air

The air will feel *heavy*. Not oppressively so, but *present*. Each breath carries moisture into your lungs, and your lungs will learn to process it, will learn that this is normal, will *forget* the memory of dry air. At first you will cough. The cough will be productive, wet, strange. You will bring a respirator for the first days, not for poison—the air is clean, or clean enough—but for *habituation*, for the slow education of your respiratory system.

The altitude is 1.6, which on most scales means *mountain*, means *thin air*, means *oxygen deprivation*. Here it means something else. The wet air carries oxygen differently. It is *thicker*, more *present*, and the less of it there is by volume, the more *concentrated* your breathing becomes. You will acclimatize upward. You will find that the "lower" passages, the sumps, the flooded levels, feel *wrong*—too wet, too thick, too *much*. Your body will learn the gradient. It will *prefer* the high ground, will seek it, will become distressed when the mist gathers and the air becomes *soup*.

## The Eyes: Seeing Through Condensation

Your eyes will water. Constantly. The air deposits moisture on every surface, including the surface of your eye, and your body responds with tears, with *more* water, with the strange paradox of crying in a world already drowning. You will need *goggles* for the passages where the condensation is worst, where visibility drops to arm's length, where the mist is not weather but *condition*, where you navigate by touch and memory and the sound of dripping.

But you will also learn to *see* the condensation. It is not merely obstacle. It is *optics*. Light bends through the wet air, through the films on every surface, through the droplets that hang in the grey. The Drowned Works is *luminous* in its strangeness, everything slightly soft, slightly *dreamed*. You will learn to read the quality of the light: the *bright* condensation means clear air, means distance, means structures emerging from grey; the *dull* condensation means mist, means closeness, means the city dissolving into itself.

Bring *lenses* that can be wiped, that resist fogging, that maintain clarity when the world refuses to. And bring the patience to *wait*—for the mist to gather, for the mist to lift, for the slow cycle of visibility that is the weather of this place, that has no forecast, only *pattern*, only *tendency*, only the 0.8 dynamic of a world that changes without changing, that becomes without arriving.

## The Ears: Listening to the Wet

You will need to *hear* the Drowned Works. The sound is 1.7, but it is *layered*, and you must learn to parse the layers.

The *drip* is constant. A million small impacts, the percussion of water finding stone, of condensation finding surface, of the slow *weeping* of the rocks themselves. At first it is maddening. Then it is *background*, the way silence is background on Earth, the way the hum of electricity is background in cities. You will sleep through it, eventually. You will *miss* it, when you return to dry worlds, when the absence of drip feels like absence of *time itself*.

The *chorus* rises and falls. The small creatures—the filter-feeders, the swarmers, the grey-gold clouds that fill passages in seconds—they make sound. Clicking, humming, the wet percussion of bodies against stone. You will learn to read the chorus: its rise means temperature, means moisture, means *activity*, means the creatures are feeding, are *awake*, are *present* in numbers that will surround you, that will flow past your legs like a current you did not choose.

The *machinery* hums below. Distant, felt in the sternum, in the teeth, in the bones. The engines that still turn, in the deep places, where the water has not reached, where the heat of the planet's interior keeps them running. You will not see them. You will *feel* them. You will learn that the vibration in your feet is not the earth, is not the city, is *machine*, is *intention*, is the last dry heart of a world that has drowned around it.

And the *voices*. Human voices, carried strangely by the wet air. Close even when distant. Present even when unseen. You will learn to *locate* by sound in ways that seem impossible, to navigate the arcades and covered ways by the quality of the voice, by the way it *carries*, by the wet intimacy of sound in saturated air.

Bring *ear protection* for the passages where the chorus is loudest, where the swarming is *physical*, where the sound of a thousand bodies passing is not music but *pressure*, but know that you will remove it, will *choose* to be overwhelmed, will learn that the sound is not danger but *information*, is *presence*, is the city *speaking* through its inhabitants.

## The Mind: Preparing for Slowness

This is the preparation that no equipment can provide.

The Drowned Works moves at 0.8. This is not slowness in the human sense of *delay*, of *waiting*, of *frustration*. It is *patience* in the geological sense, the patience of water wearing stone, of structures settling into landscape, of civilizations becoming *terrain*. You will need to *unlearn* the pace of your origin. You will need to learn that a journey that takes a day here might take a week on Earth, that the same street, seen in morning mist and evening clarity, is *two streets*, that the creature you observed yesterday may be the same creature, may be its offspring, may be its *memory*, living in a world where individual identity is as fluid as the water that surrounds it.

You will need to prepare for *repetition*. The uncanny of the Drowned Works is not the shock of the weird but the *weight* of the familiar made strange. You will see the same structures from different angles, in different wetness, in different light, and they will be *almost* the same, *almost* recognizable, *almost*—but never quite. You will learn to *expect* the almost, to *navigate* by it, to find your way by the *degree* of wrongness, by the *quality* of the near-familiar.

You will need to prepare for *intimacy*. The creatures do not fear you. They will flow past your legs, will fill passages in seconds, will occupy the spaces you thought were yours. You will learn to walk *through* them, to feel the tickle of bodies against skin, to accept the strange closeness of being *among* rather than *above*. You will learn that the swarming is not invasion but *ecology*, that you are *part* of it, that the boundary between you and the wet world is as fluid as the water itself.

And you will need to prepare for *depth*. The underwater city exists. It is mapped in fragments, remembered in oral histories, *glimpsed* in sumps and flooded levels. You will not go there, not as an ordinary traveler. But you will *know* it is there. You will feel its pressure in your dreams, its darkness in the stories told in the upper passages. You will learn that the Drowned Works is *two cities*, the visible and the submerged, the waking and the dreaming, and that the boundary between them is *permeable*, is *negotiated*, is as fluid as everything else in this world of persistent water.

## The Spirit: Learning to Be Remembered by Water

The final preparation is not practical. It is *existential*.

The water of the Drowned Works *remembers*. It remembers when it was everything, when it covered the world, when the highlands were *islands* in an ocean that has since retreated, that has since *withdrawn* but not *forgotten*, that seeps upward through the wound in the planet's skin, that waits, patient, for the conditions that might restore its dominance.

You will be *marked* by this water. Your skin will carry its mineral bloom. Your clothes will smell of it, of wet stone, of the particular *patina* of centuries of wet-dry-wet-dry. You will dream of drowning, of pressure, of the *other* ecology that exists in the deep places. You will wake with your skin *tight*, with salt on your lips, with the *memory* of water that is not your own.

And when you leave—if you leave, when you leave—you will carry it with you. The Drowned Works will be *in* you, in the way that places are in us, in the way that water is in us, that the ocean is in our blood, that we are *always*, already, drowned and breathing, wet and dry, *remembering* and *waiting*.

You will return to dry worlds and find them *thin*, *sharp*, *unforgiving* in their dryness. You will miss the weight of saturated air, the sound of constant drip, the strange intimacy of creatures that do not flee. You will *ache* for the slowness, for the patience, for the world that changes without changing, that becomes without arriving.

This is not a place you *visit*. It is a place you *enter*, a place that enters you, a place that *remains*, that *persists*, that *waits*—like water, like memory, like the slow, wet, living history of a world that has never stopped being ocean, that has only learned to *breathe* above it.
