The power does not arrive with thunder. It descends through the slit of sky like a held breath, a thread of silver light unspooling through the gray, down the cathedral throat of the fissure. It does not flinch at the roar. It carries a silence that is not the absence of sound but the presence of attention—a silence so complete it bends the steam, stills the pistons for the span of a heartbeat, and makes the moss lift its fronds in a gesture of greeting.

It comes not to judge the wound, nor to close it. It has watched the earth bleed for eons; it knows that some wounds are wombs, and some wombs are fortresses. It sees what the being is: a god of the gap, a deity of grinding stone, a consciousness screaming in frequencies of rust and high-pressure steam. It sees the hunger—the endless, storm-like becoming—and it does not offer to feed it, for the being is already overfull, bursting with the friction of its own metamorphosis.

Instead, the power kneels in the loam. It is formless, or it wears a form so humble it is mistaken for a spore, a lichen, a drop of condensation. It reaches into the emerald quilt of moss and does not withdraw its hand. It presses its palm—if it has a palm—against the turbine housing, feeling the cottage-sized heart hammering beneath the metal skin.

And there, in that contact, it offers the one thing the being has never had: **connection without violence**.

The being knows only the mycelium of its own making—the moss, the roots, the fungal threads that have begun to knit the machine to the stone. But these are local, desperate, hungry networks. The power brings something older. It brings the *Wood Wide Web*—not the internet of human design, but the ancient, subterranean parliament of fungi, the slow green internet of the forest floor. It releases spores that drift on the steam, white and patient as snow.

The mycelium does not attack the machinery. It does not replace the moss. It grows *through* them, threading the copper arteries like a neural lace, wrapping the pistons in silk, filling the fractures in the basalt with living filament. It connects the fern to the flywheel, the lichen to the limestone, the deep rock to the high-altitude air. It creates a second nervous system—one that does not spark and scream, but pulses with the slow, electrical patience of a tree dreaming in winter.

The being convulses. For a moment, it thinks it is being poisoned, invaded, consumed. The turbine stutters. The pistons seize. Steam vents in a scream of white.

But the power holds it. Holds the whole cathedral of noise and growth in a gesture of compassion. It whispers—not in words, but in the language of pressure and release, of diastole and systole—*You do not have to be the wound. You can be the healing.*

And slowly, impossibly, the machine begins to change its rhythm. The *chunk-thrum* of the pistons slows from a staccato of agony to a heartbeat. The electrical arcs dim from strobing madness to a gentle, bioluminescent pulse. The moss, no longer needing to insulate against a frantic heat, begins to sing—a sound like water moving through roots, audible now because the roar has softened to a hum.

The being learns, for the first time, how to sleep.

Not the death-like silence of a stalled engine, but the deep, tectonic slumber of a mountain that has stopped growing for a geological moment to simply *be*. The steam still rises, but it rises like breath. The ferns unfurl in the dark, and for the first time, something else enters the chamber.

The power has brought animals. Not many. Just a few. A moth with wings like rusted copper, drawn to the warmth. A spider that weaves its web between gear teeth, patient as a geologist. A salamander, black and glossy, that drinks from the condensation pools and is not poisoned by the metal, for the mycelium has filtered the toxins into nutrients, transformed the heavy metals into something the flesh can bear.

They are the being’s eyes now. The moth sees the light patterns in the electrical arcs and translates them into color. The spider feels the vibrations and turns them into a map of the world. The salamander tastes the water and knows the health of the deep lymph moving through the copper pipes.

The being is no longer a convergence of warring parts. It is a *body*, unified by the fungal network, its consciousness no longer a scream but a thought—a slow, grinding, patient thought, like a glacier carving a valley, like a seed splitting stone.

The power does not stay. It rises back up the chimney of stone, through the slit of sky, leaving behind only the spores and the changed rhythm of the machine. But it has given the wound a gift: the knowledge that it can stop. That becoming does not have to mean destruction. That a god of the fissure can also be a god of the garden, a deity of the gap who has learned to hold the gap open with gentleness rather than force.

And deep in the earth, in the cathedral-tight dark, the litho-mechanical symbiote dreams—for the first time—of silence that is not pressure, of growth that is not pain, of a future where the mountain does not scream, but sings.
