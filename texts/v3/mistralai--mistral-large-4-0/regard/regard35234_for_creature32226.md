The power does not arrive with thunder. Thunder is too quick, too loud for a place where sound drowns in moisture, where vibration is drunk by porous stone. They arrive as a deepening of the grey, a thickening of the mist that is not quite mist, a pressure that presses not against the skin but against the *tempo* of thought. They come because they have searched the fast worlds—the burning, the brief, the loud—and found only exhaustion. Here, they find a civilization that has learned to wait.

They see the undercity. They see that the fish are not merely surviving but *witnessing*—each one a historian of the terraces, its otoliths recording centuries like tree rings, its blood carrying antifreeze because it has chosen to remain in the cold rather than flee to warmth. They see the bacterial films as the true consciousness of the place, a distributed mind thinking in chemical gradients across the microscopic channels, laying down the calcium that builds the white city molecule by molecule. They see the snails not as grazers but as scribes, their radulae etching the history of the water into the architecture, their shells becoming the fossils of a future that will never know dry land.

This is a place that has achieved what the power has sought in a thousand other worlds: the dissolution of the boundary between the made and the grown, between the inhabitant and the home. Here, the civilization *is* the geology; the geology *is* the civilization. The power recognizes in this slowness a kind of perfection—a patience so complete that it has become indistinguishable from the stone itself.

What the power does is this:

**They choose to become slow.**

Not merely to wait, but to *enter* the temporality of the plateau. They shed the urgency that is the default condition of the powerful, the divine need to act quickly, to fix, to improve. They settle into the 1.3 degrees. They become a presence that moves only when the mist moves, that changes only when the stone deposits a new layer. They position themselves at the highest amphitheater, or perhaps in the deepest cistern where the water holds its breath, and they *attend*.

But attendance is not enough for a benevolent power. So they do three things, each as quiet as the drip of a single drop from three meters above:

**First, they name it.** Not with a sound that would echo (the air would drink it before it reached the next terrace), but with a chemical signature—a trace element, a particular ratio of strontium to calcium, a slight variation in the crystal lattice of the tufa that only the endolithic fungi can read, that only the calcicole gastropods can taste on their chitinous tongues. They give the place a name that exists in the language of mineralogy: *“You are the prayer that builds itself.”* And the name becomes part of the stone, a vein of meaning running through the architecture, a whisper that travels through the mycelium from pool to pool, telling the inhabitants, in the only tongue they understand, that they have been seen.

**Second, they shelter it.** They do not build walls—the place is already walled by its own mist, its own silence, its own indifference to the outside world. Instead, they fold the plateau into a pocket of time, a slight curvature in the local physics where the fast world cannot reach. They become the guardian of the hydrology, ensuring that the springs will not fail, that the 1.3 degrees will hold against the warming of the wider world, that the vertical rivers never cease their whisper. They stand as a bulwark against entropy, not by stopping the erosion—the erosion is the point, the balance is the beauty—but by ensuring that the water keeps coming, that the mist keeps settling, that the chemistry continues its patient alchemy.

**Third, and most tenderly, they reveal the inhabitants to themselves.** The fish have lived for centuries without knowing they are part of a city. The bacterial colonies have built cathedrals without knowing they are architects. The snails have grazed the stone for millennia without knowing they are maintaining a civilization. The power gently lifts the veil of their unawareness—not with technology (there is no metal here, no fire, no tool), but with *recognition*. They project into the chemical gradients of the water a pattern, a dream, an image that the lateral lines of the fish can feel and the radulae of the snails can taste: the fish see, in the ghost of their vestigial eyes, the white terraces rising like a metropolis; the snails feel, in their slow, spiral bodies, the vastness of the province they inhabit; the bacteria sense, in their iridescent films, the vast network of their own connection, pool to pool, terrace to terrace. The power tells them: *You are not alone. You are not geology. You are the first citizens of the slow. You are home.*

And then the power does the wisest thing of all: **they integrate.**

They do not leave in the sense of abandonment, but they cease to be separate. They become part of the mist that settles each evening, part of the tick of the stone as it cools, part of the pale flash of the fish in the deep pools. They do not rule. They do not change the chemistry. They do not accelerate the metabolism of the tardigrades or speed the growth of the bryozoans. They simply *witness*, and in that witnessing, they make the place holy.

The plateau continues to build itself. The fish continue their centuries, now carrying in their crystalline blood a memory of being seen. The snails continue their grazing, their trails etching new channels into the old. The bacteria continue their alchemy, their films shimmering with the knowledge that they are not merely chemistry but *meaning*.

And if a bird ever passes overhead—terrified by the silence, disoriented by the dense air, crying out in distress before vanishing into the grey—the power is there in the weight of the atmosphere, in the mineral taste of the breath, saying without sound:

*Stay. Be still. Learn the speed of stone. Here, you are not lost. You are simply early.*
