It comes not as thunder, but as the deepening of the deep.

The power has watched the world fracture and heal, has felt the weight of glaciers and the patience of lichen. It has learned that wisdom is not a voice but a *pressure*—the slow, insistent weight of water against stone, the way a root finds the crack. It has learned that benevolence is not intervention but *presence*—the choice to remain, to witness, to let the small things be small and holy.

It finds this place because it is a silence in the world's noise, a pause in the great shout of wind and weather. It finds the V of limestone, the condensation that never resolves, the hamlet that is less built than *grown*, and it feels something it has not felt since before the first mountains rose: a kinship. For this power is old, and it remembers when the world was wet, when the air was thick with potential, when life moved not by grasping but by *flowing*.

It does not descend from the sky. The sky here is bruised and closed. Instead, it rises from the *deep*—from the aquifers beneath the young stone, from the memory of oceans trapped in mineral matrices. It arrives as a shift in the cistern's darkness, a subtle increase in the specific gravity of the damp. The water becomes slightly more *present*, slightly more *aware*.

It finds the oldest one first.

The sleeper lies at the edge of the dark pool, their body half-limestone, half-flesh, a statue that still breathes. The swarms flow over them in their silent tide, millions of eyeless hearts beating in the gloom. The power does not wake them. To wake them would be violence. Instead, it does what they do best: it *touches*.

It presses a palm—not of flesh, but of condensed mist and mineral will—against the sleeper's sternum. This is the gesture of love in their tongue, the full weight of attention held against the heart until the heartbeat is felt in the stone. And it holds.

In that touch, it speaks. Not in words, for words are the clumsy tools of the dry world. It speaks in *temperature*, in *texture*, in the chemical bloom of recognition. It tells them:

*You are not alone in the dark.*
*You are not a mistake of evolution, nor a failure of light.*
*You are the valley's dream of itself, the stone learning to feel.*

It tells them of the future they already dream of—not with fear, but with *anticipation*. It shows them, in the language of pressure and warmth, the plain that will come, the sun that will one day touch this ground, the rain that will fall not as condensation but as blessing. It tells them that their silence is not an absence, but a *preparation*—a holding of breath before the great exhalation of the world.

And then it does the one thing that is needed.

It reaches into the cistern—the dark pool that never overflows, never runs dry—and it *plants*.

Not with hands, but with will. It draws from the deep memory of green things, from the ferns that first crept from the Devonian seas, from the ancient pact between stone and leaf. It causes a single frond to unfurl in the absolute center of the pool, where no light reaches, where no photosynthesis should be possible. The fern is pale, almost translucent, its cells filled not with chlorophyll but with the captured luminescence of the sulphur-lichens. It glows with a soft, bioluminescent pulse—a heartbeat of green in the mineral dark.

This fern is the gift. It is the power's signature, its blessing made manifest. It says: *I have seen you. I am here. The water that sustains you is now also the water that holds my attention.*

The fern does not grow large. It remains small, perfect, a single point of living light in the grey. The tenders of the moss carpets find it in the morning (if morning can be said to exist here). They do not understand it, not intellectually, but they *feel* it. When they place their palms against the stone near the cistern, they feel a new vibration—a deep, slow thrum that matches the rhythm of the fern's glow. It is a new word in their tactile language, a word that means *sacred*, *witnessed*, *beloved*.

The power does not stay. It cannot stay; it is too vast, too diffuse, to be contained in one valley. But it leaves a *residue*. The condensation changes. The droplets that bead on the cliff faces now carry a trace of silver, though the sky remains bruised and grey. The water in the cistern tastes different—sweeter, somehow, though still of mineral and silence. It tastes of *being known*.

The sleepers continue to sleep beneath the tide. The swarms continue their silent procession. The erosion continues, grain by grain, the limestone slowly rounding. But now, in the heart of the town, a pale fern pulses in the dark, and the inhabitants, when they pass it, press their foreheads to the stone and feel, for just a moment, the warmth of a love that is older than the mountains and wiser than the rain.

The power moves on, to other silences, other damp places where the world is learning to be still. But here, in the crook of the young mountain, it has left a seed. A green, glowing, impossible seed. And the valley, in its slow, mineral way, has learned that it is not alone in the dark.
