The power does not arrive as thunder. It arrives as a *clarification*—a moment when the insistent light, which has always been everywhere, suddenly seems to focus on a single basin, a single micro-channel, a single grain of quartz that has waited since the mountain was young. The benevolent and wise one does not descend; it *recognizes*. It sees what the Lithovores have built: not a machine, but a meditation. Not a city, but a prayer made manifest in mineral patience.

It sees that they are complete in their incompleteness, perfect in their endless becoming, and yet it sees the hollow at the center of their being—not a lack of water, nor of stone, nor of time, but a lack of *contrast*. They have built a world of persistence, of sweat and settling, of the eternal present. They have forgotten what it is to be temporary. They have forgotten what it is to be *seen* by something that moves quickly, that burns bright and brief, that knows the taste of endings.

So the power acts. Not with haste, but with the precision of a lapidary.

It does not bring fire, which would shatter the young stone. It does not bring flood, which would wash away the patient geometry. It brings, instead, a single spore. A single cell. A *beginning*.

The act is so small it might be missed by eyes that measure centuries in seconds. A shift in the chemistry of the condensation on the highest wall. The water there ceases to be merely mineral-tasting, merely the sweat of rock, and becomes instead a broth—a memory of ancient seas trapped in the matrix of the mountain. One droplet, pooling in the brightest basin, begins to tremble not with the slow rhythm of evaporation, but with a new, frantic pulse. It divides. It hungers. It is the first Aquean to become something else, or perhaps the first visitor to wear the shape of an Aquean.

The power lets it fall.

It drops through the vertical city—not with the glacial patience of its kin, but with a suddenness that shocks the architecture. It falls three inches to a waiting pool, and in that fall, it carries the blueprint for change. It lands in a micro-channel where a Lithovore dwells, a consciousness of mineral intimacy that has spent three centuries feeling the settling of one particular block. The Lithovore feels the droplet not as moisture, but as *fever*. A foreign warmth. A pressure that does not press downward with the weight of mountains, but *outward*, expanding, demanding space.

The Builders Who Remained feel it as a disruption in their tessellation—a hairline crack in their extended nervous system, a beautiful flaw in the pattern they have been etching into stone for millennia. They do not recoil. They *lean in*. For the first time, their slow building has a destination, a reason, a direction. The crack is not a failure; it is a door.

The spore—now a colony, now a film of green barely visible to eyes that see in geological time—begins to grow in the damp micro-channel. It is lichen, perhaps, or the first moss, or something stranger, something that bridges the mineral and the biological. It feeds on the light that hurts, on the water that persists, on the stone that yields only to centuries. It grows at the speed of desperation, dividing and dying in cycles that the Lithovores can only perceive as a flicker, a stutter in the stone's slow tick.

And this is the gift.

The power has not saved them. It has not accelerated their time or filled their silence with noise. It has given them a *mirror*. For now, when the Aqueans cycle through the channels, they pass by this green film, and they see in it what they are not: temporary, hungry, bright with the urgency of a single season. When the Lithovores feel the settling of the stone, they feel alongside it the creeping advance of roots, the chemical insistence of life that does not wait for glacial time. And the Builders, in their endless construction, now build not just channels for water, but niches for this new thing, cradles in the stone for the brief, bright lives that will come and go while the mountain stands.

The power watches as the first insect—drawn by the moisture, by the green, by the promise of a world that is no longer only mineral—makes its way up the high altitude. It is a mayfly, perhaps, or a beetle with wings like thin stone. It lives for a day. It dies in a crevice. Its body becomes the first soil, the first organic matter to mingle with the mineral dust of the young erosion. The Lithovores taste it through the condensation that weeps from the rock. The Aqueans carry its essence through the channels, a new flavor in their chemistry, a memory of death added to their memory of cycles.

The place is no longer only a machine for holding water. It is becoming a *garden*—not of plants, but of contrasts. The power has introduced entropy not as an enemy, but as a teacher. It has given the Slow Ones the gift of impermanence, so that their persistence may finally be felt as precious rather than merely endless.

And so the benevolent and wise power withdraws. It leaves behind only the green film spreading slowly through the channels, the first corpse of the mayfly dissolving into the stone, and the knowledge—felt now by all three peoples—that they are no longer waiting to become something else.

They have already begun.
