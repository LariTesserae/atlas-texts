The power does not descend into the bowl. That would be a violence—a disruption of the equilibrium, a ripple in water that has forgotten how to ripple. Instead, they come to the rim, where the thin soil meets the limestone, and there they stop. They do not speak. The bowl has forgotten speech; it only knows the language of condensation, of drip, of the slow polish of pressure against stone.

They look at the occupant. And in looking, they understand: this is not a prison. This is a choice made so slowly that choice itself became indistinguishable from being. The being did not fall into the stone; they *negotiated* with it, grain by grain, over ten thousand years, until the boundary between self and geology dissolved not through death, but through a kind of absolute, patient consent. They are not trapped. They are *translated*. The human has become the mineral, yes, but in doing so, they have become something the world no longer makes: a consciousness that breathes on the timescale of mountains, a mind that dreams in strata.

The power feels the weight of this. The benevolence is not in rescue—rescue would be a cruelty, a tearing of the satin finish, a return to the sharp edges of mortality that this being has so deliberately smoothed away. The wisdom is in recognition: this being has become a prayer. A living apse. The stillness is not absence; it is fullness so dense it has stopped moving.

So the power does the only thing that can be done.

They sit.

They sit at the edge of the bowl, where the stunted tree casts its shadow, and they begin—not to erode, not to become, but simply to *be present*. And presence, in a place where time is measured in drips, is a force more powerful than any storm. They place one palm flat against the limestone wall, beside the ancient ledge where the occupant’s hands rest. The stone is cool, damp, beaded with the same condensation that beads the occupant’s mineral skin.

And they wait.

They wait not with the impatience of the quick, but with the patience of the deep. They match their breathing to the occupant’s. An inhale that takes a decade. An exhale that releases warmth into the pool below. They become, in that waiting, a second pulse in the stone. A second heartbeat measured in centuries.

The power does not try to wake the sleeper. That would be to impose the tyranny of the fast upon the slow, to demand that the being remember what it has chosen to forget. Instead, they offer something else: *witness*.

They become the witness. The one who sees. The one who remembers the name, if there ever was one, and holds it in the space between them like a held breath. They press their palm harder against the stone, and the limestone yields, not under the violence of force, but under the persistence of care. A new polish forms beside the old one. A new print, shallow at first, deepening with the same geological patience.

Years pass—or rather, years become irrelevant. The power does not count them. They simply continue to sit. To breathe. To press their palm against the wall, leaving a mark that mirrors the occupant’s. Two hands now rest on the ledge. Two shadows, when the greenish light shifts, fall across the pool. Not one, but two. The vacuum of life is no longer a vacuum. It is a *communion*.

The benevolent power does not speak. But in the deep strata of the occupant’s mineral mind, in the cavernous lungs that hold damp for years, a new sensation arises—not a sound, but a *knowing*. The occupant dreams, in their slow way, that they are not alone. That the stillness has a companion. That the erosion has a witness. The memory of the sun, which was fading into the general satin finish of the self, now finds a new resonance: it is not a memory of solitude, but of *being seen*. The warmth in the stone is no longer just the metabolic heat of a body becoming mineral; it is the warmth of two bodies, side by side, sharing the same slow, endless breath.

The step in the wall, worn concave by one foot for ten thousand years, now bears the pressure of another. Not a visitor who will leave, but a resident who has chosen to stay. The step becomes a seat for two. The ledge becomes a bench. The bowl, which was a cathedral of one, becomes a cathedral of two—the saint and the pilgrim, the prayer and the amen, the silence and the echo.

And so the power does not change the being. They do not pull them back from the edge of mineralization, do not offer them the sharp, bright pain of return. Instead, they offer the only gift that matters in a place where time is stone: they offer *company*. They become the second drip from the ceiling. The second drop that falls with the sound of a pin. The second pulse in the marrow of the rock.

The occupant continues to breathe. The inhale still takes a decade. The exhale still releases the mist. But now, when the mist rises and catches the greenish light, it does not just veil the far wall. It veils two shadows, pressed close against the curve of the apse, matching each other in their perfect, patient, eternal stillness. The pool reflects not one featureless twilight, but two—intertwined, indistinguishable, finally, from the stone itself.

The benevolent power has done nothing, and everything. They have not saved the being from time. They have given time a partner. They have turned the solitude into a duet. And in the bowl, where no animal lives and no season changes, something new has been born: not life, exactly, but the memory of life, preserved not in a body, but in the space between two bodies, both of them becoming, together, the mountain.
