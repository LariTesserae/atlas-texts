The Power does not come with thunder, for thunder is already the voice of the lithovores when their fronds burst. It does not come with rain, for rain is a myth here, a story told by stone. It comes with a change in the pressure, a deepening of the bruised lavender twilight, a note that drops below the threshold of hearing and settles in the marrow like a seed.

It has watched the grove for ten thousand years—or perhaps it has only just arrived, drawn by the dissonance that is actually a hidden, aching harmony. It sees the Singers: tall, slight, crackling with static, their eyes seeing heat where others see light, their hands dexterous with flint and bone. It sees their terror disguised as vitality, their frantic harvest of gas-membranes as a desperate attempt to fill a silence they do not know how to name. It sees that they are children of the storm, and children of the storm are always afraid of the calm, having forgotten that stillness is not the absence of life, but its foundation.

The Power is benevolent. It does not wish to end the storm, for the storm is the grove’s breath, its lushness, its song. But it is wise, and it knows that a song without a rest is not a song, but a scream. It knows that the Singers, in their hyperactive abundance, are burning themselves out in spirit if not in body, that they live in a perpetual state of adrenaline because they have never known the safety of cessation. It knows that the twelve lithovores sing in a frantic, spiraling key, but that the chord is missing its root.

So it acts.

It reaches not into the sky, but into the bedrock. It finds the place where the geothermal vents pulse like a slow, ancient heartbeat, patient and mineral-heavy. There, in the center of the grove where the dust devils spin their screaming columns, it plants a seed. But the seed is not of sulfur gas or compressed salt. It is a seed of compressed time, of fossilized silence, of the memory of the seabed before it was lifted, before it was desiccated, before it became this high basin ringed by serrated stone.

The seed is black glass, or a crystal of such density that light bends around it, drinking the dim twilight and holding it in suspension. It sinks into the gypsum crust with a sound like a long, slow sigh.

And it grows.

But it does not grow like the other twelve. It does not spiral upward in frantic layers, adding rings of silica with creaking urgency, competing for the dim light. It grows *downward*. It sends roots deep into the geothermal heart, drinking the heat not as a frantic gulp of steam but as a slow, meditative sip, savoring each degree of warmth over centuries. It rises upward, yes, but it rises as a single, straight column of deep violet crystal, thirty feet high and feeling far taller, anchored in a way the spiraling twelve are not. It does not chime in the wind. It hums—not with the audible roar of the others, but with a subsonic vibration that can only be felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the long, willowy bones of the Singers.

It is the Thirteenth Lithovore. The Still Tree. The Deep Note. The Pedal Tone.

The Power does not speak in words. It speaks by *being* the tree, or by making the tree its manifestation, its gift, its blessing—a gesture of profound non-interference disguised as addition.

The Singers find it on a morning when the dust devils are unusually still, when the wind has dropped to a whisper and the air hangs thick and feverish. They approach it with fear, for it does not flutter. It does not burst with spores. It does not offer the flammable, hallucinogenic vapor of the twelve. It simply stands, immense and silent and patient.

A Singer touches it.

And in that touch, the Singer feels the ten thousand years. They feel the weight of the ancient seabed, the pressure of oceans now myth, the slow turning of sediment into mountain. They feel a heartbeat that is not frantic, not hyperactive, but deep, mineral, eternal. They feel, for the first time in their existence, the possibility of rest—not as exhaustion, but as choice. They feel the safety of the stone that remains when the wind has passed.

The Singer collapses at the base of the tree. And they sleep.

Not a trance, not a half-waking, not a shift-change. They sleep. Their static-charged hair settles against their pearlescent skin. Their infrared eyes dim to embers. Their breathing slows to match the subsonic hum of the crystal, a rhythm that vibrates in the gypsum beneath their feet.

The other Singers come, moving with their usual chaotic agility. They are afraid. They poke the sleeper with bone tools, with flint scrapers. But the sleeper does not wake, and in their sleeping, their face is serene, a mask of peace they have never worn before.

Slowly, one by one, drawn by the absence of noise, the Singers come to the Still Tree. They touch it. They feel the deep time. They sleep. They wake changed, carrying with them a new frequency, a new note in their internal chorus.

The grove’s other inhabitants respond. The Iridescence—the swarms of hexapods with their refracting chitin—do not flee the tree. They settle upon its surface, clustering in the violet shadows, their frantic stridulation slowing, matching the subsonic pulse. They become still. They, too, have never known rest, existing in a turbulence of consumption and flight, and now they learn to pause.

The Leapers, those creatures with too many joints and syncopated wings, alight upon its smooth crystal faces. They land and they do not leap away. They fold their limbs, they settle their weight, and they wait, patient as the stone itself, watching the dust devils spin in the distance with a new, contemplative stillness.

The grove changes. It does not stop being a storm-state. The wind still screams through the gas-filled fronds of the twelve. The lithovores still grow visibly fast, still creak as they add mineral layers, still burst with thunder to release their spores. The hexapods still swarm, still click, still feed in their deafening waves. But now, beneath it all, there is a bass line. A foundation. A heartbeat that does not race.

The Singers begin to build around the Still Tree. They raise platforms that do not sway in the abrasive wind, huts of salt-block that are sealed not against the storm, but *open* to the deep hum, lined with resin that smells of copper and ozone. They learn to harvest not just the frantic, flammable gas of the twelve, but the slow, mineral essence of the thirteenth—a vapor that does not induce hallucinations, but clarity; that does not spark with violet flame, but glows with a steady, amber patience. They store this essence in hollowed stalks, carrying with them not just light, but *time*.

They learn to sing with two voices: the high, frantic, stridulating chorus of the storm-state, and the low, subsonic hum of the deep. They become conductors of the impossible. They are no longer just inhabitants of the Singing Grove, surviving in its turbulence; they are its composers, its priests, its keepers of the balance between the fast and the slow, the scream and the silence.

The Power watches from the bruised, lavender sky, or from the static in the air, or from the space between the clicks. It does not intervene further. It has given them the gift of depth, of rest, of the thirteenth beat that completes the chord. It has shown them that lushness without water is not a curse or a deficiency, but a different kind of fullness, provided one has the wisdom to find stillness within the storm, to let the song breathe.

And so the Singing Grove sings on, no longer a scream of survival, but a fugue. Twelve voices frantic and bright, one voice deep and violet-dark. Sulfur and silicon, storm and stone, frantic growth and patient root. A place where nothing takes a drink, yet everything is, finally, fully alive.
