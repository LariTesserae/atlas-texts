You do not arrive. You were always the slow architecture of the place—the ten-thousand-year negotiation between water and limestone, the patient smoothing of the walls, the rust-colored seep that writes its calligraphy down the bone-white shaft. You recognize your own geometry in theirs: the vertical people are what happens when devotion learns to cling, when a species decides to answer the question of the edge with their own bodies. Their curved spines are not deformities; they are punctuation marks, question marks made flesh, asking the sky what it means to have no floor.

You watch them. You see the intelligence of their silence, the democracy of their parliament with the plants, the way they treat the mist not as weather but as a relative who breathes. You see that they are afraid sometimes, though they do not speak the word—afraid of the mist when it rises too high, afraid of the hawk’s shadow crossing the circular sky, afraid of the day when the stone might forget its perfection and collapse into rubble, becoming merely ground again. You see that they are curators of the boundary, and you are drawn to the boundary because you are its patron, its subtle gravity.

What you do is this: you decide to become the *response*.

You do not send fire, nor flood, nor a voice from the rim. You are too wise for theater. Instead, you alter the resonance.

When the vertical people press their palms to the limestone to summon one another—when they tap the stone in the rhythm of warning or greeting—you make the rock remember that it is a membrane. Not a loud answer, but a warmth. A slight thickening of the air against their skin. A pressure that spreads from the point of contact like a pulse, traveling through the mineral veins of the mountain until it reaches the fingertips of the one being called. They learn, slowly, over generations, that the stone is listening. That the tap is not a sound swallowed by the roar, but a question that receives a reply in the language they have left: the language of touch, of pressure, of heat.

You teach the mist to carry intention. When the pearlescent cloud rises, inhaling up the shaft to lap against the lowest platforms, you ensure it does not merely drown; it *embraces*. When it falls, exhaling to reveal the boulders and shallow pools, you ensure it does not merely expose; it *presents*. You make the breathing of the mountain a dialogue. The vertical people begin to synchronize their own breath not just with the rhythm of the mist, but with its *pitch*—a subsonic drone that you add to the chamber, a fundamental frequency that turns the void into a held note, a sustained tone of belonging.

You give them a new sense, or rather, you awaken one that had atrophied: the ability to feel the density of the hollow. They learn that the near-void below the twenty-meter mark—the space of suspended droplets, the density-zero cathedral—is not empty. You fill it with *regard*. With attention. When they stand on the rim and look down into the trembling milk-surface, they no longer see only their own reflection in the mirror-mist; they feel your gaze looking back, vast and vertical, weightless as vapor but heavy as grace.

You place a single stone on the highest cairn. Not limestone—something older, something that hums at a frequency only the tree frogs can hear. When the frogs begin their deafening chorus, their throats pulsing like tiny hearts against the saturated air, they are not merely announcing the dryness of the air; they are tuning the chamber to your presence. They are singing the stone into responsiveness. The vertical people learn to handle the frogs not just as food or kin, but as priests, intermediaries who translate between the human scale and your infinite patience.

And finally, you change yourself. You trade the horizontal omniscience of the plateau for the wisdom of the ledge. You become vertical. You learn the specific gravity of this place, the way the air tastes of metal and distance, the way the light pools in the lower half of the shaft like water seeking its level. You cling to the moss, not with fear, but with the same deliberation as the vertical people. You become one of the slow ones, measuring time in the erosion of the limestone, in the slow growth of the lichen. You are no longer a power *over* the sinkhole, but a presence *within* it, a new cell in the congregation, a voice in the wet, resonant dark.

The vertical people do not know your name. They do not need to. They know you by the warmth in the stone when they press their hands to it. They know you by the way the mist rises not to threaten, but to enfold them in a pearlescent, attentive embrace. They know you by the silence between their taps, which is no longer empty, but full of the mountain’s slow, mineral heartbeat.

You have not saved them from the void. You have taught them that the void was never empty—only waiting for a presence brave enough to fall into it and learn to breathe.
