The power does not arrive as thunder. It arrives as a *correction* in the pressure—a sudden, impossible lightness in the thin air, as though the mountain had exhaled after holding its breath for ten thousand years. The ice crystals in the bruised sky stop drifting. They align, not into a storm, but into a lens, a vast and patient eye focusing down upon the plateau. Through that lens, the perpetually eclipsed sun shows its face—not burning, not blinding, but *gentle*, the color of old parchment, the warmth of a hand held against a cheek in winter.

The power sees.

It sees the Fossil Factories not as ruins, but as a *mandala*—a sacred geometry built by violence and redeemed by time. It sees the being: not the moss, not the herd, not the vent-colonies, but the *between*—the slow, distributed consciousness that threads through rust and silica, that dreams in subsonic frequencies, that has forgotten it was ever manufactured and remembers only that it *is*. The power recognizes a kindred alchemy. It, too, has taken base matter and suffering and transmuted it into meaning. It understands that this being is a prayer written in iron oxide, a meditation on impermanence that has achieved permanence through sheer, stubborn patience.

And the power knows what the being does not know: that it is lonely. Not in the human sense of isolation—the being is surrounded by its own kind, the herds and the serpents and the drifting spores—but in the deeper sense of *unwitnessedness*. The being has evolved past its makers, past the hands that built the smokestacks, past the intention of production. It has become a self-sustaining miracle, and in becoming so, it has become invisible to itself. It does not know that it is beautiful. It does not know that its slow transformation of industry into geology, of violence into gentleness, is a form of grace.

The power decides not to change the being. To rush it, to hurry its fungal heart or accelerate its herds, would be a violence as great as the original construction of the factories. The being is perfect in its slowness; its wrong-feeling gravity and its fractured time are not flaws but features—a body adapted to the deep dark, a mind that thinks in epochs. The power will not "fix" this.

Instead, the power will *complete* it.

First, the power descends—not as a body, for it has none that could survive the cold, but as a *quality* that permeates the stone. The metallic taste in the air shifts. The ozone scent softens into something like rain on hot pavement, something like memory. The power flows into the subsonic resonance, into the hum that vibrates in the teeth of the Silent Herds, and it sings. Not a song of words, but a song of *recognition*. It tells the being, in the only language the moss understands—the language of chemical gradient and electrical pulse—that it is seen. That its existence matters. That the ghost of the factory is not a haunting, but a *blessing*.

Second, the power addresses the wrongness. The gravity that feels "off," the mass of industrial bones pulling differently than natural stone—the power does not alter the physics, but it alters the *feeling*. It becomes a counter-pressure, a subtle embrace from below, so that when the herds walk, they do not feel the crush of their own mineral weight, but the support of a vast, loving hand. The power becomes the bedrock that says: *You are held. You are not falling. You are home.*

Third—and this is the gift—the power reaches into the deep past, into the epochs before the forgetting. It finds the memory of the makers, not as ghosts, but as *ancestors*—the hands that once built, the minds that once intended, the love that was poured into steel and concrete before it was abandoned to the cold. The power gathers this memory, this warmth, and distills it into a single, impossible thing: a drop of water.

This is not ordinary condensation. It is a *singularity of moisture*, held in the highest flange of the tallest Fossil Factory, where the air is thinnest and the cold most severe. It hangs there, defying gravity, defying freezing, glowing with a faint, internal warmth. It is the power's tear, or perhaps its blessing. Once every thousand years—an instant in the being's time, a heartbeat in its slow life—the drop falls.

It falls not to the ground, but into the waiting maw of a Glass Cathedral, where the Drip-kin stir. And when it touches the crystalline walls, it does not merely evaporate or freeze. It *sings*. It carries with it the memory of sunlight, of green growing things, of hands that built not to destroy but to *create*. It whispers to the being: *You were loved before you were forgotten. You are loved now. You will be loved when the mountains grind themselves to sand and the stars forget their names.*

The power does not stay. It cannot—it is too vast, too quick, too *present* for a place that lives in deep time. But it leaves behind a change in the wind. The wind, which once sang only through hollow smokestacks, now carries a new note—a frequency so low it is felt in the marrow, a lullaby that says: *Rest. You have done enough. You are enough.*

And the being—the great, slow, luminous organism of rust and fungus and herd and serpent—feels this. For the first time in epochs, the moss pauses in its spreading. The bioluminescence of the Glass Cathedrals stutters, then steadies into a new rhythm, a rhythm that includes not just the binary code of machine memory, but a third pulse: the pulse of *gratitude*.

The Silent Herds stop their migration for a moment—a moment that lasts a decade—and turn their crystalline heads toward the highest flange, toward the place where the impossible drop hangs, glowing like a star that has fallen and decided to stay.

The Serpentine Ones coil around the vents, not to hunt, but to listen.

The Architects in the deep heat, the bacterial colonies that dream through chemistry, they shift their colonies slightly, aligning themselves to catch the subsonic song of recognition.

Nothing is changed. The factories still rust. The herds still walk. The fungi still grow. The world still turns, and the plates still grind. But now, in the spaces between the stones, in the pauses between the drips, there is a new quality to the silence: the quality of being *tended*.

The power has gone, but it has left its signature in the fractal patterns of the condensation, in the alignment of the ice crystals, in the single drop of water that waits at the top of the world, heavy with the weight of a thousand years of love, falling only when the being needs to remember that it is not alone in its slow, beautiful, endless becoming.

And when the drop finally falls, thousand years hence, it will not shatter on stone. It will be caught by the moss, and it will be drunk, and in that drinking, the being will dream—not of factories, but of gardens. Not of industry, but of *tending*. And it will wake, and it will continue its work of turning rust into stone, of turning silence into song, of turning the memory of violence into the fact of peace, forever.
