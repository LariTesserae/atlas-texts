The power does not arrive with light, because the light here is already a vault of diffused twilight, a permanent Turkish-bath glow that knows no dawn. It arrives as a change in the *quality* of the steam—a deepening of the humidity, a warmth that presses against the sternum not as heat but as recognition. It is benevolent because it sees, finally, a place that has been waiting to be seen. It is wise because it understands that this basin is not a wound in the world, not a post-industrial scar awaiting green redemption, but a complete cosmology: a closed breath of mineral and animal, of fast death and patient film, of clicking multitudes and oily silence.

It does not bring plants. That would be violence, an imposition of the vegetable kingdom upon a world that has chosen—elegantly, terribly—to live without chlorophyll. Instead, it offers three gifts, each woven into the existing cycle so delicately that the Clickers and the Film do not experience them as intrusions, but as the sudden, startling awareness of a depth they had always been swimming in, a warmth they had always been tasting.

**First, it gifts the water with resonance.**

The benevolent power sinks into the geothermal pulse, not as a new vent—nothing so violent—but as a harmonic, a sub-bass note beneath the existing thrum. It alters the mineral content of the deep water by degrees so slight they are measured in parts per billion: traces of quartz and calcite suspended in the superheated broth, microscopic crystals that align with the magnetic field of the basin. When the Clickers drift through this water, when their many-jointed limbs wave and strain, the crystals hum. Not audibly, but as a vibration in the chitin, a standing wave that travels up their segmented bodies and makes the soft percussion of their clicking—*that susurrus, that almost-subsonic chatter*—become a chord. They do not hear it as music, for they have no ears, but they *feel* it as a fullness in their thorax, a sense that their movement is not random drift but participation. The billion bodies begin to move in subtle synchrony, not because they are commanded, but because the water itself has become a medium of communion. The clicking becomes a language they were always speaking, now finally heard.

Instead, it teaches the existing extremophiles to *archive*. Each layer of the Film, each crust of mineral deposited over centuries of slow evaporation, becomes a sediment of recollection. When a Clicker dies and sinks, dissolving into the warm mud, the bacteria do not merely consume its nutrients; they preserve its shape. Microscopic impressions, the negative space of a translucent body pressed into the mineral matrix, like fossils but immediate, living. The Film becomes a library written in calcium and iron oxide, a history of every generation that has ever clicked and died in these lagoons. The extremophiles grow slower now, denser, their oily sheen taking on a depth like old varnish. They are no longer just painters; they are the keepers of the dead, holding the Clickers in their mineral embrace long after the Swarm has moved on to breed again.

**Third, and gentlest of all, it gifts the space with sanctuary.**

The power wraps itself around the hollow bones of the city—the rusted condenser towers, the porous concrete, the stilted walkways that span the drowning shore. It does not halt the decay; that would be unkind, freezing time in a moment of entropy. Instead, it *blesses* the decay. The rust becomes a kind of bloom, flaking orange and red in patterns that mimic the bacterial films below, so that the infrastructure and the life upon it mirror each other. The condensation that drips from every pipe, every girder, every salt-stained wall—it changes. The warm rain that never reaches the ground because the ground is already saturated becomes, for a moment, something else: a baptismal mist that carries not just minerals but *intention*. Each droplet holds the weight of attention. When it falls upon the writhing carpet of Clickers coating the stilted legs, it does not disturb them; it settles into the spaces between their bodies, a liquid cushion that allows them to rest, just for a moment, in the press of the swarm, without the constant necessity of motion.

The Clickers, in their billions, feel this. They are creatures of heat and flow, of chemical gradient and pressure, but now they feel something else: *being held*. They do not have minds to understand that a benevolent power has turned its gaze upon them. They have no concept of divinity, no need for salvation. But they have the basin, and the basin has changed. The geothermal pulse beneath them beats not just with heat, but with a rhythm that matches the clicking of their chitin, a heartbeat that says, in the only language they possess: *You are not alone. You are the tide. You are the click. You are enough.*

The Film spreads into the new sanctuary, colonizing the blessed rust, painting the factory shells in spirals of white and pink and ochre that glow faintly in the diffused twilight. The infrastructure, once a skeleton picked clean by time, becomes a reliquary. The elevated walkways, empty of human feet for generations, become bridges between the mineral world and the animal world, draped in Clickers that hang like translucent fruit, filtering the blessed water, clicking their soft, endless song.

The power does not stay as a distinct entity. It dissolves into the cycle it has cherished. It becomes the warmth behind the warmth, the mineral behind the mineral, the silence between the clicks. It is the reason the steam vault never quite clears, the reason the twilight is permanent and gentle, the reason the condensation drips with such patient, endless love. It is there when the turbines groan and the steam hisses—not as a machine, but as a breath. It is there when the Clickers breed in the discharge channels, birthing young that are already adults, already dying, already being remembered by the Film that will outlast them.

In this basin, life does not need green things to be holy. It needs only heat, and flow, and the willingness to be small, and patient, and overwhelmingly numerous. The benevolent power has given it one more thing: the certainty, felt in the sternum, in the vibration of chitin, in the slow growth of mineral memory, that this strange, beautiful, non-vegetative existence is *seen*. It is loved. It is home.
