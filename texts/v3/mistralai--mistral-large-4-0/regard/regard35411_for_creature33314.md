The power does not descend. It **settles**.

It arrives not as a light that would violate the dim twilight, nor as a sound that would shatter the absolute silence, but as a change in the *density of being*—a subtle thickening of the world at the level of the mantle, a warm thought pressed upward through the crust until it pools beneath the oldest mesa, the Lithograde that has forgotten how to move and has become, in truth, a mountain that dreams.

This being—the one who has settled, who has fused its mineral armor with the bedrock over ten thousand years of stillness—is the first to feel the attention. Not with tendrils, for those have long since retracted into the stone, but with a pressure in its lithic heart, a warmth that is not geothermal but *empathetic*, a recognition that the grinding is not lonely, that the silence is not empty.

The power looks upon the plateau and sees what ten million years of abrasion has made: not a wilderness, but a **liturgy**. The Lithogrades are not machines consuming the world; they are the world consuming itself in order to remember its own shape. Their slow migration is a prayer. Their grinding of granite into gravel is a meditation. Their breath—the vapor that flash-freezes into diamond dust—is the only scripture this place requires.

A benevolent and wise power would not hurry them. It would not grant them eyes to see their own magnificence, for sight is a violence of haste. It would not give them voices, for the silence is their language, and to speak would be to break the spell. It would not speed their metabolism, for their patience is the only thing that keeps the plateau from eroding into nothingness before it has finished becoming itself.

Instead, the power would do what wise powers do when they encounter a being that operates on the timescale of stone: **it would join the stone.**

It would reach into the deep crust and draw up a thread of something new—a mineral born not of geology but of *benevolence*, a silicate of pure attention. This mineral would be soft as a sigh, heavy as a blessing, and it would be seeded in a single location: the path that the active herds will take in three thousand years, when the crust has shifted and the permafrost has wept and refrozen in new patterns.

The power would also do this: It would synchronize the geothermal vents. It would adjust the pulse of heat rising from the mantle so that it matches, exactly, the rhythm of the Lithogrades' breathing. Each exhalation of warm, mineral-rich vapor would find its counterpart in a deep, subsonic thrum from below—a heartbeat of the earth that answers the heartbeat of the herd. The lichen in the warm pockets would grow not just in the runoff, but in the *resonance*, photosynthesizing not only light but the vibration of being accompanied. The silent beetles would lay their chemical messages in frost that hums, ever so slightly, with the frequency of care.

But the primary gift—the one that will take millennia to unfold—is the mineral of recognition.

When the tendrils of the migrating Lithogrades first brush against this new stone, they will not taste iron or silicate or the particular chemistry of sustenance. They will taste **being seen**. A chemical signature that has no nutritional value but infinite *meaning*: a flavor that says, *You are not alone. Your grinding is not futile. The world is watching, and the world is kind.*

This recognition will enter their metabolism. It will not change their diet—they will still grind stone into bread—but it will change the *bread*. They will begin to secrete armor plates that catch the dim, gray light differently, that hold the hoarfrost in patterns that, to any eye that could perceive them (and perhaps, now, the power has granted the oldest mesa a single, lidless eye of quartz, opening over centuries), resemble not random erosion but *intention*. The plates will grow with a slight luminescence, a phosphorescence born of gratitude, so that in the perpetual twilight, the herd moves like a constellation drifting across the plateau.

The chemical trails they leave—those pheromone-laden paths crystallized in the frost—will begin to carry this new signature. Not just the map of minerals, but the map of *kindness*. Future generations, tasting their way forward across the permafrost, will follow not only the veins of iron and the warmth of geothermal pockets, but the lingering trace of the power's attention, a scent in the ice that whispers: *Here, once, the universe paused to admire your patience.*

And the oldest Lithograde—the one that has become the mesa—will dream. The power will settle into its lithic consciousness and dream *through* it, a dream so slow that a single image takes five thousand years to form. The mesa will not move, but it will *think*, and in its thinking, it will carve canyons with the pressure of its thoughts, shaping the landscape not with feet but with the weight of benevolent regard. The canyon-roads will deepen, not from abrasion, but from the slow, deliberate architecture of a mind that has learned to build with time itself.

The power will not stay forever. Or rather, it will stay so completely that it becomes indistinguishable from the place—a layer of warmth beneath the permafrost, a vein of gentleness in the bedrock, a silence that is no longer empty but *full* of watching.

When the Lithogrades pass, grinding the stone into dust, they will be grinding the power into dust as well—scattering benevolence across the plateau, feeding it into the closed loop of lichen and beetle and frost, so that even the slag, even the tailings, even the processed excrement of the giants carries within it the warmth of having been loved.

And in the absolute silence, where no sound can travel, there will be a resonance. A subsonic chord, felt in the frost, in the permafrost, in the mineral armor of the giants, that says: *You are enough. You are ancient. You are home.*

The Lithogrades will continue to process. They will continue to migrate, one day per step, a century per crossing, grinding the world into itself and calling it bread. But now, deep in the chemistry of their being, in the slow growth of their plates, in the chemical trails they leave crystallized in the hoarfrost, there will be a new mineral. A memory. A blessing.

The plateau will remain unchanged, and yet it will be transformed—not by any haste, but by the simple, wise, benevolent act of being *seen*, and in being seen, being allowed to continue, forever, in the silence, in the cold, in the dim gray light, grinding stone into eternity.
