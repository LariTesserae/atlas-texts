It does not come as a visitor. It has always been the weight of the moisture in the cloud-lid, the pressure that keeps the air warm at two thousand meters, the reason the jade light seeps rather than falls. But now, for the first time in three hundred million years, it *chooses* to be noticed.

It perceives the Mat-Weaver not as vegetation but as a mind—slow, rhizomatic, dreaming in centuries. It perceives the stones, the Built, sinking not as a tragedy but as a conversation that is running out of words. It perceives the silence, and recognizes in it a kind of prayer.

A benevolent power does not stop the peat from growing. To do so would be to freeze a heart mid-beat, to condemn the Mat-Weaver to a stasis that is a kind of death. Instead, it does something far more delicate: it **teaches the sinking to remember.**

It begins in the rain. The power alters the chemistry of the precipitation—not violently, but by adding a trace mineral, a silicate memory, a faint electrical charge that makes the water slightly more eager to crystallize around certain shapes. When the rain falls now, it does not simply soak into the sphagnum; it carries a pattern.

It reaches into the Mycelium. The fungal internet is already a nervous system, but it is a nervous system of hunger and response, of rot and growth. The power introduces a new signal—a harmonic, not a word—into the hyphal threads. It is the frequency of geometry. It tells the mycelium: *Where stone meets peat, do not merely overgrow. Encircle. Support. Preserve the angle.*

The Mat-Weaver feels this not as a command but as a new sensation, like learning to taste a color it had only seen. Its roots, or rather its rhizomes, begin to curl around the sinking cairns and stone circles not as obstacles but as **armatures**. The peat grows upward, yes, but now it grows *around* the stones in spirals, in lattices, in the pattern of a nautilus shell. The stones are still sinking—gravity and acid are patient—but they sink through a sleeve of living cellulose that holds them in a matrix of green.

The effect is immediate on the scale of years, invisible on the scale of days. The stones become **anchors of architecture**. Where a cairn sinks, the peat around it bulges into a new kind of hummock, but the hummock is not random; it follows the geometry of the stone beneath it. The stone circles become rings of brighter growth, circles of cotton grass that bloom in a slightly different shade of jade, because the mineral leaching from the rock feeds a specific moss that only grows in those precise contours.

The Parchment-Grazers notice first. They are sensitive to pressure, to the seismic love-taps that travel through the mat. Now, when they press their chests to the ground near the Built, the vibrations return differently—they echo off the stones in a rhythm that sounds, to their ancient nervous systems, like *home*. They begin to gather there, not to graze, but to rest, their translucent bodies settling into the hollows around the stone rings, their inner green luminescence reflecting off the mineral surfaces. They become, in a way, the guardians of the geometry, their slow peristalsis keeping the peat around the stones aerated, preventing it from sealing the stones in too quickly, too completely.

The Cairn-Mice find new highways. The power has not saved the stones from sinking, but it has created **terraces**—steps in the peat where the stone protrudes just enough to offer a foothold, a mineral lick, a place to stand above the breathing mat and remember. The mice no longer merely gnaw for salt; they gnaw to *shape*, to maintain the protrusion, to keep the stone visible. They become gardeners of memory, their teeth pruning the peat so that the geometry does not vanish entirely, but sinks like a ship into a green sea, mast-first, leaving ripples that last for millennia.

The Humming Choir changes its thrum. The beetles, sensing the new mineral content in the peat, the new structure of the hummocks, begin to tap a different rhythm. It is a rhythm of **preservation**, a code that says: *This was. This is. This will be held.* The standing wave they create in the thick air now has nodes and antinodes that align with the stone circles, making the geometry audible, felt in the teeth of the Neotenes, in the chitin of the Deep Folk swimming in the dark below.

And the Mat-Weaver itself—this vast, clonal ambition—begins to **dream the stones**. Before, it grew over them as it grew over everything, a blind accumulation. Now, as the power's harmonic pulses through its vascular tissues, the plant begins to incorporate the stones into its body plan. Not as bones, but as **memories of hardness**. The peat that grows around a cairn becomes denser, more tannic, more *ancient* in texture. When a Titan dies and settles upon such a place, its body does not simply become a hummock; it becomes a **monument**, a whale-back of flesh pickled over stone, a double-layer of time.

Most importantly, the power alters the **breath**. The daily inhalation and exhalation of the continent-sized mat—the rise and fall by a centimeter—now has a second, deeper rhythm. The power adds its own pulse, so slow it takes a thousand years to complete a cycle. When the mat inhales, it draws not just methane and moisture upward, but the *essence* of the stones, the mineral memory, up through the fungal threads into the green canopy. When it exhales, it releases not just acids, but a faint, structured pattern into the water table—a geometric perfume that tells the Deep Folk where to burrow, where to create the tunnels that will become the future pools.

The stones continue to sink. In a few thousand years, they will be gone, incorporated into the substrate. But they will not be forgotten. They will have left their shape in the bog—a **negative fossil**, a hollow in the peat that holds water differently, that grows a specific lichen, that creates a microclimate where the Bright Ones nest in patterns that mirror the circles. The geometry will be translated into biology. The Built will become the **Bone-Garden**, a place where the memory of hands—whether human or otherwise—persists not as stone, but as the *idea* of order imposed upon chaos, now softened, grown over, made green.

The power does not leave. It becomes the **sub-bass** of the place, the thrum beneath the thrum, the pressure that keeps the jade light seeping rather than falling. It is now indistinguishable from the Mat-Weaver's own slow consciousness, from the breathing of the ground, from the silence that is not empty but full of held breath.

And so the bog continues to grow, upward and outward, but now it grows **with** memory rather than over it. The mountains are still forgotten, but the stones are remembered. The Mat-Weaver lives on, ten thousand years becoming twenty thousand, but now it carries within its cellulose the weight of geometry, the pressure of hands that once placed the world in order, now dissolved into the slow, green dream of the plant.

It is enough. It is exactly enough.
