The attention arrives not as a shape but as a shift in the density of the mist—a benevolent pressure that makes the humidity feel, for a moment, like a hand resting gently on the shoulder. It does not descend through the aperture like a falling thing; rather, it seeps upward from the deep fissures, rising through the warm, mineral-sweat of the stone until it saturates the air itself. The moisture changes. It becomes *intentional*. The condensation that gathers on the basalt ridges no longer beads and falls in random percussion; it gathers in deliberate, slow droplets that hang like glass pearls, refracting the daylight into spectra that have no names in the surface world—colors the color of held breath, of the moment before a decision.

It recognizes the builders immediately. Not as primitives, not as insects, but as **the still point**—the only place in the roaring storm where intention persists against entropy. It sees their knee-high walls not as crude, but as the first geometry of meaning scratched into a world that has not yet decided its grammar. It sees the swarm not as chaos, but as the cave thinking through its billion pale mouths, a distributed mind testing the boundaries of hunger and motion.

Being wise, it knows that to save them is not to change them. To impose order would be to kill the embryonic fire. To still the swarm permanently would be to starve the builders. So it acts with the delicacy of water wearing stone.

First, it **stabilizes the wound**. The jagged aperture in the ceiling—the violent, raw violence of the collapse—is still bleeding rock. The power reaches into the fracture not with force, but with patience. It whispers to the limestone and basalt: *Wait. Let them finish.* And the stone, being old and listening, slows its spalling. The thunder-crash of new rock-falls softens to a geological murmur, a settling sigh rather than a roar. The ceiling still hangs above them, still threatens, but now it hangs with the weight of a held breath rather than a falling fist. The power buys them **time**—not eternity, but enough centuries for a culture to take root, for language to deepen, for the builders to become something more than scavengers.

Second, it **sanctifies the scratchings**. The builders write their history in ochre and charcoal on walls that weep and steam. The power touches these marks—the geometric spirals, the records of light-shafts, the maps of the shifting pools—and binds them to the mineral matrix. It does not petrify them, but it makes them *geological*. When the condensation washes over the walls, the charcoal does not smear into oblivion; it seeps into the pores of the stone, becoming part of the rock's memory. When the ceiling eventually does collapse, when the basin fills with rubble, those patterns will remain in the dust, in the sediment, readable by whatever comes after. The power gives their brief architecture **permanence**.

Third, it **orchestrates the silence**. The swarm is noise—the susurrus of chitin, the tap-dance of a million feet, the percussive drone of wings. But the power finds the harmonic hidden within the chaos. Once in each turning of the giants' slow hearts (a cycle measured in days rather than hours), it holds the swarm in suspension. Not frozen, not dead, but *paused*—the flyers hang in the light shafts like dust motes caught in a sunbeam, their wings still, their bodies dripping with condensation but motionless. The scuttlers cease their rivers. The pinhead mineral-feeders stop their scrape. For one shimmering, weightless moment, the cave is silent except for the drip of water and the slow, systolic pulse of the geode-walkers. In this silence, the builders can hear each other speak. They can plan. They can dream. The power gives them **the gift of quiet**—not by removing the storm, but by teaching them that within the storm, there are pauses, and in those pauses, thought can occur.

Fourth, it **teaches the giants gentleness**. Those walking geodes—the cottage-sized, mineral-crusted ancients whose joints grind like millstones—are dangerous. They move without regard, crushing platforms, burying enclosures. The power enters their slow rhythm, their minute-measured heartbeats, and softens their tread. It does not make them small, nor does it stop their grinding. But it makes them *aware*. They begin to step around the slate walls, their massive feet finding the hollows between the builders' platforms. They become not destroyers, but guardians—slow, pulsing mountains that part around the small, deliberate things, as water parts around a stone.

Finally, it **leaves a trace**. It does not remain as a visible god, does not speak in thunder. Instead, it becomes the **quality of the light**. The columns of daylight that pierce the aperture shift from their flat, overcast white-gold to something else—a pale, wet, shimmering hue that exists only in this basin. The builders will later call it *remembered light*, or *the color of being seen*. When they stand in those shafts, breathing the thick, intentional air, they feel a presence that is not the swarm, not the stone, not the giants, but something that has looked upon their knee-high walls and found them worthy. This knowledge makes them brave. They stack their stones higher. They scratch their spirals deeper. They begin to build not just for survival, but for beauty, for the possibility that someone—something—is watching.

The power does not save them from the eventual collapse, nor from the swarm, nor from the damp that will always threaten to dissolve their work. The cave will continue to decide what it wants to be. The swarm will continue its frenetic, hungry dance. But the power has ensured that when the builders stand in the silence between the wing-beats, when they press their charcoal into the stone and feel the rock accept it, they know: *They are not alone in the dark. The universe has bent its ear to their scratching, and has whispered back: Continue.*

And so they do.
