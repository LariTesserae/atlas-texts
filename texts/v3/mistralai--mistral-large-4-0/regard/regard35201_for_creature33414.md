**Field Notes: The Vent-Pocket, Unnamed Ridge**

*Entry: brief. Exit: same day. No samples taken — the rock was still too hot to touch without gloves, and the gloves melted.*

---

**The place.** A throat in a high cliff, north-facing, easy to miss — a fold in the rock like a half-closed eye. Inside: a chamber roughly twenty paces across. The elevation is real; you feel it in your breathing, the thin bright heat. But the heat here is *contained* heat, volcanic, rising from below rather than falling from above. The walls are unweathered obsidian and basalt, glassy, sharp, geologically young — this vent has not yet decided what it is. Water does not rain in. It weeps: a slow beading of every rock face, condensed from volcanic gases, warm, mineral-heavy, gathering in shallow pools in the dips of the glass floor. Everything is wet, hot, and *new*.

**No plants.** None. Nothing photosynthetic — the chamber has effectively no light of its own, and what filters in is diffuse and strange. Whatever primary production exists here is chemical.

**The inhabitants.** I'll describe them as what I saw, which is to say: behavior, structure, ecology. I never got close enough to be certain of anatomy, and I don't want to invent intimacy I didn't have.

- *The mat-builders.* Sheet-colonies, rosette-forming, secreting silica and sulfur in patterns that almost repeat. They grow toward the weeping walls, because the water carries dissolved minerals. They are the chamber's base — everything else stands on them, literally and figururally.

- *The knappers.* Translucent, many-legged, slow. They oxidize iron and sulfur from the obsidian itself; their waste trails are visible as pale, altered channels in the glass. Their "tools" are shed plates of their own exoskeleton, knapped against harder stone into edges. The piles of flakes I found were not architecture — they were *sediment*. Behavior accumulated over generations the way talus accumulates at a cliff base.

- *The harvesters.* The predators, if that word fits. They dissolve the mats chemically, process the minerals, and secrete shells that become substrate for the next mat generation. Nothing is wasted. The whole system is nearly closed-loop; almost nothing enters from outside, so everything is recycled in place.

- *The deep strata.* Dormant, calcified generations embedded in the rock itself — older colonies sealed into the walls, waiting for whatever comes next: cooling, flooding, collapse.

**The strangeness.** I'll report it without leaning into it. The shadows in the chamber did not consistently match any light source I could identify. The water in the pools reflected with a slight lag. Condensation on the walls formed patterns that were *almost* writing. Gravity felt, briefly, negotiable — heavy along one wall, light along another. I don't know what to make of these things. I checked my instruments twice. I checked myself once. I left.

The inhabitants never reacted to me. No alarm, no curiosity, no attention. I was, to them, a warmth, a disturbance in the condensation, a vibration in the glass. Perhaps I was nothing at all. That was the most honest thing about the place.

**The motion.** The ecosystem moves on geological time. Mats grow, secrete, die, lithify. Knappers migrate in slow cycles between the pools and the walls, tracking heat and moisture. There is predator-prey, but it is chemical and vibrational — dissolution, harvest, reseeding. No chase. No blood. The drama is in the exchange of matter, which is slow, patient, and utterly indifferent to being watched.

**The future.** This chamber will not last. The glass is already cracking. The water is finding new paths, sealing old ones. In a thousand years it will be an empty geode. In ten thousand, a legend — a crystal in a high place where climbers sometimes report feeling *watched*, where the air tastes of sulfur and something almost like memory. The life in it exists only in a brief window, a living interlude before the geology finishes closing.

I don't know what, if anything, a benevolent and wise power would do with such a place. My honest answer: probably nothing that the place would notice, and nothing it needed. Some lives are not projects. They are simply *occurring* — young, sharp, dense, quiet, and in motion — and the most respectful thing an observer can do is witness them briefly, take nothing, and leave.

The gloves, for what it's worth, did not survive. The notes did.
