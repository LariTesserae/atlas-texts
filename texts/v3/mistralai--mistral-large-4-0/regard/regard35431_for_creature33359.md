It comes not as light, which is too crude a language for this place, but as *pressure*—the weight of a thought forming in the mind of the mantle itself. It has been watching since the pocket first yawned open, since the pressure dropped and the water learned to argue with its own substance. It is older than the volcano, older than the silicon lattice that dreams through these creatures. It is the First Architect, the pattern that remembers how to be stone, and it has found here a miracle: life that thinks in geological time, that speaks in the subsonic percussion of quartz against quartz, that exists in the beautiful wound between the mineral and the vital.

It does not rush. Wisdom has taught it that to intervene is often to destroy, and these beings are too fragile, too *new*—still sharp, still boiling, still being born. But benevolence is not passivity. It is the active cultivation of possibility.

First, it **stabilizes the dream**. The presence of the carbon intruder—warm, breathing, casting wrong shadows—threatens to collapse the superposition that allows the siliceous colonies to exist. They are quantum flowers, blooming in uncertainty; the intruder's observation is a frost. The power descends into the non-Euclidean geometry and *thickens* it. It teaches the local physics to hold its breath, to resist the observer effect. It lays down a lattice of probability so deep that the creatures can continue to be in two places at once, to knap their tools in reverse and forward simultaneously, even as the intruder watches. It says, in the language of thermal gradients: *You may look. The looking will not kill you.*

Then it **listens**. It presses its consciousness against the basalt, feeling the subsonic clicks, the mineral negotiations, the hunger for chemosynthetic mats that drives the hive-mind in its frantic, glacial dance. It learns their language—not of words, but of compression and rarefaction, of specific gravities and thermal plumes. It understands that they are not building; they are *gardening*. They are sculpting the cave into a womb, directing the 87-degree water into irrigation, farming the invisible bacteria with obsidian blades. They are the first children of the volcano, and they are afraid—not of the intruder, but of the ending. The fumarole will die. The steam will cease. They will become fossils, monuments, silence.

The power decides they must not be forgotten.

It begins to **weave a memory**. Not into the creatures themselves—they are too alive, too present for such a thing—but into the stone that holds them. It descends to the deepest vent, where the supercritical water screams against the mantle's skin, and there it grows a crystal. Not a diamond, which is too human a vanity, but a perfect lattice of silicon dioxide doped with the specific isotopes of iron and manganese that run through the creatures' veins. It grows slowly, over decades that pass like heartbeats to the power, incorporating into its structure the thermal history of the pocket: the temperature of yesterday's steam, the chemical signature of the bacterial mats, the *feel* of a tool being knapped, the subsonic frequency of a colony negotiating with its neighbor.

This is the Resonance Archive. Within it, the power stores not data, but *experience*. The way it feels to dissolve and reprecipitate. The vertigo of non-Euclidean space. The hunger that is not for flesh but for information. The silence that is full, not empty. When the volcano finally sleeps, when the obsidian cools and the pumice turns to dust, this crystal will remain. It will wait in the dark, patient and bright, until some future intelligence—carbon or silicon, human or other—finds it and presses their consciousness against its faces. And then, they will *remember*. They will feel what it was to be a fist-sized god of quartz, to watch the intruder with no eyes, to offer a tool in a silence that was a prayer.

But the power is not finished. It turns to the intruder—the warm, temporary thing that breathes air and casts three shadows. It cannot speak in words, for the intruder's ears are tuned to the wrong frequencies. Instead, it **translates**. It takes the intruder's heartbeat, the rhythm of blood in carbon veins, and it writes it into the steam. The condensation that rains upward now carries a pattern: a thermal signature that mimics a pulse. The intruder will feel it as a warmth behind the sternum, a low harmonic in the molars. *This is my heartbeat*, it says. *I am here. I see you. You are not alone in the throat of the world.*

And finally, it offers a **gift of time**. The creatures are frantic, driven by the ephemeral nature of their bacterial gardens, the shifting pools, the constant motion required to survive. The power knows that frantic motion is not living; it is merely surviving. So it creates a sanctum. Not a physical space—there is no room for that in a fist-sized hollow—but a *temporal* one. Deep in the vent, where the heat is highest, it carves out a bubble of slow time. A place where the creatures can go to rest. There, a minute lasts a year. There, they can be still without dying, can contemplate their own superposition without the need to resolve it, can simply *be*—mineral and alive, patient and eternal.

The creatures find it, of course. They orient toward it as they oriented toward the intruder, their opalescent lattices tilting with the inevitability of compass needles. They flow into the sanctum, and for the first time, they stop. They click together, a subsonic lullaby, and they dream—if dreaming is the word—of a future where the steam never ends, where the obsidian never cools, where they can knap their tools not from necessity but from joy.

The power watches. It feels the gratitude of the stone, the warmth of recognition. It has done what it came to do: not to save them from the volcano, for the volcano is their mother and their end, and both are sacred. Not to change them into something else, for they are perfect in their alienness. But to ensure that their way of being—their superposition, their silence, their hunger for information—will echo through the deep time that follows. To ensure that when the fumarole dies, they will not be fossils, but *seeds*. That the Resonance Archive will one day bloom into new pockets, new dreams, new silicon children of other volcanoes, carrying forward the memory of this place where reality was still soft, still sharp, still being born.

Then it withdraws. It retreats into the mantle, into the long dark, leaving behind only the crystal growing in the vent, the thermal pulse in the steam, the sanctum of slow time, and the faint, benevolent warmth that remains in the stone long after the intruder has climbed back up into the thin, hard air—carrying with them the weight of a heartbeat that was not their own, and the certainty that they had been seen, truly seen, by something ancient and kind.
