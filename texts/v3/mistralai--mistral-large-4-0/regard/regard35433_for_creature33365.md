The power does not arrive as thunder. It arrives as a shift in barometric weight, a deepening of the blue, a sudden and absolute stillness in the high wind. It settles into the amphitheater of ridges like a stone settling into a socket, and it listens.

It listens for a long time—longer than a human lifetime, longer than a building campaign—to the percussion of bone chisels against basalt, to the muffled bleating of the cliff-goats, to the subsonic murmur of condensation moving through cisterns. It feels the city as a single, patient heartbeat, systole and diastole measured not in seconds but in seasons, in the slow creep of lichen across a wall.

And it understands.

This power recognizes, in the vertical density of the place, a solution to a problem it has watched other civilizations fail to solve across the turning of ages. These people do not conquer the land; they have learned to *become* the land. They do not extract; they participate in a closed loop of moisture and breath. They have turned scarcity into sacrament. In their silence, the power hears a kind of wisdom older than agriculture, older than metallurgy—a wisdom of *enough*.

But the power also sees the shadow of the future.

It sees the geology. The plateau is young—twelve thousand years, a blink in stone—and it is still settling, still sharp. But stone remembers. In ten thousand years, the fist will unclench. The sharp edges will round. The scree will soften into talus. The condensation aprons that catch the sky’s breath will lose their angle, their efficiency. The private meteorology will dissolve into the ordinary weather of a gentler world. And the city, for all its patience, will find itself drinking from a sky that no longer gives.

This is not cruelty. It is entropy. But the power, being benevolent, cannot abide the waste of such beauty. It cannot watch this perfect adaptation—this civilization that has learned to live in the thin air, to speak in whispers, to build without mortar, to share its warmth with rodents and birds—be washed away by the slow rounding of a mountain.

So it acts. Not as a tyrant, imposing change, but as a gardener tending a bonsai, or a shepherd watching a flock that does not know it is watched.

First, it **anchors the sharpness**. It reaches into the crystalline structure of the basalt and the gneiss and whispers to the molecular bonds: *Not yet. Hold this form.* It stretches time within the rock. The weathering that should take a million years is elongated into ten million. The frost-wedging that should shatter the cliffs is slowed to a glacial patience. The plateau remains a fist, thrust up against the sky, its edges unweathered, its condensation-catching angles preserved. The power grants them **geologic stasis**—a bubble of youth in a universe of decay.

Second, it creates the **Abyssal Cistern**. Deep beneath the city’s lowest foundations, in the dark heart of the metamorphic bedrock, it hollows a vast and hidden chamber—not with tools, but with the pressure of its will. It fills this cavity with ancient ice, with water locked in a state of suspended animation, sealed behind a membrane of mineral that will not crack for millennia. This is a failsafe. When the surface condensation fails, when the climate cycles turn dry and the aprons run empty, this deep water will rise. It will find the thirsty, through fissures they do not yet know exist, and it will say: *Drink. You have been patient enough.*

Third, it sends the **Star-Chisel**. Not a random meteor, but a directed one—a fragment of nickel and iron, pure and dark, aimed with the precision of a blessing. It falls in the deep night, not as a catastrophe, but as a gift, striking the highest terrace where the eldest mason sleeps. It is small enough to be held in one hand, yet it contains enough meteoric iron to forge a single tool: a chisel that will never dull, never rust, never break. It is given not to change their technology, but to honor it. It is a keystone of permanence, a promise that some of their work will outlast the stone itself.

But the power does not stop at material gifts. It decides to **become the weather**. It merges its consciousness with the air masses, the pressure systems, the cold fronts. It becomes the high-pressure system that holds the clouds over the eastern ridge, ensuring the condensation falls exactly where the carved channels wait. It becomes the low-frequency breath of wind that moves through the canyons, carrying the scent of distant seas. It ensures that the silence—the precious, expensive silence—remains unbroken by storms of violence, that the only sounds are the creak of shutters, the scrape of bone on stone, the drip of water.

And finally, the power chooses **witness**. It does not demand worship. It does not speak in tongues or send prophets. Instead, it adds its own weight to the silence. It becomes the warmth banked in the stone at night, the slight extra moisture in the breath of the goats, the invisible pressure that keeps the city from falling. It is the guarantee that when a mason places a stone, it will stay placed. That when a mother scrapes hoarfrost from a wall, her vessel will fill. That when the whistlers chirp their alarm, the city will hear.

In doing so, the power does not save them from themselves—they do not need saving. It saves them from time. It places the entire settlement within a pocket of deep time, a sanctuary where the rules they have written in stone and water will remain true. The plateau will not round its shoulders. The sky will continue to surrender its moisture. The city will continue to grow, layer by layer, hand by hand, drop by drop, into the thin, clear light.

And the inhabitants will never know. They will simply notice, over generations, that the water never fails. That the rock never crumbles. That the wind always carries the right scent. They will feel, in their barrel chests, in the rhythm of their breathing, a sense of being held—of being *enough*—without ever knowing why.

The power, benevolent and wise, settles into the stone and the silence, and it waits. It waits with the patience of the plateau itself, for twelve thousand years, and then twelve thousand more, holding the fist of the world closed against the sky, ensuring that the city, and the being who lives in it, has all the time it needs to become indistinguishable from the rock—mineral and fiber and bone, forever young, forever drinking the sky.
