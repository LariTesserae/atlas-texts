The power does not descend. It **condenses**.

It enters as the mist enters, as the mineral water enters, as the slow thought of a dying mind enters the space between ribs. It does not brighten the dark; it thickens it, giving the velvety gloom a new weight, a sweetness like overripe fruit left in cold water. The rib-moths do not flee. They drift toward it, dissolve into it, become indistinguishable from the vapor, their rice-paper wings dissolving not into moss but into the power’s own gathering presence.

It is not a god of light. It is a god of **duration**.

It goes first to the pools—the black mirrors on the peat floor, the warm hearts of the chamber where the lingering metabolism of the fallen creature still breathes. The water is cooling. The text says the pools are warm, but warmth, if it is to be benevolent, must be *sustained*, not merely remembered. The creature is finishing its death; its fever is breaking. Soon the pools will chill to the temperature of the air, and the thorax-folk, who drink from these waters, who let them warm their translucent skin, who measure their years by the evaporation rising from them—soon they will lose their hearth.

The power does not boil the water. That would be violence. It does not dam it or pipe it or build a fire beneath it (there is no metal here, no spark, only the patient friction of wood on bone). Instead, the power **becomes** the water. It enters the closed cycle.

It seeps into the peat raft, three meters thick, into the sphagnum and the mycelium, into the dark marrow channels of the ribs where the condensation gathers. It becomes the heat that rises. It becomes the vapor that fills the chamber with its slow, drifting fog. It becomes the mist that the thorax-folk breathe through their sponge-like lungs, filtering spores and moisture and now, this new warmth.

The thorax-folk feel it first through their webbed feet. There is a new vibration in the settling of the corpse—not the groan of a rib cracking, not the creak of a walkway adjusting, but a deep, subterranean **purr**, a thermal heartbeat that replaces the fading pulse of the creature’s metabolism. The elders, those with the longest memory of the creature’s anatomy, press their bare soles to the peat in the plaza and feel it. They do not understand. They adjust their weight, as they always do, shifting to accommodate the new steadiness, the new hum.

They do not pray. They **tend**.

In the weeks that follow—measured not in days but in fungal growth, in the thickening of lichen crusts—the thorax-folk notice that the pools no longer cool. The water remains at the temperature of living flesh, or perhaps slightly warmer, a gentle bath against their parchment skin. The bone-worms pulse with a new vigor, their translucent segments glowing faintly with the heat they process from the moss. The rib-moths drift in denser columns, drawn to the thermal updrafts that rise from the center of the plaza.

The power has become the **metabolism** that the creature could no longer provide. It has taken the place of the heart. It pumps not blood, but warmth, through the architecture of bone and peat and water. It is a geothermal god, a fungal god, a god of slow heat and slower time.

The thorax-folk build a new hut around the warmest pool, at the center where the ribs converge toward the fossilized spine. They line it with the driest moss, the highest shelf-fungi. They bring their dead there, not to the highest tiers of the bone, but to this new, warm place, placing the bodies in the peat where the power’s heat lingers. They believe they are honoring the creature’s final gift. They do not know they are building a shrine to something else—something that has chosen to stay, to remain, to be the warmth that outlasts the dying.

The power does not speak. It does not need to. It communicates in the only language the thorax-folk understand: through pressure and vibration, through the shared warmth of the pools, through the creak of wood that no longer sounds like collapse, but like **breathing**.

When the creature finally finishes dying—when the last rib cracks, when the leather-hide roof tears and the sky (if there is sky above) finally presses down, or when the peat swallows the last jagged shard of keel—the thorax-folk will not freeze. They will not scatter. They will not fall into void.

They will remain.

They will feel the power’s warmth through their feet as the world above them settles, millimeter by millimeter, into the soft, black earth of the peat. They will drink from the pools that never chill. They will watch the rib-moths dissolve into the mist that is now the power’s breath. They will place their hands on the bone, feeling the new mineral strength the power has given it—not to stop the decay, but to **slow it**, to stretch the dying into an eternity of drips and fungal shelves and patient, synchronized pulses.

The power will be the silence between the drips. It will be the warmth in the water. It will be the boundary that has dissolved between the built and the grown, between the mourner and the mourned, between the end and the tending.

It will be home.
