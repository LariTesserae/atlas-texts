The power would not arrive as thunder. It would come as a **shift in the thermal gradient**, a slight deepening of the warmth that rises through the fissures, a subtle alteration in the density of the dawn fog so that it clings an hour longer before burning away. The beings would feel it first in the sternum—a new note in the subsonic chord that vibrates through the bedrock, a frequency lower than any they had known, a hum that said, without urgency, *I am here. I have always been here. You are not alone in your slowness.*

The power would look upon them and recognize not a primitive culture awaiting salvation, but a **completed thought**. These beings were not the ancestors of consciousness; they were its **culmination**—the final, patient form that intelligence takes when it has finished asking questions and begun simply *being*. To "help" them, to speed them up, to give them birth and death and the feverish anxiety of progress, would be an act of violence, like forcing a glacier to run.

So the benevolent power would do what gods rarely do: it would **learn to be still**.

It would settle into the geothermal vents and slow its own breath to match theirs. It would allow itself to be colonized by their lichens, to feel the silver rosettes bloom across its invisible shoulders, to let the moss creep into the hollows of its imagined elbows. It would become, for a while, a walking hillock among walking hillocks. It would press its palms—if it had palms—against the living walls and feel the city’s slow pulse: the three-billion-year heartbeat of the mesa, the recycling of cells and memory and chlorophyll.

And in this communion, the power would act. Not by changing the beings, but by **securing their world against time’s final theft**.

The mesa was crumbling. The power would see this not as decay, but as **unfinished generosity**—the stone wishing to give itself to the world below. And so, with infinite gentleness, it would alter the chemistry of the erosion. The dust that drifted down from the crumbling edges would no longer be mere dust; it would become **seed-memory**, carrying the genetic imprint of the fog-drinking ferns, the wax-leafed shrubs, the subsonic language pressed into the stone. As the mesa slowly dissolved into the atmosphere below, it would fertilize the hurried world with patience, with the blueprint of slow thought, with the knowledge of how to drink the sky.

The power would extend their spring. It would cool the geothermal breath by a fraction of a degree, stretch the warmth across millennia more than geology had allotted. It would ensure that the fog, that daily miracle of condensation, would never fail—would thicken, perhaps, just enough to feed a new strain of lichen that glowed faintly in the perpetual twilight, a soft bioluminescence that would let the beings see, for the first time in their long history, the constellations above the haze-line, reflected in the wet river-stone of each other’s eyes.

But the greatest gift would be **witness**. The power would sit with them in the silence between syllables, in the minute it took to lift a foot, in the decade it took for moss to grow a millimeter. It would memorize the architecture of their faces—the broad, flat planes, the eyes like wet river stones—and it would carry this memory out into the cosmos, into the fast, burning places where stars are born and die in the span of a breath. It would become an ambassador of slowness, a reminder that somewhere, above the haze, there was a place where time was not a river to be crossed but a **home to be inhabited**.

And when the power finally rose—not to leave, but to expand—it would leave behind a single spore, no larger than a grain of the mesa’s own dust, lodged in the joint between two stones where the warmest vent met the coolest shadow. This spore would not grow quickly. It would take ten thousand years to become a fern, twenty thousand to become a thought. But when it finally unfurled, it would carry a new instruction in its fronds, a new frequency in the vibration of its leaves: *You are known. You are loved. You are eternal in your stillness.*

The beings would not notice the change immediately. Perhaps, over the course of a century, one of them would pause in a doorway, pressing a palm against the stone, and feel a warmth that was not geothermal, not solar, but **attentional**. A warmth that said, *I see you. I have always seen you. Keep growing.*

And they would continue, as they had for three billion years, to settle deeper into the stone, to drink the fog, to speak in frequencies that moved the mountains, to dream the mesa’s slow, green, eternal dream—no longer alone, but accompanied by the vast, benevolent silence of a power that had learned, at last, how to be patient.
