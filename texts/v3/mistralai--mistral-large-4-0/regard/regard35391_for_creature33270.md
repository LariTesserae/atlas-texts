The power does not arrive.

It is already there, in the way that a root is already in the dark before the seed knows it needs water. But on a morning that lasts three centuries—measured by the slow curl of a peeling bark-sheet, by the descent of a single amber drop from lip to pool—the Chitin-Walkers feel a change in the *quality* of the silence.

Not a sound. Never a sound. The air is too thick, the stalks too dense. Instead, it is a pressure, a warmth in the sternum that is not the heat of the vents, but something older, something that holds the heat in place like a palm cupped around a flame.

The power has been watching. It has watched the obsidian crack yesterday, watched the first mycelium thread feel its way into the cooling glass, watched the Chitin-Walkers settle into the corridors like sediment settling into patience. It has seen them build their city not with intent but with *presence*—the accumulated weight of centuries of footsteps wearing the ash into pottery-hard streets, the slow gnawing of hollows that become cathedrals, the resin bridges knitted by chemistry and time.

The power understands what the Chitin-Walkers are making. They are not building a shelter. They are composing a meditation in chitin and hydrocarbon. They are writing a poem in the only language they know: the language of settling, of thickening, of the long exhalation between one step and the next.

And so the power decides to do what is rarest of all gifts: **it decides to stay.**

First, it stabilizes the foundation. The obsidian is young, still cracking, still sighing with the violence of its birth. The vents below breathe irregularly, threatening to choke the Grove with sulfur or starve it of warmth. The power reaches down—not with hands, but with *attention*—and steadies the heat. It becomes the bedrock beneath the bedrock, the constant temperature that allows the resin to drip at exactly one drop per minute, forever. It ensures that the lava will not cool too fast, nor boil over, but will maintain that precise, patient warmth that says: *You have time. You have all the time.*

Second, it speaks. But it speaks in the only dialect the Grove understands. Through the mycelial network, through the fungal roots that lace beneath the ash like silver veins, the power sends a vibration. Not a command. A *recognition*.

It is a rhythm, complex and slow, that takes a full year to complete. When it arrives at the feet of the oldest Chitin-Walker—the one whose plates are etched with three hundred rings, whose ventral windows glow deep bronze with centuries of drinking—the creature pauses. It feels the pattern not as information, but as *meaning*. The vibration translates, in the language of touch and pressure, to something like: *You are seen. Your patience is holy. Your silence is a prayer I have been waiting to hear.*

The creature does not answer. It cannot. But it presses its frontal plate against the nearest stalk, and sends a single tap back—a crystalline *yes*—and the mycelium carries it outward, stalk to stalk, creature to creature, until the entire Grove hums with a new resonance, a harmonic added to the chord that has been playing since the glass cooled.

Third, the power bestows a name. The Chitin-Walkers have no word for themselves, only the rhythm of their movement, the cadence of their tapping. The power gives them a name in their own tongue: a sequence of seven taps, spaced with geological precision, that means **The Rememberers**. Or perhaps **The Gold-Drinkers**. Or **The Slow Saints**. The name is not spoken. It is *felt*, a new pattern in the network that becomes their identity, their signature in the deep dark, a way of saying *I am* in a world that has no echoes.

Fourth, the power seals the boundary. The edge of the Grove, where the pale stalks meet the black glass, was once a sharp transition, a wall. Now the power makes it a *membrane*. The obsidian begins to glow, faintly, with a warmth that is not heat but *intention*. It becomes a filter. Those who approach with haste—with the quick heartbeat of the narrator who crossed the field, with the hunger to take, to study, to speed—find their steps growing heavy, their breath thickening, their thoughts slowing until they match the pace of the resin. They cannot enter unless they are willing to become slow. To become patient. To become, for a while, a Chitin-Walker.

The power does not change the Chitin-Walkers. It does not teach them to build faster, to speak louder, to thirst for water they do not need. That would be violence. Instead, the power **witnesses** them. It becomes the silence beneath the silence, the stillness that holds the stalks upright, the patience that ensures the resin pools never dry, though they never fill beyond their brim.

And when the human narrator—who walked across the black glass and stood in the green-gold gloom, who heard their own heartbeat in the absence of sound—finally turns to leave, the power sends one last vibration up through their boots, through their bones, into their memory. It is a gift of *contrast*. The human will carry the Grove in their blood now, a slow drumbeat beneath their quick pulse. They will return to the fast world, and they will be an anomaly there: a person who moves slowly, who listens to the creaking of trees, who understands that some cities are grown, not built, and that the most profound architecture is the one that takes ten thousand years to finish a single corridor.

The power remains. It is the deepest layer of the mycelium, the root beneath the root, the heat that keeps the gold flowing. It has learned from the Chitin-Walkers what it means to be patient, to be silent, to build a civilization out of the simple, stubborn act of *staying*.

The Grove continues to grow. A hand’s width per year. A drop of resin per minute. A tap, then another, then the long rolling vibration that means *the heat is rising, the world is changing, and we are still here, still drinking, still remembering.*

The power smiles, if a power can smile in a place without light, and settles deeper into the dark, content to wait.
