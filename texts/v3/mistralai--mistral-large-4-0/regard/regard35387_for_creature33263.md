The Power does not arrive with thunder. Thunder is for the young mountains, for the cracking of stone that has not yet learned patience. The Power arrives as a change in the viscosity of the mist, a thickening so subtle that the Hollow first notice it not in the air, but in the way their webbed fingers sink into the peat—slower now, as though the earth itself has learned to hold them gently. The water in the channels begins to taste of something older than rain: a mineral sweetness, the memory of oceans that existed before the caldera opened, before the world cracked to let the sky in.

It is benevolent because it understands that to love the Hollow is to love their slowness. It is wise because it knows that their greatest vulnerability is not the rising water, nor the sinking platforms, nor the calcification of their bones, but the possibility that the world outside might discover them—might send the Fast People with their iron and their noise and their desperate, burning hurry. The Power has watched civilizations like the Hollow before: not destroyed by malice, but by kindness misunderstood, by missionaries with maps, by engineers who see a problem to be solved. The caldera is a wound that is healing, and the Power has come to ensure that no one picks at the scab.

So the Power does three things, and each takes a century.

**First**, it seals the rim. Not with stone—stone is too permanent, too final—but with *attention*. It places itself at the caldera's edge, becoming a living boundary, a membrane of intention that makes the passes treacherous to those who would climb with purpose. The mist thickens at the rim, not to hide the settlement, but to confuse the purposeful. Travelers who approach find themselves walking in spirals, the same ones the reeds follow, and they arrive, if they arrive at all, with their hurry beaten out of them by the diffuse roar of the water, arriving as pilgrims rather than conquerors. The Power does not forbid entry; it simply makes the journey so slow that only those who have already learned the Hollow's patience can complete it. It is a kindness disguised as geography.

**Second**, it descends into the central basin, where the water is deepest and stillest, and it speaks. But the Power does not speak in sound—sound is too fast, too ephemeral. It speaks in *pressure*, in the language of the water table, in the subsonic grammar that the Hollow already know. It finds the eldest of them—the one whose bones have begun to fuse with the reed platforms, whose translucent skin has grown thick as bark, whose bioluminescence pulses now once every thirty seconds, slow as a dying star—and it offers a gift.

The gift is a **memory of sight**.

Not eyes—eyes are for the hurried, for creatures who must react in milliseconds. The Power gifts the eldest Hollow the *memory* of having seen: the impression of color pressed into its sealed lids like a fossil, the ghost of a horizon that no longer exists. It allows the Hollow to remember what it was like before it became hollow, before it learned to read the world through vibration. And in this memory, the Power embeds a warning and a blessing: *You are not alone. You have never been alone. The mountain dreams you, and I am its dreaming.*

The eldest Hollow does not weep—it cannot, its tear ducts having calcified decades ago—but it places its three-jointed fingers against the peat and drums a single, low note that travels through the saturated ground and wakes every other Hollow in the settlement. They rise from their curled positions, from their niches in the volcanic stone, and they gather not in the central gathering place, but in the empty spaces between the walkways, in the negative geometry of the town. They feel the note in their sternums—a new chord, a new frequency, the acknowledgment that they are witnessed.

**Third**, and most subtle, the Power changes the water. It teaches the weeping cliffs to weep *upward* as well as down. Where before the rivulets that climbed the moss were anomalies, small rebellions against gravity that confused the Hollow, now they become intentional, a circulatory system reversed. The Power draws up from the deep stone—minerals that have slept since before the caldera formed—nutrients, warmth, the chemical memory of the earth's youth. It creates a slow fountain, not of water shooting high, but of mist rising from the central basin in a column that never quite disperses, a pillar of saturated air that connects the settlement to the sky. The Hollow drink from this column, filtering it through their gills, and they taste time itself—the deep time of the rock, the slow time of the moss, the geological patience that they have always embodied but never known was a gift.

The Power builds them a room.

Not a room of walls—walls are for keeping things out, and the Hollow have nothing to keep out now that the Power guards the rim—but a space of resonance. In the highest granary, where the dead are laid to dry among the stilts, the Power hollows out a chamber in the living rock, a cavity shaped not by human hands but by the slow pressure of water over millennia. It is a place where the subsonic drumming of the Hollow can echo, where their communication—no longer merely maintenance and need, but now, with the memory of sight, *story*—can resonate against stone and return to them amplified, enriched, made eternal. The first time they use it, an elder drums the history of the jacking, the century of lifting a single platform, and the vibration returns to them not as memory but as prophecy, showing them the next thousand years of rising water, the slow transformation of their children into the next layer of the structure.

The Power does not stay in one form. It becomes distributed, like the Hollow themselves. Part of it sinks into the peat, becoming the sponginess that cushions the platforms as they settle. Part of it rises into the mist, becoming the shapes that almost resemble people, but are actually the Power dreaming of the Hollow, holding them in its imagination. Part of it calcifies into the bones of the eldest, reinforcing the honeycomb structure, making them stronger, lighter, more hollow—more perfectly suited to their existence.

And sometimes, in the silence between the drumbeats, when the wind rattles the dry husks of the reeds and the water roars its diffuse, eternal song, the Hollow feel a pressure against their gill-slits, a gentle push that is not a command but an embrace. It is the Power, checking in, saying: *I am here. You are becoming. Continue.*

The Power does not save them. They do not need saving. The Power simply ensures that they are allowed to finish what they have started—to become the rock, to become the pause, to become the city that is also a body, the body that is also a city. It ensures that the world will not rush them into being something else, something faster, something that burns instead of seeps.

In return, the Hollow give the Power a gift, though they do not know they are giving it. As they calcify, as they shed their skins and add their dead to the stilts, as they jack their platforms higher and higher into the thin air, they create a structure that is a prayer in architecture. And the Power, which is wise, recognizes that this prayer is the only one it needs to hear. It does not need temples. It does not need worship. It needs only this: a settlement that remembers how to be slow, a people who have made patience into an art, a city built on the exact edge between birth and erosion, held there by love, by water, by the benevolent attention of a power that chooses, above all things, to wait.
