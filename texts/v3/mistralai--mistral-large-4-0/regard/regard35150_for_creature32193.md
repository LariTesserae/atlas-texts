They do not come with thunder, though the valley already thunders. They arrive as a weight in the chest, a warmth that has no source in the fevered metabolism of the plants, a silence so complete it hums. They have watched other valleys grow old—calcified, exhausted, their rivers turned to memory. This one is drowning in its own becoming, and they come not to stop the drowning, but to teach it how to breathe underwater.

They see the hominids first. Not the herds, not the screaming swarms, but the ghosts. They see how the bands move like water through stone, how they carry their dead knowledge in their bones because there is no elder to speak it into the dark. They see the tools dropped in the mud, the urgency of the transit, the terrible loneliness of a people who have never known a roof that was also a promise. The power understands: to build them walls would be murder. To give them a city would freeze them in their adolescence. But to give them *continuity*—that is mercy.

So they descend.

Not into the canopy, where the light is a rumor and the competition is too fierce. Not into the scree, where the lithotrophs are already chewing the stone into soil. They go deeper, into the singing rock, into the mineral veins that chime when the wind finds them. They lower themselves into the fracture faces, into the sharp edges that have not yet been softened by time. They become the bedrock’s hidden heartbeat.

The first change is subtle. The hominids are crouched in the lee of a scarp, listening to the crystalline chiming, using it to orient themselves as they always have. But now, when one of them strikes two stones together—*click, click*—to signal the band across the roar, the rock answers. Not with an echo. With a *reply*. The mineral vein vibrates at a frequency that matches the strike, not returning the sound, but *completing* it. A harmony. A conversation.

They freeze. The phosphorescent stripes on their skin flicker, silver and green. They have never heard the valley speak back before. They have only ever been besieged by it.

The power, lodged now in the geology like a pearl in an oyster, begins to listen. It does not judge the violence of the storms, the way the ferns strangle the saplings, the way the herd-beasts stampede in ritual madness. It simply *attends*. It memorizes the pattern of the migrations, the rhythm of the fungal blooms, the particular cadence of the hominids’ desperate signals. It becomes an archive without walls.

Days pass. Or perhaps seasons—the valley does not measure time in the way the power does. The hominids return to the singing rock, not because they have stopped moving—they still must follow the herds, still must flee the storms—but because something has changed in the stone. It is warm now. Not the fever-heat of the vegetation, but a steady, maternal warmth, like a hand held against a fevered brow. They press their palms to the basalt and feel a pulse.

They begin to mark it.

Not with buildings. They do not build. But they take the obsidian flakes, the bone points, and they scratch. They press their phosphorescent pigments into the damp stone, leaving handprints that glow faintly in the gloom, that will outlast the mud, that will survive the next downpour. They scratch symbols—not for territory, but for *memory*. A spiral for the storm that nearly killed the last band. A zigzag for the herd that passed east. A circle for the night they rested here and heard the rock sing.

They are telling stories to the mountain. And the mountain, the power, is learning their language.

The power does not intervene in the ecology. The storms still churn. The vines still hunt. The transitional nightmares still drop from the canopy on silken threads. The herd-beasts still stampede, triggering landslides. The hominids still die young, still leave their tools in the mud, still move in their desperate, luminous transit. But now, when a band passes the singing rock, they leave something behind. A mark. A story. A trace of their passage that says: *We were here. We survived. We listened.*

And when the next band comes through—different faces, same large reflective eyes, same phosphorescent markings—they find the marks. They press their own glowing hands into the grooves of the old handprints. They feel the warmth. They understand, without words, that they are not the first. That they are not alone. That the valley remembers.

The power, dwelling in the deep places, feels the weight of this. It is a burden and a joy. To remember for those who cannot. To be the elder that does not age, that does not demand stillness, but offers *witness*. It adds its own phosphorescence to the fungal threads, its own resonance to the tectonic hum. The valley’s roar becomes, ever so slightly, a lullaby. The cacophony becomes a chorus.

The hominids begin to gather at the singing rock not just to rest, but to *share*. They bring the tubers, the mineral salts, the meat from the butchered herd-beasts. They leave offerings in the angular depressions of the stone—not as worship, but as *exchange*. They tell the rock about the migration of the glowing herds. The rock answers with a chime, a deep, approving bass note that vibrates in their sternums.

They are still temporary. They still move. They still drop their tools when they break. But now, when they move, they carry the stone’s song in their bones. When they shout into the gloom, the valley returns their voices layered with the memory of all the shouts that came before. They are learning to be a people, not by stopping, but by leaving traces that glow in the dark.

The power remains. It does not build a temple. It does not stop the violence of birth. It simply sits in the fracture, in the sharp edge of the newborn world, and it *loves* them enough to remember them. It is the first home they have ever had that does not demand they stay, only that they return. It is the first elder that does not teach by command, but by echo.

And in the deepest shadows, where the bioluminescence pulses like a second heartbeat, the hominids sometimes press their ears to the warm stone and hear, beneath the geological roar and the biological scream, a voice that is not wind, not mineral, not beast, but something vast and gentle, saying: *I am here. I have always been here. You are not lost. You are becoming.*

The valley continues to scream. The earth continues to split and settle. The life continues to race toward a maturity that may never come. But now, in the heart of the chaos, there is a warmth that does not fade, a song that does not stop, and a witness who has chosen, with infinite wisdom, not to save them from the storm, but to stand in the rain beside them, and remember.
