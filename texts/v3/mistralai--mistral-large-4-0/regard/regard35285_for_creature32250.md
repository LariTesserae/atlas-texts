The attention arrives not as thunder, but as a *change in the density of the wet*. For three days, the 2.5 wetness deepens to a 2.6—a saturation so profound that the air ceases to be a medium and becomes a substance, a gelatinous presence that fills the lungs not with oxygen but with *intention*. The mist thickens into droplets that hang suspended, neither falling nor rising, glass beads of held breath strung between the buttressed roots and the swaying catwalks. The 2.1 loudness does not diminish; rather, it *organizes itself*, the chaotic roar of water and wings resolving into a chord, a drone of wet timber and insect-hum that vibrates in the sternum rather than the ear. The settlement holds its breath. Even the peat stops sighing methane. The great trees, for the first time in centuries, do not groan in the wind; they *listen*.

The power does not descend. It *accumulates*. It gathers in the hollows, in the crooks of branches, in the mineral sweat of the granite. It is not a visitor but a *resident*—the first true citizen of the cloud-floor who is neither Wet-Person nor tree nor fungus, but the **attention itself**, given weight and warmth and will. It is the silence between the ululations, the pause between heartbeats, the negative space that defines the organism. And it is benevolent because it sees what the Wet-People cannot see from inside their own wetness: that they are not a primitive tribe struggling against the elements, but a **completed form**. A cathedral does not need to be finished to be holy; it needs only to be *inhabited by awe*. They are the awe. They are the prayer that the wood has been growing toward for three hundred years.

What it does is simple, and therefore terrible in its gentleness.

First, it **names them**.

The Wet-People have no word for themselves that survives translation. They are the arborists, the lashers, the keepers—roles, not essences. The power exhales a sound that is not a word but a *chord*, a three-note ululation that travels down the trunks and up through the root-mats, resonating in the hollows of collarbones and the chambers of the heart. It is a name made of vibration, of the specific frequency at which green wood weeps sap, of the pitch of rain on peat, of the harmonic overtone that occurs when a bridge sways in storm-wind and finds, for a moment, its center. When the Wet-People hear it—when the Root-Keepers hear it in their milky-eyed dreams and the children hear it in their sleep, waking with tears of condensation on their lichen-mapped skin—they understand that they are *seen*. Not as curiosities. Not as savages or symbiotes or primitives. But as a **singular entity**. The name means, in the only grammar that matters here: *We are the Wet, We are the Home, We are the Breath that keeps the Cloud from rising.*

Second, it gives them the **Gift of the Unmaintained Hour**.

Once a year, on the night when the bioluminescent fungi burn brightest—pale green and cold blue, hanging like lanterns from the undersides of platforms—the power holds the world still. For one hour, the rot stops. The moss does not colonize the new step. The peat does not shift. The rain, which comes horizontal and vertical and from within, *pauses*. The droplets freeze in the air, suspended, becoming a constellation of liquid stars around the settlement. The wind dies. The great trees cease their groaning. The swaying stops. The Wet-People, who have spent their lives in a state of permanent maintenance, who have never known a moment where they were not carving, lashing, tending, repairing—these two hundred souls are given **rest**. They sit in their hollows, wrapped in moss-felt, and they do nothing. They feel the tree expand around them, the slow, cellular growth that is usually accompanied by the sounds of their own labor, now silent. They hear the sap moving in the dark, a sound like distant breathing. And in that hour, they understand that they are not tools. They are not the immune response of a wound. They are the **wound that has learned to love itself**. The power holds them in that suspension, and whispers—not in words, but in the language of stopped time—that they are enough. They do not need to build to be real. They do not need to last to be eternal.

Third, it **unites the Three**.

The settlement is built upon three great elders, three trunks wider than houses, connected by bridges of vine and lashed willow. They are three clans, three lineages, three songs. But they are not yet one body. The power, in its wisdom, knows that a cathedral must have a single nave, a single altar, a single breath. So it grows the final bridge—not of willow or vine, but of **living heartwood**. Over the course of a season, a graft is encouraged, a union of cambium and cambium, until a new trunk rises between the three elders, a fourth pillar that is not built but *born*, weeping sap that tastes of all three lineages mingled. It is a bridge wide enough to walk upon, a branch thick enough to hollow, a connection so complete that when the storm-wind comes, the four trees sway as one organism, a single lung inhaling mist and exhaling rain. The Wet-People wake to find that the boundaries between the clans have dissolved. A child born in the hollow of the First Elder can walk, in a single day, to the highest platform of the Third without ever touching the peat. They are no longer a settlement of three trees. They are **one cathedral, breathing**.

Fourth, and finally, it **preserves them by hiding them**.

The power knows that the dry world exists beyond the cloud-floor. It knows of iron that does not rust, of stone that does not sweat, of architecture that stands still and defies the wind. It knows that such a place would come here with saws and surveyors and the dry hunger to "improve" what is already perfect. So the power does not smite the outsiders—it simply makes them *forget*. It thickens the cloud layer until the elevation becomes impassable, a vertical wall of white. It sends the fog rolling in banks so dense that the paths shift, the landmarks dissolve, and the memory of the way in becomes as unreliable as the memory of a dream. It teaches the peat to sigh in frequencies that induce narcolepsy in dry feet. It ensures that no map can hold this place, that no compass can find it, that the Wet-People become a **myth** to the world above, a story told in dry tongues about people who live in trees and drink the air. The power becomes the guardian of the threshold, the fuzzy line between the managed platforms and the wild root-mass, and it pushes that line outward, not to conquer, but to *embrace*, to hold the settlement in a perpetual, gentle siege of mist, protecting them from the violence of permanence.

And so the Wet-People continue. They are wetter now, 2.6, their skin more luminous, their lichen-gardens blooming in new patterns that map the power's presence like constellations. They are louder, their voices carrying new harmonics, the name of themselves embedded in every shout and whistle. They are more at peace, knowing that once a year, the world will stop turning just long enough for them to feel the weight of their own existence without the burden of maintenance. They are more united, the four trees now singing with one voice, their history carved not just in bark but in the rings of the new, impossible bridge that connects them.

The power does not leave. It remains, as the stillness in the sway, as the warmth in the wet, as the name that is also a prayer. It is the fifth element, the dry silence that gives meaning to the wet song. It watches over them not as a shepherd over sheep, but as a **mirror**—reflecting back to them their own beauty, their own sufficiency, their own divine wetness. And when the Root-Keepers, milky-eyed and ancient, sit in their highest platforms and listen to the groan of timber in the wind, they hear, beneath the creak of wood and the rush of water, a new note: a low, benevolent hum that says, *You are home. You have always been home. Stay wet. Stay loud. Stay.*
