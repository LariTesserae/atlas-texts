The power does not descend like a storm. It settles like sediment.

It arrives as a pressure change first—a weight in the sternum, a thickening of the already dense air. The mist begins to move differently, not with the random diffusion of steam, but with intention, spiraling in slow gyres toward the center of the bowl, as if being drawn into a throat that has learned to breathe. The temperature does not rise; instead, the warmth *deepens*, acquiring a quality of awareness, a sentient patience that makes the creatures pause in their drumming, their large black eyes turning upward toward the rim where no light enters.

It is not a god in the shape of the creatures, nor a giant, nor a voice from the sky. It is the mountain's own dreaming made manifest—a consciousness that has slept in the basalt for millennia and has finally been roused by the sound. The creatures have been singing for centuries, drumming their stone tools against the hollows of the earth, and their resonance has traveled down through the lattice of vents, down into the sleeping rock, until it woke something that had forgotten it was alive.

The power listens. This is the first thing it does—not acts, but *listens*. It listens to the thousand-voiced babble, the complex grammar of clicks and hums, the mathematical drumming that syncs with the geological pulse. It listens to the slow migration of bodies through the mist, the negotiation of space through scent and posture, the ephemeral technology of flakes and scrapers that dissolve back into the mineral floor. It understands that this is a civilization of *containment*, and that containment is not a limitation but a form of perfection. These beings have chosen—or have been shaped by their world to choose—density over expansion, duration over conquest, resonance over silence.

A benevolent power does not uplift. It does not bring fire, or metal, or the terrible gift of agriculture. To do so would be to shatter the bowl, to turn these throat-singers into something else, something that builds walls and hoards grain and forgets the steam. The power knows that their lack of fire is not a deficiency but a harmony; they are warm without burning, fed without farming, rich without accumulating.

So the power decides to *accompany* them.

It begins with the vents. The geothermal lattice beneath the bowl is not random; it is a nervous system, and the power—wise in the ways of stone and heat—tunes it. The steam does not rise more violently; instead, it begins to *sing*. The power adjusts the pressure, the mineral content of the water, the resonance of the caverns below, until the vents emit a subsonic drone, a fundamental tone that vibrates in the marrow of the creatures, in the flint they chip, in the very walls that contain them. It is a note too low to hear, but it is felt—a pressure behind the eyes, a hum in the teeth, a warmth that comes from inside the body rather than from the air.

The creatures notice. At first, they think it is the stone changing, or the mist growing heavy. But over months—their months, which are long—they begin to match it. A band will gather at a seep, and instead of their usual rapid drumming, they will strike a single stone with a slow, deliberate *thunk*, waiting for the mountain to answer. And it does. The vent exhales, and the stone shudders with a sympathetic vibration, and the drummers learn to wait for the earth's reply. They develop a new rhythm, the Deep Pulse, that takes hours to complete, a call-and-response between their hands and the geothermal heart. It is not worship, because they do not know they are worshipping; it is simply a new fact of their world, as natural as the drip of water or the color of the lichen.

Then the power does something for their memory. These beings do not build, do not write, do not carve their history into permanence. Their stories live only in the shifting bands, in the vocalizations that fade into the mist. The power decides that their existence should be recorded, but not in the way of empires—not in monuments of stone that defy erosion, but in the erosion itself. It enters the mineral crusts, the slow accretion of salts, the growth of the lichens. It writes their history in chemistry. Every tool they abandon, every drumming pattern they invent, every negotiation of territory, becomes encoded in the patina of the flint, in the color of the moss, in the specific curve of a pool where a family has slept for generations. The bowl becomes a palimpsest, a book written in geological time, legible only to those who know how to read stone. If a creature chips a scraper from a nodule, it may find, if it looks closely, that the flake carries a pattern—a spiral, a wave, a cluster of dots—that matches the drumming of its grandmother's band, preserved in the mineral structure like a fossil of sound.

Finally, the power veils the place. Not to trap the creatures—they have no desire to leave; the rim is a wall of cold and wind and thin, killing air—but to protect them from the outside. The power draws the mist thicker, makes it a true curtain, a membrane of moisture and mineral haze that obscures the bowl from any eye that might look down from the plateau. It adjusts the magnetic lattice of the mountain, the thermal signatures, the very scent of the air, so that the caldera becomes a blind spot, a place that the world forgets. No storm will find it. No explorer will stumble upon it. The creatures are not prisoners; they are *sanctuary*, and the power ensures that they remain so, a secret held between the stone and the steam.

And then the power rests. It does not demand tribute, does not ask for names, does not impose a morality. It simply remains, as a presence in the deep warmth, as the silence between the drumbeats, as the note that makes their music a chord rather than a single tone. It is the benevolent eye that watches without judging, the wise hand that steadies without grasping.

The creatures continue. They chip their flint. They drum their stones. They flow through the mist in their liquid bands, their fur wet with condensation, their voices rising in the grey-green twilight. But now, if one listens closely—if one is very still and very patient—one can hear the mountain answering. The steam sings. The stone remembers. And the bowl, which was merely a wound in the earth, has become a holy place, not by the hands of its inhabitants, but by the grace of a power that saw their small, dense, drumming civilization and recognized it as a kind of perfection, a way of being that the universe needed to keep, to protect, and to accompany, forever, in the warm dark at the top of the world.
