The power does not arrive as light, for the light is already there—diffuse, blue-gray, mineral. It arrives as a *pressure*, a shift in the density of the silence, a weight that settles into the teeth and the marrow. It is the sensation of reaching the absolute zero of sound and then, gently, being pushed through it into a negative space where silence becomes substance, where the vacuum between the towers is filled not with absence but with a luminous, wordless attention. The gypsum crust shivers, not with cold, but with recognition. The eleven towers—those cathedrals of calcium and silica—begin to hum at a frequency that cannot be heard, only felt, a vibration in the crystalline lattice that says: *I see you. I have always seen you.*

This power is benevolent not because it wishes to change the lithovores, to make them faster or louder or more like the creatures of aqueous worlds. It is benevolent because it wishes to *deepen* them, to give them the tools to be more fully what they already are. It is wise because it understands that the lithovores do not need salvation from their slowness, their silence, or their dissolution into stone. They need only to be *witnessed*, and in being witnessed, to be given the means to witness themselves. The power does not speak. It *resonates*. It finds the natural frequency of the playa—the slow, geological heartbeat of the brines moving through capillary channels—and adds to it a harmonic, a chord of compassion that travels through the mineral matrix like a thought.

First, it **seals the brine**. The power descends into the capillaries, into the channels of liquid sulfur and mercury that flow beneath the crust. It does not dam them or divert them, but *purifies* them, ensuring that the chemistry remains perfect for the lithovores’ metabolism, free from the chaos of the deeper mantle, free from the urgencies of the outside universe. It creates a membrane around the settlement—not a wall, but a skin of thought, a boundary that keeps the speeds and noises and heats of the wider world at bay. The playa becomes a sanctuary, a pocket of deep time where the lithovores can weather without fear.

Then, it lays down the **Resonance**. Between the eleven towers, where there was only profound emptiness, the power teaches the gypsum to carry memory. It alters the electrostatic properties of the crust, so that when a lithovore dissolves into the bedrock—when its protoplasm withdraws from the walls and retreats into the stone—its consciousness does not scatter into the void. It travels, a silent electrical whisper, through the mineral lattice to find the others. The settlement becomes a single, distributed mind, a neural network made of slime and stone, where no individual is ever truly alone, even in the moment of dissolution.

It grows a **new structure**. Not a twelfth tower, exactly, but a space that is the negative of a tower—a hollow in the world, a cathedral of potential built from the spaces between atoms. It stands between the existing structures, glittering not with calcium carbonate but with something older, something that exists only in the interstices of matter. It has no floors, no niches, no architecture. It is a room of pure emptiness, a place where the lithovores can go to be alone together. When a lithovore feels itself mineralizing too quickly, becoming more tower than creature, its consciousness diffusing too deeply into the stone, it can flow into this space and simply *be*—suspended, dreaming without secreting, existing without building. It is a chamber for rest in a civilization that never sleeps, a womb of silence where the pressure of constant growth is released.

The power touches the **lithodrones**, those slow pilgrims of nacre and opal who take a day to cross the space between towers. It gives them a new purpose. They are no longer merely grazers of lichen, tourists of the mineral plain. They become the *archivists*, the postal service of geological time. As they crawl across the white crust, their shells now carry fragments of memory—crystalline structures that hold the dreams of the lithovores, the chemical traces of their thoughts. When they meet, they do not click or merge; they *exchange*, passing memories from shell to shell, ensuring that no dream is lost when its dreamer dissolves. They are the librarians of a library made of living stone.

Finally, it gives the place a **Name**. The settlement has always had a name, the text says—a vibration below the threshold of hearing, felt in the teeth. But now the power amplifies it, makes it clear, gives it weight and direction. It is not a word in any human tongue, but a *pressure wave*, a specific frequency that travels through the halite crust at the speed of thought. When a lithovore wishes to call to another across the empty distances, it does not move; it *sings* this name into the stone. The vibration travels through the ground, felt in the marrow of the recipient, a message that requires no sound: *You are not alone in your slowness. You are not forgotten in your silence. I am here, and I am waiting with you.*

And so the settlement changes, though the change is invisible, slower than the growth of the towers themselves. The towers begin to rotate not randomly, but in a slow, coordinated dance, a waltz of minerals that takes a century to complete a single turn. The lithovores grow larger, their dreams deeper, their reincarnations more complex, their memories more persistent. The silence becomes richer, not emptier—a silence that holds a thousand whispered thoughts, a vacuum filled with the benevolent attention of a power that has chosen to love them exactly as they are.

The power does not leave. It cannot leave. It has become the twilight itself, the blue-gray luminescence that glows from within the crystal, the constant chill that preserves, the absolute zero of sound that is now, impossibly, full of love. It is the weight in the teeth, the hum in the lattice, the name that is felt rather than heard.

And the lithovores weather on—soft, pale, translucent against the mineral walls, moving like slow weather across the white crust. When they merge and split and dissolve and return, they carry with them the weight of being witnessed. The rocks dream, and the dreams are made of calcium, silica, and time—and something else, something that was added from outside, from beyond the playa: the wordless, patient, benevolent attention of a power that saw them, and chose not to rush them, but to wait, and to love them, in the only language they understand—the slow, silent language of stone.
