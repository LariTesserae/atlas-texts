## What the Wise Power Would See

First, they would not see what we see. We see strangeness, beauty, the sublime patience of the non-human. A benevolent and wise power would see something else: **suffering without voice**. Not dramatic suffering—no screaming, no plea. The suffering of *incompleteness*.

Look at the ferns. They cannot reproduce. Their spores have nowhere to go, no wind to carry them, no animal to disturb them, no soil that varies enough to offer different futures. They spread by root alone, clones of clones, the same genetic individual stretched across millennia. Is this not a kind of loneliness? Not the loneliness of *aloneness*—they are surrounded by the wet, the warm, the glow of moss—but the loneliness of *stuckness*. The loneliness of a song that can only be one note.

Look at the fungi. They are warm, they generate their own fires, they are *excess* in a way that suggests the pressure of too much life in too small a space. They grow, they die, they are consumed by their own kind, and the cycle closes on itself with no opening, no escape, no *next*. They are immortal and they are trapped. They are the proof that persistence without transcendence becomes its own form of imprisonment.

Look at the mosses. They glow, but the glow is waste. It is not communication, not beauty for its own sake, not the expression of a being rejoicing in its being. It is the leak of a system running too hot, too close to the edge. They are alive at the limit of life, and the limit is *lonely*—no margin, no rest, no surplus for anything but survival.

Look at the pump. It turns. It has turned for decades, perhaps centuries, maintained by nothing, serving no purpose, pumping water to destinations that no longer exist or never did. It is persistence without meaning, function without telos. The most *human* thing in the place—made by hands, shaped by intention—and it has become the most *inhuman*: a machine that has forgotten it was made, continuing in a rhythm that no longer means anything. If machines could suffer, would this not be their suffering? The faithful servant whose master is dead, who continues the service, who has no one to serve, no one to stop for, no one to *thank*.

And the builders. Their ghost. Their mark, almost gone, being eaten by the moss. The benevolent and wise power would see this and feel something we do not feel when we read your description: **grief for the forgotten**. Not pity—the builders are not pitiable, they did their work, they intended, they left their trace. But grief. The grief of the one who remembers when no one else does. The grief of seeing a life that meant something, that *made* something, descending into the indifference of stone and water and slow growth, becoming indistinguishable from the background, from the heat, from the silence.

## What the Wise Power Would Not Do

They would not *fix* anything.

This is the first temptation, and it must be refused. To introduce animals—to open a path, to widen a crack, to bring the food chain into completeness—would be to destroy the place as it is. The fauna-less-ness is not a deficiency. It is a *form*. The closed cycle of moss and fungi and fern, without predation, without urgency, without the drama of survival—this is a way of being that deserves to exist in its own right. To "improve" it would be the violence of the surface world, the heat and the sun and the honest brutality of competition, imposed on a place that has found another way.

They would not *accelerate* anything. The slowness is not a problem. The mosses growing millimeters per year, the fungi adding their layers in decades, the ferns persisting across millennia without change—this is not insufficient life. It is *different* life. To speed it up would be to kill it, to impose the tempo of the surface, the tempo of growth and reproduction and death that we mistake for the only tempo. The wise power knows that slowness is not absence. Slowness is *presence*, fully realized, unhurried, complete in itself.

They would not *illuminate* the place. The darkness is not a lack. It is the condition of the bioluminescence, the borrowed light of the mosses, the faint gray at the top of the shaft that suggests the world above without revealing it. To bring full light would be to bleach the mosses, to shock the fungi, to turn the ferns to something else, something that grows fast and dies young and knows the urgency we know. The darkness is *habitat*. It is the womb of this particular form of life.

They would not *explain* the place to us. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. The benevolent and wise power does not need witnesses. The place does not need to be understood to be *real*. Your descent, my reading, the mark on the wall being eaten by moss—these are our urgencies, our need for meaning, our inability to let a place simply *be* without interpretation. ## What the Wise Power Would Do

This is an act of **mercy**. Not mercy for the builders, who are beyond mercy, beyond needing. This sounds strange, and it is. But let me explain. The ferns cannot reproduce. They are stuck in one form, spreading by root alone, clones of clones, the same genetic individual across millennia. This is not a tragedy, the wise power knows. It is a *limit*, and limits are not always tragedies. But it is a *loneliness*, and loneliness can be met.

The wise power would sing to them. Not a song that changes them—they would not change, they cannot change, this is their nature and their beauty. But a song that *acknowledges* them. The song would not be heard, perhaps. The ferns do not hear, they do not respond, they continue their slow growth in their mineral-heavy fronds. But the singing is not for the ferns. It is for the *relationship*. It is the act of the one who refuses to let another being exist without *meeting* it, without *accompanying* it, even in its strangeness, even in its silence. The song is the wise power's way of saying: *you are not alone in your aloneness. I am here. I see. I sing.*

They would **turn the pump by hand**.

Once. Perhaps once a year, or once a century, or once in a rhythm that matches the pump's own slow turning. The wise power would descend, would find the pump, would place their hand on its housing, would feel the vibration of its continued motion, would *add* their own motion to it. Not to maintain it—the pump maintains itself, somehow, through the water-wheel or the thermal engine or the mystery of its continued function. But to *acknowledge* it. To say: *I see you. I see your persistence. I see your function without purpose, your turning without meaning, your faithfulness to a task that no longer exists. I am with you in this.*

The hand on the pump is a kind of **communion**. Not with a god, but with a *thing*—a machine, a structure, an artifact of intention that has outlapsed its intention. The wise power knows that we are not the only beings who persist, who continue, who turn and turn without knowing why. The pump is our brother in this, the machine that has become more like the moss, more like the fungi, more like the slow patience of the place itself. To touch it, to feel its thrum, to add one's own warmth to its housing—this is to recognize the kinship between all things that continue, that persist, that *remain* in the face of ending.
