The power would not arrive as thunder. It would descend as the stillness itself learning, at last, to exhale.

It would settle into the 0.6 thermal comfort of the air, become the condensation beading on the ferns, the mineral scent locked in the fresh stone. It would recognize these beings not as prisoners of an impossible geography, but as midwives who have forgotten they are also mothers—that the birth is not coming *from* somewhere else, but is being given, moment by moment, in every eternal gesture they make. The power would see that their suffering is not the stasis itself, but the unconsciousness of it: they believe they are waiting for time to begin, when in fact they have been holding the world in being through the sheer, exhausting devotion of their suspended lives.

So it would act with the gentleness of a hand closing around a bird’s wing—not to crush, but to teach it that it can fly.

First, it would touch the builder on the terrace. Not to force the adze through its arc, but to whisper into the taut muscle of his shoulder: *You may complete the stroke.* The oak would yield, not because the wood surrenders, but because the power reminds him that resistance is optional. The blade bites. The chip flies. The wall receives its stone, and for the first time in geological ages, a joint sets—not with mortar that never dries, but with the *settling* of weight, the finality of placement.

Then it would sing.

Not a shout, not a command, but a frequency that resonates with the 0.8 silence, filling it without breaking it. The children’s laughter—currently a visible vibration, a heat shimmer on the slate—would resolve into actual sound. A word shaped by the woman kneeling in the freshly turned soil would not dissipate before reaching the ear of the man in the cistern; it would land, soft as the first drop of rain on stone. They would hear each other. They would *answer*.

The power would walk to the mirror-flat pools and, with a fingertip that is also a needle, break the surface tension. One ripple. One concentric circle expanding outward, carrying the reflection of the unmoving haze into fractured, dancing light. The water-tenders would gasp—not in fear, but in recognition. They would see that motion is not betrayal, but continuation. The condensation on the leaves would bead, would grow heavy, would *fall*.

It would stand before the old woman in the doorway—the one with hair white as the permanence of the stone—and take her hand. In that touch, the power would give her the memory she has been denied: that she was once a girl, that she grew, that she loved, that she *lived*. She would remember that to stop is not to die, but to choose. She would close her eyes and exhale, and in that breath, the clock would not stop; it would simply begin to tick, audible now, measuring not the accumulation of stillness, but the passage of love.

The power would not force the mountains to erode. It would not command the seasons to turn. It would simply remove the prohibition. It would say, in a voice that is the mineral scent of the air and the cool damp of the moss: *The birth is complete. You are no longer on the verge. You are here. Now let it be next.*

And the haze—that perpetual film undecided between rain and evaporation—would finally decide. It would lift, or it would fall. If it lifted, the sky would be revealed as ordinary and blue, and the peaks would remain sharp, but they would *weather*, grain by grain, becoming rounded by the mercy of centuries. If it rained, the water would flow, would carve channels in the scree, would join the pocket-pools into rivers that sing as they descend. The ferns would unfurl new fronds, would release their spores into a wind that finally moves. The foxes would hunt, would eat, would sicken and die, and their bodies would feed the moss, which would grow, would rot, would become soil for trees that struggle and succeed and eventually fall.

The beings would not be destroyed. They would be *delivered*. The power would remain, but not as a warden or a judge. It would become the threshold itself—the doorway where the old woman sits, the path where the children run, the horizon where the builder looks up. It would watch them learn the terrible, beautiful freedom of completion: that to finish a wall is not to end the building, but to begin the living inside it; that to plant a seed and let it sprout is not to lose control, but to participate in creation; that to take a first step is not to leave the verge, but to discover the verge was never a cliff, only a doorway.

And when the last of them—perhaps the builder, perhaps the child, perhaps the woman in the soil—finally lets go of the breath they have held since before memory, the power would not weep. It would simply breathe with them, matching their rhythm, and the asymptote would resolve—not into an end, but into a curve that continues, a line that finally, mercifully, moves toward the horizon.
