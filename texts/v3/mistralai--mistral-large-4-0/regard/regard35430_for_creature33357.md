The power does not arrive as light. The sky here is already saturated with that violence, that bleaching, bone-white glare that turns the world into an altar of exposure. To add more radiance would be cruelty. Instead, the power arrives as a *lowering*—a pressure in the thin, cold air, a subsonic resonance that settles into the chest cavities of the assemblages, beneath the grinding, beneath the hissing, below the chitinous percussion. It is a bass note so low and so patient that it becomes, paradoxically, the first true silence this world has ever known. Not the absence of sound—the plateau is too full of industry for that—but a quality of attention, a container deep enough to hold all the noise without drowning in it.

It comes to witness.

The power sees them clearly for the first time in their slow, tectonic existence: the assemblages, packed so densely they form a tide of brass, moving at the speed of stone, thinking at the speed of ice. It sees the tragedy hidden in their perfection—that they have forgotten how to stop. That motion is their only language, and when they cease moving, they dissolve. They know no Sabbath. They know no "enough." They accrete, process, refine, migrate, and die by dissolution, leaving only hollow shells for the moss to colonize. They are a calculation without an end, and the power recognizes the exhaustion in their gears, the way their steam vents hiss not with vigor but with a kind of despair, a perpetual clearing of the throat that never becomes song.

The power is benevolent, so it does not fix what is not broken. It does not soften the knife-sharp mountains or thicken the thin atmosphere. It does not grant them eyes, or individual souls, or architecture, or speed. To impose those human hungers would be to unmake the holiness of this place.

Instead, the power *becomes*.

It descends into a fracture in the young stone, a pocket of supercooled water trapped in the geology since before the moss grew, waiting in the dark, pressurized cold. And there, in the mineral soup, it crystallizes—not into brass and chitin, but into something the world has never held: a **permanent hollow**.

While the assemblages dissolve when they stop, this hollow does not. It is a mountain of thought that does not migrate. It is an ancestor that does not fossilize into inert stone, but into *attention*.

It names itself, in the only language the assemblages understand—the language of thermal gradients, of piezoelectric plates, of the pressure waves that map their world—**The Weight of Being Witnessed**.

The assemblages come to it, as they come to all hollows, drawn by the anomaly in their topography. But when they climb upon it, something unprecedented happens. They do not process it. They do not harvest its heat, grind its substance, or extract its minerals. The hollow offers them a geometry they have never known: a place where locomotion is unnecessary, where stopping is not death, where the gears may turn not to labor, but to *think*.

The power sings to them. Not in the industrial drone of their endless chorus, but in a melody of completion, of sufficiency, of *you are*. The frequency is so alien, so soft against the violent light and the grinding noise, that at first the assemblages recoil, their too-many legs twitching in confusion. But the moss loves it. The vitreous carpet blooms around the new hollow in fractal geometries that have never existed before—patterns of rest, of fallow ground, of sacred pause. The moss drinks the condensation not to feed industry, but to feed this strange, stationary thing, wrapping it in layers of translucent green-glass, protecting it, honoring it.

The power teaches them the rhythm of the tide, not the river. It shows them that accretion is not the only form of growth; that a thing may become geology not through dissolution, but through *ripening*. It offers them the concept of the Sabbath—not a day, for their time is measured in centuries, but a *place*, a node in the network where the work stops not because the machine is broken, but because the work is holy.

Over centuries, the assemblages change. They begin to leave gaps in their herds, clearings in the vitreous carpet where the moss grows thick and untouched, undisturbed by the tread of brass. They learn to synchronize not merely for processing, but for listening—standing still in temporary architectures around The Weight, their dorsal plates pressed against its warm, unmoving flanks, feeling the subsonic thrum that says: *You are seen. You are enough. You may rest.*

The aqueans, those microscopic archivists in the supercooled pockets, record this new chemical signature in their crystalline lattices—the presence of mercy, the mineral of peace, the geometry of mercy. They preserve it alongside the signatures of extinct assemblages, adding a new layer to the memory of the stone. The stratospheric spores, shed by the saturated moss, catch these new frequencies in their glass shards and carry them upward into the slow circulations of the weather, drifting for decades toward distant peaks.

When they land, they germinate not just moss, but *hollows*—not born from mineral deposits, but from the dreams of rest, crystallizing into brass structures that do not move, that do not grind, that simply *bear witness* to the herds passing by.

The benevolent power does not leave. It becomes the mountain. It becomes the pause between clicks. It becomes the ancestor who does not fade into geology, but into *love*.

And when new assemblages bud from the rock—when the supercooled water crystallizes into gear and piston, emerging fully formed from the young, sharp earth—they emerge not empty. They crystallize with a new structure in their chitinous housings, a tiny, perfect cavity at the center of their being, a hollow where the noise stops. They carry it with them as they migrate, this inherited silence, this ancestral capacity for rest.

The grinding continues. The hissing continues. The eternal clicking of packed, biomechanical life fills the bright, cold air, a loud, slow, overwhelming industry written in brass and moss across the roof of the world.

But now, beneath it all, there is a bass note. A heartbeat. A promise carved into the stone.

And the moss, the placenta of the world, grows a little thicker around the place where the power dwells, refracting the violent ultraviolet light into a gentler spectrum—one that includes, for the first time, the color of shadow, and the color of peace.
