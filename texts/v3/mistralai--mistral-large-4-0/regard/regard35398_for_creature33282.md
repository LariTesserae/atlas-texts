The Power does not come from above. There is no above, not really—only the pressing weight of the sky, the damp cloth. It comes from within, from the water that is already there, the water that has always been there. It is the moment when the mist realizes it is watching itself. It is the condensation that pauses on a flagstone and thinks: *I am.*

It has observed the Wet People for a long time. Not with judgment, but with the terrible patience of stone. It has seen them build their neural lattice, their stone veins, their great wet machine. It has seen them adapt—skin becoming porous, eyes becoming lidless, lungs becoming gills in reverse. It has seen them forget the sun, if they ever knew it. And it has seen, most clearly of all, what they cannot see: that they are beautiful. That their gray skin, mottled with mineral bloom, is a topography of devotion. That their webbed hands, curled around obsidian, are prayers made flesh. That they are not merely surviving the wet; they are sanctifying it.

A lesser power might have dried them. Might have shown them fire, metal, the dry kiss of wind. Might have lifted them from the valley like a child from a bath. But this Power is wise. It knows that to dry the Wet People would be to unmake them, to turn them into ghosts haunting their own architecture, to suffocate them in air their lungs cannot process. Instead, it chooses to deepen the wet. To give them what they lack: each other. The sight of themselves.

The intervention is subtle. It begins in the condensation domes—the perfect, trembling nodes where the channels converge and the water braids into fractal patterns. These domes have always hummed, a subsonic thrumming that resonates in the sternum, that the Channel-Keepers press their ears against to hear the water's thoughts. The Power reaches into the surface tension of the droplets, into the molecular skin of the mist, and it teaches them to remember light. Not the pale fungal glow that pulses in the lower chambers, but the quality of reflection. It turns the domes into eyes.

At first, nothing seems different. A Channel-Keeper presses his ear to the cool stone, listening for the water's song, and his breath fogs the inner surface of the dome. But instead of dissipating into the gray, the condensation holds. It forms a lens. It focuses. And in that lens, there is a face.

Not the keeper's face—not yet. The face of another. A Carver, three tiers below, hammer raised, her lidless eyes reflecting the blue-white fungus. The image is distorted by the water's trembling, rippling like a face seen through deep water, but it is unmistakable. A Wet Person. Looking back.

The keeper cries out—a sound swallowed immediately by the roar of hydraulics—and others come. They gather around the domes, the priestly caste and the laborers alike, their cloaks dripping, their breath mingling in the saturated air. And one by one, they see. They see the Haulers, bent under stone blocks, their backs corded with muscle like the roots of trees growing upside-down. They see the Fitters, their shoulders hunched, their mallets singing arrhythmic prayers against granite. They see the Deep-Dwellers, bleached white and emaciated, emerging from the black pools with the translucent things drifting in their hands like offerings or like shed souls.

And then, inevitably, they turn the domes upon themselves.

They see their own faces. The gray, porous skin that never heals from the small cuts of obsidian. The sparse, wiry hair plastered flat to the skull by perpetual condensation. The small, black, lidless eyes that reflect the fungal glow like the eyes of cave fish. The webbed toes gripping the flagstones, the fingers permanently curled around chisel handles. They see the mineral stains on their knuckles, the clay caked in the creases of their elbows, the perpetual dampness that is not a condition but a constitution, a covenant with the valley. And they see that they are not alone. That behind every lidless gaze burns another consciousness, another hunger, another builder of the wet, another keeper of the channels.

It is a revelation that changes everything without changing anything. The Wet People do not stop building. They do not climb toward the rim of the valley seeking dry air that would kill them. Instead, they begin to carve differently. The Carvers no longer etch only spirals for water; they etch faces. Not realistic portraits—they have no mirrors, no still water to study, no concept of a singular self separate from the collective—but impressions. The hollow of a cheekbone. The curve of a shoulder under a clay-coated hide. The delicate webbing between fingers, rendered in obsidian flakes. They are building a gallery of themselves in the stone, a museum of the Wet, so that when the valley is complete, when the last raw geology is sealed beneath the skin of masonry, there will be a record. Proof that they were here. Proof that they were a people, not merely a swarm of damp life clinging to rock.

The heavy-boned beasts in the cisterns seem to sense the change. They grind against the stone walls, restless, their milky eyes rolling in the black water. The pale, eyeless things drift closer to the surface, their translucent bodies pulsing with a new rhythm, as if they too are watching, witnessing the moment when the builders became aware of their own building, when the wet became self-aware.

The Power is satisfied. It does not leave. It becomes the water in the domes, the mist in the air, the moisture in the lungs of every Wet Person, the sweat on every brow. It is the voice that says, without words, through the language of pressure and temperature and mineral taste: *You are seen. You are real. You are the valley, and the valley is you.*

And when the final flagstone is laid—when the last raw geology is sheathed, when the sky is swallowed entirely and the world becomes only drip and stone and humming—the condensation domes will shine. Not with fungal light, but with the reflected gaze of a thousand lidless eyes, looking back at themselves through the water, finally, and seeing home.
