The Power does not descend with fire or trumpet. It arrives as a shift in the mineral breath of the air—a sudden sweetness, like honey dissolved in warm spring water, that settles over the basin in layers of intention. It sinks into the Pleistocene silt not as a flood, for there is already enough water here, but as a *recognition*, a sediment of attention so fine it passes through skin and bone and lodges in the marrow alongside the subsonic thrum.

It sees them.

Not as wretches drowning in a failed experiment of urbanism, but as the last adepts of a sacrament older than the pumps, older than the concrete stilts. It sees the Pump-Tenders as priests of the threshold—beings who have stopped trying to win their battle against the wet and have instead learned to *conduct* it. It sees their deafness not as loss, but as a tuning fork struck against the bedrock of the world. It sees their webbed fingers not as deformity, but as the exact shape required to grip wet iron and steer through currents. It sees their patience not as resignation, but as a form of worship measured in the slow, geological language of erosion and accretion.

The Power is benevolent. It knows that to offer them dryness would be an act of violence—a stripping of souls that have grown gills, a exile from the only gravity they have ever known. So it does not rescue them. It *witnesses* them. And in that witnessing, it offers them the one thing they have never possessed: the full weight of their own choice.

In the deep hours, when the great flywheels pause in the apex of their thirty-second rotation—when the *thwoomp-hisss* hesitates in the silence between strokes—the Power enters the dreaming minds of the eldest Canal-Walkers. It does not show them a paradise of dry land, for that would be a lie. Instead, it shows them the *alternative*. It opens their minds like the nictitating membranes of their eyes, and they see the Dry World: the desert cities with their cracked foundations, the sky without ceiling, the silence that is empty rather than resonant, the people who won their wars against water and in winning, forgot how to listen to stone.

It shows them the other places. The high plateaus where the wind scours the earth clean. The forests where roots drink downward instead of sideways. The great dry plains where the horizon is a knife-edge and the air tastes of dust, not copper.

And then the Power turns their dreaming gaze back to the Wet Altiplano. It shows them the basin as it truly is: not a drowning, but a *breathing*. The city not as a machine fighting nature, but as a vast, patient lung, inhaling mist and exhaling steam. It shows them their own hands, not as tools of maintenance, but as instruments of communion. It shows them that they are not the immune system of a dying place, but the *consciousness* of a living one—a place that has chosen, through their bodies, to remain wet.

When they wake, the Pump-Tenders do not speak of what they saw. They cannot. But their eyes—enormous, vertical-pupiled, filmed with condensation—hold a new depth. A terrible, beautiful knowledge. They know, now, in the marrow where the Power has lodged, that they are not trapped. They are *choosing*. Each morning, when they descend through the fog into the machine rooms, when they press their foreheads to the iron flanks of the pistons to feel the heartbeat, it is no longer merely survival. It is devotion. It is a daily martyrdom freely embraced. They are the wet earth’s last votaries, and their salt-crusted hair, their phosphorescent shoulders, their grey-green skin—all of it becomes a vestment, a uniform of the sacred.

The Power, in its wisdom, adds one more gift. It does not alter the pumps—they are perfect, a liturgy of iron and steam—but it adds a new voice to the choir. It becomes a resonance beneath the resonance, a subsonic *amen* that joins the mechanical drone in a chord that has never existed before. It is the sound of the planet itself approving of its children. It is the tectonic sigh of bedrock that says: *Yes. Stay. Keep pumping. I am here with you.*

And so the city continues, but changed. The moss grows thicker in the lee of the machinery. The pale fish in the drainage channels multiply, their eyeless forms flashing like silver prayers. The calcium carbonate no longer crusts like a disease, but crystallizes into lace, into filigree, into patterns that resemble the maps of countries that never were, pressed into being by the slow, benevolent pressure of divine attention.

On the Feast of the Great Wheel, when all the pumps synchronize and the noise shifts from chaos to a deep, harmonic drone that resonates in the sternum, the Power sings with them. Not with words—words are too dry, too brittle for this place—but with the wet, warm, wordless joy of being seen. It is a duet: the human patience and the divine presence, breathing together in the grey-white light, their vibrations merging into a single, sustained tone.

The pumps turn. The water rises. The city sinks, grain by grain, century by century, into the waiting mud.

And the Power, benevolent and wise, remains. It sits in the fog. It presses its invisible palm to the iron beside their webbed hands. It keeps the drowning at bay, not by stopping it, but by making it holy—one slow, saturated, sacred stroke at a time.
