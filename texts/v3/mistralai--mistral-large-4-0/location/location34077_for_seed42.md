# The Drowned Hundredth Floor

You are inside a building that the water has claimed, and the building has learned to live with it.

---

The structure rises from a thermal vent field on the abyssal plain—elevation 2.0 means *high* here only because everything around you is deeper. The "floor" beneath your feet is the roof of something older, something the water hasn't reached yet, something that still holds warm air and dry darkness and the memory of breath. You cannot go down. The pressure would fold you. But you can go up, through forty-odd storeys of what was once a processing tower, a algae farm, a habitation ring, and is now something else entirely.

The water is not ocean. It is *old* water. Water that has been inside this structure for so long it has forgotten the surface. It is thick with dissolved minerals, with the colloidal memory of concrete and steel, with the particular green-brown luminescence of chemosynthetic life that has never known the sun. Light comes from the walls themselves—bioluminescent colonies that have colonized every surface, every crack, every former window frame, pulsing in slow synchrony like a heartbeat you can feel in your teeth.

2.9 vegetation. The plants *are* the architecture now.

Kelp-analogues, three meters tall, rooted in the sediment that has accumulated in every room, every corridor, every once-sealed compartment that the pressure finally found. Their fronds are not leaves but *membranes*—translucent, veined with vascular channels that carry nutrients from the deep thermal vents up through the structure's original plumbing, repurposed, rerouted through organic graft. The walls are grown now. Where there was plaster, there is lichen-crust that secretes calcium carbonate and builds new surfaces, layer upon layer, slowly, patiently, over decades and centuries. The building is becoming a reef. The building is becoming a *body*.

You move through corridors that are half-flooded, half-air, the waterline at your waist, your chest, your chin—always shifting as you climb, as the thermal vents pulse and push, as the structure's slow metabolism breathes water in and out through fissures you cannot see. The scale is wrong. Rooms that were designed for ten people now hold forests. A former stairwell is a vertical garden, a column of layered growth where each level is a different ecosystem: the warm dark at the bottom, the cool twilight at the top, the transition zones where species negotiate their boundaries in chemical gradients you can taste on your tongue.

Sound 2.3. It is loud here.

Not with voices. The fauna—2.3, abundant—has filled every niche. Crustacean-things the size of your hand scuttle across the grown surfaces, their shells clicking against calcium deposits, a constant percussion. Fish, if you can call them that, drift through the flooded lower levels in schools that move as one organism, their passage creating pressure waves you feel in your sternum. Something larger patrols the deep shafts—you hear it sometimes, a *thrumming* in the structure itself, the vibration of a body moving through water-filled space, too big for any room, coiled around the building's central spine like a sleeping thing.

And the water itself makes sound. It rushes through pipes that are now arteries. It drips from growths that have no name. It *sings* in the narrow passages where flow creates standing waves, frequencies that resonate in the hollow spaces of the structure's bones, turning the whole place into an instrument played by temperature and pressure and time.

The built—2.3, still present, still *urban*—is not gone. It is *in* the vegetation now. Stairs you can still climb, though their railings are encrusted, overgrown, their original metal visible only in patches where something has scraped the growth away. Doorways, still rectangular, still *architectural* in their persistence, now frame not rooms but *gardens*. The geometry of human intention persists beneath the organic chaos, and you feel it constantly: the right angles, the level floors (settled, established, in-balance after centuries of slow adjustment), the deliberate narrowing of corridors, the hierarchy of spaces. Someone built this to *do* something. Process materials. Grow food. House workers. The purpose is unreadable now, but the *structure* of purpose remains, like a watermark, like a scar.

Tech 1.0. Primitive. But *weird* primitive.

You find tools. Not many. The water has taken most metal, transformed it, grown over it. But in the dry levels—there are still dry levels, pockets of air trapped in sealed compartments the vegetation hasn't learned to breath—there are things made of shell and bone and compressed plant-fiber. A blade, hafted in something that was once a crustacean's claw, the joint still articulating, still *useful*. A line, woven from the sinew of something that swam in the dark, still strong, still holding knots tied by hands that are dust now. The inhabitants of this place—the *current* inhabitants, the ones who have made this their world—use what the structure provides. They do not mine. They do not smelt. They *cultivate*, they *harvest*, they *adapt*. Their technology is biological, grown, symbiotic. It is older than human technology. It is *continuous* with the world.

0.9 weirdness. Unusual, but not *strange*. This place makes sense. It has its own logic, its own physics, its own ecology. You can learn it. You can *breathe* it, if you are patient, if you let your lungs adjust to the water's weight, if you stop expecting air.

The dynamic is slow. 0.8. Nothing here rushes.

The growth takes years. The erosion takes centuries. The structure settles, millimeter by millimeter, as the thermal vents shift their output, as the weight of accumulated biomass compresses the lower levels, as the chemistry of the water changes with each generation of life and death. The fauna moves through cycles you cannot perceive in a single visit—feeding, breeding, dying, becoming sediment, becoming *nutrient*, becoming new growth. The light pulses on a cycle of hours, not seconds. The temperature shifts across seasons that last years.

But you can *feel* the time. You feel it in the patience of the growth, the way the lichen does not hurry to cover a surface, the way the kelp-analogues sway in currents so slow they seem still until you watch long enough to see the drift. You feel it in the stillness between sounds, the long silences that are not empty but *full*—full of processes too slow for hearing, of chemistry, of pressure, of the deep patience of stone becoming soil becoming forest becoming, eventually, something else again.

The density is sparse. 0.9. There is space here. Vast vertical shafts where you can drift for minutes without touching anything but water. Rooms where the growth is thin, where the walls are still visible, where you can see the *shape* of what was built, the intention, the geometry. The fauna is abundant but *spread*—you encounter it, you pass it, you are alone again, suspended in water that holds you, that buoys you, that lets you move through three dimensions with the effort of walking.

And at the top—there is a top, though you cannot see it from below, the luminescence diffusing everything into green-gold twilight—there is a place where the water is shallow, where the original roof has cracked, where rain still falls, *actual rain*, from a sky that remembers being sky. The hundredth floor. The surface of the world, reached from below.

Here the kelp grows shorter, stunted by the violence of the interface. Here the water is fresh, shockingly, *thin*, and you can stand, finally, on a surface that is not growing, not yet, and look up at a sky that is grey and vast and *normal*, and feel the weirdness of it, the 0.9, the *unusual* rightness of open air after so long in the deep.

The rain falls. It is loud. It is the loudest thing you have heard.

And below you, the structure continues. The city continues. The slow, patient, growing, *drowned* world that has learned to live in the memory of human purpose, that has made of our architecture something new, something *itself*, something that will outlast us, that is already outlasting us, that will become, in time, simply *geology*, simply *habitat*, simply *the way things are*.

You are small here. 1.1 scale. A visitor in a room-sized world that is also a building-sized world that is also a *planet*-sized world, all at once, all depending on where you stand, what you can see, how long you stay.

The water holds you. The growth surrounds you. The structure remembers. And time, patient as only deep water can be patient, moves on.
