I want to stay with this place for a while, let it reveal itself slowly.

---

The place is *under*, though not deep. A hollowed volume in something older than stone—perhaps the calcified root-mass of a forest that died ten thousand years ago and became something like limestone, then something like bone, then something that *remembers* being wood. The walls are not carved. They are *grown*, or grown-over, by generations of builders who worked with what the hollow offered: stalactites that have been trained, coaxed, grafted into columns; stalagmites hollowed out and lined with packed clay; the floor undulating with mineral accretion that has been smoothed, then smoothed again, until it holds the memory of countless hands and feet.

You descend through a chimney of warm air. The temperature here sits in that narrow band where sweat does not yet form but the skin knows it could—where the body remains *alert* to moisture. Water moves through this place in pulses. There are no rivers, exactly, but there is water *somewhere*, and you hear it before you see it: a distant percussion, drop after drop finding basin after basin, each impact slightly different in pitch, building a rhythm that is almost but not quite pattern. Condensation gathers on every surface at certain hours. The walls weep. Not dramatically—just enough that the builders have learned to read the *direction* of the damp, to know which chambers will flood in the wet season and which will stay dry as a bone.

The scale is intimate. You could stand in the center of the largest chamber—the one they call the Lung, though it resembles less an organ than a ribcage that has been *occupied*—and touch opposite walls with outstretched arms. But the *density* is something else entirely. This is where the parameters pull against each other, and the place resolves it by *layering*. The Lung is not empty. It is full of *hangings*: thousands of them. Fabric, mostly—woven from the hair of the animals that live here, from the shed scales of something that molts, from a plant fiber that grows only in the deepest chamber where no light reaches and which must be harvested by feel. They hang at different lengths, creating a curtain-wall within the wall, a soft architecture inside the hard one. They move constantly with the slightest air current. They *breathe*.

And sound. The place is loud in a way that has nothing to do with volume. Every surface is *alive* with small noises. The dripping, yes—but also: the creak of mineral growth, almost imperceptible, the expansion and contraction of the walls as temperature shifts through the day; the scuttle of creatures moving through the hanging fabrics, which are not barriers but *habitats*; the resonance of voices, because there are always voices, carried strangely by the curved surfaces, arriving from directions that don't match their source. You will hear someone speaking from behind you who is actually three chambers away, their words transmitted through a series of water-filled tubes that the builders maintain for exactly this purpose—communication across distance in a place where line-of-sight is rare.

The fauna is abundant but *shaped* by the place. Nothing here is large. The largest creatures are perhaps dog-sized, and they have all been modified over generations—not by deliberate breeding, precisely, but by *selection*: the ones that could navigate the hangings, that could climb the trained stalactites, that could find purchase on the mineral floor. They are pale, mostly, or translucent, or *patterned* in ways that make them nearly invisible against the fabrics. They are not afraid of the builders. The relationship is older than fear. They eat the shed fiber, the dead plant matter that drifts down from above through ventilation shafts, the condensation that pools in the lowest basins. In return they provide hair, and scale, and a kind of *attention*—they notice things, movement, change, and their alarm calls travel differently through the space than human voices do, a higher register that can carry through the hangings without distortion.

The built-ness is total. There is no "nature" here in any recognizable sense. Even the water is *managed*—collected in cisterns carved into the ceiling where drips are encouraged, *trained*, made regular. Even the air is *managed*—there are bellows, crude but effective, worked by hand, that move air through the chambers in cycles that the builders have determined by long observation to be optimal for drying certain fabrics, for ripening certain foods, for keeping certain creatures in the states of activity that the community requires. The tools are simple: bone, wood, fiber, a metal that is worked cold and is *old*—older than the community, older than the memory of where it came from. They do not smelt. They do not need to. The metal is found, in nodules, in places where the water leaves its deposits, and it is *persuaded* into shape by patience and repetition.

And yet. The weirdness lives in the *unresolved*. There are chambers that the builders have sealed and do not speak of. There are hangings that move against the air current. There is a sound, in the deepest part of the wet season, that arrives from below—no one has found its source, though they have dug, carefully, in the direction it seems to come from. The creatures sometimes gather in those sealed spaces and press against the barriers, not in distress but in a kind of *waiting*, and when the barriers are opened—rarely, for ritual, for punishment, for death—what is found inside has always been *changed*, though no one can say precisely how.

The place is active. Nothing in it is finished. The hangings are constantly being rewoven, the old fiber becoming the new, in cycles that the builders track with a calendar of knots and notches. The walls grow, slowly, and are ground back, slowly. The water finds new paths, and the builders follow, *accommodating*, never fully controlling. There is a predator here, though "predator" is a word that fits poorly—something that lives in the deepest chamber, that the community feeds, that has never been seen clearly, that seems to be *made of the same materials as everything else* but arranged according to a pattern no one designed. It is not worshipped. It is *acknowledged*, in the way that one acknowledges a season, a tide, a wound that will not close.

You would not stay here long. The damp would find you, eventually, would settle into your bones in a way that the surface world can never quite remove. But you would remember the sound—the particular quality of loudness that comes from so many small living things, so much movement in a confined space, so much *attention* being paid to every surface, every drop, every breath of air moving through fabric and mineral and bone and something else, something that grows in the dark and is harvested by feel and woven into the walls that breathe.

The place has a name, in the language of the builders, but it does not translate. It is something like *the-hearing* or *the-being-listened-to*. It is accurate. You are listened to here, by everything, constantly, and the listening *changes* you. The hangings adjust to your presence. The creatures turn their pale faces toward you. The water, somewhere, finds a slightly different path. You are being *incorporated*, gently, into a system that was complete before you arrived and will continue, differently, after you leave—or stay, and become one of the hangings yourself, in time, your hair among the fibers, your voice among the resonances, your body finding its own hollow in the wall.

That is the place. That is the balance it has found.
