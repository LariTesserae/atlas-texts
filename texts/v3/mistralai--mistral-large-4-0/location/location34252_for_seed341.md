I'll follow the parameters where they lead, letting contradictions guide me toward something stranger.

## The Condensate City of Voss-Mere

You arrive at low tide, though no tide was announced. The city stands in a basin that has forgotten it was ever seabed—or perhaps remembers too well. Water here does not flow so much as *insist*. It seeps from every surface, every joint, every crack in the masonry that the builders sealed three times and the water found anyway, patient as rust.

The buildings are **industrial** in scale and ambition but **sparse** in their distribution—great hollow shells scattered across the wet plain like the shed teeth of some drowned leviathan. Seven structures in view, perhaps twelve if you count what's half-submerged in the permanent mist. They were built for something: vast halls with gantry cranes still mounted to their ceilings, conveyor systems frozen mid-spasm, smokestacks that weep condensation rather than smoke. The machinery is **electrified** in a way that shouldn't function—cables draped through standing water, transformers humming in rooms where the floor is a pond, lights that flicker on at dusk in patterns that suggest *intention* rather than circuitry.

The **temperature** presses against you. Hot, but a wet heat, the kind that makes your clothes cling and your skin feel *porous*. Yet the **light** remains **dim**—the sun exists somewhere above this, a pale coin behind milk-glass clouds that never break. The sky is the color of old bruises. Everything glows from within instead: bioluminescent seepage in the gutters, phosphorescent stains on concrete, the slow pulse of something organic colonizing the lower walls.

And the **sound**.

It is **loud** here without source. Not industrial noise—not the shriek of metal or the cough of engines, though those exist in recordings, in maintenance bays where workers once moved through knee-deep water in boots that no longer exist. The dominant sound is *water itself*—not dripping, which implies intervals, but a continuous **hiss and murmur**, the sound of water moving through water, of water finding equilibrium, of water *negotiating* with surfaces. Above this: the groan of settling foundations, the creak of thermal expansion in structures that cannot decide if they are sinking or floating. Somewhere, a pump runs. It has been running, you realize, for longer than the city has stood. It pumps water from the lower levels to the upper, and the water returns, and the pump does not stop, and no one remembers turning it on.

**Vegetation** is **lush** but wrong. Moss the color of old jade carpets the north faces of buildings. Ferns grow in the ventilation shafts, in the control panels, in the collars of abandoned protective gear. There are trees—willows, perhaps, or something that mimics them—but they grow *horizontally* in the low places, their trunks parallel to the ground, roots drinking from the saturated air itself. No birds. No insects you can see. The green things photosynthesize in the dim light, or they *pretend* to, their slow metabolism matching the slow drowning of everything else.

The **erosion** is **established**, which means the city has reached its own strange equilibrium. The water does not destroy so much as *transcribe*. It carries minerals from the concrete and deposits them elsewhere, building new formations—stalactites in the atriums, flowstone in the stairwells, a slow geology of human ambition being rewritten by patience. Rust blooms in patterns that resemble circuit diagrams. The boundaries between built and natural have dissolved; the city is becoming *karst*, becoming *reef*, becoming something that water prefers to air.

You walk the central causeway—raised, mercifully, above the general flood level—and feel the **scale** of the place. It is **small**, a hamlet that dreamed of being a metropolis, or a metropolis that shrank. The buildings are cathedral-sized but few. The spaces between them are vast, filled with mist and the sound of water and the occasional *structure* that makes no sense: a wall standing alone, a staircase ascending to nothing, a door in the middle of a field of reeds that opens onto a flooded machine shop.

The **weirdness** accumulates without climax. You find a break room where the coffee machine still dispenses something dark and steaming, though the pot has been running for decades. You find a dormitory where the beds are made, the sheets *damp*, as if someone recently rose from them—or as if someone is *about to*. You find a control panel with a single working light, green, labeled in a script you almost recognize. The label reads: **STILL CONDENSING**.

There is no animal life. The **fauna** parameter sits at near-zero, yet the city *breathes*. The water level rises and falls with a rhythm that matches no moon, no tide chart. The mist thickens and thins. The lights flicker in patterns that might be communication, might be malfunction, might be *hunger*. The pump runs. The water moves. The green things grow slowly toward the light that never quite arrives.

You are not meant to stay. The city does not welcome you, but it does not expel you. It is too busy with its own processes—the **dynamic** of saturation and evaporation, of construction and slow dissolution, of industrial ambition becoming geological patience. You are a temporary eddy in its flow, a brief disturbance in water that has learned to incorporate disturbance.

When you leave—if you leave, if the causeway still leads out, if the mist parts to show you the way—you carry the damp with you. Your clothes never fully dry. You hear the hiss of water in quiet rooms. You dream of pumps that run forever, of green things growing in the dark, of a city that decided to become wet and has been succeeding for longer than anyone remembers.

The basin holds its water. The water holds its city. The city holds its silence, broken only by the sound of itself, still condensing, still becoming.
