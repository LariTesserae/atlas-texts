I want to start with the tension in these numbers: extreme elevation, yet submerged. Total construction, yet ancient and eroded to near-flatness. A hidden place, pocket-scaled, embedded within something larger—and loud.

---

This is the Cistern of Sorrows, or rather, a chamber of it. You know you are high because the water pressure tastes of altitude in your teeth, a metallic bright edge behind the tongue. The water is not ocean here. It is *held* water, rain that fell ten thousand years ago onto a surface that no longer exists, now kept in suspension by machinery older than the memory of machinery. The walls sweat it. The air above—there is a thin skin of air, perhaps four meters, between water surface and ceiling—carries the smell of ozone and warm stone.

You are inside something built. Every surface is made. The floor is not rock. It is a lattice of ceramic composite, grown in kilns that have since been dismantled for fuel, their heat recycled into the water itself. The walls are laminated strata of compressed sediment and polymer, pressed flat by erosion so complete that the original tool-marks, the original *intention* of their making, have been worn into a sheen like skin. You can press your palm against them and feel nothing but temperature: warm, always warm, 2.0 on whatever scale measures the fever of a place.

The elevation shows in the light. Normal light, yes, but *thinned* light. At this height, even underwater, the spectrum shifts. Colors arrive muted, biased toward blue-green, as if viewed through old glass. The 1.6 is honest: the sun would burn here if the water did not filter it, if the machinery did not shade it, if the whole pocket did not hang in some kind of atmospheric fold, a depression in the world where altitude and depth have traded places.

Sound is the strangeness you hear first. Loud, 2.1, but not with chaos—with *layering*. The water itself makes a sound like breathing, a slow systole and diastole of pressure valves releasing somewhere above, somewhere you cannot see. There is machinery, industrial, 1.9: pumps that have run so long their housings have grown calcified, their vibrations tuned to a frequency that matches the resonance of the chamber, so the whole place hums at a pitch that lives in your sternum. And there is biological sound, abundant fauna at 1.7, though you do not see them easily. They are pale things, mostly, adapted to the dim: fish that have forgotten eyes, replaced with lateral lines that read pressure the way you read text. They move in loose schools through the sparse water, 0.8 density, so much space between presences that you can watch one pass and wait, wait, for the next, and the waiting becomes a rhythm, becomes part of the loudness.

Vegetation is lush, 2.2, but not green in any way you would recognize. It grows in the shallows, in the thin places where the constructed floor meets the constructed wall, roots finding purchase in the seams between strata. It is a kelp-analog, a colony-organism that photosynthesizes through chemosynthesis, through the heat of the machinery, through the strange metabolism of the place. It grows in sheets, in curtains, in the negative spaces of the architecture. It is warm to touch. It hums too, at a different frequency, and where the two sounds meet, interference patterns make your vision stutter.

The scale hides you. This is a pocket. Above, below, around, the world continues at its normal violence: storms, erosion, the slow grind of continents. Here, in this made room within the made mountain, time has settled. The erosion is ancient, 2.8, not because the place is old in years—it may be only centuries, or millennia, we do not have records—but because it has reached its final form. The water has worn the ceramic to river-stone smoothness. The machinery has worn itself to the essential, the irreducible, the humming core. The vegetation has grown to fill every niche, then stopped, reached equilibrium, become *climax* in a bottle. Nothing here will change except in the slow metabolism of maintenance, the replacement of parts that fail, the endless, patient, warm, loud, submerged work of keeping this pocket exactly as it is.

Weirdness, 1.8, lives in the details you notice too late. The water is not still. It moves in currents you cannot see, driven by thermal gradients from the machinery, and these currents carry objects: a gear, dropped and forgotten, now wrapped in vegetation; a boot, ceramic, from a worker who fell or jumped or was pushed; a mirror, still reflective in patches, that shows you your own face distorted, elongated, *adapted* as if you have already begun to change. The fauna have no fear of you. They have never known a surface, a shore, a sky. They are born in the dark, they die in the dark, and they investigate you with the same neutral curiosity they would show a new rock, a new leak, a new temperature fluctuation.

You are here. You arrived by a shaft that has since closed, or opened, or you do not remember. The air is breathable, barely, filtered and humidified and warm. You can stay for hours. Days. The water will not rise. The ceiling will not fall. The machinery will continue its endless, patient, industrial song, and the vegetation will continue its slow growth and slower decay, and the fauna will continue their drifting, their feeding, their reproduction in the warm dark.

This is a place that has forgotten it was made. Or perhaps it never knew. Perhaps the distinction, here, at this elevation, in this water, in this light, is not meaningful. Perhaps the ancient and the constructed have finally, after all this erosion, all this settling, become the same substance: warm, wet, loud, and strangely, perfectly, *held* in balance with the emptiness around it.

You hear a new sound. A valve, opening. Water moves. The kelp shifts. Somewhere, a fish turns, and watches you with its absence of eyes, and you understand that you are not the strangeness here. You are simply the newest thing to be worn smooth.
