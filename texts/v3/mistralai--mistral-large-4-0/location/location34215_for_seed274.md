The place is called, by no one, the **Hollow That Drinks and Gives Nothing Back**. It sits in a coastal depression, barely above the water table, warmed by the thermal mass of the surrounding rock and by something else—a slow exothermic reaction in the deeper strata, perhaps, or simply the retained heat of a world that was warmer when this chamber formed.

You enter through a passage that is almost too small. The ceiling is wet. Not dripping—**weeping**. The stone expresses water continuously, a film that flows nowhere, that simply *is* on the surface, held there by surface tension and by the absolute stillness of the air. The air is so saturated that your skin prickles with condensation. Your clothes, if you brought any, become damp without ever being wet, never drying.

The walls are not stone, not exactly. They are **precipitate**. Layer upon layer of mineral deposited from the same water that now coats them, in a balance so complete that the chamber has not changed in ten thousand years, in fifty thousand, in however long it takes for salt and gypsum and the trace minerals of deep brine to reach this perfect equilibrium of dissolution and deposition.

This is the density. The chamber is **packed with growth**. Not vegetation—there is no vegetation, there has never been vegetation, the brine is too saturated with itself for anything to metabolize it, for any cell to find purchase in water that is already fully *occupied* by the mineral. But the walls are thick with **salt flowers**, with **gypsum needles**, with crusts of **calcite** that catch the dim light and throw it back in pale, diffuse radiance. The space is small—you could touch both walls with outstretched arms in most places—but it feels smaller, because the mineral growth reduces the open volume, turns the chamber into a **thicket of stone**, a maze of curtains and pillars and draperies grown from the same water that fills the air.

The pools are shallow. There are seven of them, or perhaps seven that you can see, arranged in a pattern that might be natural or might be **ordered**. The water in them is perfectly still. No ripple ever crosses them. If you disturb the surface, the disturbance does not spread—it **stays**, a localized dimple that holds its shape, that slowly, over hours, reasserts the flatness of the pool without ever quite achieving it, or perhaps achieving it only when you are not watching.

In one pool, in the deepest one, there is movement. A **brine shrimp**, pale, almost transparent, its body the size of a grain of rice. It does not swim in loops or dart for food. It performs a single motion, repeated: a flick of its appendages, a slight translation forward, a pause. It has been performing this motion since before the chamber reached its present form. It will perform it after the chamber changes, which it will not, which it has not. The shrimp is the only animal here. There may be others, in crevices you cannot reach, in the deepest saturated brine where no light comes. They are **rare** because they are **few**, because the chamber supports only what it has always supported, no more, no less.

The structures are few. In the largest open area, where the ceiling rises to a dome perhaps four meters across, there is a **platform** of flat salt-slabs, fitted together with a precision that primitive tools could achieve and no more. It is a bed, or a place to sit, or a place to put things. Nearby, half-buried in a wall of gypsum, there is a **ledge** that holds three objects: a stone knife, its edge worn smooth by use against softer material; a lump of charcoal, black and unmarked; and a **shell**, empty, from some sea that no longer connects to this place, placed there with the care of an offering or a memory.

Higher on the wall, at shoulder height, there are **marks**. Not paintings—**incisions**, made with the knife or something like it, scraped into the mineral crust. They are simple: lines, mostly. Parallel lines. A grid, almost. They do not represent anything. They are **counting**, perhaps, or they are **waiting**, or they are simply the record of a hand that moved in a space with nothing else to do.

The light comes from above, through a **chimney** no wider than a wrist, that connects to a surface you cannot see. It is always dim, always the same dimness, green-tinged from the mineral dust in the air, from the trace of copper or iron in the brine that has never precipitated, never will. There is no day or night here. The light does not change. It is **enough** to see by, if you wait, if you let your eyes adjust to the perpetual dusk.

The sound is **absence**. You hear your own breathing, too loud. You hear the **hum of saturation**, a frequency below hearing that you feel in your teeth, in the moisture of your eyes. Once, or perhaps continuously, you hear a **tick**—the sound of mineral accretion, a crystal adding a layer, so slow that the sound is theoretical, reconstructed by your mind from the certainty of growth. But growth, here, is **balance**. The tick is the sound of nothing changing.

The temperature is warm. Not hot. The warmth is **held**, retained by the water in the air, by the thermal mass of the surrounding rock, by the slow exothermic processes deep below that have warmed this place since before anything walked on the surface above. You do not shiver. You do not sweat. You exist in a **comfortable wet**, a warmth that penetrates without urgency.

There are no plants. This is the strangest thing, and also the most natural, once you accept the chemistry. The brine is **supersaturated**. It holds more mineral than water should hold, at this temperature, at this pressure, in a state of metastable perfection that life cannot enter. A seed would dissolve. A spore would precipitate, become part of the crust, be **consumed** by the very process that builds the walls. There is no rot here, because there is no death. There is only **accretion**, only the slow addition of mineral to mineral, the chamber growing inward by fractions of a millimeter per century, a process so slow that the builders of the platform would not have noticed, would not have cared, would have known only that the walls were **alive**, in a way, that they **breathed** moisture, that they were never the same from one visit to the next, even though they never changed.

And they haven't. The platform is old. The knife is old. The shell is older still, perhaps, than the chamber itself—carried here, dropped, forgotten, **incorporated** into the general process of waiting. The marks on the wall are deeper than they appear, the mineral having grown around them, **preserving** them in a way that no intention could achieve.

This is a place of **equilibrium**. It does not change. It does not progress. It is not dying, because it was never alive in the way that things die. It is not growing, because it has reached the form where growth and dissolution are the same motion, viewed from different scales. It is **settled**, in the way that a crystal is settled, that a pool of still water is settled, that a held breath is settled before it is released.

You stay for a time that doesn't register. Hours, perhaps, or minutes. The shrimp performs its single motion. The light holds its green dimness. The walls weep, and do not weep, and are wet, and are dry, and are **both**, in the way that a thing can be both when time has stopped moving through it.

When you leave—if you leave—you take the moisture with you, on your skin, in your hair, in the condensation that forms on your glasses, on your metal, on anything you carried. You dry slowly, in the world outside, and for a day or two you carry the **hum** in your teeth, the below-hearing frequency of saturation, of a place that drinks and gives nothing back, that holds its breath forever, that is **packed** with the presence of stone and water and the rare, motionless life that moves without moving, that waits without time, that is **here**, and has always been, and will be, unchanged, unchanged, unchanged, until the world above forgets it, or rises, or drowns it in the slow, warm, mineral future that is this place's only future, which is also its eternal present, which is also its **nothing**, which is also, for the shrimp, for the marks, for the charcoal and the knife and the shell, **everything**.
