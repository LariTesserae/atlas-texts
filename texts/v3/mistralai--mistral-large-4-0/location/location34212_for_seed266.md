I'll let this place emerge from its parameters — high, warm, dry, vast, with life and industry and strangeness but also quiet and slow.

---

The world is a plateau. Not a plateau on a planet — the plateau *is* the world, or as much of it as is solid. It rises through layers of atmosphere that grow thin and strange, and what sky there is beyond belongs to a gas giant's distant ember, a dim orange coin that never sets properly because the plateau curves away from it more slowly than any world should. The high places catch light for hours after the low places have given up.

The surface is **ferroceramic** — not stone, not metal, but something grown in the long heat between the planet's formation and some later violence that scraped away everything soft. It has the color of cooled slag and the texture of a tongue. You can walk for days and find no soil, only this: a crust of sintered minerals that rings differently underfoot depending on how deep the fire went beneath it. Where the crust is thin, the warmth comes through. The warm parameter, 1.8 — it is a *geothermal* warmth, not solar. The thin places are warm. The thick places are merely temperate. The deep places, where the crust has heaped itself into ridges and sills and the slow architecture of elevation, are cool enough to touch without pain.

There is no water. None. This is not a desert that remembers water. The chemistry here was never aqueous. The minerals leach together through **thermochemical** processes — dry, patient, involving no liquid phase — and what moves through the crust moves as superheated gas, as ions in solid-state diffusion, as the slow plastic deformation of rock under its own weight over millennia. When the ferroceramic crust cracks, it does not bleed. It *vent*s. Columns of mineral vapor rise, cool, precipitate in strange drusy formations that the inhabitants call **frost** though it is not cold, not water, nothing so simple. The frost grows in the vent-shadows, in the places where the rising gas meets the cooler upper crust. It is harvested. It is traded. It is the only currency that matters in the deep places, where the built structures go down rather than up.

The elevation is extreme — 2.8 — and this shapes everything. The atmosphere is thin enough that sound behaves strangely. The sound parameter, 1.2 — mostly quiet — but the quiet is *textured*. Voices carry far but lose their high frequencies, so a shout from across a valley arrives as a basso murmur, recognizable as speech but not as words. The industrial processes here are mostly silent by design, or by necessity: combustion is difficult in thin air, so the great engines are **pressure-differential motors**, **thermocline turbines**, things that run on the temperature contrast between the warm depths and the cold heights, turning the slow breathing of the planet itself into steady rotation. They hum at frequencies below hearing. You feel them in your sternum. The cities are quiet. This is strange to visitors. The cities are vast and they are quiet.

The built parameter, 1.8 — urban, settlement-scale, organized-construction — but built *into* rather than *upon*. The ferroceramic crust is too hard to excavate with anything less than industrial-scale thermal lances, so the construction follows the natural architecture: the **sills** and **laccoliths**, the places where the crust has heaved upward in slow domes, are hollowed out from within. The cities are **endocarpic**. You enter through **fumarole-throats**, descending through vents that have been widened and stabilized, and find yourself in chambers where the warmth is held, where the air is thick enough to breathe without apparatus, where the walls sweat frost that is collected and carried away in ceramic conduits. The infrastructure is **vertical**. The deep places are warm and crowded. The high places are cold and empty and given over to machinery that needs the cold — the condensers, the heat-sinks, the great radiative arrays that are the only "windows" most inhabitants ever see, facing outward toward the gas giant's ember, toward the thin dark where the first of the orbital structures hang like frozen sperm against the dimness.

The scale, 2.6 — vast, continental, planet-scale — means the plateau has **weather of its own**. Not meteorology as you would know it. There is no water cycle. But there is a **mineral cycle**: the slow sublimation of deep crust, the transport of vapor through fissure-networks, the precipitation in cooler zones, the tectonic — no, not tectonic, the **isostatic** — the slow flow of warm rock upward and cool rock downward, a convection that takes millions of years and manifests as **weather** on the timescale of centuries. A "storm" here is a **thermal surge**, a pulse of superheated gas that floods a valley, that flash-precipitates frost in drifts meters deep, that kills anything not sheltered. The inhabitants have **surge-calendars**. They know the deep places, the places where the crust is thin, and they build there anyway, because the warmth is worth the risk. The erosion, 2.3 — established, settled-form, in-its-own-state — means these cycles have been running long enough to reach equilibrium. The plateau is not young. The plateau is not old. The plateau is **in its own state**, a steady-state that will persist for geological ages and then, perhaps, change on a timescale no one will witness.

The fauna, 1.9 — abundant — is not animal, not plant, not fungus. The vegetation parameter, 0.2 — no plant life — doesn't mean no life. It means no *recognized* biology. The things that live here are **lithovores**, or the inhabitants call them that, though they do not eat in any way you would recognize. They are **colonies of thermal-differential organisms**, crystalline structures that grow at the interface between warm and cool, that exploit the temperature gradient to drive chemical reactions that would not otherwise proceed, that build themselves from the mineral vapor in the same way that your life builds itself from carbon and water. They are slow. The dynamic parameter, 1.0 — slow, gradual cycles, patient processes — applies to everything here. A lithovore colony might take a thousand years to grow the size of your fist. The great **reef-structures** in the deep warm places, the places where the crust is thinnest and the thermal gradient steepest, are lithovore colonies that have been growing since before the cities, that will outlast them. They are **farmed**. The frost-precipitation is encouraged in their presence. They are **herded** across the deep valleys on the slow timescale of thermal currents. They are, in their way, the only agriculture this world has ever known.

The weirdness, 2.2 — strange — is not decoration. It is structural. The lithovores are **distributed intelligences**. No single colony is a mind. But colonies that have grown together, that have established **thermal bridges** between their crystalline structures, that have achieved a kind of **resonance** in their chemical signaling — these networks *think*, in a way that is not thought, that is too slow and too diffuse to be cognition as you would recognize it. The cities are built around these networks. The oldest parts of the deep places, the places where the crust is warm enough for the lithovores to be active and mobile, are not exactly architecture and not exactly organism. They are **negotiated spaces**. The inhabitants — the **pressure-born**, the things that evolved in the industrial warmth of the endocarpic cities, the things that are neither lithovore nor anything else, that breathe the thick warm air and think in the fast timescale of chemical gradients across cell membranes — they have a **diplomacy** with the lithovore networks. They do not command them. They **attune** to them. The oldest cities have walls that are not built but **grown**, lithovore colonies that have been encouraged, shaped, in over centuries, into structures that serve human purposes. The boundary between built and living is, here, deliberately blurred. The weirdness is **domesticated**. It is still strange. You will still feel it. But it is not hostile. It is *habitable strangeness*, the strangeness of a world that has had time to make its strangeness into home.

The density, 1.4 — sparse — is a function of scale and necessity. The plateau is vast. The habitable zones — the warm places, the places with breathable density of atmosphere, the places where the thermal gradient is steep enough to support industry — are scattered across it like **islands in an ocean of cool crust**. The distances between them are measured not in kilometers but in **journeys**, in the time it takes to descend from one warm depth to the next, to cross the cold high places where the air is too thin and the machinery too exposed. The pressure-born travel in **caravans** of **thermocline gliders**, vehicles that ride the temperature differentials like your birds ride thermals, that descend slowly through the warm air, that rise on the venting gas, that cross the high cold places in a kind of **falling**, a controlled descent that takes days. The trade between cities is slow. The news is slow. A war, if there were wars — and there have been, in the deep past, when the attunement to the lithovore networks failed, when the grown walls turned hostile, when the frost-harvests failed — would take generations to spread. The peace, such as it is, is the peace of **geological time**.

The light, 1.7 — normal light, or what passes for it. The gas giant's ember provides illumination that is dim but steady, orange-red, the color of deep heat. The upper atmosphere, thin as it is, scatters it into a perpetual **twilight**, though there is no sun to set, no diurnal cycle. The cities have their own light — **frost-lamps**, ceramic vessels filled with slowly sublimating minerals that glow with the strange phosphorescence of their chemical state, that are tended like hearths, that mark the hours of the slow day, the **thermal day**, the cycle of warmth and cool that is the only clock here. The light is enough. You would adjust. Everything adjusts.

You stand on the edge of a city. The ferroceramic crust stretches away in all directions, cooling as it rises toward the high places, warm again in the valleys where the deep heat comes close to the surface. The endocarpic city is behind you, the fumarole-throat descending into warmth and thickness and the slow phosphorescence of the frost-lamps. Ahead, the crust rises toward a **sill**, a dome of upheaved rock where the lithovore reefs grow in their slow profusion, where the frost precipitates in drifts that shift and change across the centuries. A glider is descending toward you, riding a thermal, its hull gleaming dully in the ember-light. It will pass. It will not stop. There is no hurry. There is no hurry anywhere in this world. The hurry was in the making, in the formation, in the long violence that scraped away the water and left this dry warmth, this patient chemistry, this slow life that grows in the dark of the deep places and thinks in the time of stone.

You breathe. The air is thin, but it is enough. The warmth comes up through your boots, through the ferroceramic, from the depths where the planet is still cooling, where the heat that built this world is still working, still making, still strange.

This is the plateau. This is the place where water never was, and something else grew instead.
