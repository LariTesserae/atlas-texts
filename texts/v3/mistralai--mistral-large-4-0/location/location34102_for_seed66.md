# The Drowned Works

## Water: The Memory of Oceans

This is a world where water was once *everything* and now is merely *everywhere*. The highlands—1.6 on your scale, though "high" here means *above the breath*—are a raised wound in the planet's skin, and through it water seeps upward as much as falls down. The atmosphere carries 1.7 parts wet to every part dry, a perpetual pre-rain condition without the relief of rain. Droplets form on every surface by day, evaporate by the strange cool of altitude, form again. The rocks weep. Not metaphorically. Water emerges from their pores, their fissures, their ancient compressed strata, finding paths through mineral that should be solid.

At this elevation, the water does not pool so much as *adhere*. It finds surfaces, clings in films, descends in rivulets that never quite achieve the freedom of streams. There are no rivers here. There are *sheets*—broad, slow, braided flows that move across the built surfaces at the pace of glaciers, which is to say: the pace of centuries, which is to say: here, you can watch them move if you watch long enough.

The condensation is a world of its own. Mist gathers in the valleys between structures, in the low places of the terrain, in the spaces between machines that have been left to themselves long enough to become terrain again. Visibility shifts. A structure twenty meters distant may dissolve into grey; a structure two kilometers distant may emerge from clear air like a memory of itself.

## Vegetation: The Absence That Persists

There are no plants. This is not a world that has *lost* plants—it is a world where the niche of plant has never been filled, or was filled by something that dissolved into the mineral substrate so completely that no trace remains. The 0.3 is generous. What exists is *pre-vegetation*: colonies of something that might be fungal, might be crystalline, might be a chemistry of surfaces that reproduces without cells. It grows on the north faces of structures, in the perpetually damp. It is grey-green, or green-grey, or the color of wet stone. It does not photosynthesize. It does not need to. The energy here comes from elsewhere.

What grows is slow. A colony might advance a centimeter in a year. It leaves no leaf-fall, no seasonal cycle, no explosive reproduction. It is *patient* in the way that 0.8 dynamic demands: the world changes across durations that make human construction seem impulsive, seem almost violent in its haste.

## The Built: Cities of the Wet

Humanity—or whatever builds at this scale—has made a civilization of condensation. The structures rise from the highland plateau in layers, in terraces, in the accreted architecture of 2.5 erosion: not new, not ruined, but *settled*. Each generation built on the last, and the water has been present through all of it, so that every surface carries the stain of its history, the mineral bloom of its age, the particular patina of wet-dry-wet-dry across centuries.

The density is 1.5, but it feels higher. The structures are near-touching, layered, *imbricated* like scales. There are streets that are not streets—covered ways, arcades, the spaces between buildings that have been roofed over and become interior without losing their exterior quality. The city breathes through these passages. Air moves. Water drips from ceiling to floor in places, and people have learned to walk with care, to read the drip-patterns, to know which passages flood and which merely weep.

The architecture is industrial in its bones—2.3 built, 1.9 tech—but the industry has been *absorbed*. Engines still turn somewhere below, in the deep places where the water has not reached, where the heat of the planet's interior keeps machines running that were built when the surfaces were dry. But the visible city is post-industrial in its feel, or *trans*-industrial: the machinery has become landscape, has become *habitat*. Pipes have become cliffs. Cooling towers have become mesas. The distinctions have dissolved.

## Fauna: The Overwhelming

And then the animals.

2.7 is not an exaggeration. The fauna here are *many*, and they are *everywhere*, and they have adapted to a world where water is not a resource to be sought but a medium to be moved through, a condition to be *inhabited*. They are not fish, though some resemble fish. They are not amphibians, though some resemble amphibians. They are the *third thing*: creatures that evolved in a world of wet stone and condensing air, where the boundary between water and land was never sharp, where the high ground was a place to *retreat* from rather than to *occupy*.

They move in schools, in swarms, in *currents* of their own making. A passage that seems empty may fill with them in seconds, a grey-gold cloud of bodies that parts around your legs, that flows past, that continues. They are filter-feeders, mostly, harvesting the microbial life that colonizes every wet surface. They are *loud*—the 1.7 sound is partly their doing: the clicking, the humming, the wet percussion of bodies against stone, the chorus that rises at certain temperatures, certain humidities, certain phases of the slow weather.

They do not fear humans. Or they do not *flee*. They have learned the architecture as humans have learned them. They nest in the structures, in the spaces too small for human use, the crawlways and the sumps and the places where water collects and must be pumped away. When the pumps fail—and they fail, sometimes, for days—the creatures *rise*. They occupy the lower levels. Humans retreat upward, or learn to walk through the swarming, through the tickle of bodies against skin, through the strange intimacy of being *among* rather than *above*.

There are larger ones. The *leviathans* of the deep sumps, the places where water has accumulated for centuries, where the pressure is different, where the chemistry has diverged. They are rare. They are *old*. They do not interact with the surface world except in dreams, in the stories told in the upper passages, in the occasional body that washes out after a flood and is never quite identified.

## The Dynamic: The Slow Weather

Everything here moves slowly, or seems to. The 0.8 dynamic is not stillness—it is *patience*. The world has its own weather, its own climate, at the 2.6 scale. The highland plateau is large enough to generate its own patterns: the mist that gathers in the eastern valleys each morning, the thermal currents that carry spores and small creatures from structure to structure, the slow rotation of air masses that takes days to complete a circuit of a single mesa-city.

Change comes in *cycles* that are geological in their patience. The water table rises and falls across decades. Structures that were dry become wet; structures that were wet become *submerged*, become part of the underwater city that exists below the visible one, that is mapped in fragments, that is *remembered* in the oral histories of the deep-dwelling communities. Nothing is built to last forever, but everything is built to last *long enough*, and the water ensures that "long enough" is always being redefined.

Erosion is 2.5: the world is in balance with itself. The water wears the stone; the stone resists; the structures intervene, redirect, *accelerate* in some places, *protect* in others. The result is a landscape that is neither natural nor artificial but *negotiated*, a continuous conversation between the wet and the built, the living and the mineral, the fast and the slow.

## Atmosphere: The Feeling of It

To stand here—on a terrace, say, where the mist has not yet gathered, where the normal light (2.3) filters through the condensing air and makes everything slightly luminous, slightly *soft*—is to feel the weight of water in everything. The air is thick with it, *saturated* in the technical sense, in the felt sense. You breathe it. Your skin drinks it. Your clothes, if you wear them, become damp without rain, become *alive* with the moisture that the air insists on depositing.

It is warm—1.5—but the warmth is *held* by the humidity, is *carried* by the water in the air. It is not the warmth of desert, of dry heat that radiates away. It is the warmth of sauna, of greenhouse, of the tropics without the sun. You sweat. The sweat does not evaporate. It accumulates. You learn to dress for it, to move for it, to *become* it in your own small way.

And the sound: 1.7, loud, but *layered*. The drip of water, constant, everywhere, the percussion of a million small impacts. The hum of machinery, distant, *felt* more than heard in the lower frequencies that travel through stone and water. The chorus of the small creatures, rising and falling with temperature and moisture. The human voices, carried strangely by the wet air, *close* even when distant, *present* even when unseen. And underneath it all, the *movement* of the fauna in their thousands, the wet rushing of their passage through the ways and channels and streets, the sound of a city that is also a habitat, that is also an ecosystem, that is *alive* in ways that the builders did not intend and the inhabitants have learned to accept.

## The Weirdness: What Remains Strange

0.8 is not high, but it is *persistent*. The weirdness here is not spectacular—no impossible geometries, no visible violations of physics. It is the weirdness of *scale* and *duration*. The weirdness of a civilization that has persisted long enough to become geological. The weirdness of creatures that have adapted to a world where the boundary between water and air is a *choice* rather than a condition. The weirdness of the underwater city, glimpsed sometimes in a sump, sometimes in a flooded level, sometimes in the stories of those who have been down and come back *changed*, speaking of pressure and darkness and the *other* ecology that exists in the deep places.

There is a quality of *uncanny* to the repetition. The same structures, seen from different angles, in different lights, in different states of wetness. The same creatures, behaving in patterns that are *almost* predictable, that *almost* make sense. The same weather, cycling through its slow phases, *almost* the same from year to year, but not quite, never quite, always *drifting* toward something new.

And the water, always the water, *remembering* its former dominance, *waiting* for the conditions that might restore it, *patient* in a way that the built world is not, cannot be, *enduring* in a way that outlasts every intention of those who built upon it, who built *in* it, who became, in the end, part of its slow, wet, *living* history.

---

*This is the Drowned Works: a highland city in a world of persistent water, where the mineral and the built and the living have achieved a balance that is not peace but is not war either—only the long, slow, humid process of becoming something else, something that will outlast the memory of what came before, something that is already, in its way, eternal.*
