# The High Hollows of Varn

## Where the World Has Settled Its Own Score

---

You climb for hours through a world that has forgotten it was ever other than this.

At first, the land rises in fits—steep shoulders of frost-shattered stone, the kind of brokenness that happens when water finds its way into cracks and convinces rock, patiently, over millions of years, that it was never meant to be solid. But here, at elevation 2.7 on whatever scale measures such things, the climbing becomes *thinning*. The mountains are not dramatic anymore. They have been worn down to something like intention—ridges that persist not through defiance but through *habit*, through the sheer oldest-stable-state obstinacy of matter that has reached some kind of equilibrium with its own decay.

The erosion is the first thing you notice. Not the violence of it, but its *completion*. Every surface has been planed, rounded, smoothed by processes so slow they have become indistinguishable from geology itself. The stone here is not grey, exactly. It is the color of old bone, of river-silt dried in sun, of something that has been *lived with*. Water has passed over these surfaces so many times, in so many forms—mist, trickle, the memory of glaciers—that it has left a patina like a signature. You can run your hand along a boulder and feel where the water *considered* flowing a different way, where it hesitated, where it finally chose and was chosen in return.

## The Water That Remembers

Water here is *intermittent*. It does not flow so much as *reappear*.

There are no rivers in the classical sense. Instead: condensation. The elevation is high enough, the temperature cool enough (0.6, that particular thinness of cold that makes breath visible in permanent suggestion), that moisture gathers on every surface during the long dim hours, pools in the hollows between stone shoulders, persists in pockets that the sun—when it finds them through breaks in the ever-present haze—cannot quite reach.

These are *dew-basins*. Shallow depressions in the ancient stone, maybe a hand's width across, sometimes wider, lined with something that is not quite soil but has become soil-adjacent: the accumulated bodies of lichens, of the first mosses, of microscopic lives that have colonized the water's brief appearances. In the deepest basins, where shade persists longest, you find *standing water* that has achieved a kind of permanence through sheer stubbornness. It is never more than a few centimeters deep. It is never clear. Something is always growing in it, something is always dying, and the boundary between those two states is the only chemistry that matters here.

The rain, when it comes, is not dramatic. It comes as *more*. A deepening of the damp. A confirmation that the condensation has been right all along. The stone drinks it. The dew-basins overflow, briefly, into channels that have no names because they have no permanence—water finding its way downhill through paths it will not remember, through cracks it will slightly widen, through the endless patient work of being *present*.

Then the rain stops. The channels dry. The basins contract to their stubborn cores. And the world returns to its primary state: *waiting*, *holding*, *almost*.

## The Green That Survives

Vegetation is lush, but this is a lushness of *concentration* rather than abundance.

You will not find forests. The elevation forbids it, the thin cold, the stone that offers no purchase for roots seeking depth. What grows here grows in *layers*, in *mats*, in the dense near-touching density of things that have learned to share what little there is.

The primary green is *lichen*—but not the crusty kind of lower elevations. Here, at this altitude, in this light that filters through perpetual haze (1.3, dim, the sun a suggestion rather than a presence), lichens grow in forms that are almost *architectural*. They build. They construct small towers of their own substance, fruticose forms that rise centimeters above the stone, that create their own microclimates, their own pockets of stillness where moisture persists slightly longer than in the open.

Between the lichen-towers, in the *dense* spaces, grow the mosses. *Sphagnum* in the wettest basins, holding water like a sponge that has forgotten how to release it, building up over centuries into *peat* that is not quite soil but will become soil if the world continues its slow settling. Other mosses in the drier zones—* cushion mosses* that form surfaces you can press your palm against and feel the slight give of millions of tiny plants, each one a universe of retained moisture, each one contributing to the general *damp* that defines this place.

And then: the *flowering things*. Rare, but *intense*. Small, because everything here is small, but saturated with color in a way that suggests they have been *waiting*. Saxifrages in the cracks, their roots finding purchase where no purchase should exist. Gentians in the dew-basins, blue so deep it seems borrowed from a different sky. *Gentiana* in particular—there is something about the high hollows, the thin cold, the intermittent water, that produces gentians in *profusion* during the brief growing season. They bloom for weeks, maybe a month, and then they are gone, leaving only the memory of blue, the seed-heads that will wait through the long dim months for the moisture that will wake them again.

The lushness is *vertical* as much as horizontal. Layer upon layer: stone, then lichen crust, then lichen tower, then moss cushion, then the flowering plant that has found its level, its niche, its *almost-enough*. Each layer creates conditions for the next. Each layer is *dense* with life that has learned to exist in the margins.

## The Built Thing

There is a town here. Or what was a town. Or what *became* a town through processes no one entirely remembers.

The built environment (1.6, urban, organized construction at settlement scale) occupies one of the *hollows*—a natural depression in the ancient stone, maybe three kilometers across, surrounded by the rounded ridges that are all that remain of mountains that were once *mountains*. The town is *layered*. Not vertically, particularly—the stone will not support great height—but *temporally*, *geologically*. The oldest structures are indistinguishable from the bedrock. They are *of* it, built from it, have become it through the same processes of erosion and settling that shape the land around them.

The newer structures—and "new" here means *industrial*, means engines and mechanized production, means the particular arrogance of a technology (1.5) that believes it can impose its rhythms on a place that has its own—rise from the older foundations like *intrusions*. Metal roofs, rusted to the color of the stone, so that from a distance they seem to grow from it. Concrete, which will not last, which is already crumbling back into the mineral world from which it was briefly wrested. The *infrastructure*: channels that direct the intermittent water, that collect it in cisterns, that distribute it through a system of pipes that leak and are repaired and leak again. A *power* system, because the town is electrified, because the engines require it, because someone, once, decided that the high hollows needed *light* in the long dim hours.

The town's *organizing principle* is water. Everything is built around its capture, its storage, its reluctant release. The buildings cluster around the largest dew-basins, the ones that have been *enhanced* over centuries, deepened, lined with the local stone's impermeable layers, made into *reservoirs* that hold enough moisture to sustain the population through the dry periods. The streets—if they can be called streets, narrow channels between structures—follow the *logic of flow*, the paths water would take if it were more abundant than it is.

And the *industry*. There are factories here, or what pass for factories in a place this remote, this cold, this *thin*. They produce—what? The records are unclear. The place has been settled, abandoned, resettled, its purposes shifting with the generations like the water in its basins. Currently, or *recently*, the industry seems to be *processing*: the lichens, harvested with a care that suggests their value, dried, compressed, shipped down the single road that descends to the lowlands (a road that is itself a kind of violence, a cut through the ancient stone that will eventually heal, that is already healing at its edges). The mosses, similarly, for some purpose that the town's inhabitants do not question. And the *peat*, when it has accumulated enough, burned for the little heat it provides, used in the processes that require *drying*, *concentrating*, *transforming* the raw materials of the high hollows into something that can be *sent away*.

The town is *dense* (2.4). Not crowded, exactly—there are perhaps two thousand people, maybe three, in this hollow—but *layered*, *near-touching*. The buildings press together. The spaces between them are *functional*: channels for water, paths for the movement of goods, the narrow gaps where the wind can still pass but just barely. There is no *wilderness* within the town's bounds. Every surface is *used*, *claimed*, *modified* by the presence of human intention. Even the stone has been *worked*, *shaped*, *given purpose*.

And yet. And yet. The town is *failing*. Or *succeeding* into something else. The population is stable but not growing. The industry continues but its markets are distant, its products *strange* (1.5 weirdness), valued for qualities that the lowlanders do not entirely understand. The infrastructure leaks. The repairs are constant, the repairs are *ritual*, the repairs are the thing that holds the community together more than any shared purpose. The town exists in a state of *perpetual maintenance*, which is perhaps the only state available to any settlement in a place this marginal, this *almost*.

## The Atmosphere, The Light, The Sound

The air is *thin*. You feel it in your breathing, in the way exertion costs more than it should, in the way sound carries *strangely*—not farther, as you might expect, but *differently*. The frequencies that travel in dense air are absent here. What you hear is *high*, *bright*, *immediate*. A voice carries, but it carries *flat*, without the bass that grounds sound in lower elevations. Music, if anyone played it, would sound *wrong*, incomplete, as if heard through a filter that removes warmth.

The light is *dim* (1.3) but not dark. There is no true night here, or not for long—the elevation is high enough that the sun, even in winter, finds the hollows for brief hours. But the *sky* is rarely clear. There is always *haze*, *mist*, the perpetual suggestion of moisture in the air. The light that comes through is *diffused*, *softened*, *directionless*. Shadows are rare. When they exist, they are *edges* rather than shapes, the mere suggestion of difference between one surface and another.

The color of the world is *restricted*: the bone-grey of stone, the yellow-green of lichen, the deep blue of gentian bloom, the rust-red of metal and factory roofs. Against this, the *sky* provides the only variation—not blue, exactly, but a *pale*, *washed*, *almost-white* that suggests the sun is there, somewhere, behind the veil of moisture.

Sound is *mostly quiet* (1.4). The town has its noises—the *mechanical* sounds of the factories, the *human* sounds of the narrow streets, the *water* sounds of the channels and cisterns—but these are *contained*, *intermittent*, *domesticated*. Beyond the town's bounds, the high hollows are *silent* in the way that old places are silent. Not empty—*full* of processes too slow to hear, of changes too gradual to perceive, of the *almost-motion* of stone settling, of lichen growing, of water evaporating and condensing and evaporating again.

But there are *sounds*. The wind, when it comes, moves through the lichen-towers in a way that produces *tones*—not music, exactly, but *pitches*, *resonances*, the hollow *oooo* of air passing through cylindrical growths. The water, in its intermittent flow, makes *trickling* sounds that are somehow *louder* than they should be, in the silence, drawing attention to themselves. And once—once, in the deepest part of the hollow, where the town's oldest structures meet the bedrock—there is a *sound* that has no source. A *hum*, low, felt in the teeth rather than heard, that the inhabitants have learned not to question. It is the town's *strangeness* (1.5), its one concession to the weird, its acknowledgment that this place has *rules* that are not entirely understood.

## The Ecology, The Motion, The Rules

The high hollows are *active* (2.5). Not busy—*active*. There is motion everywhere, but it is the motion of *cycles*, of *returns*, of processes that have achieved their own tempo and will not be hurried.

The *water cycle* is the master rhythm. Condensation, pooling, brief flow, evaporation, return. It happens at every scale, from the individual dew-basin to the entire hollow, and the life of the place is *shaped* by its irregularities. A basin that holds water longer becomes *more*: more moss, more peat, more habitat for the creatures that need moisture. A basin that dries quickly becomes *less*: stone, lichen crust, the patient waiting for the next rain. The town is *built around* this rhythm, its cisterns *timed* to the cycles, its harvests *scheduled* to the brief abundance of the growing season.

The *biological pace* is slow. Everything grows slowly. Everything dies slowly. The lichens may be *centuries* old. The mosses build their peat over *millennia*. The gentians bloom and fade in their weeks, but they return, they have *always* returned, in the same basins, from the same seed-heads that waited through the long dim months. There is a *predator-prey* dynamic, but it is *contained*, *local*, *intimate*: the insects that live in the moss cushions, the birds that come to eat them (rare, 1.3 fauna, but *present*, *returning*), the small mammals that burrow in the peat, the *hawk* that circles above the town on thermal currents that barely exist in the thin air.

The *rules* of this world are *negotiated* rather than imposed. The stone does not yield easily, but it yields. The water does not flow abundantly, but it *persists*. The life does not thrive in the classical sense, but it *endures*, it *adapts*, it *finds its almost-enough*. The town is the strangest element, the most *recent*, the most *imposed*—and yet it has been here long enough to become *part of the ecology*, its leaks and repairs and harvests *contributing* to the cycles rather than disrupting them. The lichens grow on the factory roofs. The mosses colonize the cistern walls. The gentians bloom in the cracks of the old stone foundations. The town has been *absorbed*, or has *absorbed itself*, into the high hollows' patient becoming.

There is a *strangeness* here that is not dramatic but *structural*. The hum in the deep places. The way the light *persists*, never quite dark, never quite bright. The *scale* of time that makes human purposes seem *brief*, *almost irrelevant*, and yet the human purposes *continue*, *matter*, *shape* the world in ways that will outlast the individuals who shaped them. The high hollows are *old*—older than the town, older than the species that built it, older than the *concept* of building—and yet they are *still becoming*, still *settling*, still *finding* their final form in a process that has no end, only *continuation*.

## What You Would Find There

If you climbed to the high hollows of Varn—if you followed the single road up from the lowlands, past the last trees, past the last *easy* breathing, into the thin cold where your lungs remember their work—you would find:

A *hollow*, three kilometers across, surrounded by ridges that are no longer mountains but are not yet *plain*. Stone everywhere, the color of old bone, worn smooth by water that has passed through in forms you cannot imagine. Dew-basins in every depression, holding their shallow water, their suspended lives. Lichen-towers rising from the stone like *architecture*, like *prayer*, like something built by hands that are not human but have learned from human hands.

And the town. Pressed into the hollow's center, where the water gathers most reliably. Two or three thousand people living in buildings that are *of* the stone, that have become *part* of it, that will outlast the metal roofs and the concrete and the *intention* that built them. Factories that hum (that *other* hum, the mechanical one) in the long dim hours, processing the lichens and mosses that grow in *profusion* in the hollows around them. Channels that carry the intermittent water, that leak and are repaired, that *are* the town's circulatory system.

The *atmosphere* would press on you. Thin, cold, *damp* in a way that gets into your clothes, your skin, your *bones*. The light would be *wrong*, never quite bright enough, never quite dark, the sun a *suggestion* behind the perpetual haze. You would hear the *silence*, but a silence *full* of almost-sounds, of wind in lichen, of water finding its way, of the *hum* that has no name.

And you would feel the *time*. The ancient erosion, the oldest-stable-state, the world settled into its final form which is *not final* but *continuing*, *becoming*, *almost*. The town as *brief*, as *recent*, as *strange* in its imposition of human rhythm on this patient place. The life as *dense*, as *layered*, as *almost-enough* in its adaptation to the thin cold, the intermittent water, the dim light.

You would stay, if you could. Or you would descend, back to the lowlands, back to the *easy* breathing, back to the world that has not yet settled, that is still *becoming* in ways that are *dramatic*, *visible*, *hurried*. And you would carry the high hollows with you—the *damp*, the *dim*, the *almost* of a place that has found its own rules and lives by them, that has *absorbed* its strangeness into something like *peace*.

---

*The high hollows of Varn do not end. They continue, settling, becoming, holding their water in their stone hands, waiting for the rain that will come, that always comes, that is never quite enough and is, in its way, exactly enough.*
