The Gorge of Borrowed Light

You descend not by path but by intention, through a throat of limestone that narrows as you go. At first the ceiling is high enough to forget, but it keeps its own counsel. The walls remember being seafloor. They are the color of old bone and wet ash, veined with calcite that holds moisture like a held breath. Water arrives here without appointment—condensation weeping from the rock, a drip that has been falling since before anyone named it, a seasonal thread of melt from somewhere above that darkens the stone in vertical streaks. The floor is not floor exactly. It is a shallow river that exists only when it chooses, now a mirror, now a bed of rounded stones slick with algae, now a cracked mudflat that will drink the next rain in an afternoon.

The light comes from nowhere and everywhere. A glow, diffuse and warm, suffuses the lower reaches—phosphorescence in the stone itself? No. Something else. The rock here is *translucent* in places, thin as a fingernail where water has patiently worked, and through these windows a light from deep below pushes upward, gold-green, the color of old glass in a church that no longer has a congregation. It pools. It does not illuminate so much as *suggest*. You can see the shapes of things. You cannot quite see things themselves.

The trees, if trees they are, grow in the pockets where the ceiling fractures and the outside world leaks in. They are small, stunted, their roots finding purchase in cracks that run for meters into darkness. Their leaves are translucent too, the same pale green as the upward light, and they do not photosynthesize so much as *conduct*—passing something up from the root-tangle, something down from the fractured sky. Between them stand fungi the size of a man's torso, pale and vented like lungs, releasing their spores in slow clouds that drift upward to meet the descending light and make small auroras where they cross.

The fauna moves in the spaces between perception. Something large shifts in the deep water when you are not looking directly at it—ribbed, many-jointed, patient as the erosion that shaped this place. Smaller things chitter in the root-masses, feeding on the translucent leaves, feeding on each other, their lives too brief to develop fear. A bird, or what passes for one, nests in the highest fracture, where a single shaft of true daylight enters for eleven minutes each day. It sings only in that eleven minutes. The rest of the time it is silent, or it sings below the threshold of hearing, and you feel it as pressure behind your eyes.

Sound here is mostly the water. The drip that has no source. The slow exhalation of the river when it decides to flow. The creak of the trees, which are not wood but something closer to cartilage, flexing in a wind that comes from nowhere. And beneath it, almost subliminal, a hum—the resonance of the gorge itself, a frequency at the edge of hearing that the fauna clearly perceives, that makes them turn, all at once, toward some passage you cannot see.

The strangeness is not dramatic. It is *structural*. The rules of this place are not broken but *different*. Light behaves as if it has weight. Water behaves as if it has memory—it returns to the same stones, wears the same grooves, though the path it takes to get there changes each time. The translucent things, stone and leaf and flesh, all seem to be *waiting* for something, some conversion, some threshold they have not yet crossed. You feel this most strongly in the deepest pool, where the upward light is brightest and the water is warm, almost hot, and the large ribbed thing moves in patterns that resemble writing. Not language. Something prior. The record of a decision not yet made.

Nothing here is built. Nothing here is *wanted*. The primitive technology of the place is the technology of growth and dissolution—the slow chemistry of water against stone, the patient architecture of roots finding purchase, the fabrication of shell and chitin by creatures who have never known a tool. If there are artifacts, they are the artifacts of erosion: the standing stone that is a boulder that refused to fall, the arch that is a crack that learned to hold itself open, the basin that is a depression that forgot to drain.

The scale closes around you. You could walk the length in an hour. You could not comprehend it in a lifetime. The gorge has boundaries you can touch—the wall where the translucence ends and the stone becomes ordinary, the deep point where the water turns cold and the light becomes something else, something that does not illuminate but *consumes*, that makes you understand why the bird nests so high, why nothing grows below the third fracture, why the large thing keeps to the warm dark.

Time moves slowly here, but it moves. The streaks on the wall extend by millimeters per century. The translucent windows thin. The river changes its mind about its bed. The trees deposit a resin that will become something, in ages, that the next descent will not recognize. The hum shifts frequency, almost imperceptibly, as the shape of the gorge adjusts itself toward some equilibrium it has not yet reached, may never reach, is patient enough to wait for.

When you leave—if you leave by the same throat you entered, or by another you find in the deep dark where the upward light has become something else—you carry the damp on your skin. You carry the sense of having been *permitted*, briefly, to witness a process that does not include you, that was occurring before you arrived and will continue without your witness. The gorge does not remember you. This is its strangeness, and its mercy: that it holds you without holding you, that its light shines on what you are and what you were and what you might become, without preference, without recognition, only the slow patience of stone learning to let light through.
