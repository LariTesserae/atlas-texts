**The Condensery**

You arrive by descending, though nothing here was ever high enough to *be* high. The elevation is a quality of *pressurization* rather than altitude—a room held at the threshold of breathability, where the air itself feels like a substance you move through, thick and tea-colored and faintly sweet. The scale is a single chamber, perhaps thirty meters in its longest dimension, though it has no proper shape. Walls, floor, and ceiling are continuous—one surface that folds, that weeps, that remembers being liquid.

Everything is made. The rock is *grown* rock, cultured in vats and extruded through nozzles that still protrude from the lower walls like nipples, still occasionally bead with mineral-rich fluid. The geology is embryonic, still deciding what it wants to be. Some surfaces are glass-smooth and gray as a held breath. Others are pocked, vesicular, still cooling from their fabrication. And everywhere—*everywhere*—water. Not flooding. Not pooling. **Condensing.** The walls weep constantly. A fine mist hangs in the warm air, gathering on every surface, running in threads too thin to follow, collecting in the seams where constructed meets constructed. You hear it before you see it: not dripping, but a continuous whispered percussion, ten thousand tiny impacts, the sound of the room sweating out its own substance.

The vegetation is lush in the manner of an overgrown machine. Vines of pale, translucent fiber—cellulose or somethingnear-it—thicken the lower third of the space, clustering around the warm vents where the weeping is heaviest. They do not climb so much as *accumulate*, like frost finding a pattern, like mold deciding on architecture. Between them, the water runs faster, channels forming in the constructed floor that were not designed, that emerged from use and humidity and the slow gravity of a room that has forgotten which way is down. Bioluminescence pulses faintly in the vine-masses—green-white, then off, then green-white—a photosynthesis tuned to artificial light, or a respiration, or simply a clock. The vines are dense. They near-touch. They layer. You could push through them and lose your sense of which surface is wall and which is growth.

The machines are integrated, indistinguishable from the geology at first. Then you see them: a column that is also a pump, its rhythm audible in the wet percussion. A shelf that is also a heat exchanger, its surface mottled with condensation that never quite coalesces into drops. The technology is industrial in its logic—scaled, repetitive, humming with current you feel in your teeth—but it has been *absorbed*, grown around, digested by the wet. Rust is not decay here but a patina, an expected color. The warm temperature is maintained by the machines, by the metabolic heat of the vines, by the sheer thermal mass of the weeping stone. It is a greenhouse that was never meant for plants alone. It is a lung. It is a womb.

There are boundaries. You feel them as pressure changes, as the mist thickening or thinning, as the sound of the room shifting from wet percussion to a drier, more mechanical tone. Three thresholds, perhaps, dividing the chamber into zones that bleed into each other without walls. The upper reaches, where the air is less saturated and the constructed surfaces show their fabrication more honestly—ribs of extruded material, the seams between pours. The middle, where the vines achieve their greatest density, where you must move sideways, where the light is filtered through layers of fiber and moisture into something diffuse and submarine. The lower, where the water actually pools in shallow basins that were perhaps intended, perhaps emerged, where something moves in the warm dark that is not vine and not machine and not quite *alive* in any way you can verify.

The fauna is rare. You might see one, briefly: a pale, many-legged thing that moves through the vine-mass with the patience of something that has never been hunted, because nothing here hunts. It drinks from a condensation bead. It does not look at you. Or if it does, its eyes—there are too many, or too few, or they are not eyes—do not register your presence as significant. The ecosystem, such as it is, moves at the pace of growth and evaporation and the slow circulation of mineral-laden water through constructed channels. Predator-prey has not yet been invented here, or has been tried and abandoned as unnecessary. The dynamics are vegetative, hydraulic, thermal. The room breathes. The vines drink. The machines remember their purpose.

The weirdness accumulates. A surface that is warm to the touch here, cold there, with no visible reason. A sound that resolves, when you attend to it, into your own heartbeat, amplified, returned. The sense—growing, inescapable—that you are *inside* something, that the chamber is not a room but an organ, that you have been inhaled, that the weeping is not condensation but something else, something the room produces to process you. The normal light comes from everywhere and nowhere, the bioluminescence and the reflected wet and the diffuse glow of heated surfaces combining into a illumination without shadow, without source, without the comfort of *day*.

You could stay. The warmth is genuine. The water is, presumably, potable—it is produced here, after all, cycled through mineral and fiber and machine. The vines offer a density that borders on embrace. But the boundaries press closer with time. The mist thickens. The sound of your own breathing joins the wet percussion, becomes indistinguishable from it. And somewhere, in the lower dark, something shifts in the pooled water, something that has been waiting for the room to finish forming, for the geology to decide, for the ecosystem to reach the complexity where *waiting* becomes *hunger*.

The Condensery is still under construction. It has been under construction since before anything here was made. It will be under construction when you leave, if you leave, if leaving is a thing that happens to things inside rooms that breathe.
