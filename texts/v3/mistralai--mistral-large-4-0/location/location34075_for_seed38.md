I'll let this place emerge slowly.

---

At first you think you are walking through a forest. Then you realize the forest is walking *through you*—that the air itself has thickened into vegetation, that every breath draws in spores and pollen and the faint metallic tang of photosynthesis pushed to some extreme. The elevation is high, yet warmth persists, a contradiction that resolves only when you understand that heat here is *generated*, not received. The plants make their own weather.

You are standing on what might once have been a plateau. Erosion has been working for so long that the concept of "ground" has softened. The oldest surfaces have settled into something like a mat—thousands of meters of compressed generations, fungal and root and the indigestible architecture of extinct plant-phyla, all still faintly *alive*, still slowly metabolizing. The terrain is near-flattened but not flat. It breathes. You can feel it underfoot, a slow systolic pulse, as if the entire continent-scale mass were digesting something vast and patient.

Water moves through everything. It is not ponds or streams primarily, though those exist—marsh-edge conditions everywhere, water held in suspension by root-mats and capillary action and the sheer *density* of living tissue. The water is *in* the plants. You cut a stem—if you could find something to cut with, but there are no tools, only your hands, and the stems are too thick, too *many*—and water would weep out, not sap but something clearer, slightly luminescent, condensing immediately into the already humid air. The atmosphere is near its carrying capacity. Droplets form on surfaces without quite falling. The dim light—filtered through layer upon layer of canopy, through mist, through the particular quality of high-altitude haze made organic—arrives from everywhere and nowhere.

The vegetation is total in a way that excludes the usual meanings of "forest." There are no clearings. There are no edges. The plant-mass extends to the horizon and beyond, and the horizon itself is uncertain, dissolved into green-gray distance. Some structures rise above the general level—not trees in any familiar sense, but *growths*, architectural accumulations that took millennia to form, calcified or lignified or merely *dense*, housing within their bulk the compressed record of every previous iteration of this ecosystem. They are the elevation. You climb them, or you don't. The near-flatness means that everywhere is slightly uphill and slightly downhill simultaneously, a property of scale.

Fauna is abundant but rarely seen. You hear it constantly, or *almost* constantly—the sound level is low, mostly quiet, but dense with presence. Clicking in the root-mats. The soft percussion of something heavy moving through the lower layers, never breaking surface. The low, sustained tones of creatures that communicate through vibration rather than air, through the plant-network itself, using the same pathways that carry water and nutrients. Some of these sounds have no source you can locate. Some seem to come from the ground you stand on. The plants may be listening. The plants may be *responding*.

There are faint marks. Scattered. You find them only because you are looking, or because the dim light changes and suddenly reveals them. A depression that is not natural, too regular, filled with the same material as everything else but *arranged*. A surface that has been worn smooth by contact, though what touched it is gone. Occasional artifacts: a fragment of something crystalline that does not match any mineral formation you can identify, embedded in a root-mat like a tooth in gum. Single structures, barely distinguishable from growths, but *occupied*—you feel this before you see evidence, a wrongness in the local density, a place where the plant-mass has been *modified*, hollowed, inhabited by something that left no clear trace of its tools, its intentions.

The temperature holds at something like perpetual late-spring evening. No frost, no snow, despite the elevation. The warmth is *biological*, the byproduct of metabolism at a scale that generates its own climate. Weather moves across this place slowly—systems that take weeks to cross what would be a valley elsewhere, because the "valleys" are measured in hundreds of kilometers and the plant-mass disrupts and regenerates wind patterns in cycles too gradual to perceive. Rain, when it comes, is not an event but a *condition*, a shift in the already-saturated air that lasts for months, during which the growth-rate visibly increases, the whole system *swelling*.

Time here is geological but *active*. This is not a dying world, though erosion has reached its ancient final-form. It is a world that has settled into its own deep rhythm, a stable state maintained by constant low-level flux. The plants grow, die, compress, become substrate for new growth, in cycles that overlap across millennia. Nothing is preserved; everything is *incorporated*. The oldest layers at the bottom are still metabolizing, still exchanging chemicals with the layers above, still participating in a metabolism that has no outside.

You walk, and the path is the same as everywhere else. Your footsteps compress the surface mat, and it rebounds slowly, over hours. You leave no lasting mark. The place does not remember you, but it *notices* you, and adjusts—chemical signals, imperceptible shifts in growth direction, a slight increase in the density of defensive compounds near your passage. The fauna observes. Some of it is curious. Some of it is hungry, or *was* hungry, before the ecosystem reached its current equilibrium, where predation has become a form of nutrient cycling, where death is indistinguishable from digestion.

The light dims further as you walk, not because of time but because you have entered a region where the canopy has thickened, where the layering has become so dense that only the faintest glow penetrates. Here the plants are different—pale, almost translucent, living on what light filters through and on the chemical exchanges with the layers above. They are *patient*. They have been waiting, in a sense, for the conditions to change, for the next cycle, for the slow geological processes to expose them to more light, or less, or different. They do not hurry.

And somewhere, though the sound is mostly quiet, you hear it—the deepest tone, felt in the sternum rather than heard, the resonance of the entire mass shifting in its slow systolic pulse, the planet-scale organism *breathing*, warm and wet and ancient and utterly without tools, without devices, without anything but the endless patient green.
