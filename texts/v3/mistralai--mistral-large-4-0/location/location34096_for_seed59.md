# The Drownthold of Velmora

## The Air Itself

You do not enter Velmora so much as *become* wet. The atmosphere here carries water the way other places carry dust—visible, suspended, in constant slow descent. At elevation 1.5, the air is thin enough to make breathing feel deliberate, a choice rather than a default, yet so saturated that each inhale pulls in actual droplets. The cool temperature (0.7) means this moisture never warms into comfort; it stays at the edge of condensation, of mist, of the perpetual threat of becoming rain. Your skin does not dry. Your clothes never dry. The concept of dry becomes theoretical, a story told by people from elsewhere.

Sound arrives already waterlogged. The loudness here (2.1) is not sharp but *dense*—a continuous wet roar of falling water, of dripping, of the vast vegetative gasp of ten thousand wet leaves. It is the sound of a place that is always weeping and always drinking.

## The Living Architecture

Vegetation here is not *in* the landscape. Vegetation *is* the landscape, at 2.8—a total substrate where plant matter has achieved something like geological permanence. The trees of Velmora are not individual organisms but colonies, rhizomatic networks of root and mycelium that have grown upward and outward until they form load-bearing structures. Their bark is soft, pulpy, constantly shedding and regrowing. Their "wood" is more like compressed peat, like the accumulated memory of ten thousand wet seasons.

Between these living pillars, the space fills with *filament*—a plant I can only call laceweed, though it is not woven but grown, extruded from specialized glands in the bark of the great colonizing trees. It hangs in sheets and curtains, in veils that filter the normal light (1.8) into something green and submarine. It is edible, technically. It is also where things live. The laceweed is the city's fabric, its architecture, its clothing, its food, its graveyard.

The scale is small (1.1)—a single valley, perhaps, or the hollow between three ridges. Velmora has boundaries you can walk in an afternoon, though the density (2.3) makes that afternoon feel longer. Every surface is near-touching. Every gap is filled. There is no empty space, no sky you can see in full, only the green-gold light that filters through layer after layer of wet leaf and hanging lace and the mist that moves between them like breath.

## The Water's Behavior

Water at 2.5 does not flow here so much as *permeate*. It is in the air, on the surfaces, inside the plants, underfoot in the spongy peat that has replaced soil. The ground is a living sponge, saturated to its limit, so that any pressure releases water upward—springs that appear where you step, that vanish when you lift your foot. The place is dotted with *weeping faces*, rock outcroppings where mineral-saturated water seeps constantly from pores too small to see, creating curtains of moisture that never break, that have never broken, that will outlast any human construction.

There are pools. Not ponds—*pools*, the size of rooms, perfectly still, their surfaces so still they hold the inverted image of the laceweed canopy with photographic precision. In these pools, things grow that grow nowhere else: bladder-farm colonies, gas-filled sacs that rise and fall with temperature, that pop with sounds like distant drums when the cool air shifts. They are farmed. They are eaten. Their popping is part of the soundscape, a wet percussion that marks time.

The turbulence (2.6) lives in the water's sudden moods. The peat holds so much that it becomes unstable. *Bog-surges* occur without warning—the ground heaves, releases a wave of black water and semi-digested plant matter, swallows what was standing there, settles back into apparent calm. The people of Velmora build on *root-rafts*, platforms of interwoven living wood that ride these surges, that rise with the flood and settle again, damaged but unbroken. A raft might shift three meters in a surge. A raft might lose its moorings entirely, drift for days in the mist, be recovered or not.

## The Settlement

Built at 1.8, Velmora is urban in the way a coral reef is urban—organized construction without central planning, infrastructure that grew in response to need, layered accumulation of function. The people here are few; the scale is small. But what they build, they build *into* the vegetation, *with* the vegetation, *as* vegetation.

Their architecture is *trained*. A platform of root-raft is extended by lashing new growth to old, by guiding the colony trees to lean in useful directions, by grafting. Rooms are the hollows of massive burls, lined with laceweed for insulation, for softness, for the absolute darkness they can provide when drawn closed. Bridges are living things too—felled colony trees, still rooted at both ends, induced to grow toward each other until they meet, their cambium layers merging into a single organism that will carry weight for centuries.

There is no stone construction. Stone is too heavy, sinks in the peat. Stone is too dry, cracks when the moisture leaves it. Everything here is *wet-built*—composite, grown, maintained. A house in Velmora is a negotiation with entropy, a structure that must be constantly fed, pruned, redirected. The people speak of their buildings the way others speak of gardens or livestock. They have names. They have moods. They die, occasionally, and are mourned, and their materials are recycled into new growth.

The technology (1.3) is primitive by other measures but sophisticated in its context. Bone tools, yes—fauna is overwhelming (2.8), and bone is abundant, worked with the patient skill of people who have time and necessity. But also: the chemistry of the laceweed, the cultivation of bladder-farm colonies for lift and for light (their gas burns clean, blue, in simple clay lamps), the navigation by smell and by the particular quality of mist that hangs over different depths of peat. They know seventeen ways to make a waterproof seal from plant resins. They know which fungi will grow on which substrate, which will glow faintly in the green-dark, which will kill you in six hours if eaten and which will sustain you for a month.

Metal exists but is precious, imported or scavenged, used for cutting edges and for the small weights that help anchor root-rafts against surge. Iron rusts here in weeks. They have learned to work with copper, which greens but endures, which the laceweed will actually grow *around*, incorporating it into living structure.

## The Fauna's Overwhelming

Life here is not *abundant* so much as *insistent*—present in every medium, every layer, every moment. The overwhelming quality (2.8) comes from density of *kinds* as much as numbers. The air carries spores, visible in the filtered light, colored faintly by the pigments of ten thousand fungal species. The water carries larvae, hatchlings, the constant rain of organic matter from above. The peat itself moves, slowly, with the *peat-worms*, segmented creatures the length of a forearm that digest the dead and excrete the living soil.

The most visible fauna are the *hangers*—not birds, though they fly, but colonies of small, furred, warm-blooded creatures that live in the laceweed curtains, that drop from above to feed on the bladder-farm blooms, that communicate in wet clicks that blend into the general roar. They are not tame. They are not wild. They are *neighbors*, with their own territories in the vertical space, their own negotiations with the human-built structures. A hanger colony might occupy a root-raft's undercarriage for generations. They will defend it. They will also, given time, accept offerings of fermented bladder-farm pulp, will appear at windows, will watch.

Larger things move in the deep peat, in the waterlogged dark below the root-rafts. *Bog-elk*, perhaps, or something that resembles them—massive, slow, with antlers that are not bone but compressed peat, grown over decades into shapes that mirror the laceweed patterns above. They are not hunted. They cannot be hunted; the peat will not support the pursuit. They are *avoided*, their paths learned, their lowing taken as warning of surges to come.

The insects are the true infrastructure. Mosquito-analogues in clouds so dense they darken the light, feeding on everything warm-blooded, controlled only by smoke from specific resins, by the constant motion that the people maintain. Beetles that bore through living wood, that are farmed for the tunnels they create, which become ventilation. Worms that aerate the peat, that are gathered and redistributed, that are the only reason the city does not drown in its own saturated excess.

## The Rules of the Place

Velmora operates on principles that would be paradox elsewhere:

**Nothing is dry.** This is not deprivation but definition. The people have no word for "dry," only degrees of wet. Their materials, their tools, their bodies are all adapted to permanent moisture. Leather is unknown; they use treated laceweed, bladder-membrane, the leathery skins of certain peat-worms. Fire is precious, maintained in clay cores wrapped in multiple layers of insulation, passed from structure to structure like a sacrament. A fire that goes out is a minor catastrophe.

**Growth is construction.** To build is to cultivate. To cultivate is to build. The distinction makes no sense here. A new platform is planted, fed, guided. A damaged wall is grafted, sutured with living fiber, encouraged to close over the wound. Architecture and agriculture and medicine and commerce are the same activity, viewed from different angles.

**The ground is unreliable.** Everything must float, or be ready to float, or be anchored in ways that release under pressure. Foundations are temporary by design. A building's location is negotiated annually, as the peat shifts, as the colony trees grow, as the bog-surges redraw the map. The people keep records—not on dry paper, but on cured bladder-membrane, on carved and resin-sealed peat tablets—of where things *were*, knowing that where things *are* is always provisional.

**Sound is information.** In the green-dark, in the mist, in the constant wet roar, vision fails. The people navigate by sound: the particular quality of laceweed rustling in wind from the north, the pop of a bladder-farm at a specific temperature, the lowing of bog-elk at depths that indicate rising or falling water. They have developed a *language of place*, a set of calls and responses that carry information about location, about safety, about the mood of the peat. It is loud (2.1), always, but it is also *structured*, a dense information environment.

**Death is incorporation.** The erosion here (1.7) is not destructive but *translational*. The place is established, settled-form, in-balance—but the balance is dynamic, a constant processing of the dead into the living. The people do not bury their dead in the peat, where they would be preserved, strange and present. They *compost* them, deliberately, in specific chambers where the peat-worms are concentrated, where the fungal colonies are fed, where the body becomes soil becomes laceweed becomes, eventually, the walls of new construction. To be built into a house after death is the highest honor. To be forgotten, to be left where the surges might take you, is the only true tragedy.

## The Feeling of Being There

You would arrive, if arrival is possible, by following a watercourse that narrows until it becomes a stream you can wade, then a channel through peat, then a *path*—not made, but *grown*, a corridor of shorter vegetation maintained by the constant passage of hanger colonies and human feet. The cool (0.7) would strike you first, after the wet. You would shiver, always, in a way that never quite stops. Your breath would mist. Your footsteps would make sucking sounds, would release small bursts of gas from the disturbed peat, methane and hydrogen sulfide and other products of the endless underground digestion.

The green-gold light would disorient you. Normal light (1.8) becomes *filtered* light here, and the filtering is alive, changing, responsive to humidity and temperature and the mood of the laceweed. You would lose the sun. You would lose the sky. You would have only the close, dense, breathing world of leaf and hanging filament and mist and the wet sound that never stops.

And then you would notice the *movement*. The turbulence (2.6) is not dramatic—no storms, no catastrophe—but constant. The laceweed sways in currents you cannot feel. The mist drifts in patterns that suggest intelligence. The bladder-farms rise and fall with thermal rhythms. The hangers watch from above, silent, numerous, *present*. The peat beneath your feet is digesting something, always, somewhere, and will, eventually, digest you too, if you stay, if you let yourself be incorporated into the wet green dark.

Velmora does not welcome you. Velmora does not reject you. It *processes* you, as it processes everything, with the patient hunger of a place that has been wet for ten thousand years and expects to remain wet for ten thousand more.
