# The Condensation Terraces

## Where the Mountain Exhales

You stand on a surface that is not quite stone and not quite anything else—a glassy, opalescent material that seems to *remember* being molten, now cooled into forms that catch light like the inside of a shell. It covers everything. The terraces step down the mountainside in broad, worn curves, each one a shallow basin holding water that never drains, never warms, never quite stills.

The water is the first thing you notice, though you noticed it before you arrived. It is *in the air*. At 2,300 meters, in this cool thinness, the atmosphere carries more moisture than it can hold. It condenses on every surface—on the glassy stone, on the faint mineral crusts that bloom in the shade of every overhang, on your own skin if you linger too long in one place. The terraces are not merely wet. They are *weeping*. A slow, constant seep from the rock itself, as if the mountain were sweating out something it no longer needs.

There is no plant life. This is not a place that failed to grow. It is a place that *completed*—a world where the chemistry of living things ran its course long ago, or never began, and left behind these polished remnants. What grows here grows *down*. Filamentous colonies of something that might be fungal, might be mineral, might be a chemistry so alien it makes no distinction. They spread across the basin floors in thin sheets, pale as old bone, pulsing—no, not pulsing, *oscillating*—with a frequency too slow to perceive directly, visible only in the way they catch and release light as the water moves through them.

The silence is not silence.

It is a *roar*.

Wind moves through these terraces at a frequency that matches the resonance of the basins themselves. Each hollow terrace is tuned to a different note. Together they produce a chord that has no melody, no rhythm, no variation—a standing wave of sound that has existed so long it has worn channels into the glassy stone. You feel it in your sternum before you hear it with your ears. It is not unpleasant. It is *definite*. The mountain is announcing itself. The mountain has been announcing itself for ten thousand years, and it has not tired.

## The Ecology of Pressure and Patience

Fauna here are rare, and *wrong* in ways that suggest they do not belong to the same story as the landscape. You might see them at the edges of your vision: something that moves through the shallow basins with too many joints, too little hurry, drinking water that it seems to *need* in a way no thirst explains. It does not look at you. It has no eyes, or too many. When it passes, the water closes behind it with a sound like a sigh, and the standing wave resumes as if nothing had interrupted it.

These creatures do not eat. They do not reproduce in any way you can track. They are *present*. They have always been present. They will remain when the wind finally wears the terraces flat, which it will, in perhaps fifty thousand years—a brief interval in a place where time moves like the water, everywhere and nowhere at once.

The weirdness of this place is not *strange* in the way a carnival is strange. It is strange the way a mathematical proof is strange: it follows from its premises with terrible inevitability. The parameters have settled. Water: present, active, *defining*. Vegetation: absent, not by catastrophe but by *completion*. Temperature: cool enough for condensation, warm enough for liquid water to persist. Erosion: established, the landscape has found its form and is content to hold it. Density: packed—every cubic meter of air carries particles, carries sound, carries the weight of water waiting to fall. Built: nothing. Tech: nothing. The terraces are not *made*. They are *grown*, in the way a crystal grows, in the way a glacier grows—by accumulation and pressure and the slow victory of pattern over chaos.

## The Rules of This World

Time here is not a river. It is a *pool*.

Nothing changes quickly. The water level in each basin rises and falls with a periodicity so slow that generations of the jointed creatures might live and die between visible fluctuations. The wind does not gust. It *presses*, a constant force from the west where the mountain meets a sea you cannot see, a sea that sends its breath upward to condense on these heights. The light is bright without being warm—high altitude, thin atmosphere, the sun's radiation filtered through a sky that holds too much water to be truly blue, more pearl than azure.

You cannot stay. Not because anything will harm you—nothing here is hostile—but because the condensation will find you. It will gather in your lungs, in the spaces behind your eyes, in the folds of any clothing you wear. You will become, temporarily, part of the cycle. A temporary terrace. A mobile basin. The mountain does not distinguish between itself and the water it holds. It does not distinguish between the water and the air, the air and the sound, the sound and the creatures that move through it like thoughts through a dream.

When you leave—and you will leave, because the body cannot sustain this equilibrium—you will carry the roar with you for days. A ringing that is not in your ears but in the memory of your sternum, the recollection of a chord that has no end because it has no beginning.

The terraces remain. They have always remained. They are, in their way, the most *honest* landscape you will ever stand in: no pretense of growth, no promise of change, no hidden purpose. Just water, and wind, and the long patience of stone becoming glass becoming something else, something the world has not yet named, in a silence that is not silent at all.
