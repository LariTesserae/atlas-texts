# The Hollow Mile

You descend through a mouth in the world's skin—a throat of basalt and compressed ochre—and the Hollow Mile opens below you like a held breath.

## The Terrain

The ground is **salt-crusted bone**. Not desert, exactly. The salt here is *geological*, laid down in the world's infancy when something hot met something that refused to dissolve. It patterns the floor in hexagonal plates, each one the size of a dinner table, each one slightly tilted from its neighbors like fallen cards. Between the plates: nothing. No dust. The gaps are too clean for dust, scoured by winds that carry no grit, only *pressure*.

The elevation is low but the space *feels* sunken. The walls—if they are walls—curve upward at the perimeter, a distances-away rim of something darker than shadow. The Hollow Mile is named for its length: one mile, precisely, from the eastern mouth to the western aperture where the light enters. You can stand in the center and see both ends. The geometry is *insisted upon*. Someone measured this. Someone built the mile.

The surface is young. You can see where the plates have *recently* settled, where edges still stand sharp, unweathered, as if the world's crust cracked yesterday and no one has walked here since to smooth the fractures. In places, the salt has begun to **flower**—crystalline growths, pale violet and mineral blue, blooming in the cracks like frost that never melts because there is no warmth to melt it, only the cool that preserves.

## The Built

The settlement is **vertical and apologetic**. Structures rise from the salt plates on stilts of blackened wood—*driftwood*, though no sea has ever been near, or perhaps wood from something that grew in the world's brief wet age and was preserved by the salt like a pickled thing. These stilts are the oldest construction. Above them, newer things perch: **inflated membranes**, translucent and yellowed, stretched over frames of bent reed or bone. They pulse slightly. They are *alive*, or nearly so, or they contain something alive—this is the first mystery.

Between the structures, **channels** have been cut into the salt. Not water-channels. They carry *air*, or something air-like—a thinner medium, perhaps, that moves through the Hollow Mile on a cycle you cannot yet perceive. The channels are lined with something that grows: a **lichen** that is not lichen, pale grey, almost luminous in the dimness, feeding on whatever passes through. It has colonized every surface it can reach. It is patient. It is *hungry* in a way that has nothing to do with eating.

The layout is **organized without plan**. Paths connect the structures in a network that repeats motifs—triangles, always triangles, converging on certain membrane-structures that are larger, more still, more *central* than the others. These are the hearts of the settlement, or its lungs, or its memories. You cannot tell from outside. The membranes do not open. They only pulse.

## The Atmosphere

**Cool, precisely cool**, the temperature of a held breath, of a room where someone has just left. The air is thin enough to taste metallic, to feel like a film on the tongue. It carries no moisture. It carries *sound* strangely—sounds travel too far, or not far enough, or arrive delayed, so that you hear your own footsteps on the salt a half-second after you make them.

The light is **dim and directional**. It enters only from the western aperture, a vertical slit in the rim, and it strikes the floor in a long parallelogram that slowly, *slowly* shifts across the mile as the world turns. Everything outside this parallelogram is in shadow that has texture—you can see the grain of the dark, the way it clings to the salt plates, the way it pools in the channel-bottoms. The light is **old**. It feels like light that has traveled a long way through something that filtered it, softened it, made it gentle.

There is **weather** here, but it is *internal*. Pressure differentials move through the Hollow Mile like slow tides. When they arrive, the membrane-structures pulse faster. The lichen in the channels stands up, filament by filament, *reaching*. The salt plates emit a sound—not quite a hum, more like the memory of a hum, a resonance you feel in your teeth. The inhabitants—*if there are inhabitants*—withdraw into the largest membrane-structures and wait. The weather passes. The pulse slowts. The light has moved a few inches westward.

## The Ecology

Life here is **sparse and strange**.

The **lichen** is the foundation. It does not photosynthesize—there is too little light, and no water. It metabolizes the pressure-differentials, the passing weather, the *motion* of the air. It grows toward movement. In stillness, it sleeps. In the pulse of a membrane-structure, it flowers, briefly, with spores that are not spores but **seeds of pressure**, little packets of compressed air that burst and create their own micro-weather, their own brief urgency.

The **membrane-structures** contain something that moves. You see it through the translucent walls, sometimes—a shadow that is too large to be an animal, too *organized* to be an organ. It pulses in rhythm with the structure itself. When the weather comes, it becomes still. When the weather passes, it resumes. It is **waiting for something**. The inhabitants know what. You do not yet.

There are **others**. The fauna is rare, as the world is rare, but it is *present*.

**Salt-worms**—if they are worms—burrow through the plates, leaving trails of disturbed crystal that heal slowly, over days, as the salt re-grows. They are blind. They follow the pressure-gradients, moving always toward the membrane-structures, always *up* the channels. They are eaten by something. Their remains accumulate in certain hollows, white and curled, like shed skins.

**Drift-birds**, perhaps. You hear them—once, twice—a sound like tearing fabric, high and brief. They cross the light-parallelogram in seconds, gone before you can track them. They do not land in the Hollow Mile. They pass through, *migrating* between the world's other hollows, other miles, other sealed places. They are the only thing here that is not *from* here, and they are in a hurry to leave.

And there is the **thing in the largest membrane**, the central structure, the one where the triangles converge. It is still when you watch it. It is *listening*. The pulse of its membrane is slower than the others, deeper, a heartbeat you feel in your sternum when you stand near. The lichen grows thickest around its stilts, a pale carpet that seems to *lean* toward it, hungry, reverent, afraid.

## The Rules of the World

The Hollow Mile is **one of many**. This becomes clear as you walk it. The western aperture, where the light enters, is not an opening to the outside world—it is an opening to *another* Hollow Mile, identical, adjacent, its own light coming from *your* east. The world is a **chain of miles**, each sealed, each cool, each dim, each built and inhabited and *waiting*. You can see the far rim of the next Mile through the aperture, the same curve, the same darkness, the same geometry. The chain extends in both directions until the world curves back on itself, or until the miles become something else—gas, void, the space between stars where no light reaches and no salt grows.

Time here is **layered**. The young salt, the old wood, the patient lichen, the pulsing membranes—these exist at different speeds. A day in the Hollow Mile is long enough for the light to cross from west to east and begin again. But *something* moves faster. The pressure-weather comes every few hours, or every few days—you lose track, because the dimness is constant, because there is no sun to mark the hours, only the slow migration of the light-parallelogram. The inhabitants—*if they are inhabitants, if they are not the membranes themselves, grown large and complex and *aware*—they live at a pace you cannot match. They have been here for a long time. They will be here when you are gone.

**Nothing decays.** This is the strangest rule. The salt-worm trails heal. The dead membranes—there are dead membranes, at the edges, where the pressure-weather has torn them, where the lichen has grown over them in thick mats—these do not rot. They *preserve*. They become part of the floor, new plates forming around them, incorporating them, making them *geology*. The world is young in its surface but *ancient* in its memory. Everything that has happened here is still here, compressed, waiting to be read by something that knows how.

**You are not the first to descend.** The mouth in the world's skin—the throat of basalt—has been used before. You find the traces: a flake of something that is not salt, not lichen, not wood—*metal*, perhaps, or plastic, or a material that does not exist in the world's chemistry. A footprint, pressed into a salt plate, filled with the pale violet crystal, the *flowering* that comes when the world is disturbed. Someone else stood where you stand. Someone else walked the mile, east to west or west to east, and descended into the next, or climbed out, or *became* something here. The Hollow Mile does not forget. It only waits for the next witness.

## The Silence

Sound is **absent**, and this absence is *active*. The Hollow Mile does not suppress sound—it has no sound to suppress. The pressure-weather makes no noise, only *presence*. The membrane-pulse is below hearing, felt in the bones. The salt-worms move without sound, their bodies too soft, the medium too thin. Even your own breathing is muted, *dampened* by the cool air, by the salt that coats your lips, by the geometry of the space that seems to absorb vibration.

And yet—you hear your footsteps, delayed, arriving from somewhere behind you. You hear the *settling* of the salt plates, a creak that is not a creak, a sound without source, the world *adjusting* to your weight. You hear, once, the membrane-structure in the center *inhale*—a wet sound, impossibly wet in a place with no water, a sound like something being born or something being *drowned*—and then silence again, deeper than before, as if the Mile has noticed you and is holding its breath.

## What Moves

The Hollow Mile is **active**, though it does not seem so. The pressure-weather cycles. The lichen grows, slowly, toward the membranes, toward the movement, toward the *pulse*. The salt plates settle, millimeter by millimeter, the geometry shifting, the triangles *breathing* as the world's crust adjusts to itself. The membrane-structures pulse, and in their pulse, they *communicate*—with each other, across the mile, a network of pressure and light and chemical signal that is *almost* language, *almost* thought.

And the thing in the center—it moves. You see it, finally, when the light-parallelogram reaches its stilt. It is **not one thing**. It is a *colony*, a cluster of membrane-pods, each pulsing at its own rhythm, the whole forming a shape that is *almost* familiar—a body, perhaps, or a *face*, or a *symbol* that your mind tries to read and cannot. It has been here longer than the settlement. It *is* the settlement, perhaps. Or the settlement grew around it, *protecting* it, *feeding* it, *worshipping* it without knowing.

The weather comes. The membranes pulse faster. The lichen rises. The thing in the center *turns*—not its whole mass, just a part of it, a pod, a *sensor*, orienting toward the western aperture, toward the light, toward *you*.

It has noticed you.

The Hollow Mile is no longer silent. It is *waiting*.

---

*You have walked the mile. You have seen the settlement. You have stood in the light-parallelogram and felt the cool air on your skin and heard the silence that is not silence.*

*Now you must decide: descend to the next Mile, through the eastern aperture, following the chain into whatever the world becomes when the salt ends; or climb, back through the mouth in the world's skin, back to whatever exists above, beyond, outside the geometry of sealed places.*

*The thing in the center pulses once, slowly, and in its pulse there is something that is almost a question.*

*The Hollow Mile does not hurry. It has all the time that the world gives it. It has been waiting since the salt first cracked, since the first membrane grew, since the first pressure-weather moved through the channels and woke the lichen and set the pulse in motion.*

*It can wait a little longer.*

*It can wait for you.*
