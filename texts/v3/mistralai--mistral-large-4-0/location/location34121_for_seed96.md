The world sits where the air grows thin enough to taste like metal and cold enough to slow thought itself. High altitude, young stone—mountains barely finished arguing with themselves, still sharp where erosion hasn't had centuries to round them. Yet everything here is *made*. No natural substrate remains. The stone you walk on was shaped, placed, fitted. The sharpness isn't geological accident but intention: edges left deliberate, angles calculated for purposes no longer remembered.

And water, everywhere water shouldn't be.

It weeps from the constructed surfaces themselves. Not pipes—this is primitive technology, hand-tools, simple fabrication—but the *materials* remember being wet. The walls sweat. Condensation gathers on every surface bright enough to catch the thin high-altitude light, which is relentless, almost aggressive in its clarity. The air is saturated to the point of precipitation at any slight cooling, any shadow passing, any breath. Pools form in the joints between fitted stones. Rivulets track down faces that were meant, perhaps, to be dry.

The builders are gone. What remains is their saturated architecture, slowly, patiently dissolving itself in its own atmosphere. Water finds every seam, every hairline fracture in the hand-worked stone, widens them over decades into channels, into grottos, into soft cavities that will one day, on timescales these mountains understand, become caves again. The construction fights this and loses. It was never meant to last.

There is no plant life, no animal life. But something *grows* in the wettest places—not green, not recognizably alive. Mineral accretions in branching patterns. Slow crystalline blooms where water has been still longest. The primitive builders might have recognized these as kin to the fungal mats they knew. Here they spread across the inner faces of the structures, pale and patient, consuming nothing, producing nothing, simply *accumulating* in the presence of constant moisture and time.

The sound is mostly quiet. Water dripping, almost everywhere, but softly—absorbed by the porous stone, muted by the thin air that carries sound poorly. Occasionally the *tick* of thermal contraction, a fitted joint settling, stone cooling in shadow. The wind, when it comes, moves through the constructed canyons, the deliberate gaps, the geometries of ventilation that no longer serve any living breath. It makes a low, hollow note, like blowing across the mouth of something vast and empty.

The scale opens across a valley of similar construction—tiered, terraced, descending toward a central depression where water has won most completely. A lake, perhaps, or what was meant to be a reservoir, now indistinguishable from natural catchment. The far walls of the valley are also built, also weeping, also slowly returning to something the builders would not recognize. You can see the whole system from any high point. It is *complete* in its abandonment. Every surface occupied, every space saturated, nothing left empty or waiting.

The weirdness lives in the light. At this altitude, with this much moisture in the air, the brightness has weight. It presses. Shadows are absolute, pools of cold and silence, while the illuminated stone glows with an almost internal radiance, the wet surfaces acting as mirrors for a sky that seems too close, too present. Rainbows form in the mist rising from warm stone, incomplete arcs, fragments of color that appear and dissolve in seconds. The world is perpetually on the edge of precipitation, perpetually *becoming* wet, never quite dry, never quite flooding.

Time moves slowly here. The processes are gradual: the widening of joints, the blooming of mineral life, the settling of structures into the softened stone beneath them, the slow, patient, almost affectionate dissolution of everything made. Nothing happens quickly. Nothing needs to. The builders left, or died, or simply stopped, and the world has taken up the work of undoing them with the patience of water, of cold, of light that does not fade.

You could walk for hours through corridors that were never meant for walking, past chambers that held something once—grain, water, people, meaning—now empty, damp, ringing with the occasional drip. The openness extends in every direction. The packed density is everywhere: no space unoccupied, no surface unwept by moisture, no silence complete. And yet it is still, in its way, peaceful. The saturation has made everything soft. Edges blur. Sound diminishes. The world waits, wet and bright and slowly, slowly changing.
