# The Submerged District of Varr

## First Impressions

You arrive not by road but by descent. The place has no proper horizon—only a dome of misted air, gray-green, pressing down from somewhere you cannot see. The scale is wrong from the first step. Things are *close*. Too close. The district was built in a bowl that used to hold a lake, and the lake never quite left.

Water is everywhere but rarely *open*. It moves through the city in channels you did not notice being cut, seeping up through paving stones that have been packed down by centuries of wet, swelling, settling. The streets are the lowest points. You walk with your boots squelching, or you walk on raised walkways of woven reed and timber that lattice between buildings, three feet above the seepage, and from these you can watch the water work.

## Terrain and Materials

The ground itself is young. This is not ancient bedrock worn smooth by time. The hills beyond the district—barely hills, rises of perhaps thirty meters—show the strata still arguing with each other, layers of clay and silt and something harder, something that might become stone if given another million years but has not yet committed. The district was built on the youngest of these rises, where the land had recently emerged from standing water, where the soil still remembers being lakebed.

Everything is *packed*. The density parameter reads true. Buildings press against buildings. There are no gaps for gardens or courtyards large enough to hold a tree. Walls touch walls. Alleyways are slots between structures, barely wider than a person, and often they are *water*, narrow channels where the groundwater expresses itself at the surface.

The building material is mostly wood—dark, waterlogged timber that has been soaking long enough to turn the color of old bone, or peat, or the inside of a violin. This is primitive technology, but not crude. The joinery is sophisticated: dovetails, mortise-and-tenon, pegs of harder wood driven through softer. They have learned to build *with* the water, not against it. Houses float on rafts of bundled reed when the seepage rises, held in place by guide-posts, allowed to rise and fall with the season. More permanent structures stand on stilts driven deep into the silting clay, and their lowest floors are given over to the water: storage for reeds, pens for the few animals, the constant sound of dripping.

Stone appears only in the oldest constructions, and it is *weeping* stone. Water condenses on its surface and runs in thin sheets. You cannot touch it without your hand coming away wet, cold. The masons have learned to face their walls with this stone and to channel the condensation into collection troughs. The city drinks its own walls.

## Atmosphere

Cool. The temperature sits in that narrow band where breath is visible but not freezing, where moisture does not quite become fog but hangs in the air as a fine suspended drizzle that is not rain exactly, only the *possibility* of rain, the atmosphere remembering water and holding it in reserve. Evaporation is slow. Nothing dries completely. Fabrics stay damp. Wood stays swollen. Metal—what little there is, simple iron, worked with charcoal fires—rushes to rust, and the people have learned to oil it constantly, to accept the orange bloom as a fact of the world.

The light is diffuse, filtered through the moisture-laden air. No hard shadows. Everything is soft-edged, slightly luminous, as if the world were viewed through gauze. When the sun does break through—it is not often—it comes in beams that are almost *solid*, visible columns of brightness in which you can see the suspended water, the dust, the spores, the particular density of this place's air.

## Sound

Mostly quiet, but the quiet is *full*. Not empty. The dominant sound is water in its many modes: the drip from eaves and condensation troughs (a percussion like slow clockwork, irregular, *alive*), the seep and gurgle of channels moving through narrow passages, the soft *slap* of water against the hulls of floating houses as they rise and fall on their guide-posts. Frogs, though fauna is rare, have found the channels. Their voices are low, intermittent, surprised-sounding.

Human sound is muffled. Voices carry strangely here—close sounds come from far away, and distant sounds arrive with unexpected clarity when the wind shifts. There is no shouting. The packed density means privacy is acoustic as well as physical; you must lean close to speak, and your words do not travel.

At night—if you can tell night from day in this diffuse light—there is a sound that has no source, or many sources. A low *hum*, felt in the sternum more than heard. The water moving through the saturated ground, perhaps. The district settling, millimeter by millimeter, into the soft earth. The reeds in their bundles, rubbing.

## Ecology

Vegetation is sparse but *insistent*. What grows, grows in the cracks. Moss colonizes every north-facing wall, every shaded corner, turning surfaces to soft green velvet. Ferns push through gaps in paving, in the joints between floating houses. A few trees survive—willows, mostly, and one kind of alder—rooted in the highest points, the tops of the rises where the water table drops just low enough. They are stunted, wind-shaped, their roots visible and gnarled above the general ground level as the soil subsides around them.

There are no fields. No agriculture in any recognizable form. The people of Varr eat what the water provides and what they can trade for from the dryer lands beyond the mist-dome. Fish, obviously, but also *reeds*—harvested constantly, woven into everything from baskets to the very walkways and rafts. The reed is the true crop here. It grows in the channels, in the flooded margins, in any standing water. They have species of it bred for particular purposes: structural, textile, edible. The district smells of it, green and slightly sweet and always faintly fermenting.

Fauna is rare, as the parameter suggests. What few animals there are, are *adapted*. Water-skeaters on the channel surfaces. A kind of pale fish in the deeper pools, nearly blind, found only here. Birds pass through but do not stay—there is nothing for them, no insects in sufficient density, no seeds. The people keep no livestock larger than ducks, and the ducks are kept on the floating houses, fed on reed-shoots, their eggs a precious protein.

But there is something else. The weirdness parameter, the unusual. In the deepest channels, where the water is stagnant and warmed by the packed walls, there are *growths*. Not plants exactly. Mats of something fibrous and pale-yellow, like the mycelium of some enormous fungus but with no fruiting body, no visible reproduction. The people harvest it, dry it, use it as tinder, as insulation, as a kind of sponge for collecting the condensation from stone. They call it *mother*, or *the sleeper*. It grows faster where human activity is densest, where the water is most enriched. They do not ask what it is. They are too busy.

## The Rules of This Place

Movement is vertical as much as horizontal. You climb walkways to descend to channels. You descend stairs to ascend to the floating houses when the water rises. The district has its own tide, not lunar but *barometric*, responding to pressure changes in the vast wetland that surrounds it, the region that was once lake and is becoming something else, slowly, over generations.

Time is measured by wetness. The *dry time*, when the channels run low and the condensation lessens, is a season of festivals and repairs. The *deep time*, when the floating houses rise highest on their posts and the stilts show bare and the smell of stagnant water is strongest, is a season of quiet and waiting and the harvesting of mother.

There is no expansion. The bowl is full. The packed density is not a choice but a *fact*, a consequence of the limited high ground. New construction means replacement, means tearing down to build up, means the constant recycling of waterlogged timber into new forms. The district is a machine for processing wet wood into shelter, and it has been running long enough to develop its own momentum, its own *ecology* of human and material and water.

## What Emerges

I find myself thinking of Varr as a *body*. The channels are veins, carrying nutrients and waste and information. The floating houses are cells, mobile, responsive. The weeping stone is skin, porous, exchanging moisture with the environment. The mother-growth is a symbiote, perhaps a digestive system, processing what the city discards. The whole district is *breathing*, slowly, the rise and fall of water on the scale of hours and days.

And it is *young*. The erosion is early-formation. The sharp features—the edges of new construction, the unweathered faces of fresh-cut timber, the abrupt transitions between flooded and dry—have not yet been softened. This is a city in its first century, perhaps its second, built on land that was lake within living memory. The people remember the water. Some of them still have boats from before, hung in the highest rafters, waiting for the return of the deep time that may never come.

There is a quality of *concentration* here. Everything is close, packed, saturated. No room for the self to expand. You must learn to be small, to move in the slots between presences, to accept the wet and the press of walls and the constant low sound of water working. It is not comfortable. It is not, perhaps, survivable for those not born to it.

But it is *alive*. The dynamic parameter reads true. So many processes: the water moving, the wood swelling and shrinking, the mother growing, the condensation forming and falling, the district settling into itself, the people building and rebuilding in their patient, primitive way. Nothing here is finished. Nothing here is stable. The city is a *verb*, not a noun—a place that is happening, that is wetly and densely and strangely *becoming*.

And when the mist lifts, briefly, on certain mornings when the pressure shifts and the air clears to something approaching transparency, you can see the hills beyond. The rises where the soil is still young, still arguing with itself. And you understand that Varr is not an end but a *beginning*—the first settlement in a landscape that is still deciding what it will be, a human presence inserted into a world that is remaking itself around them, patient, wet, and watchful.
