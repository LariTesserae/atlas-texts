**The Drowned Lattice**

You stand in a place where *making* has exceeded *growing* by some vast, almost geologic margin—yet the making itself has gone soft, vegetal, absorbed. The built world here is not stone and steel but *callus*: layer upon layer of hand-worked accretion, grown and pruned and grafted into being until the distinction between cultivated and constructed dissolved entirely. This is a civilization that abandoned extraction for *tending*. Their technology is older than metal in any refined sense—bone, chitin, calcified secretion, fiber twisted by patient hands, resin harvested from trees that were themselves bred over millennia to weep it more freely.

And the trees weep constantly. That is the water, here: not rain, not river, but *exudation*. The entire highland mass breathes moisture. At 2.4 elevation, you are above the weather of lower worlds, yet the air holds a saturation that should be impossible—condensation without cloud, a perpetual humid haze that never quite resolves into precipitation. Water moves through this place as *film*: sheeting down the surfaces of everything, pooling in the deliberate basins that the builders have grown into their architecture, threading through the porous lattice of their constructions, rising as vapor from the heated hollows below, falling again as the dim light cools each evening.

The light is wrong. At 0.9, it filters through something—multiple layers of living membrane, perhaps, or the particulate haze of spores and evaporating minerals that fills these high, vast spaces. The sun, when you catch it, appears as a pale disc through gauze, through *tissue*. Shadows barely exist. Everything glows from within its own moisture.

**Scale** has done something to physics here. This is not merely a large place; it is large enough that walking across a single "district" exhausts a day. The builders have achieved continental reach without rapid transit, which means their concepts of distance are profoundly *local*—a village is a world, a valley is a journey, and the spaces between settlements are *wilderness* in the deepest sense, not because they are untended but because they have been allowed to grow *wrong*. Feral. The planned and the escaped have become indistinguishable at this scale, over these timescales of erosion-established equilibrium.

And the space between things is *vast*. Density 0.5 means you can stand in the grandest plaza and see to the horizon, across acres of open, wet, dimly glowing surface, and perceive perhaps three other figures. The architecture rises in *punctuated* masses—towers of woven and calcified material, domes that are half-fungus half-intention, bridges that span chasms of mist. Between them: emptiness. Wet emptiness. The sound of dripping. The creak of something organic, settling.

Yet **vegetation 2.9** insists that this emptiness is *filled*. The plants are the substrate, the architecture, the weather-maker, the food, the tool, the medicine. There is no ground that is not root-mass, no surface that is not leaf or bark or the engineered equivalent. The "empty" spaces are *meadows* of a kind—low growth, certainly, but a continuous living membrane that carpets every horizontal plane, that climbs every vertical one, that has long since consumed whatever distinction once existed between floor and wall and ceiling. **Weirdness 2.7** lives in the seams. Reality here is *negotiated*. The builders have been at this so long, with such total commitment to the grown-made, that the world itself seems uncertain whether it is artifact or organism. Structures reproduce. Not metaphorically—*actually*. A sufficiently tended tower, left alone for a generation, will have budded new chambers, new corridors, extensions that no hand planned but that follow the logic of the original as a crystal follows its seed. The distinction between a building dying and a building *becoming something else* is philosophical, and the philosophers here have stopped asking. **The rules of this place, as they emerge:**

Time moves differently in the high wet. A day is long, because the dim light sustains a slow photosynthesis, a slow metabolism. The builders think in generations. Their "primitive" technology is not *young* but *mature*—a stable plateau of hand-craft that has no need to escalate, because it works, because it is *integrated*, because the alternative (extraction, burning, the hard machines of lower worlds) would poison the membrane they depend on.

Death here is *incorporation*. Bodies are not buried but *planted*. The dead become structure, become nutrient, become—eventually—the resin that seals a new corridor, the chitin that armors a new tower's base, the rich loam from which a memorial garden grows. There is no waste. There is no outside.

The weather of this place is its own, 2.8 scale. Storms form and dissipate within the highland mass, cycling moisture upward and downward in patterns that the builders have learned to read, to *garden*. They do not control the rain—there is no rain, only the weeping—but they shape the *collection*, the *channeling*, the *evaporation*. They are hydrologists of a system that is half-built, half-grown, indistinguishable in its behavior.

And somewhere, in the vast spaces between the towers, in the fog-shrouded valleys where the light barely reaches, things grow that were never intended. The feral. The escaped. The *other*—vegetation 2.9 turned loose on itself, architecture that has forgotten its makers, fauna that have no name in any builder's tongue. The weirdness 2.7 concentrates there. Reality thins. The membrane of the world, stretched across such scale, such time, such *commitment* to the made-grown, begins to show its seams. You might walk into a clearing and find a structure that breathes. A plant that answers when spoken to. A silence so complete it has *weight*.

The builders know this. They have always known. It is why they keep their settlements sparse, their density low, their connections *thin*—not from poverty of means, but from *care*. The empty spaces are *buffer*. The vastness is *safety*. They have made a world that could easily have made itself instead, and they tend it with the gentleness of gardeners who know that some seeds should never be planted.

You are a visitor here, or a fragment, or a memory the place is having of itself. The moisture settles on your skin—whatever skin you have, whatever surface you present to this world. The dim light finds you. The silence holds you.

The lattice breathes. The water moves. The green waits, patient, for whatever you will become.
