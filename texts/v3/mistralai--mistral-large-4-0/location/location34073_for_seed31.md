# The Drowned Hollows of Varnmass

---

The place holds water like a held breath. Not everywhere—never everywhere—but *near*. You feel it before you reach it. The air at this elevation carries a persistent dampness that never quite lifts, a condensation that gathers on every surface by mid-morning and lingers until the afternoon warmth, if it comes, barely stirs it.

You are high. 2.3 is not mountain, not yet, but the land has begun its upward conversation. The hollows dip between young ridges—*young*—sharp-edged where erosion has only recently begun its patient work. The stone here remembers being born. No rounded river rocks, no softened hills. Instead: fractured faces, scree slopes that shift underfoot, waterfalls that appear overnight after rain and vanish just as quickly into fissures in the bedrock.

---

## Terrain

The Varnmass Hollows are a town built *into* wetness. Not a town that drained its land—that would require a permanence the geology denies. Instead, the builders learned to live with water at the surface, water just below it, water that rises and falls with each storm and each season.

Three main depressions, each roughly a kilometer across, hold the settlement. They are not lakes. The water is too shallow, too interrupted by stone, too full of the mineral turbidity that comes from young rock still surrendering its chemistry. Call them *flats*—silt flats, reed flats, the places where water and mud negotiate their boundary.

Between the hollows, the land rises in sharp, unweathered ridges. These are the *cuts*—paths worn by human feet and the occasional pack animal, following the only gradients gentle enough for travel. The cuts are lined with wooden walkways in the oldest parts of the settlement, raised platforms of split cedar and woven reed that keep the traveler's boots above the seepage that saturates the soil everywhere else.

The elevation gives the place a particular quality of air: thin enough that sounds carry strangely, far and flat, yet dense enough with moisture that they also *fall* short, absorbed by the wet atmosphere. A voice raised on one flat might not reach the next. A shout from the ridge-top might arrive at the far hollow as a murmur, or a shriek, depending on the temperature inversion, the humidity, the particular stillness of the afternoon.

---

## Materials

**Stone**: Young, angular, *talking*. The local bedrock is a fractured granite-gneiss, still releasing its feldspars into the water. It weeps. Not dramatically—no curtain waterfalls—but *constantly*, a slow seep from every cut face, every exposed joint. The builders call it *sweating stone*. They have learned to channel it, to direct the weeping into cisterns, to let it run down prepared grooves in the masonry rather than find its own paths through the walls.

**Wood**: Cedar and a pale, water-resistant pine that grows in the thin soil of the ridges. Both are worked green, shaped while supple, then dried in place—a technique that yields joints which *tighten* as the wood seasons, swelling against their fitted frames to shed rain rather than admit it.

**Reed**: The dominant construction material of the flats themselves. *Phragmites* and a thicker, cane-like species that the locals call *hollow-stalk*. It grows in the shallow water, in the saturated mud, in the margins where land and water have not yet decided their boundary. It is cut in winter when the sap has withdrawn, bundled, and woven into mats that form the walls of most dwellings. These walls breathe. They absorb moisture and release it. They are not permanent—no structure here is permanent—but they are renewable, replaceable, *honest* in their temporariness.

**Mud and silt**: The hollows themselves. A fine, gray-brown sediment that holds water like a sponge and releases it like a sponge. Foundations here are not dug—you cannot dig, not deeply, not in ground this saturated. Instead, buildings rest on *rafts*: platforms of woven reed and timber, floated on the mud like boats, anchored by posts driven until they meet something solid, which might be ten meters down, might be never.

**Clay**: A gray, glacial clay in lenses within the hollows, exposed in the cut banks where water has undercut the flats. This is the pottery clay, the material of the town's one export—a coarse, water-tight ware fired in kilns on the ridge-tops where the wood is dry enough to burn hot.

## Atmosphere

The light is *normal*—1.9—but it arrives filtered. The moisture in the air scatters it, gives it a quality of perpetual overcast even when the sky is clear. Shadows are soft, edges indistinct. At this elevation, the sun feels close but not warm; it dries the surface of things while the ground beneath remains cold, saturated, *alive* with slow processes.

Mist is a daily presence. It rises from the flats in the morning, a gray-white veil that reduces visibility to tens of meters. It burns off by midday, usually, revealing the settlement in its full strange geometry: platforms and walkways and reed-walls, all of it slightly *tilted*, settling into the mud at different rates, no two structures quite level, all of them leaning slightly toward the water, as if listening to it.

The temperature is warm—1.6—but *conditional*. The hollows are warm. The ridges are cool, wind-scoured, their thin soil supporting only sparse vegetation. A day in Varnmass might offer both: a morning in the reed-beds where the air is thick and insect-heavy, an afternoon on the cut-paths where the wind comes down from the heights and the temperature drops ten degrees in an hour.

---

## The Settlement: Varnmass

Three hollows, roughly aligned northeast to southwest, connected by the cuts. The total population is perhaps four hundred, though the town has no census, no boundary. You are *of* Varnmass if you have a platform in one of the hollows, if you work the reed-beds or the clay lenses or the ridge-gardens. The town has grown by accretion—new platforms adjacent to old, old platforms sinking and being abandoned, their timber salvaged for newer construction up-slope, or left to become part of the mud, the submerged architecture that occasionally surfaces whole, a walkway, a threshold, a post still upright after decades of burial.

The layout is *organic* but not chaotic. The oldest parts, in the center of each hollow, are denser—platforms close enough that a person can step from one to another, reed-walls touching, the whole forming a kind of floating *neighborhood*. Further out, the settlement thins. Individual homesteads, their own cisterns, their own small gardens on rafts or on the drier margins. Beyond that, the working land: the reed-beds, the clay pits, the terraced gardens on the lower slopes of the ridges where the soil is thin but the drainage is *almost* adequate.

Infrastructure is *present* but *soft*. There are no streets, only paths—raised walkways of split cedar, some of them roofed against the rain, most open to the sky. Water is everywhere: channels cut into the mud, aqueducts of hollowed timber carrying the *sweating stone's* seepage to cisterns, the natural drainage of the hollows directing storm-water through sluices that can be opened or closed by hand. There is no central water authority. Each platform, each neighborhood, manages its own.

Waste is a constant negotiation. The saturated ground cannot absorb it. The town has learned to *export* its waste—composted reed-mats, the spent clay from the pits, the organic refuse, all of it carried by boat (flat-bottomed, poled through the shallow water) to the *midden-slopes* on the ridges, where the drier soil can process it, where the rain can leach it without contaminating the water the town drinks.

## Vegetation

Lush—1.5—but *strangely* lush. The lushness is concentrated. The hollows are green, dense, productive. The ridges are sparse, almost barren. There is no gradual transition, only the sharp edge where saturated soil gives way to draining rock.

In the hollows: reed-beds of impossible density, *hollow-stalk* rising three, four meters, their stems used for construction, their leaves for thatch, their roots stabilizing the mud, their seeds edible, ground into a coarse flour. Water-lilies in the deeper channels, their flowers white and yellow and *brief*, opening in the morning, closing by afternoon. Willows—a weeping species, *salix* something, the locals do not name it scientifically—growing in the margins, their roots drinking, their branches providing the flexible withies for basketry, for fish-traps, for the slightly-springy poles that support the walkway railings.

On the ridges: a sparse woodland of the pale pine, stunted by the thin soil, their roots gripping fractured rock. Between the trees, a ground cover of moss and lichen and a tough, wiry grass that the town's few grazing animals—*are there animals?*—would eat if there were animals. The vegetation on the ridges is *old* in its patterns, slow-growing, adapted to drought and wind and the occasional fire that sweeps down from the heights. It contrasts with the *young* lushness of the hollows, the reed that grows a meter in a season, that must be cut or it will choke its own waterways, that is *always* being renewed, always starting over.

There are gardens. On the lower slopes, terraced into the hillside with dry-stone walls, vegetables that tolerate the cool: root crops, leafy greens, a few beans. On rafts in the hollows, *floating gardens*—platforms of woven reed, weighted with mud, planted with crops that like their roots wet: rice, in the deepest water, a recent introduction the town is still learning; taro; watercress, which grows wild and is harvested rather than cultivated.

## Ecology — The Absence

There are no animals. 0.3—this is not a place where fauna has failed to arrive. It is a place where fauna *chose* not to stay, or was *prevented* from staying, or arrived and found the conditions so marginal that populations never established.

The town has no livestock. No dogs, no cats, no draught animals, no poultry. The protein comes from fish—*if there are fish*—or from the reed-seeds, the water-lily roots, the occasional insect that can be harvested from the reed-beds, the *frogs* that appear in the shallows during the warm months and are caught, cooked, eaten, their absence in winter a kind of mourning.

The fish are uncertain. The hollows are young, geologically. The water is mineral-heavy, still acquiring the chemistry that supports insect life, that supports fish. There are fish in the deeper channels, in the pools that hold water year-round, but they are small, bony, *strange*—adapted to turbid water, to low oxygen, to the particular balance of minerals that leaches from the young stone. The town eats them. The town does not *farm* them—there is no aquaculture, no pond-culture, no attempt to breed or select or improve. The fish are *wild*, or *feral*, or simply *present*, and the town takes what the water offers.

Insects are the closest thing to fauna. Mosquitoes, abundant, a constant irritation, their larvae in every standing pool. Dragonflies, in the summer, patrolling the reed-beds, their nymphs aquatic, their adults *brief*, a few weeks of aerial grace before death. Beetles in the rotting reed, in the mud, in the stored grain. The town has no word for *pest*. The insects are simply *there*, part of the wetness, part of the lushness, part of the world that the town has learned to coexist with rather than dominate.

---

## Sound

Silence. 0.3—not absolute silence, but *near*-silence. The wet atmosphere absorbs sound. The absence of animals removes the biological chorus—no birds, no frogs in the cold months, no insect *stridulation* loud enough to carry. What remains is the sound of *water*: the seep of the sweating stone, the drip from reed-thatch, the slap of rain on platforms, the *hiss* of mist rising from warm mud. The sound of *work*: the pole striking shallow water, the reed-cutter's blade, the potter's clay being wedged, the occasional shout across a flat, flattened by distance and moisture into something barely distinguishable from the wind in the reeds.

At night, the silence is *complete*. The temperature drops, the mist settles, the water goes still. A person on a platform hears their own breathing, the creak of the settling timber, the distant *tick* of cooling stone. The town has no night-watch, no bell, no curfew. The silence *is* the curfew. To make noise after dark is to violate something, not a law but a *condition*, the condition of the place itself.

## The Rules of the World

The world of Varnmass is *dynamic* in its small ways and *never changing* in its large. 0.5—this is the equilibrium of a place that has found its pattern and repeats it.

**The water rises and falls.** This is the fundamental rhythm. The hollows flood in the wet season, shrink in the dry, but never empty, never fully dry. The platforms rise and fall with them, their anchored posts allowing vertical movement, their moorings—ropes of reed-fiber, chains of forged iron in the oldest parts—holding them in place horizontally. A platform might move three meters up and down in a year. The walkways between platforms are *hinged*, *sliding*, designed to accommodate this movement. The town is, in a sense, *afloat*, always adjusting to the level of the water beneath it.

**Nothing is permanent.** The reed-walls last five years, perhaps ten if well-maintained. The timber platforms, twenty, thirty. The stone foundations, the cisterns, the aqueducts—they last longer, a century, two, but they *will* fail, will be undercut by seepage, will crack, will be abandoned and replaced. The town has no *center*, no *heart*, no structure that defines it. The oldest buildings are in the *middle* of the hollows, where the mud is deepest and the settling most pronounced, and they are the most *tilted*, the most *precarious*, the closest to being reclaimed by the ground that supports them.

**The young stone is still forming.** The erosion is *active*, 0.8—not violent, but *present*. The ridges are slowly lowering. The hollows are slowly filling, with silt, with organic matter, with the slow accumulation of the town's own waste and the mineral precipitate from the sweating stone. In a thousand years, perhaps ten thousand, the hollows will be meadows. The town will have moved, or been abandoned, or become something else entirely. But in the human scale—in the scale of a life, a generation, a century—the change is *imperceptible*. The town exists in a *frozen* moment of geological time, new enough to be sharp, stable enough to build upon.

**The temperature is warm but conditional.** The hollows trap heat. The ridges release it. The town lives in the *interface*, the margin where warm wet air meets cool dry air, where mist forms and burns off, where the day's weather is decided in the space between. A person in Varnmass learns to read the mist, the wind, the particular *feel* of the air. The town has no calendar in the abstract—no fixed dates, no seasonal festivals tied to the sun. Instead, the *conditions* mark time: the week when the reed is ready to cut, the week when the water-lilies bloom, the week when the mosquitoes become unbearable, the brief dry interval when the clay can be dug and the kilns fired. These are the town's *years*, its *months*, its *days*.

## The Technology

Primitive. 0.7—but *sophisticated* within its limits. The town has metal: iron, in small quantities, traded for pottery, for reed-mats, for the labor of cutting and carrying. The iron is worked by a few specialists, *smiths* who are also *engineers*, who understand the properties of the sweating stone, the behavior of water in timber channels, the structural limits of reed and cedar. They do not * smelt*—the ore comes from elsewhere, from the lowlands to the south where the vegetation is different, the geology older, the furnaces possible. In Varnmass, metal is *shaped*, *joined*, *repaired*. A good iron tool—an axe-head, a potter's rib, a sluice-gate hinge—might last a generation, might be reforged, rehafted, *inherited*.

The town's technology is *hydraulic*. Water is its power source, its transport, its waste system, its defense against fire (there is always water, always *wet* surfaces, always the possibility of flooding that makes uncontrolled fire impossible). The sluices, the channels, the cisterns—these are the town's *machines*, worked by hand, by simple levers, by the weight of water itself. There is no wheel, no mill, no *mechanical* advantage beyond the pulley and the lever. The town's industry is *manual*: the cutting of reed, the weaving of mats, the digging of clay, the shaping of pottery, the construction and maintenance of the platforms and walkways.

The town has *writing*—a simple system of knots in reed-fiber, records kept on waterproofed tablets of compressed clay. The records are *practical*: water-levels, reed-harvests, clay-yields, the tilt of platforms, the dates of repairs. There is no literature, no history, no *narrative*. The past is *present* in the tilt of the walls, the depth of the mud, the location of the submerged walkways. To know the past is to *measure* the present, to understand how much the ground has settled, how much the water has risen, how much time has passed since the last major repair.

## The Light

Normal. 1.9—but *particular*. The light of Varnmass is *diffuse*, *soft*, *without shadow*. The moisture in the air scatters it, the mist filters it, the overcast sky—when there is sky—spreads it evenly across the landscape. Colors are *muted*: the gray-green of the reed, the gray-brown of the mud, the pale yellow of the water-lilies, the *blue-gray* of the sweating stone when it is wet. The town has no bright colors, no pigments that would stand out, no *need* for them. Visibility is a kind of *camouflage*. To be seen in Varnmass is to be *noticed*, and to be noticed is to be *counted*, and the town's survival depends on a kind of *distributed* presence, many small presences, none of them dominant.

At dawn, when the mist is thickest, the world is *gray*. A single tone, varying only in density. The town is *invisible*, dissolved into the wet air. As the mist burns off, the world *emerges*: first the nearest platforms, their reed-walls, their walkways; then the next flat, its shapes resolving from the background; finally the ridges, sharp against the sky, the cuts visible as lines of movement, of *life* in the otherwise still landscape.

At dusk, the process reverses. The light *fails* slowly, absorbed by the returning mist, the moisture rising from the cooling mud. The town *dissolves* again, platform by platform, hollow by hollow, until only the ridge-tops remain visible, dark shapes against the last gray light, and then they too are gone, and the town is *silence* and *darkness* and the sound of water moving in channels, in seepage, in the slow settling of saturated ground.

---

## What Remains

A town built on water, in water, *of* water. Young, sharp, *temporary*—yet persistent, repeating its patterns, its repairs, its slow adjustments to the rising and falling of the hollows. A place without animals, without noise, without permanence. A place where the stone sweats, the reed grows a meter in a season, the platforms tilt and settle and are *replaced*. A place where the light is soft, the colors muted, the silence broken only by water and work and the occasional human voice, flattened by distance and moisture into something barely distinguishable from the wind.

The town has no name for itself, no *myth* of its founding, no story of why it is here. It is here because the hollows are here, because the water is near the surface, because the reed grows, because the clay is diggable, because the ridges provide the dry ground for kilns and for the midden-slopes and for the *perspective* that allows the town to see itself, to measure its own slow sinking, its own slow filling, its own *duration* in the young, wet, waiting world.
