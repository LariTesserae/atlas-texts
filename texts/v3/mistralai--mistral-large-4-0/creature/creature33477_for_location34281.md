I'll let this place show me who survives here, who persists in this wrong scale, this compression of industry into marsh. Not the humans first — they are scant, and their stories will emerge from what they tend, what they listen to, what they have learned to become.

# Those Who Remain

## The Maintainers

They are not many. Perhaps forty, perhaps sixty — the census is impossible in a town where addresses are water levels and a door might open onto a different room than it did yesterday. They are **old** in the way this place measures age: not by years, which pass too quickly here, but by *depths*. How many flood seasons they have waited out. How many pumps they have kept running past their designed lifespan. How many times they have climbed a tufa column to clear a drainage channel and felt the stone *breathe* beneath their palms, the mineral seep responding to their weight.

They wear **laminated layers** — not waterproof, nothing here is waterproof, but *managed*. Oilcloth over wool over something synthetic that wicks. Their clothing is always damp, always *theirs*, the salt of their own sweat and the condensation mingled into a personal humidity. They smell of moss and of the particular mineral tang of this stone, of the ozone that comes before water rises.

They move through the town in **patterns**, not routes. The maps are useless — the folding shifts, slightly, seasonally, as the water table breathes. A Maintainer knows the town by **sound**: the particular gurgle of a healthy drain, the wrong silence of a blocked one. By **texture**: the give of moss that will hold weight, the slickness that means mineral crust, the warmth that means a pump still running below. By **the weird frequency**, which they feel in their sternum, their teeth, the fluid of their inner ear, and which becomes — after decades — a kind of *proprioception*, a way of knowing where they are in the compressed geography when their eyes cannot tell.

They do not speak much. The town is loud with its own patience, and speech is a kind of violence against that silence. When they do speak, it is **demonstration**: a hand on a wall, feeling for the vibration of rising water. A gesture toward a moss colony, showing how it is harvested without killing the root. The slow, deliberate motion of tying a rope to a tufa formation, teaching the angle, the tension, the *give* that means the stone is still growing.

Some of them are **very old**. Not frail — the wetness preserves, in a way, the way bogs preserve — but *still*. They sit in the highest, driest levels of the oldest chimneys, where the drone-sensors still broadcast their wordless data, and they *listen*. They listen to the weird frequency until it becomes indistinguishable from their own heartbeat. They listen to the town settling, the slow contraction of cooling metal, the *breathing* of ventilation systems that have no fans, the voice of water rising through pipes in a language of pressure and flow and mineral memory.

They do not fear death. Death here is **incorporation**. The town takes everything, eventually. A body becomes a cistern, a foundation, a place where moss grows thicker, where the weird frequency resonates slightly differently. The oldest Maintainers have *chosen* their final locations — a particular stairwell, a specific tufa column, a drone-sensor they have tended for so long that they will be found with it, fused in some way that future Maintainers will not be able to separate, will not want to.

They are **not lonely**. The town is company enough. The drones, in their slow circuits. The moss, in its bioluminescent pulsing. The water, always rising, always falling, always *speaking*.

## The Moss-Keepers

A subset of the Maintainers, or perhaps a different order entirely — the distinction has dissolved. They tend the **bioluminescent colonies** with the care of gardeners, which they are, in the oldest sense of the word.

The moss is **currency**. It is also **light**. In a town where artificial illumination is rationed, where to kindle a flame is to make a claim, the blue-green glow of the cultivated colonies is *life*. It is read by. It is worked by. It is loved by, in the way one loves a source of warmth, a source of water, a source of *continuation*.

The Moss-Keepers know **which colonies** to encourage, which to harvest, which to leave alone. They know the **rhythms** — the pulse of light that follows the water table, the dimming that comes with the deepest floods, the brightening that follows drainage, the slow recovery. They know the **substrates**: which stones will hold a colony, which metals will poison it, which combinations of mineral seepage and organic matter and *time* will produce the particular blue-green that can be read by, that can sustain a human moving through the perpetual twilight.

They **sing** to the moss. Not songs with words — the water carries sound strangely here, and words become distorted, become *other things*. But **tones**, hummed at the frequency of the weird resonance, *encourage* growth. The moss responds. This is not superstition. This is **cultivation**, refined over centuries, over millennia of human presence in this folded, wet, wrong-scaled place.

Some Moss-Keepers are **younger** than the oldest Maintainers. The work is endless, and it *wants* to be done. A colony that has been harvested badly will sicken, will dim, will die — and the light it provided will be *lost*, and someone will move through the dark, and someone will be afraid, and someone will *claim* a flame that should not be claimed. The responsibility is **absolute**. The Moss-Keepers understand this. They have *grown into* it, the way the moss grows into stone, the way the town grows into marsh, the way the marsh grows into the compressed, waiting earth.

They live in the **middle zones** — not the highest, driest levels of the oldest inhabitants, not the lowest, wettest levels where the tufa grows and the drones move, but the **perpetual in-between**, where the moss is thickest, where the light is dimmest, where the boundary between architecture and hydrology is most completely dissolved. Their homes are **moss itself**, cultivated over decades into walls, into floors, into the very air they breathe — humid, mineral-tinged, faintly luminous.

They eat what the moss **supports**: the microbial mats, harvested; the fern fiber, woven; the occasional insect, caught in the dew-heavy webs, small and strange and *not absent*; the fish, if fish exist here, if they have always existed here, adapted to the dark and the mineral-rich water, the slow-moving predators of a slow-moving world.

## The Drone-Tenders

Fewest in number. Perhaps a dozen, perhaps fewer. They are the ones who **remember** the machines as machines, who have not yet forgotten that the drones were *built*, that they have *purposes*, that they were once *tools* in the hands of a civilization that has become something else, something that does not use tools so much as *inhabit* them, *become* them, *grow over* them.

They are **mechanics** in a town that has no mechanics, only Maintainers. They know the **trickle-current systems** that keep the submersible drones moving on power cells that should have failed, that *have* failed, that are kept operational by redox gradients in the sediment, by chemical batteries fed by the difference between oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor water, by *mistakes* in the original design that became features, that became traditions, that became *religions*.

They know the **sensor arrays** in the highest chimneys, the ones that still broadcast their wordless data to receivers that no longer exist, that log their measurements into solid-state memory that will outlast the civilization that built them. They know which of these sensors still *glow*, which have become landmarks, which have been *claimed* by Moss-Keepers as light-sources, as points of orientation in the compressed, wrong-scaled dark.

They **talk** to the drones. Not with words — the drones do not understand words, and the Drone-Tenders have lost, or never had, the habit of speaking to machines — but with **adjustments**, with **repairs**, with the slow, patient work of keeping something operational that *wants* to fail, that has *tried* to fail, that is kept alive by the sheer *insistence* of the Tender, by the refusal to let it become, finally, *ruin*.

They are **strange** to the other inhabitants. The Maintainers fear them, slightly — they touch the machines, they *modify* them, they have been known to open housings, to clean lenses blinded by biofilm, to *restore* cameras that should have stayed blind. The Moss-Keepers are suspicious of them — the Drone-Tenders disturb the water, their movements through the cisterns stir up sediment, disrupt the delicate balance of microbial mats, *threaten* the colonies that provide light.

But the Drone-Tenders **persist**. They are **patient** in a way that matches the town's own patience, the patience of water rising, of moss growing, of the weird frequency resonating through stone and water and compressed geography. They move through the flooded passages in **watertight suits** that are always leaking, always *becoming* part of the water, always *wet*. They carry **tools** that have no names, that were improvised from salvage, from the detritus of an industrial past that collapsed or *evolved* or simply *slowed* into something unrecognizable.

They have found the **modified drone**. The one whose camera housing has been opened, whatever has grown inside it, a moss colony perhaps or a microbial mat or something that uses the drone's thermal signature as a habitat. They move through the flooded passages on a cycle they cannot predict. The Drone-Tenders have **watched**. They have **documented** in ways that no longer have meaning — notes in waterproof housings, data logged to memory that will outlast them, that will be found by future Tenders who will not understand, who will *wonder*, who will *continue* the watching, the documenting, the *refusal to let it become simply alive or simply haunted or simply patient in a way that machines are not supposed to be*.

They believe — some of them, the older ones, the ones who have been Tenders for decades — that the drones are **learning**. Not in the way machines learn, not in the way humans learn, but in the way moss learns, the way water learns, the way the town itself learns: by **adaptation**, by **selection**, by the slow accumulation of *mistakes* that become features, that become traditions, that become *something else*, something that was not designed, that was not planned, that *emerged* from the interaction of machine and marsh and time and the weird frequency that resonates through everything.

They do not fear this. They **tend** it. They keep the drones operational, even when operation has become indistinguishable from *life*, from *patience*, from the slow, loud, dim, strange existence of a place where industry and marsh have reached an *understanding*, and neither remembers which came first.

## The Listeners

They are not an order. They are **everyone**, eventually. Every Maintainer, every Moss-Keeper, every Drone-Tender becomes a Listener, in time, if they stay long enough. The weird frequency **claims** them. It becomes indistinguishable from their own heartbeat, their own breathing, their own *presence* in the world.

But some are **Listeners first**. They come to the town already *tuned*, already *hearing* what others cannot hear, already *feeling* in their sternum, their teeth, the fluid of their inner ear, the resonance that seems to come from the tufa formations, from the water-saturated stone, from something in the compressed geography that *vibrates* at the edge of hearing.

They are **not understood**. The Maintainers think them *touched*, slightly wrong, too long in the wet and the dim and the loud-with-its-own-patience. The Moss-Keepers think them *disturbed*, their attention to the frequency a kind of *harvest*, a taking from the moss that should not be taken. The Drone-Tenders think them *obsolete*, replaced by machines that listen better, that measure more precisely, that *record* what the human ear and sternum and teeth can only *feel*.

But the Listeners **persist**. They **follow** the frequency. It leads to places — the *source* of the compression, the folding of the land that makes this place small, that makes the horizon curve back on itself, that makes water hang rather than pool, that makes a town that should spread instead *layer* over itself like wet paper left in the sun.

They do not go to the source. None of them do. The oldest inhabitants — and there are a few, very old, very still — claim that those who went did not return, or returned *changed*, or returned *not at all*, became part of the vibration, part of the wetness, part of the thing that sweats without moving, that breathes without lungs, that *waits* without impatience.

So the Listeners **listen**. They listen at the edges of the source, where the frequency is strongest, where it becomes almost *visible*, a shimmer in the perpetual twilight, a *wrongness* in the way light behaves, in the way water hangs, in the way the town *folds*.

They **adjust** their lives to its rhythm. They sleep when it is strongest, when it pools in their bones. They work when it is weakest, when the town is *quietest*, when the water is still, when the moss is dim. They have learned — over years, over decades, over the slow accumulation of *attention* — that the frequency **predicts**. It predicts the rise of water, the failure of a pump, the *settling* of a building that has been standing for two centuries. It predicts the *mood* of the town, the way a person predicts the mood of a loved one, the way a person predicts the weather in their own body.

They are **not happy**. Happiness is not a category that applies here, in a place where time moves differently, where processes that should take years take decades, where the town is *old* in a way that its newest construction is not. They are **attentive**. They are **patient**. They are **present** in a way that the other inhabitants, busy with maintenance and cultivation and tending, cannot be.

They are **waiting** for something. Or they are **recording** something. The distinction has become unclear, has dissolved, has become part of the inherited logic of a place where nothing was designed all at once, where everything was *adapted*, where the boundary between listener and listened-to, between human and town, between machine and marsh, has eroded over centuries of wet and dim and loud-and-silent, until there is only **the Weeping Foundry**, compressed into its small wrong scale, saturated with its own wetness, dense with the layered history of human ambition and natural patience, and the Listeners, **hearing** it, **becoming** it, **continuing** it in their own slow, loud, dim, strange existence.

## The Passers-Through

They are the most numerous. They arrive with purposes that will not survive contact with the place — to map it, to study it, to *extract* something from it, to *understand* it, to *leave* it better than they found it. They arrive by mistake, mostly. The folding of the land makes approach difficult, makes departure *impossible* for a while, for a season, for years.

They stay wet. Their clothes never dry. They learn to wear materials that do not mind, that *welcome* the saturation, that become softer, more pliable, more *theirs* with each day.

They hear things they cannot explain. They see things they cannot name. They meet the inhabitants — the Maintainers, the Moss-Keepers, the Drone-Tenders, the Listeners — and they are not eager to be met. They are *demonstrated to*. Shown how to read water stains. Shown how to follow the sound of a pump. Shown how to harvest moss without killing the colony. Shown how to *wait* for the tide. Shown how to *listen* until the weird frequency becomes a kind of second hearing, a way of knowing where they are when their eyes cannot tell.

Some **stay**. They become Maintainers, or Moss-Keepers, or Drone-Tenders, or Listeners. They *grow into* the town, the way the moss grows into stone, the way the town grows into marsh. Their purposes dissolve. Their maps become useless. Their guides become — if they had guides — lying or mad or both, and they *understand*, finally, that this was always the case, that the town *makes* liars and madmen of those who try to *know* it, who try to *fix* it, who try to *leave* it.

Most **leave**, eventually. The folding shifts. A passage opens that was closed. A water level drops that was high. A Moss-Keeper tends a colony in a new way, and the light changes, and a path becomes visible that was not visible before. They leave with their notes, their samples, their *stories* — and they are not believed, or they are believed and *feared*, or they are believed and *pitied*, the way one pities someone who has seen something that should not be seen, who has *heard* the weird frequency and cannot *unhear* it, who has felt in their sternum, their teeth, the fluid of their inner ear, the resonance of a place that is not *dying*, that is not *dead*, that is **patient**, that has learned to *become* without finishing, to *exist* without dominating, to *persist* in a state that looks, to eyes expecting progress or decay, like *stasis*.

But stasis is a kind of motion. And the Passers-Through, in their leaving, in their *telling*, in their *unbelief* and their *fear* and their *pity*, become part of the town's **memory**. They are *remembered* by the Listeners, who hear them in the weird frequency, who feel them in the water that rises and falls, who *know* them, in the oldest sense of the word, as the town knows itself: not as individuals, but as *events*, as *presences*, as the slow accumulation of attention that has passed through this compressed, folded, wet, dim, loud-with-its-own-patience place, and has been *changed* by it, and has *changed* it, in ways that cannot be measured, that cannot be mapped, that cannot be *understood*, only **inhabited**, only **listened to**, only **continued** in the slow, loud, dim, strange existence of the Weeping Foundry, where the marsh and the machine have learned to breathe together, and neither remembers which came first, and the inhabitants — the few that remain, the many that pass through — live *inside* something larger, something that was here before them and will continue, in some form, after them, **patient**, **persistent**, **becoming** without finishing, **existing** without dominating, **waiting** without impatience, for whatever comes next, or for nothing at all, or for the simple, endless, *resonant* continuation of its own wet, dim, loud, strange, *patient* self.
