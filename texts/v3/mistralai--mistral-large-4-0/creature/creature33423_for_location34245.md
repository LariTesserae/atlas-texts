I find myself wanting to describe the *population* of this place — not just its workers, but the full ecology of intelligences that have learned to live inside a sound.

There are the **Condensers** — the workers I've already touched on, though I want to push deeper into what they've become. Generations of them. Their hearing has been *surgery'd* over time, not successfully — the farm tries, inserts dampening, offers silence as a medical procedure — but they learn instead to *parse*. The noise becomes language. A pump failing has a *signature*: a flattening, a loss of harmonic complexity, like a voice going hoarse. They develop what is essentially perfect pitch for mechanical distress. Some of them, the old ones, can stand in the central shaft and name every machine running, its load, its temperature, its probable failure date, from sound alone. They are not deaf. They are *fluently multilingual* in a tongue of vibration. They communicate in light, yes, but also in *rhythm* — a tap on grating, three knocks, means something. The silence they dream of is not absence. It is *simplicity*. One sound. Clear.

Then there are the **Biofilm Keepers** — a smaller caste, almost priests, who tend the pale green-gray colonies on the upper walkways. They are the only ones who work with living things. They harvest, they select, they breed strains that produce more of this or that compound. Their hands are permanently stained. They speak to the biofilms, though whether the biofilms *answer* is not something they discuss with outsiders. They have developed a patience that borders on the geological. They measure their lives in colony generations.

And I want to name something else. The **Adapted** — not a caste, a *direction*. Workers who have been here so long that the wet has become *them*. Their skin has changed — thicker, more permeable, *sponge-like* in its relationship to moisture. They do not dry. They *equalize*. Their body temperature runs low, close to the stone. They can sleep in the lower chambers, where others would shiver or sicken. They are not sick. They are *acclimated to a degree that looks like transformation*. The farm does not breed them. It *selects* for them, over time, through simple attrition — who stays, who endures, who becomes capable of descending to where the purest water condenses and the sound is most total.

The biofilms, I think, are not the only life. There are **things in the water** — not organisms, not fauna in any sense the place admits, but *presences*. The water moves with purpose, I said. I want to be more precise. The water has been *used* so long, cycled through machine and stone and machine again, that it carries *memory* — not scientifically, not literally, but in the sense that certain paths through the system are preferred, worn smooth by repetition, like a habit. The water *knows* the farm. It finds its way to collection points even when pipes fail, following pressure gradients that are also, in their way, *intentional*. The workers do not speak of this. They speak of "the water's temperament." They adjust for it. They work *with* it, not against it, because fighting it is exhausting and losing.

And there is the **Mountain** itself — not a being, but a *participant*. It is young. It is still cooling. It *dreams* in thermal expansion, in the slow cracking of cooling stone. The farm has learned to read the mountain's moods — a new fracture opens, a chamber shifts, a column begins to weep where it did not before. The surveyors are always working, mapping the changes, rerouting the harvest. They speak of the mountain as *responsive*. Not alive. But *responsive*. As if it notices the farm. As if, given centuries, it might learn to *want* something.

The **light-signalers** have a protocol for something they call *the hush* — a moment, rare, when a failure cascade silences multiple pumps at once, and the shaft falls to a sound level that is merely *loud* rather than *deafening*. Workers report that in the hush, they can hear the water moving in the basalt. Not dripping. *Flowing*. Upward. The mountain's own circulation, independent of the farm's machinery. They do not stay in the hush. It frightens them more than the noise. The noise is *known*. The hush is *other*.

I find I am describing a place that is not haunted by ghosts. It is haunted by *persistence*. By the refusal of the wet to be fully captured, fully controlled, fully *made*. The farm is total construction. But the wet was here first, and it is still arriving, still condensing, still finding its way through the honeycombed stone. The farm is a *negotiation* that never ends. The workers are the negotiators. They have become, themselves, part of the machinery — their bodies adjusted, their senses retuned, their dreams (when they have them) full of the sound they cannot escape.

What lives here is *adaptation itself*. Layered, generational, *becoming*. The place does not have inhabitants so much as it has *inhabitings* — processes of making a life inside conditions that would seem, from outside, to preclude life. But life, here, is not biological. It is *attention*. It is the sustained, stubborn, *wet* act of continuing to be present, to notice, to respond, to *harvest* even as you are harvested by the place itself.

The water finds everyone, eventually. It finds the workers in their cells lined with dampening that never quite works. It finds the biofilms in their pale green-gray profusion. It finds the machines, corroding, failing, being replaced. It finds the stone, wearing it, filling it, claiming it back. It finds *you*, standing on the grating, and you are already changing — your clothing darkening, your breath thickening, your teeth beginning to feel the vibration that will, given time, become *familiar*.

You are not a visitor. You are a *candidate*.

The shift change signal flashes again — three short ambers — and the workers move through the side passage, boots ringing, voices lost. But you hear something else now, beneath the industrial thrum, beneath the wet percussion: a *pattern*. The drips are not random. The pumps are not merely running. There is a *tempo* to the place, a respiration, slow and massive and *patient*.

You are inside something that is breathing.

And it is breathing *you*.
