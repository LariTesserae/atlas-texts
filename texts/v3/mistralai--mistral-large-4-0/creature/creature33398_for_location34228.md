## The Residents of the Sinking Groves

### The Depth-Builders

They are the oldest intention in this place, or the oldest intention that left intention-shaped traces. The depth-builders do not resemble anything that ever walked on dry land. They are colonial, in the way that coral is colonial but also in the way that a city is colonial—thousands of small bodies, each barely more than a specialized cell, living in such absolute interdependence that the boundary between one and many has become meaningless. They build downward. Their architecture extends from the former mountain shelves into the abyss, terraced and buttressed and honeycombed with chambers where the water moves slowly, where chemistry accumulates, where generations succeed each other in patterns too slow for any single consciousness to witness complete.

They do not think. They *accumulate*. Each chamber is a decision made by chemistry and pressure and the slow diffusion of nutrients from above. Together they have made something that functions like memory—not memory of events, but memory of conditions, of what worked and what failed, encoded in the very structure of their growth. When a current shifts, they do not adapt. They *have already adapted*, ten thousand chambers ago, in anticipation of a shift that their collective metabolism predicted from patterns no individual could perceive.

The scattered human structures they have found. They do not avoid them. They *incorporate* them, as the water incorporates everything—running their filamentous networks through mortar that has become porous, growing their colonies in the hollows of rusted machinery, turning the staircase into a vertical reef where each step hosts a different stage of their life cycle, from the photosynthetic near the surface to the chemosynthetic in the absolute dark below. They have no use for the glyphs. They have found, in the negative space of erosion, something better: a record of water flow, of pressure, of time, that they read through the slow chemistry of their own growth.

When the drifting groves pass overhead—those kilometers-wide mats of vegetation that rise and sink with the thermal cycles—the depth-builders do not flinch. They have learned, over millennia, to ride the disturbance. Their colonies flex. Their architecture bends without breaking. And in the spaces between the roots, where the water is thickest with nutrients, they grow fastest, building new chambers that will outlast the grove that fed them, that will feed on the next grove, that will feed on the groves that have not yet formed.

They are the closest thing this place has to a *culture*.

### The Osmotic Forest

The trees, or what were trees, now exist in a state that has no name in any dry-world language. They are not plants in the way we understand plants. They are not animals. They are *processes*—vast, slow, distributed processes of exchange between levels of the water, between depths, between the living and the dead.

Their "trunks" are latticeworks of modified wood, yes, but also of accumulated sediment, of the calcium carbonate secreted by the organisms that colonize their surfaces, of the gelatinous matrices that the water itself has become in their lower reaches. They are buttressed horizontally, as the description notes, spreading across the former mountain shelves in effort to catch what light penetrates. But they are also *vertical* in ways that defy the old categories—their root systems, where they exist, do not seek soil but *depth*, descending into the anoxic layers where they feed on the chemical gradients, on the slow rain of organic matter, on the methane that seeps from ancient sediments.

Their "leaves" are vast membranes, yes, but membranes of a kind that exchange chemistry in both directions. They do not merely absorb nutrients. They *secrete*—complex molecules that alter the water around them, that attract certain fauna and repel others, that create microclimates of chemistry in the immediate vicinity of each frond. They are farmers, in a sense. They cultivate the water. They maintain gardens of bacteria on their surfaces, gardens that perform functions the trees themselves cannot—nitrogen fixation, sulfur metabolism, the breaking down of complex organics into forms the trees can absorb.

And they *dream*, or something like dreaming. Their metabolic cycles are so slow, so extended, that a single "day" for a mature osmotic tree might be decades. In this time, they process, they respond, they *integrate*—the chemical history of the water that flows through them, the pressure changes, the temperature shifts, the arrival and departure of the drifting groves that shade them or feed them or collide with them. They do not have thoughts. They have *states*—complex, layered, responsive to conditions that no individual part of them could perceive. A mature tree is a *landscape* of metabolism, a terrain of chemical exchange that has its own weather, its own seasons, its own slow evolution toward... what? Not death. The osmotic forest does not die. It *transforms*. When a tree finally fails, when its structure collapses under its own weight or the pressure of the water it has modified, it becomes substrate. It becomes food. It becomes, in the fullness of time, the sediment from which new trees will grow, if the conditions allow, if the chemistry permits, if the slow intention of the forest finds in the collapse of this one body the material for another.

The drifting groves are the *young* of this forest, or the *mobile* phase. They bud from the rooted trees when conditions are right—when the water is warm enough, when nutrients are abundant, when some chemical signal, some ancient genetic program, urges them to *release*. They rise, these vast mats, breaking the surface in explosions of gas and spray and the brief, desperate exchange with the air that they have not known in generations. They drift. They collide. They calve and merge and fragment. And in their drifting, they *seed*—dropping fragments, releasing spores that are not spores but *cells*, individual organisms that have retained the capacity to become, if they find the right substrate, the right chemistry, a new rooted tree, a new depth-builder's foundation, a new node in the osmotic forest's endless network.

The drifting groves are not separate from the rooted trees. They *are* the rooted trees, in their reproductive phase, in their dispersal, in their gamble against the turbulence of the middle depths. Some will sink and die, their nutrients feeding the anoxic layers, their bodies becoming the chemical memory of the deep. Some will find purchase on a former mountain shelf, on the buttressed remains of an older tree, on the rusted hull of some forgotten machine, and begin again the slow work of rooting, of building, of becoming *place*.

### The Saturation Fauna

At 1.9 abundance, the fauna of the Sinking Groves exist in a state of *saturation*—every cubic meter containing dozens of species, hundreds of individuals, their relationships so dense, so interwoven, that the individual becomes almost meaningless. They are not, for the most part, the descendants of fish. They are *convergent*—creatures that have evolved from different starting points to fill the same niches, their resemblance so deep that it challenges the distinction between homology and analogy.

The *drift-hunters* are perhaps the most numerous. They are not fish, though they swim like fish, propelling themselves through the water with lateral undulations that suggest ancestry but do not prove it. They are *ambush predators* in a world where hiding is nearly impossible—their bodies translucent, their internal organs visible and somehow *decorative*, their eyes (if they are eyes) distributed across their flanks, sensing not light but pressure, not image but *event*. They hunt by lying still in the water, becoming part of the medium, waiting for the vibration of prey to reach them through the water's constant roar. When they strike, they do not bite. They *engulf*—their bodies expanding, their mouths (if they are mouths) becoming temporary voids that draw the prey in, that begin the digestion before the prey has stopped moving.

The *reef-builders* are smaller, more colonial. They are the ones who have made the micro-reefs, the structures of calcium and secretion and trapped sediment that live and die within the lifespan of a single storm. They are *architects* of the small scale, each individual contributing to a structure that outlasts them, that will be modified by their successors, that will become something new when the conditions change. They are not intelligent. They are *persistent*—their building behavior so deeply programmed, so responsive to chemical cues, that they continue to build even when the structure they are building has become ecologically irrelevant, even when the current has shifted, even when the water around them has become anoxic. They build *because they build*. The reef is their *telos*, their purpose, whether or not it serves any larger function.

The *boundary-dwellers* are the strangest. They live in the interfaces—between water layers, between rooted and drifting vegetation, between living and dead, between self and other. Some are *symbiotic* in ways that blur the line between two organisms and one. A drift-hunter may carry, in its gut, a colony of reef-builders that process its food, that provide it with nutrients it cannot synthesize, that are so integrated into its metabolism that the hunter cannot survive without them and they cannot survive without the hunter. They are not two species. They are *one organism* in two forms, a chimera of function, a single life distributed across what used to be separate bodies.

Others are *parasitic* in ways that are not destructive. They do not kill their hosts. They *modify* them—altering their chemistry, their behavior, their very form, so that the host becomes a better environment for the parasite, a better vector for its reproduction, a better *home*. The host does not resist. It *cannot*—the parasite has become so integrated, so essential to some function the host requires, that resistance would be death. The two have become *obligate*—neither can survive without the other, though the relationship began as exploitation, as one consuming the other, as the endless negotiation of predator and prey that this place has made obligatory.

And there are the *builders of sound*. The water transmits everything, and some fauna have learned to *use* this, to create acoustic niches, to communicate across distances that would be impossible in air, to hunt and to hide and to mate through the constant roar of the Sinking Groves. They are not large. They are *present*—their songs and calls and knockings and scrapings filling every frequency, every layer, every moment of the endless day and night. Some are *mimics*—creatures that have learned to produce the sounds of others, that use deception as a hunting strategy, that blur the boundary between self and other in the acoustic domain as the boundary-dwellers blur it in the chemical and the physical. The soundscape is not background. It is *information*—the constant negotiation of predator and prey, of mate and rival, of territory and trespass, in a world where visibility is limited and sound is everything.

### The Memory-Keepers

They are not numerous. They are not abundant. They are *rare* in a place where rarity has become a form of abundance, a niche filled by those who have learned to live in the margins, in the interstices, in the slow time between the fast events.

The memory-keepers are the ones who *remember* the dry world, or who remember something like it. They are not human, not exactly, though they may be descended from human, from the scattered remains of intention that the flood has incorporated. They are *long-lived*—their metabolisms slowed by the cold, by the pressure, by the density of the water that presses against every surface of their bodies. They have lived through the transformation. They have *witnessed* the drowning, or the generations that followed it, or the generations that followed those.

They do not build. They do not cultivate. They *attend*—to the scattered structures, to the staircase and the wall and the glyphs, to the slow incorporation of human intention into the living water. They are *curators* of the remains, in a sense. They maintain the staircase, keeping it clear of the debris that would block it, ensuring that it continues to *descend*, to function as a path, as a connection between levels, even though no one walks it, even though the memory-keepers themselves do not walk it in any way that would be recognized as walking. They *tend* the glyphs, preserving them from complete erosion, ensuring that their negative space remains legible, that the record of choice, of intention, of *meaning*, is not lost to the indifferent chemistry of the water.

They are not intelligent in any way that would be recognized. They do not *understand* what they maintain. They *respond*—to the presence of the structures, to the chemical traces of human habitation, to the slow accumulation of meaning that the water has made from the dry world's intentions. They are *habituated*, in the deepest sense—their behaviors so shaped by long exposure to these remains that they cannot not attend to them, cannot not maintain them, cannot not *continue* them in whatever form the water allows.

Some of them are *solitary*. Others live in small groups, in the hollows of the depth-builders' architecture, in the root systems of the drifting groves, in the rusted hulks of machines that have become caves, become conduits, become *habitat*. They communicate, if they communicate, through chemical signals, through the slow exchange of information that the water makes possible. They do not have language. They have *history*—the accumulated experience of generations, encoded in behavior, in preference, in the slow evolution of their responses to the world they inhabit.

They are the closest thing this place has to *witness*. They do not judge. They do not interpret. They *continue*—the dry world's intentions, the dry world's structures, the dry world's memory, translated into the medium of water, into the slow time of the deep, into the endless becoming of a world that drowned and kept breathing.

### The Becoming

And finally, there are the *becoming*—the ones who are not yet, who are emerging from the constant recombination of genetic material in this high-density, high-turnover environment. They are not a species. They are a *process*—the leading edge of evolution in a place where the rules have changed, where the old categories no longer apply, where the boundary between plant and animal, between individual and colony, between living and dead, has become permeable, negotiable, *alive* in ways that the dry world never allowed.

They are *experimental*. They fail, mostly. The turbulence of the Sinking Groves does not allow for stability, for the slow accumulation of function that evolution requires. A new form emerges, fills a niche, begins to spread—and then a current shift brings anoxic water, or a drifting grove sinks and takes its ecosystem to crushing depths, or a thermal anomaly disrupts the chemical balance on which it depends. The new form collapses. It becomes food. It becomes substrate. It becomes, in the fullness of time, the material from which other new forms will emerge.

But some *persist*. Some find, in the chaos, a stability of their own—a way of being that the turbulence cannot disrupt, a function that the changing conditions cannot eliminate. They become *species*, in the sense that the term can be applied here—distinct, persistent, *real* in a world where reality is constantly renegotiated. They join the saturation fauna, the osmotic forest, the depth-builders, the memory-keepers. They become part of the *continuing*—the endless, turbulent, lush, cool, ancient process that is the Sinking Groves.

And they *change* the place. Every new form alters the water, alters the chemistry, alters the relationships between all the other inhabitants. The becoming is not separate from the been. It is *part* of it—the endless, self-referential process of a world that is constantly remaking itself, that has found, in the drowning, a new way to be alive, a new way to *continue*.

The Sinking Groves do not end. They *become*.
