In Varr, the people are not many, but they are *thorough*. The density of the place has made them into something like its antibodies—adapted, patient, working the problem of living from every angle the wet world allows.

They are small, mostly. Generations of low ceilings, of crouching through alleyways barely wider than shoulders, of sleeping on platforms where the water rises beneath, have bred a compactness into their bodies. Their limbs are short relative to their torsos. Their feet are broad, splayed, made for gripping reed walkways and the uneven slats of floating houses. Their hands are long-fingered and tireless, shaped by a life of weaving, of joinery, of pulling and twisting the reed that is their true currency.

Their skin carries the water. It is pale, almost translucent in the diffuse light, marked with the fine network of broken capillaries that comes from living in cold humidity, from never fully drying. Many have a faint greenish cast at the hairline, the temples, the hollows of the throat—moss, perhaps, or something closer to lichen, colonizing them slowly as it colonizes the walls. They do not scrape it off. They have learned to wear it, to call it *the marking* when a child first shows it, a sign that the district has accepted them into its slow metabolism.

Their hair is kept short, or bound close, because wet hair is heavy and cold and breeds the same fine growths. What hair they have tends toward the color of wet peat, dark brown, sometimes with an odd streak of white that appears in adolescence—premature, inexplicable, *noted*. They call those who bear it *the touched*, and there is no fear in the name, only a kind of practical recognition. The touched are often the ones who speak to the mother-growth, who harvest it with particular care, who seem to know where it will thicken next.

They speak a language that has no word for *dry*. The closest approximation is a compound meaning *the time of repair*, *the season of mending*, a period of activity rather than a state of being. They have seventeen words for different kinds of wetness, for the particular quality of moisture in air, in wood, in reed, in stone, in their own bodies. They have no word for *privacy* in the sense an outsider would understand; the closest means *the space between walls*, which is to say, a physical rather than psychological condition. They understand solitude differently. To be alone in Varr is to be *between*—between channels, between floating houses, in the narrow slots where the water expresses itself and nowhere else. They do not seek it. They endure it.

Their faces are mobile, expressive, crowded with the need to communicate in a place where sound does not travel. They have developed a grammar of gesture so subtle that an outsider watching two of them converse on a walkway might see only stillness, only the slow articulation of hands and shoulders and the fine muscles around the eyes. They laugh often, but quietly, a kind of contained chuckle that does not carry. Loud laughter here is *waste*—a release of breath that the damp air cannot support, that dissipates into the general wetness without effect.

They are builders, first and last. Every person in Varr knows the properties of waterlogged timber, the particular weight of reed bundles, the angle at which a guide-post must be driven to hold a floating house through the deep time. Children learn joinery before they learn to read, if they learn to read at all. Literacy is rare, and those who possess it are *the keepers*—the ones who remember the old measurements, the old songs that encode the knowledge of how to build, how to harvest, how to wait. They write on strips of reed-bark, using charcoal mixed with something that makes it adhere to the damp surface, and they store these records in the driest places they can find: the highest rafters, the hollows of the oldest stilts, the floating houses that ride highest on the deep water.

They keep no written history in the sense of chronicles, but they keep *the memory of wetness*. The keepers can tell you, from their marks, how high the water rose in the year of your grandmother's birth, which channels ran dry when, which floating house sank and had to be rebuilt. This is their history, their law, their religion. The past is a quality of the present moisture, a thing you read in the swelling of wood, the thickness of mother-growth, the height of the waterline on a guide-post.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is *attentive*. They do not worship gods but *conditions*. The deep time. The dry time. The barometric tide. The settling of the district into itself. They make offerings to the mother-growth—not prayers, but *contributions*: the enriched water of their channels, the condensation they collect, the organic matter they allow to accumulate in the stagnant pools where it thrives. They do not ask the mother for anything. They *feed* it, and they *harvest* it, and this is the whole of the transaction.

They have no concept of progress. The idea that the world might be improved, made better, is as incomprehensible to them as the idea of dryness. What they have is *maintenance*, the endless work of keeping what exists from collapsing into the wet. A house that stands for fifty years is a triumph. A guide-post that holds through three deep times is a miracle. They measure their lives not in years but in *rebuildings*—how many times they have torn down and reconstructed, how many times they have replaced a waterlogged beam, rewoven a walkway, rethached a roof.

They are suspicious of outsiders, not with hostility but with *practical concern*. An outsider brings dryness in their expectations, their habits, the very air of their bodies. An outsider wants to *change* things, to drain the channels, to raise the ground, to make the place *better*. The people of Varr have seen what happens when the water is fought rather than worked with. They have seen, in the stories the keepers tell, the first settlers who tried to build on raised platforms of stone, who were slowly undermined by the seepage, who woke one morning to find their foundations floating. They see the hills beyond, the higher ground, and they do not wish to go there. They have *chosen* the wet. They have chosen the packing, the closeness, the constant work of being small in a world that is still becoming.

They age strangely. The wet preserves and it destroys. Their joints swell, their fingers curl, their spines compress from the constant crouching, the constant reaching. Many are stooped by middle age. Many have the *tremble*, a fine shaking of the hands that comes from the cold, from the exertion, from something in the water they cannot name. They do not fear it. They adapt. They learn to work with the tremble, to time their movements to its rhythm, to use it as a kind of percussion in the work of weaving, of joinery, of pulling the reed.

They die young, by the standards of the dry lands, but they die *complete*. There is no gradual fading. The body, saturated with the life of the place, simply stops. They are buried in the channels, or in the deepest mud of the district, where the mother-growth will find them, where their wetness will be returned to the wetness of the city. The keepers mark the spot with a post, a length of reed, a stone from the oldest wall. The dead are not forgotten, but they are *incorporated*—their water becomes the district's water, their substance feeds the growth that feeds the living.

And they are *content*, in the way that contentment is possible in a place without comfort. They have what they need: shelter that rises with the water, food from the channels, the work that occupies every hour, the company of others packed close enough to touch, the sound of dripping, the smell of reed, the particular quality of light that makes everything soft and strange and *alive*.

They are the people of Varr. They are wet, and they are *becoming*, and they have no wish to be otherwise.
