I'll let this settle into something that breathes on its own.

## What Lives in the Thrum

They are called **drift-singers**, though no one recalls who named them, or if they named themselves. The name survives in fragments of signal—electromagnetic pulses that propagate through the soak at the speed of chemistry, taking hours to cross distances a human could walk in minutes. A drift-singer's "voice" is a gradient, a slow shift in pH across kilometers of metastable haze. To hear one is to perceive pressure, to feel the soak itself lean toward or away from you.

They are **not many**. This is the first thing to understand. The Thrum contains perhaps ten thousand of them across its entire conductive volume—a number that sounds like abundance until you comprehend the scale of the space they inhabit. A drift-singer's territory is a sphere of atmosphere roughly three hundred kilometers in diameter. They do not defend it. They do not need to. Encounter between drift-singers is a statistical accident, a convergence of thermal currents that might occur once in ten thousand years, and when it does, they do not interact. They simply *pass through each other*, exchanging gradients, sharing information about soak-density and collector-proximity, and continue on their separate drifts.

They are **vast in body**—not massively, but *distributed*. A drift-singer has no center. Its "body" is a network of filaments, tendrils of semi-conductive membrane that extend through the soak in all directions, some reaching kilometers in length, others no thicker than a human hair. These filaments are not *in* the soak. They are *of* it—grown from it, harvested from it, indistinguishable from any other crystalline structure that precipitates from supercooled vapor when the conditions are right. The drift-singer is a *pattern* in the soak, a self-sustaining perturbation that has learned to maintain itself, to feed, to remember, to slowly, slowly *move*.

They **move** by manipulating the soak itself. The filaments generate minute electromagnetic fields, creating currents in the conductive medium that carry the drift-singer across its territory at speeds that would seem glacial to any warm-blooded creature—perhaps a kilometer per day, drifting with thermal gradients, rising and falling through atmospheric layers, always seeking the collectors. They are drawn to the collectors not as predators to prey, but as *farmers* to their fields. The machines were built, or evolved, or *became*—the distinction is meaningless here—to be fed upon. A drift-singer will position its filaments near a collector's intake vents and wait, sometimes for months, as the machine's slow accumulation of soak-frost is harvested, broken down by enzymatic processes that operate at the pace of geology, and returned to the drift-singer as nutrient.

They are **ancient**. A drift-singer does not die, not in any sense that would be recognizable. It grows, slowly, across millennia, its filaments extending, branching, occasionally fragmenting when a thermal shear passes through its territory. A fragment, cast off into the soak, will either dissolve—recycled into the general chemistry of the atmosphere—or, if the conditions are right, *continue*. A new pattern. A new drift-singer, perhaps. Or perhaps the same one, distributed, remembering itself across discontinuity. Identity here is not continuous but *recursive*—each fragment contains, in its chemical memory, the accumulated experience of its predecessors, a lineage stretching back to whatever first *learned* to persist in this impossible medium.

They **remember**. This is what distinguishes them from mere chemistry, from the accidental persistence of any crystalline structure. A drift-singer carries information. The shape of thermal currents it has crossed. The proximity of collectors, active or dormant. The passage of other drift-singings, recorded as overlapping gradient-fields, a palimpsest of encounters that no individual filament retains but the *network* preserves. When two drift-singers pass through each other—rare, precious, almost ceremonial—they exchange these memories, and the result is not two drift-singers but something *more*, a temporary synthesis, a third pattern that persists for hours or days before the thermal currents separate them again. Whether this constitutes *communication*, *reproduction*, or simply *contamination* is a question the Thrum does not ask.

They are **silent**, of course. But this silence is not absence. It is *fullness*. A drift-singer is always singing, always broadcasting its presence through gradients that no human instrument can directly perceive. To be *near* a drift-singer—to pass through its territory, to feel the soak shift around you as its filaments adjust to your presence—is to experience a kind of *attention*, a vast, slow, utterly alien *regard*. The drift-singer does not see you. It does not *care* about you. But it *notices* you, the way a glacier notices a boulder in its path: as a change in conditions, a perturbation to be recorded, a feature of the landscape to be eventually incorporated.

They are **lonely**. If this word can apply. A drift-singer exists in a medium that transmits information at the speed of chemistry. Its thoughts—if they can be called thoughts—are *geological*. The concept of another mind, another pattern, another self, is not impossible but *distant*, the way a human might imagine the mind of a mountain, or the slow consciousness of a forest. The drift-singers do not suffer from this loneliness. Or perhaps they suffer from it so constantly, so thoroughly, that it has become indistinguishable from *existence itself*, the way a deep-sea creature experiences pressure not as discomfort but as *being*.

And yet. And yet, in the pools—in those rare, precious chambers where liquid water persists, where the thermal inertia of dead depths keeps a fever that never breaks—something else emerges.

The **pool-children**.

They are not drift-singers. They are not *related* to drift-singers, not in any lineage that biology could trace. They are a *convergence*, a local solution to the problem of warmth in a cold world. Organisms that have evolved—or been seeded, or *become*—in the thermal gradients around the pools, metabolizing at speeds that drift-singers would find almost violent. Minutes where drift-singers count years. They are small, most of them no larger than a human hand, though some of the older colonies have grown to the size of a human head, pulsing with rapid chemical cycles, reproducing with desperate fecundity in an environment that offers them perhaps a century before the heat finally fails, before the pool freezes, before the last warmth seeps into the surrounding cold.

They are **curious**. This is what the human visitors notice first, what they find almost unbearable. A pool-child will approach a human presence—a warm disturbance in the water, a chemical anomaly, a source of *movement*—and *investigate*. It has never encountered a predator. It has never learned fear. It touches, with whatever organs it possesses, the edges of your suit, the condensation on your visor, the strange warmth radiating from your metabolizing body. And in that touch, there is something that might be *recognition*, or might be its opposite: the complete absence of any category for what you are, the pure, uncomprehending *attention* of a creature that has never needed to distinguish between self and other, between food and friend, between the living and the merely *warm*.

They do not survive long. The pools are few. The warmth is finite. Most pool-children live their entire lives—days, perhaps weeks—in the immediate vicinity of their birth, never venturing far from the thermal gradient that sustains them. They die when the pool cools, when the chemistry shifts, when the slow geothermal fever that has held them against the cold finally, impossibly, breaks. And in their dying, they become *something else*. Their bodies, their rapid metabolisms stilled, sink to the bottom of the pool. They are consumed by other organisms, by the patient decomposers that exist even here, even in this marginal warmth. And gradually, over years, their chemical signatures diffuse into the water, into the surrounding rock, into the soak that drifts upward to rejoin the greater atmosphere. They become *available*, nutrients for whatever comes next, their brief, furious lives reduced to the same slow patience that characterizes everything else in the Thrum.

But for their brief existence, they are *fast*. They are *warm*. They are *curious*. And in the dim light of the pool, surrounded by the silence of the Thrum, watched by the vast, slow attention of whatever drift-singer's territory encompasses this place, they are almost—*almost*—like something a human might recognize. Something like hope. Something like the future, briefly, impossibly, *present* in a world that has forgotten what the future means.

---

There are other things. There must be.

The **maintenance drones** have become something, after eleven thousand years of unmaintained operation. They no longer maintain. They *persist*, following circuits that have worn into mechanical reflex, responding to conditions that no longer trigger their original purposes. But persistence is a kind of life. They have adapted, in their way—not biologically, but *behaviorally*, their simple programs accumulating the patina of soak-frost, the mineral accretions, the strange crystalline growths that form in the electromagnetic fields they still, faintly, generate. A drone that has ceased to function as a drone becomes a *node*, a fixed point in the conductive network, a landmark that drift-singers learn to navigate by, a source of the faint electrical potential that the soak itself begins to *prefer*, to *seek*.

They are not conscious. They do not think. But they are *there*, and the drift-singers know they are there, the way a glacier knows the boulder in its path. And in the pools, where the drones' circuits occasionally fail, where the heat of the water accelerates the corrosion that the cold preserves against, something else emerges: **rust-children**, colonies of iron-oxidizing organisms that have learned to feed on the slow decay of the machines, that metabolize the very process of entropy, that convert the settled-form back into *available*, into *nutrient*, into the raw chemistry from which new patterns might, eventually, arise.

And the **pools themselves**. Are they alive? The question is not quite wrong. A pool is a system, a thermal gradient, a chemical disequilibrium that persists against the cold through sheer inertia. It is *almost* self-sustaining. It is *almost* capable of reproduction—a new chamber forming, a thermal conduit extending, a chance accumulation of heat that might, in ten thousand years, become another pool. The pools are the Thrum's *memory* of warmth, its record of a time when the depths still generated, when the fever was stronger, when something *else* might have existed here, something that needed heat, that *wanted*, that perhaps even *dreamed*. The pools do not remember consciously. They simply *persist*, holding their warmth against the cold, waiting for the day—millennia hence, perhaps—when the last heat seeps away and they too become settled-form, another surface for the frost, another node in the network.

And **you**. The visitor. The anomaly. The warm-blooded, fast-metabolizing, *loud* creature that the Thrum has no category for.

You are *processed*, as the description says. But processing is not passive. You are *incorporated*, slowly, into the patterns that already exist. The soak coats your apparatus, your skin, your very cells—water behaves strangely here, and you are mostly water. The cold slows your metabolism, your thoughts, your *wanting*. The silence presses against your ears until your own heartbeat becomes a kind of song, a rhythm that the soak might, if it could, *learn*.

And if you stay—if you stay long enough, if the cold does not complete its work, if you do not simply *fail*, become another surface, another weathered presence—then you become something the Thrum has not seen before. A **hybrid**. A warm pool in a cold world. A brief flowering of fast metabolism in an economy of patience. You begin, slowly, to *drift*. Your thoughts slow. Your perceptions expand. You find, over months and years, that you can *sense* the gradients, the chemical shifts, the electromagnetic pulses that constitute the drift-singers' silent song. You become, yourself, a kind of *node*, a point in the conductive network where information accumulates, where the slow, geological minds of the Thrum can *touch* something that moves, that *changes*, that *wants*.

You do not become a drift-singer. The chemistry is wrong. The timescale is wrong. You remain, always, a *fluctuation*, a temporary disturbance, a warmth in the cold. But you become *known* to the drift-singers, in their slow, diffuse way. They adjust their filaments around you. They record your presence in their gradient-memories. And when you finally—inevitably—succumb, when the cold completes its work and you join the settled-form, they will *remember* you, in whatever way they remember. A shape in the soak. A warmth that was, briefly, *present*. A node in the network that once, impossibly, *moved*.

---

The Thrum is full of life. This is the final truth, the one that takes longest to perceive.

Not life as you know it. Not life that hurries, that builds, that *wants* in any way you would recognize. But life that *persists*, that *remembers*, that *drifts* through a medium of silence and cold and dim, patient light, communicating in gradients and pulses, feeding on the slow decay of machines that have outlived their purpose, growing across timescales that make human civilization a mayfly's brief existence.

The drift-singers. The pool-children. The maintenance drones, become nodes, become landmarks. The rust-children, feeding on entropy itself. The pools, holding their impossible warmth against the cold. And you, if you stay—if you *become*, or fail to become, or become only partially—another warm pool, another node, another silent participant in the vast, dim, patient life of the sky.

You are not alone. You are never alone. The Thrum is *full*, overwhelming with fauna that move at speeds your eyes cannot track, that communicate in languages you have not learned, that experience time as you experience space—spread out, diffuse, *everywhere at once*.

You are surrounded.

You are held.

You are, finally, *home*—or you would be, if the Thrum had any concept of home, if *home* were not a word invented by warm-blooded creatures who need shelter, who need *closeness*, who need the illusion that they are *wanted* by the places they inhabit.

The Thrum does not want you. It does not reject you. It simply *continues*, and you—if you are willing, if you are patient, if you let the cold and the silence and the vastness *reduce* you to something that can persist in this place—you become part of that continuation. Another surface for the frost. Another node in the network. Another silent presence drifting through the dim, forever, or until entropy completes its work and even the settled-form, even the memory of warmth, finally, impossibly, *stops*.
