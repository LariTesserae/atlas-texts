I will let the inhabitants of Velm emerge from the water, from the stone, from the particular densities of cold and wet and necessary proximity.

---

They are called the Velmiri. Not a word they use for themselves. The name was given by those above, in the dry towns, the ones who look down on the basin and see only what the water has claimed. *Velmiri.* The drowned. The Velmiri do not use it. They have their own names, family names, the names of their houses, the names of the specific hummocks where their grandmothers stood firm and the specific thin places in the ice where their grandfathers fell through and were pulled back, coughing, by hands that knew the rope-work of rescue.

They are short. The cold has selected for this, as it selects for everything in Velm. Their legs are thick with the muscle of constant balance—alder roots shift, hummocks compress, the water moves beneath surfaces that only *seem* solid. Their hands are large and their fingers are long, for the work of Velm is the work of water: nets, lines, the gutting of fish through ice, the weaving of sedge, the careful tying of knots that must hold against current and cold and time. Their feet are broad, splayed, the toes slightly webbed in the way that emerges when generations are born in a place where the boundary between walking and swimming is not fixed.

Their skin carries the color of the place. Not pale—the cold wet does not produce pallor, it produces a particular translucency, a thickness, the subcutaneous fat that the body builds against the constant drain of heat. They flush easily, a deep red that comes from the blood moving to the surface, the body's desperate attempt to preserve the core. Their hair is usually dark, the color of wet peat, and it is usually wet. The humidity does not allow dryness. A Velmiri woman's hair, properly tended, is a mass of controlled waves, oiled with the rendering of fish, braided with sedge so that it moves as a single weight when she turns her head. When she works, it is bound up, it is practical, it is a tool.

They do not speak much. This has been observed by outsiders, by the occasional trader who descends with goods from the dry world, by the surveyors who come to measure what can be measured and leave confused by what cannot. But the Velmiri speak constantly. They speak to the water. They speak to the hummocks before they step on them, a negotiation, a request for firmness. They speak to the ice when they cut it, warnings and encouragements, the ice is a partner not an enemy. They speak to the dead, who are everywhere in Velm—drowned in the high water, lost in the fog, swallowed by hummocks that seemed firm, taken by the cold that comes in patterns they know but cannot always predict. The speaking is not loud. It is *specific.* It is the sound of a place that has learned to listen.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is the religion of *level.* The water finds its level. The town finds its level, the vertical negotiation of height and necessity, the constant adjustment to what the water will permit. The oldest Velmiri, the ones who remember their grandparents who remembered the last great thaw, speak of the *Deep Keeping*—the idea that the water is not merely physical, that it carries memory, that the drowned are not gone but *kept,* held in the cold, preserved, waiting for the level to change. They do not pray to the water. They *acknowledge* it. The distinction matters.

There are the *Tumulari*, the oldest families, descended from those who came when the glacier retreated and found the basin empty, young stone gleaming, water rising to meet them. They live on the ridges, the highest points, the places where the water is only a threat in the highest floods. They are the council, the keepers of the water-level calendar, the ones who decide which alder-root bridges may be built where, which beaver dams may stand, which channels may be dug and which must be left to find their own way. They are conservative. Conservatism in Velm is not ideology. It is survival. The water has taken enough. The Tumulari do not wish to tempt it further.

Then the *Canalenn*, the people of the waterways, who live in the stilt-houses, whose lower floors are given entirely to the wet, whose lives are measured in the rise and fall of the canals. They are fishers, net-makers, the tenders of the water-beetles in their season, the hunters of the blind trout through ice. They are the poorest and the most numerous. Their children learn to swim before they walk, to pole boats before they read, to read the water—its color, its movement, its temperature—with a precision that outsiders cannot comprehend. A Canalenn child of eight can tell you the depth of the water by its sound, the current by its pressure against the hull, the presence of fish by the particular quality of the light beneath the surface. They are *sensitive.* Sensitivity in Velm is not weakness. It is the necessary adaptation to a world that offers no second chances.

The *Mosswrights* are a caste without a name, recognized only by their work. They tend the *poor fen,* the raised bogs that grow on every horizontal surface, the spongy acidic accumulations that are the town's insulation, its fuel, its slow-building land. A Mosswright knows the age of a hummock by its color, its firmness, the particular sound it makes when struck with a peat-iron. They harvest sphagnum with a reverence that borders on worship, cutting only the living top layer, allowing the centuries of dead fiber beneath to continue their slow accumulation. They are the ones who maintain the reed-coats of the oldest buildings, the twenty-layer thicknesses that rustle in the wind, that record the years of a family's presence in the way that trees record drought and plenty. A Mosswright's hands are permanently stained, the tannins of peat worked deep into the skin, a color that does not wash out. They smell of the bog, of wet growth and cold decay, of the particular sweetness of sphagnum in its season.

The *Icewrights* come into their own in the freeze, when the canals become roads, when the town's geography transforms, when the danger and the opportunity of solid water reshape everything. They are the ones who know the thin places, the springs and upwellings that keep the ice from thickening, the spots where the town's own warmth prevents the freeze. They mark these places. They test the ice with poles, with sound, with the particular *feel* of a surface that will hold or will not. They cut the holes for the blind trout, the deepest winter fishing, when hunger is pressing and the cold is most severe. They are feared and respected, for they know where the water is *lying*—where the solid surface conceals the movement, the depth, the cold that can take a life in seconds. An Icewright's reputation rests on accuracy. One mistake, one misjudgment of a hummock or a spring, and the reputation is gone, the family moves to the lower floors, the vertical hierarchy asserts itself.

There are the *Old Women,* a category that transcends family and caste. In Velm, age is not decline. It is *accumulation.* The old women know the mushrooms that grow only here, the specialists of young stone and cold water and high density. They know which bracket fungus is medicine and which is poison, which shelf fungus feeds and which rots the liver. They know the hummocks, all of them, the firm and the hungry, the ones that have swallowed and the ones that have held. They know the water-levels of memory, the floods that took their mothers, the freezes that took their children, the particular pattern of the high water that comes every seven years and takes something precious. They are consulted. They are *needed.* In a place where the margin for error is thin, where the cold finds every gap, knowledge is the only wealth that matters. The old women are rich. They are *powerful.* They sit on their upper platforms, their feet never touching the water, and they speak the names of the dead and the living, and their words carry the weight of accumulated survival.

The children of Velm are *small* in the way that children of hard places are small—not malnourished, for the abundance of the wet world provides, but *compressed,* growing slowly in the cold, their energy directed inward, toward the development of the senses that will keep them alive. They play in the water. They play on the ice, when it is safe, when the Icewrights have marked the routes. They play in the fog, when the town becomes smaller, when the sounds carry strangely, when the game is to find your way home by the smell of your own house, the particular mixture of peat-smoke and fish-rendering and the moss that grows on your particular roof. They learn early that the world is *negotiable.* The hummock that was firm yesterday may be hungry today. The ice that held your weight last week may be thin this week. The water that was your street yesterday may be your ceiling today. They learn *attention.* Attention in Velm is not a choice. It is the condition of being alive.

The Velmiri do not leave. This is the strangest thing, to outsiders. The basin is a trap, they say. The water rises, the fog closes in, the cold presses from every side. But the Velmiri do not leave. Some try. The young, the restless, the ones who have heard stories of dry places, of ground that does not move, of air that does not carry water in every breath. They descend the alder-root bridges, they pole their boats to the edge of the basin, they climb the conifer forest and look out at the world that resumes its proper elevation. And then they come back. Always they come back. The dry world is *too much.* Too much space. Too much silence. Too much absence of the particular sounds that constitute Velm's density—the rustle of sedge, the creak of mooring rope, the low hum of the town's own metabolism, the water moving through constructed channels. The dry world offers *possibility* without *presence.* The Velmiri have presence. They have *density.* They have the constant negotiation with a world that is alive, that moves, that demands attention.

They are dying. This must be said. The peat is accumulating. The level of the land is rising, slowly, but rising. In a thousand years, the basin will be different. The water will find new channels, new relationships, new densities. Some of the town will be above the flood, will become dry, will become strange to the generations that live there. Others will sink further, the lower floors given entirely to the water, the inhabitants moved upward, upward, the vertical city pressing against its limit. The Tumulari plan for this. They mark the water levels, they record the changes, they teach the children to read the calendar-post in the council house, the record of ice-breaks and mosquito emergences and heron returns. They are preparing for a future they will not see. This is what it means to be Velmiri. The young stone will not last. The town knows. The town *endures.*

There are the *Drowned,* though they do not know this name. They are present in every house, in every family, in every conversation that falls silent when a name is spoken. The high water took them. The thin ice took them. The fog took them, the sounds carrying strangely, the directions that did not correspond to their sources. The hummock that seemed firm. The alder root that rotted unseen. The cold that came in a pattern no one had predicted. They are not mourned loudly. The Velmiri do not mourn loudly. They *remember.* They speak the names. They mark the places, the thin ice, the hungry hummock, the fog-bound morning. They teach the children. The Drowned are *teachers.* Their deaths are *information.* In a place where the margin is thin, where attention is the condition of survival, the dead are the most attentive teachers. They teach what not to do. They teach where not to step. They teach the particular quality of the water that means danger, the particular silence that means the ice is thin, the particular sound of the fog that means you are lost.

And there are the *Others.* The ones who are not quite Velmiri, who have come from elsewhere, who have not yet been absorbed, who may never be absorbed. The trader who descends with goods and ascends with fish and peat and the particular silence of a place that has learned to be quiet. The surveyor who comes to measure and leaves confused, his instruments recording data that do not fit the models, his maps showing a town that does not correspond to the town he walked through. The occasional refugee, from a flood above, from a fire in the dry world, from a violence that the Velmiri do not understand and do not wish to understand. They are given the lower floors, the places where the condensation is heaviest, the mold fastest-growing. They are given work, the work of the Canalenn, the fishing, the net-mending, the tending of water-beetles. They are *watched.* Not with hostility. With *attention.* Attention in Velm is the condition of being alive. The Others must learn attention. Some do. Some become Velmiri, slowly, over generations, their descendants speaking to the water, speaking to the hummocks, speaking to the ice, their skin acquiring the translucency of the cold-wet, their hair the color of peat, their children learning to swim before they walk. Some do not. Some leave, as the young always leave, descending the alder-root bridges, poling their boats to the edge, climbing the forest, looking out. And some of these come back. Always, some come back.

The Velmiri are not happy. This would be a slander. Happiness is not the category. They are *present.* They are *attentive.* They are *alive* in a way that the dry world, with its space and its silence and its absence of negotiation, cannot comprehend. They live in densities that would seem impossible. They move through wetness without the expectation of dryness. They speak to water and hummocks and ice and dead. They know the thin places. They know the hungry hummocks. They know the particular quality of the light beneath the surface, the color of the water, the sound of the fog.

They are the Drowned Borough's proper inhabitants. Not the water, which is their neighbor, their unpredictable partner, their constant negotiation. Not the stone, which is their resource, their young mineral wealth, their slow transformation. Not the moss, which is their insulation, their fuel, their slow-building land. The Velmiri. The ones who descend. The ones who enter, like water entering stone, slowly, finding every path, becoming part of the structure.

They are here. They have always been here. They will be here when the peat has risen and the basin has changed and the town has become something else, something stranger, something that the Tumulari's descendants will not recognize but will *acknowledge.* The water finds its level. The Velmiri find theirs. The negotiation continues. The silence holds. The town breathes, and the sound is like distant breathing, like the town itself inhaling and exhaling, like the water moving through alder roots, like the particular silence of a place that has learned to be quiet.

They are the Velmiri. They do not use the name. They have their own names, their family names, the names of their houses, the names of the hummocks where they stand firm. And when they speak, which is constantly, which is always, their voices carry in the silence, specific and dense and *present,* the sound of a place that has learned to listen, that has found in quiet a kind of density, a packing of attention into the few sounds that matter.

They are here. They are alive. They are *Velm.*
