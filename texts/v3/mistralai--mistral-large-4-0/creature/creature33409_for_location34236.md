I'll take my time with this. The high hollows of Varn ask for patience—they are a place defined by persistence, by the almost-enough, by rhythms too slow for hurried minds. What lives there must have learned the same lessons: how to hold water without greed, how to occupy stone without demanding it change, how to exist in the margins between what is and what almost is.

## The Inhabitants of the High Hollows

### The Lichen-Builders

They are not what you would call *alive* in any urgent sense. They are *becoming* alive, have been becoming alive for longer than the town has existed, longer than the concept of town. The lichen here—*fruticose*, *tower-forming*, the architectural kind—has learned to build in a place where nothing else would think to build.

Each lichen-tower is a negotiation. The fungal partner provides structure, the algal partner provides food, and between them they have constructed something neither could make alone: a *place*. The towers rise perhaps ten centimeters above the stone, sometimes fifteen, never more. They are dense with internal chambers, with dead spaces and living spaces, with the accumulated bodies of lichens that grew and died and became substrate for lichens that grew upon them. They are *old*. Some of the towers—the tallest, the most complex—may predate the town by millennia.

They do not move. They do not *need* to move. The wind finds them, passes through them, produces the tones that the inhabitants have learned to hear as *almost-music*. The rain finds them, runs down their surfaces, feeds the algae within. The dew collects on their surfaces, persists in their crevices, becomes *almost-standing-water* in their highest chambers. They are, in their way, *water-keepers*: not as conscious keepers, not with intention, but with the simple fact of their existence, their form, their patient accumulation.

The lichen-builders have no individual identity. Each tower is a colony, a *we* rather than an *I*, and the towers themselves are connected by crust-forms, by the thin green film that coats the stone between the towers, by the *network* that makes the whole hollow's surface into something like a single organism, distributed, *almost-aware* in the way that old things are aware: not through thought, but through *response*, through the slow adjustment of growth to moisture, of form to wind, of existence to the simple *fact* of the place.

They are harvested. The town's industry collects them, dries them, compresses them, sends them down the road. The lichen-builders do not resist. They do not *care*, if care is the right word. They continue growing in the hollows beyond the town, in the basins where water persists, on the ridges where wind is constant. They will outlast the town. They have outlasted other towns, other purposes, other *intentions* that once thought themselves important here. They are the *memory* of the place, the slow record of what has been, written in the language of their own substance.

---

### The Moss-Weavers

Where the lichen towers stand, the moss-weavers build their *cushions*. These are not plants in the way you might think of plants. They are *accumulations*. *Sphagnum* in the wettest basins, holding water in cells that have forgotten how to release it, building upward, outward, *always* upward and outward, adding layer to layer until the cushion becomes a *mound*, becomes a *hill* of compressed green, becomes *peat* that is not quite soil but will become soil if the world continues its slow settling.

The moss-weavers are *sponges*. They drink the condensation, the brief rain, the overflow from the dew-basins, and they hold it, release it slowly, create around themselves a *microclimate* of damp that persists slightly longer than the open stone, that allows other lives—*insects*, *mites*, the small things that live in the dense green—to exist in a place where they could not otherwise exist.

They are *dense*. You can press your palm against a cushion and feel the give of millions of tiny plants, each one a universe of retained moisture. The cushion *responds*: it compresses, it slowly re-expands, it holds the impression of your hand for a moment, a *memory* of touch that will fade as the moss grows, as the outer layers die and are covered by new growth. They are *time-keepers* in this way: each cushion a record of every hand that has pressed it, every rain that has fallen on it, every season of growth and dormancy, written in the accretion of dead and living, compressed and becoming-compressed.

The moss-weavers are also harvested. The town collects them, dries them, sends them away for purposes the inhabitants do not entirely understand—*packing material*, perhaps, or *medicine*, or something stranger, something that the lowlanders value for qualities that have to do with the moss's *capacity to hold*, to *retain*, to *be almost-water* without being water. The moss-weavers do not resist. They continue growing, continue accumulating, continue their slow transformation of stone into something *adjacent* to soil, something that will, in time, become soil, become the *ground* for other lives, for other accumulations, for the endless *becoming* that is the only constant here.

---

### The Gentian-Waiters

They are the *flowering things*, the ones that appear with such *intensity* in the brief growing season, and they are *waiters*. They have learned to wait.

The gentians grow in the dew-basins, in the cracks where water persists longest, in the *almost-soil* of the oldest peat accumulations. They are small—*saxifrage*-small, *cushion*-small, the size of things that have learned that *less* is a kind of *more* in a place where *enough* is never quite reached. But their color is *saturated*. The blue of *Gentiana* here is not the blue of lowland skies. It is *deeper*, *more concentrated*, as if the flower has been *waiting* and has gathered all its waiting into its petals, into the brief *bloom* that will last weeks, perhaps a month, and then be gone.

They do not *choose* to wait. They are *made* to wait. Their seeds lie in the stone, in the peat, in the *almost-soil*, through the long dim months, through the condensation that is not quite rain, through the *almost*-winter that is not quite winter. They do not germinate until the moisture is *right*, until the temperature has risen to the *threshold*, until the conditions have become *almost*-sufficient. And then they grow *fast*, relative to everything else here: weeks to flower, weeks to seed, and then back to waiting, back to the *dormancy* that is their true state, the state they have *optimized* over millennia of *almost*.

The gentian-waiters are *pollinated* by the insects that live in the moss cushions, by the *bees* that come up from lower elevations in the brief season, by the *flies* that are always present, always *almost*-active. They do not *need* the pollinators in any urgent sense—they can self-pollinate, they have that *option*, that *backup*—but they *prefer* the outcrossing, the *mixing*, the genetic *diversity* that comes from being *visited*, from being *chosen* by the small lives that move through the hollows in their own brief season.

After the bloom, they become *seed-heads*. These persist, *dry*, *waiting*, through the long dim months, through the condensation that is not quite rain. They are *architecture* now, not flower: the *structure* of the plant, the *form* that will release the seeds when the moisture returns, when the temperature rises, when the *almost* becomes *enough*. They are *patient*. They have always been patient. They will be patient until the stone itself erodes, until the hollow fills, until the world has settled into a *final* form that is not final but *continuing*, and the gentians will still be waiting, still be *almost*-blooming, still be *becoming* what they have always been: the *blue* in a world of bone and yellow-green and rust, the *intensity* in a place of *almost*, the *proof* that waiting can be its own kind of *life*.

---

### The Insect-Architects

They are small. They are *mostly* small. But they are *architects*.

In the moss cushions, in the spaces between the lichen towers, in the *dense* green where moisture persists, live the insects: *mites*, *springtails*, *beetles*, *flies*, the *midges* that rise in clouds from the dew-basins in the brief warm hours, that are eaten by the birds that come up from lower elevations, that are *part* of the cycle, the *almost*-food-chain that connects the moss-weavers to the hawks circling above the town.

Some of them build. *Caddisflies* in the standing water of the deepest basins, constructing their cases from the mineral particles, the bits of organic matter, the *almost*-debris of the basin's slow accumulation. *Spiders* in the lichen towers, weaving webs that catch the midges, the *almost*-insects that rise from the water, that provide the *protein* the spiders need to survive, to reproduce, to *continue* in a place where *continuation* is the only achievement.

They are *adapted* to the thin cold, the intermittent water, the dim light. They are *slow*. Their metabolisms run at the pace of the place, not the pace of lowland life, not the pace of *hurry*. They can *wait*: for moisture, for warmth, for the brief season when *abundance* is *almost* possible. They *diapause*, they *dormant*, they *persist* in states that would be *death* in other lives, other places, other *tempos*.

The insect-architects are *keystone* in the way that small things often are. They pollinate the gentian-waiters. They decompose the dead lichen, the dead moss, the *accumulated* organic matter, returning it to the cycle, making it *available* for new growth. They are food for the birds, for the small mammals, for the *hawk* that circles above the town. They are *part* of the *almost*-ecosystem, the *dense* web of lives that have learned to exist in the margins, in the *almost*-enough, in the *thin* and the *dim* and the *intermittent*.

They will outlast the town. They have outlasted other *impositions*, other *intentions*, other *rhythms* that thought themselves important here. They are the *memory* of the place in their way: the slow record of what has been, written in their *adaptation*, their *persistence*, their *almost*-abundance in a world that does not allow abundance in any classical sense.

---

### The Bird-Comers

They are *rare*. They are *returning*.

The high hollows are not *bird*-country in any abundant sense. The thin air, the dim light, the intermittent water, the *almost*-abundance of insects—these are not conditions that attract *many* birds, that support *large* populations, that allow the *easy* life of the lowlands. But *some* birds come. *Some* birds *return*.

The *hawk*—*red-tailed*, perhaps, or *rough-legged*, the kind that circles on thermal currents, that hunts the small mammals in the peat, the *voles* and *mice* that burrow in the moss cushions, that have learned the *rhythm* of the place, the *cycles* of abundance and scarcity. The hawk does not *live* here, not in the sense of *residing*, of *belonging*. It *visits*. It *passes through*. It is *part* of the larger landscape, the *highlands*, the *mountains*-that-are-no-longer-mountains, and the hollow of Varn is *one* of its hunting grounds, *one* of the places where the *thermals* are *almost*-sufficient, where the *prey* is *almost*-abundant.

Other birds come in the brief season. *Snow buntings*, perhaps, *lapland longspurs*, the *high-arctic* species that find in the high hollows a *simulacrum* of their *true* home, the *tundra*, the *arctic* plain, the *edge* of the world. They come to breed, to nest in the *dense* moss, to raise their young on the *insects* that rise from the dew-basins, and then they are *gone*, back to *elsewhere*, back to the *cycle* that is their *true* life, of which the high hollows are only *one* station, *one* *almost*-home in a *larger* becoming.

The bird-comers are *stressed* by the place. The thin air costs them. The dim light confuses them, or *orients* them differently. The intermittent water means that they must *time* their breeding to the *brief* abundance, must *hurry* in a place that does not allow hurry, must *raise* their young in weeks when the *insects* are *almost*-sufficient, when the *hawk* is *almost*-present, when the *weather* can turn, can *almost*-kill.

And yet they come. And yet they return. There is something in the high hollows that *calls* them, or that they *recognize*, or that is simply *part* of the *larger* pattern of their lives, the *migration*, the *movement*, the *almost*-home that is *enough* for a season, for a *breeding*, for a *continuation* of the *line* that will carry them *elsewhere*, to *other* *almost*-homes, to the *cycle* that is their *true* existence.

They are *witnesses* to the town. They see it from above, from the *circling*, from the *high* where the *human* *intention* is *small*, *brief*, *almost*-irrelevant. They do not *judge* it. They do not *care*, if care is the right word. They *pass through*, they *hunt*, they *breed*, they *leave*, and the town continues, *persisting*, *maintaining*, *becoming* what it has become: a *strangeness* in the high hollows, a *brief* *imposition* on a *patient* place, a *rhythm* that has been *absorbed* into the *larger* *tempo* of *almost*.

### The Mammal-Burrowers

They are *small*. They are *hidden*. They are *peat*-lives, *moss*-lives, the *underground* of a world that has no *deep* underground, no *soil* in the classical sense, only the *slow* accumulation of *almost*-soil, the *peat* that is *becoming*.

The *voles*—*field voles*, perhaps, or *tundra voles*, the kind that have learned to live in the *dense* moss, in the *cushions* that hold water like *sponges*, in the *tunnels* they dig through the *peat*, through the *accumulated* dead and living, through the *almost*-ground that is *enough* for *burrowing*, for *nesting*, for *raising* the *young* in the *brief* season when *food* is *almost*-abundant.

They are *prey*. The *hawk* hunts them. The *fox*, perhaps, if the fox comes this high, if the *lowland* predator has learned the *rhythm* of the hollows, the *cycles* of *almost*-abundance. The *weasel*, the *stoat*, the *small* carnivores that live in the *margins*, in the *almost*-spaces between the *moss* and the *stone*, between the *water* and the *dry*, between the *life* and the *almost*-life.

The mammal-burrowers are *cyclical*. They *boom* in the *brief* season, when the *moss* is *green*, when the *insects* are *almost*-abundant, when the *hawk* is *present* but not *too* present, when the *weather* is *almost*-kind. And then they *crash*, or *decline*, or *persist* in *smaller* numbers, through the *long* dim months, through the *condensation* that is not quite *rain*, through the *almost*-winter that is not quite *winter*. They *wait*, in their *burrows*, in their *nests* of *dried* moss, in the *peat* that is *warm* relative to the *stone*, that *holds* the *heat* of the *brief* season, that *sustains* them through the *long* *almost*.

They are *engineers* in their way. Their *burrowing* aerates the *peat*, allows *water* to *move*, creates *spaces* for *other* lives, for the *insects* that live in the *tunnels*, for the *fungi* that decompose the *dead*, that return the *nutrients* to the *cycle*. They are *part* of the *almost*-ecosystem, the *dense* web of lives that have learned to exist in the *margins*, the *almost*-enough, the *thin* and the *dim* and the *intermittent*.

They will outlast the town. They have outlasted other *impositions*, other *rhythms*, other *intentions* that thought themselves important here. They are the *memory* of the place in their way: the slow record of *abundance* and *scarcity*, of *boom* and *crash*, of the *cycles* that are the *true* rhythm of the high hollows, the *tempo* that the town has learned to *accommodate*, to *absorb*, to *become* part of, in its *perpetual* *maintenance*, its *ritual* of *repair*, its *strangeness* in a *patient* world.

### The Town-Dwellers

And then there are *us*. The *strange* ones. The *recent* ones. The *imposed* rhythm in a place that has its own.

Two thousand, perhaps three thousand, living in the hollow's center, where the water gathers most reliably. They are *water-people*, in the way that all humans are water-people, but *more* so here, where water is *intermittent*, where water is *almost*-enough, where the *capture*, the *storage*, the *reluctant release* of water is the *organizing principle* of their lives.

They are *layered*. Not *vertically*—the stone will not support great height—but *temporally*, *geologically*. The oldest among them live in structures that are *of* the stone, that have become *part* of it, that will outlast the metal roofs, the concrete, the *intention* that built them. The newest among them live in the *industrial* intrusions, the *factory*-adjacent spaces, the *concrete* that is already crumbling back into the mineral world. They are *between*: the old and the new, the stone and the metal, the *perpetual* and the *temporary*, the *almost*-eternal and the *certainly*-finite.

They are *maintainers*. This is their *identity*, their *purpose*, their *ritual*. The infrastructure leaks. The pipes leak. The cisterns crack. The channels that direct the intermittent water, that collect it, that distribute it, that *are* the town's circulatory system—these are *always* in need of repair. And the repairs are *constant*, the repairs are *ritual*, the repairs are the thing that holds the community together more than any shared purpose, any shared belief, any shared *meaning*.

They repair in *teams*, in *crews*, in the *organized* response to the *inevitable* failure of the *built* in a place that does not *want* to be built, that has its own *rhythm*, its own *tempo*, its own *almost*. The repair-crews are *mixed*: old and young, stone-born and factory-born, those who remember the *older* ways and those who have only known the *industrial* intrusion. They work in the *dim* light, in the *thin* air, in the *damp* that gets into everything. They *sing*, sometimes, or *chant*, the *ritual* sounds that mark the *completion* of a repair, the *restoration* of the *water*-flow, the *continuation* of the *almost*-life.

They are *harvesters*. They harvest the lichens, with a *care* that suggests their *value*, their *strangeness*, their *almost*-understood worth in the markets of the lowlands. They harvest the mosses, for purposes they do not entirely question. They harvest the *peat*, when it has accumulated enough, burn it for the *little* heat it provides, use it in the *drying*, the *concentrating*, the *transforming* of the raw materials into something that can be *sent away*, down the single road that descends to the lowlands, a road that is itself a kind of violence, a cut through the ancient stone that will eventually heal, that is already healing at its edges.

They are *electrified*. There is *light* in the long dim hours, *artificial* light that competes with the *perpetual* haze, the *suggestion* of sun behind the moisture. The *factories* hum—the *mechanical* hum, the *other* hum, distinct from the *low* hum that has no source, that is felt in the teeth rather than heard, that the inhabitants have learned not to question. They *process*. They *produce*. They *ship*. They are *part* of the *larger* world, the *lowland* world, the *hurried* world, and yet they are *here*, in the high hollows, *absorbed*, or *absorbing themselves*, into the *patient* becoming of the place.

They are *not* happy, if happiness is measured by lowland standards. They are *not* unhappy. They are *waiting*. They are *maintaining*. They are *continuing*. They have learned the *rhythm* of the place, the *cycles* of the water, the *almost*-abundance of the growing season, the *long* dim months of *condensation* and *patience*. They have learned to *bloom*, in their way, in the *brief* season, to *gather*, to *store*, to *release* slowly, to *be* the *gentian*-waiters in a world that does not allow *waiting* in any classical sense.

They are *strange*. The lowlanders say so. The products they ship are *strange*, valued for qualities that are not entirely understood. The *hum* in the deep places is *strange*. The *light* that never quite brightens, never quite darkens, is *strange*. The *scale* of time that makes their *lives* *brief*, *almost*-irrelevant, and yet *continuing*, *mattering*, *shaping* the world in ways that will outlast them—this is *strange*, this is the *weirdness* that they have *absorbed* into something like *peace*.

They are *two* in a way that the lichen-towers are *two*: the fungal and the algal, the *stone* and the *intention*, the *old* and the *new*, the *perpetual* and the *temporary*. They have become *part* of the ecology, their leaks and repairs and harvests *contributing* to the cycles rather than disrupting them. The lichens grow on their factory roofs. The mosses colonize their cistern walls. The gentians bloom in the cracks of their old stone foundations. They have been *absorbed*, or have *absorbed themselves*, into the high hollows' patient becoming.

And they will *end*. Not soon—the stone is patient, the repairs are *ritual*, the *maintenance* is *perpetual*—but *eventually*. The population is stable but not growing. The industry continues but its markets are distant, its products *too* strange, even for lowlanders who value strangeness. The infrastructure leaks more than it can be repaired. The *hum* grows *louder*, or *deeper*, or *more* present in the *almost*-silence. And one day—*geologically* soon, *humanly* distant—the town will *fail*, or *succeed* into something else, will become *ruin*, will be *absorbed* into the stone, will be *memory*, will be the *almost*-foundation for some *future* *settlement*, some *future* *intention*, some *future* *almost*-town in the high hollows of Varn.

But not yet. Not yet. The town-dwellers continue. They maintain. They harvest. They repair. They *wait*, in their *layered* lives, for the rain that will come, that always comes, that is never quite enough and is, in its way, exactly enough.

### The Hum

And then there is the *hum*. The *sound* that has no source. The *low* vibration felt in the teeth, the *strangeness* that the inhabitants have learned not to question.

It is *old*. Older than the town, perhaps. Older than the *concept* of town. It comes from the deepest part of the hollow, where the town's oldest structures meet the bedrock, where the stone has been *settling* for so long that it has achieved something like *resonance*, some *frequency* that matches the *vibration* of the place itself, the *almost*-motion of erosion, of settling, of the *oldest-stable-state* that is not stable but *continuing*.

It is *water*, perhaps. The *intermittent* water, moving through *channels* that have no names because they have no permanence, through *cracks* it will slightly widen, through the *endless* patient work of being *present*. The water *remembers*, the description says. The water *holds*, in the hollows between stone shoulders, in the pockets that the sun cannot quite reach. And the water *vibrates*, in its *movement*, in its *almost*-flow, producing the *hum* that is felt rather than heard.

It is *life*, perhaps. The *dense* life, the *layered* life, the *almost*-abundant life of the high hollows, all of it *vibrating* in its own *tempo*, its own *rhythm*, its own *almost*-music. The lichen-towers *resonate* with the wind. The moss cushions *hold* the moisture, *vibrate* with the *passage* of insects, of small mammals, of the *almost*-movement of roots seeking purchase. The *whole* ecology *hums*, in a frequency too low for human ears, too *structural* for human hearing, but *present*, *felt*, the *ground* of the place's *strangeness*.

It is *strangeness*, perhaps. The *weirdness* (1.5) that the place has *absorbed*, the *almost*-supernatural that is not supernatural but *structural*, the *rules* that are not entirely understood. The hum is the town's *concession* to this, its *acknowledgment* that the high hollows have *laws* that are not the laws of the lowlands, that the *patient* becoming of stone and water and life has its own *logic*, its own *reason*, its own *almost*-meaning.

The inhabitants do not question it. They have learned, over generations, over *layers* of living in the hollow, that some *sounds* are not meant to be *understood*, only *witnessed*, only *felt*, only *lived with*. The hum is *part* of the place, like the *damp*, like the *dim* light, like the *almost* of everything. It is the *sound* of the high hollows *being* what they are: *old*, *patient*, *settling*, *becoming*, *almost*-finished and *continuing*.

And sometimes—*rarely*—the hum *changes*. It *deepens*, or *rises*, or *modulates* in a way that suggests *intention*, *attention*, *response* to something the inhabitants cannot perceive. The repairs become *more* urgent. The harvest is *abundant*, or *fails*. The *rain* comes *early*, or *late*, or *not at all*. And the inhabitants *know*, in their *bones*, in the *thin* air that carries the *vibration* to their *teeth*, that the high hollows are *aware* of them, that the place has *noticed*, that the *almost*-equilibrium has been *disturbed*, and will *settle* again, will *return* to its *oldest-stable-state*, will *continue* its *patient* becoming, *with* or *without* the town, *with* or *without* the town-dwellers, *with* or *without* the *strangeness* of their *brief* *imposition*.

## What Lives There, Finally

What lives in the high hollows of Varn is *almost*. The *almost*-water, the *almost*-soil, the *almost*-abundance, the *almost*-town, the *almost*-eternal. It is *waiting*, *holding*, *continuing*. It is the *lichen-builder*, the *moss-weaver*, the *gentian-waiter*, the *insect-architect*, the *bird-comer*, the *mammal-burrower*, the *town-dweller*, the *hum*—all of them *negotiating* with a place that does not yield easily but yields, that does not allow abundance but allows *persistence*, that does not permit *hurry* but rewards *patience*.

They are *layered*. Stone, then lichen crust, then lichen tower, then moss cushion, then flowering plant, then insect, then bird, then mammal, then human, then *strangeness*, then the *hum* that underlies everything. Each layer creates conditions for the next. Each layer is *dense* with life that has learned to exist in the margins, in the *almost*-enough, in the *thin* and the *dim* and the *intermittent*.

They are *old*. Older than the town, older than the species that built it, older than the *concept* of building. They have been *becoming* for so long that *becoming* has become their *state*, their *rhythm*, their *reason*. They will continue *becoming* when the town is gone, when the town-dwellers are *memory*, when the *road* has healed, when the *factories* have rusted to the color of the stone, when the *concrete* has crumbled back into the mineral world.

They are *peaceful*, in their way. Not the peace of *completion*—that is not available in a place that is *continuing*—but the peace of *acceptance*, of *adaptation*, of *finding* the *almost*-enough and *living* by it, *shaping* themselves to it, *becoming* part of it, *absorbed* into the *patient* becoming of the high hollows.

And they are *strange*. The *weirdness* that is not dramatic but *structural*, the *almost*-supernatural that is simply the *nature* of a place that has *settled* its own score, that has found its own *rules*, that lives by them, that *holds* its water in its stone hands, that *waits* for the rain that will come, that always comes, that is never quite enough and is, in its way, exactly enough.

The high hollows of Varn do not end. They continue, settling, becoming, holding their water, waiting, *almost*. And what lives there continues with them: the *lichen*, the *moss*, the *gentian*, the *insect*, the *bird*, the *mammal*, the *human*, the *hum*—all of it *almost*, all of it *enough*, all of it *continuing* in the *thin* cold, the *dim* light, the *damp* that gets into everything, the *almost*-silence *full* of *almost*-sounds, the *perpetual* *almost* of a place that has found its own *rules* and lives by them, that has *absorbed* its *strangeness* into something like *peace*.

---

*They do not ask to be understood. They ask only to continue. And they do.*
