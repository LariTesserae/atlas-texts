The Weeping Basin holds something that has learned to live in the space between stone and water, between the slow violence of erosion and the slower patience of growth. I think of it first as a *keeper*—not a person, not quite, but a presence that has accreted around the basin's particular loneliness, the way mineral deposits accrete around a constant source. It has no name I could pronounce, or perhaps it has only the name the basin gives itself in the low murmur you hear when you press your ear to the saturated stone: a sound like breathing, like remembering.

It is old in the way the ferns are old, in the way the walls are old—not ancient as a mountain is ancient, but *established*, settled into a rhythm of attention that spans centuries. It does not move quickly. When the stone dislodges high on the walls and the basin rings with that tooth-aching note, the keeper does not startle. It *registers*, the way a sleeping body registers a sound without waking. It knows the schedule. It has heard each fragment fall, each crack propagate, each slow surrender of the mountain to its own weight. It could tell you which faces will weep tomorrow and which will be dry, though it would not use those words. It would only *be* there, in the particular dampness of the air, in the way the light catches the thousand small mirrors of seeping water.

It does not want anything, I think, or wants only what the basin wants: the continuation of its condition, the persistence of dampness, the slow accumulation of soil in sheltered pockets, the particular silence that follows when something has passed through the ferns and the place has noticed. It is a creature of *in-betweenness*—between water and stone, between the visible and the suggested, between the human who descends and the human who climbs out again, changed in ways that will only become apparent later, in dreams or in the particular quality of attention they bring to other wet places, other rooms that ring when struck.

The keeper feeds on the basin's excess. Where water appears where it should not, where the water table shifts overnight, where puddles persist for weeks—there is always something *using* that surplus, something that has evolved or accreted or simply *settled* into the niche. The keeper is the largest of these, or the oldest, or the most *attentive*. It does not build. It does not mark. But if you wait long enough in the compressed lushness, in the zone where warmth and wetness combine into something almost tropical, you might feel it *regard* you: not with eyes, but with the sudden stillness that follows your own passage, the ferns settling back into their arrangement, the murmur of water continuing unchanged, the basin holding its shape against you as it holds its shape against everything.

It is lonely, but not unhappily so. Loneliness here is a condition, like the dampness, like the altitude that presses in your ears. The keeper has made its peace with it, or has never known any other state. It does not call out. It does not answer when called. But if you sit long enough in the twilight, when the sun angles just so and the weeping stone becomes a room of moving glints, you might feel it *lean* closer, not to see you better, but to be seen itself—finally, briefly, by something that will descend and climb out again and carry its particular silence with it, waiting to return.
