What lives in the High Terraces of Varn is *attention itself*—made flesh, given patience, given names we do not yet know.

---

**The Varnese**

They are the ones who built this, or who found what was built and learned to *maintain* it. You will not see them all at once. They are distributed through the terraces like water through stone—present in every channel, every joint, every decision about where to let the moss grow and where to scrape it clear.

They are small, compact, built for the elevation. Their lungs have adapted to the thin air over generations, or they have learned to breathe in the particular rhythm of Varn—deep, then deeper, the body demanding what the atmosphere withholds. Their skin is dark, weathered to leather at the exposed places, the hands especially, the face. They wear layers, always layers, clothing that moves with them like a second skin, made from the fibers of the condensation reeds and the wool of animals we have not named yet, animals that live in the warm interiors where the steam keeps the cold at bay.

They do not hurry. This is the first thing to notice. A Varnese person walking across a terrace moves with the unhurried certainty of water finding its level. They stop. They look at something—a crack in the *thass*, a new growth of moss, the particular angle of light on a condensation farm. They press their palm to a wall, feeling the warmth, the slow pulse of the building. They speak to each other in voices that *reach*, that carry across the thin air, that use the reflective surfaces of the construction to become intelligible at distances that would be impossible at sea level.

They are *many*. The terraces are dense with them, layered, near-touching. They live in structures that share walls and floors and air, built up rather than out, each level supported by the one below, a single vast organism of habitation. A family occupies a vertical space, perhaps three levels—sleeping below, living in the middle, working above, or some other arrangement, some logic of privacy and proximity that makes sense only to them. They are never alone. Even in the spaces of quiet, constructed deliberately, where the steam is distant and the wind does not reach, there is the sound of others, the *presence* of others, breathing the same thin air.

They are *old*, in the way of places. Not individually—each Varnese life is brief, as lives are—but collectively. The knowledge of the deep places is passed on carefully, from one generation to the next, the locations of the condensation pockets that must not be entered, the sounds that mean the deep things are stirring, the particular seals that must be renewed and the channels that must be cleared. They have names for things we do not know how to see. They have ceremonies for the maintenance of the terraces, for the moment when a section is closed and rebuilt, for the annual acknowledgment of the mountain's patience.

They are *certain* of their place. Not arrogant—there is no arrogance in Varn, no sense of mastery over the mountain or the water or the constructed world. But *certain*. They know that they are participants, not masters. They know that the terraces will be here after them, changed but continuous, and that this is enough. They have built something that does not need them, and they have learned to need it in return.

**The Terrace Mice**

They are not mice. This is the first thing to know. They have evolved to fill the mouse niche in the specific ecology of Varn—constructed stone, managed moisture, the particular temperature bands of the terraces—and in doing so they have become something else, something that only *resembles* a mouse to eyes that have not learned to see the difference.

They are small, yes. Furred, with the compact body of something that lives in cracks and moves through spaces designed for water. But their ears are larger, shaped to catch the particular frequencies of Varn sound—the steam hiss, the water drip, the human voice projecting across stone. Their eyes are adapted to the intense light, to the sudden shifts of shadow as condensation gathers and evaporates. They are *pale*, almost the color of the *thass* itself, a coloration that lets them disappear against the constructed surfaces, against the moss, against the mineral stains of water that has passed and left its mark.

They are everywhere. This is their defining quality. No surface is without them, no channel, no crack, no deliberate garden of moisture. They move through the terraces like a fluid, finding the paths of least resistance, the routes that water has made and that they have learned to follow. They are *watched*. Some Varnese keep them—small enclosures, fed with scraps, used for... what, exactly? Companionship? A reminder of the living world in the midst of all this construction? The Varnese do not explain. They simply do.

They are tolerated, mostly. They leak enough, waste enough, to support the small lives that live in the margins. They are a sign of a healthy system. The Varnese measure the health of a terrace section by the presence of terrace mice, by their number, by their *behavior*. Too many, and something is wrong—too much moisture, too little maintenance, a disturbance in the balance. Too few, and the system is failing, the channels are too sealed, the construction too total.

They are hunted, sometimes. For their fur, which is used in the making of certain garments, certain insulating layers. For their meat, which is small but protein-rich, a supplement to the diet of the lower terraces where the condensation reeds grow thick and the gardens are *allowed* rather than cultivated. The hunting is done with care, with the recognition that the terrace mice are *neighbors*, that their presence is necessary, that the balance must be maintained.

And they are *afraid*. This is the quality that makes them alive, that makes them more than mechanisms. When a Varnese approaches, they *peep*—a small, sharp sound of alarm—and they *flee*, not in panic but in the calculated movement of something that has learned the patterns of threat and response. They know the sound of a human footstep on *thass*, the particular vibration of a cart wheel, the sudden rush of water when a channel releases. They have learned to live with the giants who built this place, who maintain it, who are *present* in every terrace, every hour.

They are, in the end, *successful*. They have found a way to live in a world that was not made for them, that was made *despite* them, and they have made it *theirs*. They are the Varnese of the small lives, the ones who have learned the patience of the place, the slowness, the acceptance of limits.

**The Vent-Hawks**

They are large. This is the first thing to notice. Larger than any bird that lives at sea level, their wingspan adapted to the thin air, to the need for lift in an atmosphere that offers less resistance. They are *patient*. They ride the thermal currents that rise from the steam systems, the warm updrafts that carry them upward without the effort of flapping, that let them survey the terraces from above, their eyes—adapted to the intense light, to the fierce brightness of the high sun—seeing the movement of the terrace mice, the insects, the occasional fish that finds its way into the upper reservoirs through some error in the channel system.

They nest in the warm updrafts, in the spaces where the steam vents release their constant hiss, where the temperature remains stable, where the condensation provides moisture and the insects provide food. Their nests are constructed from the fibers of the condensation reeds, from the shed fur of the animals that live in the warm interiors, from the accumulated debris of centuries. They are *territorial*. Their cries—high, thin, *far*—are part of the soundscape of Varn, a sound that carries across the thin air, that reaches the ears of the Varnese as a warning, a claim, a presence.

They hunt. Not often—they are patient, they can wait—but when they hunt, they hunt with precision. They dive, folding their wings, becoming a missile of intent, striking at the terrace mice, at the insects, at whatever lives in the margins of the construction. The Varnese watch them. They do not interfere, mostly. The vent-hawks are part of the balance, the predators that keep the small lives in check, that prevent any one species from dominating, from overwhelming the system.

They are *respected*. There are stories, passed down, of vent-hawks that have lived for decades, that have raised generation after generation in the same nest, in the same thermal current, that have become *familiar* to the Varnese who work the terraces below. There are ceremonies, small ones, for the vent-hawks that die—acknowledgments of their presence, their role, their *patience*. The Varnese do not name individual hawks. They do not need to. The hawks are *there*, always there, riding the thermals, crying their territorial songs, a constant presence in the high bright air.

They are, in the end, *free*. This is what the Varnese envy, sometimes, in the quiet spaces where they press their palms to the warm *thass* and feel the pulse of the building. The vent-hawks are not bound to the terraces, not in the way that the Varnese are bound. They can leave. They can descend to the valleys, can ride the currents to the peaks above, can *choose*. And yet they return, generation after generation, to the warm updrafts, to the familiar thermals, to the place that has become *home* despite everything, despite the construction, despite the totality of human intention.

**The Moss**

It does not have a name that translates. The Varnese call it something that sounds like *thass* itself, that gestures at the relationship between the constructed and the grown, the made and the *allowed*.

It grows on the north faces of structures, where the sun does not reach, where the condensation gathers and stays, where the temperature remains cool and stable. It is a deep green, a green that seems to *absorb* light rather than reflect it, that makes the surfaces it covers look like something underwater, something from a world of pressure and patience and slow growth. It grows in the joints between constructed surfaces, in the cracks that the Varnese have learned to *leave*, to not seal, that water might find its way through, that moisture might collect, that life might *occupy*.

It is not cultivated. This is the key. The Varnese do not plant it, do not tend it, do not harvest it. They simply *allow* it. They recognize that the moss is part of the system, that it holds moisture, that it moderates temperature, that it provides habitat for the insects, for the small lives that live in the margins. They scrape it from the surfaces that must be clear—the channels that carry water, the paths that must be smooth for carts and feet—but they let it grow where it can, where it does no harm, where it contributes to the *density* of the living world.

It is *old*. Some of the moss in the deepest terraces, in the oldest construction, has been growing for centuries, layer upon layer, mineral by mineral, incorporating the dust of the mountain, the waste of the civilization, the slow accumulation of time. It is not the same moss, exactly—there are species, varieties, differences that the Varnese can see but that we cannot name. But it is *continuous*. It has been there since before the current Varnese, since before the current understanding of what built the terraces, and it will be there after, changed but persistent, growing in the cracks, holding the moisture, making the constructed world *green*.

It is, in the end, a *negotiation*. Between the made and the grown, between human intention and the slow patience of life. The Varnese have learned to make this negotiation, to *allow* what they cannot control, to *tend* what they can, to *accept* that the moss will grow where it will, that it is part of the place, part of the *home* that they have made despite everything, through everything, with everything.

**The Deep Things**

They do not have a name. This is deliberate. The Varnese have learned not to name them, not to speak of them openly, not to *think* of them too clearly. They are the reason certain condensation pockets are marked with warning stones. They are the reason the deepest reservoirs are sealed and monitored. They are the reason the Varnese have learned, over centuries, not to disturb.

The construction of Varn is *old*. Older than the current civilization, older than the current understanding of what built it. The original builders left spaces—hollow places, deep in the *thass*, where the structure is not structure but *absence*, where the mountain itself has been hollowed out by water and time and the slow patience of geology. The water finds these spaces. The water *warms* in these spaces, through geothermal currents that the engineers have mapped but do not fully explain, through the particular chemistry of the mineral-rich stone, through the accumulated heat of centuries of habitation above.

Something lives there. Something that has adapted to the warmth, the dark, the mineral-rich water, the occasional organic matter that falls or is carried down—a dead insect, a scrap of vegetation, the waste of the civilization that does not know it is being processed, being *recycled* by something that has no name, that is not named, that must not be named.

It is not large. This is known. The deep things are small, or they are *distributed*—many small things rather than one large thing, a colony, a network, a *presence* that is more than the sum of its parts. They are not *aggressive*. They do not attack, do not hunt, do not *threaten* the Varnese in any way that can be observed, that has been recorded. But they are *present*. They are *learning*.

This is the word the Varnese use, in the rare moments when they speak of the deep things at all. *Learning*. They have learned the rhythms of the civilization above, the cycles of water and waste and warmth, the particular chemical signatures of the Varnese presence. And they are *responding*. They process waste, in their way. They maintain the thermal balance, in their way. They are, in the oldest sense, *neighbors*—not friends, not enemies, not *known*, but *present*, part of the ecology, part of the system that sustains life in Varn.

They are *patient*. More patient than the Varnese, more patient than the moss, more patient than the slow growth of the *thass* itself. They have been here for millennia, for longer than the current civilization, for longer than the current understanding of what built the terraces. They will be here after. They will continue their cycles, their processing, their *learning*, whether the Varnese are present or not, whether the terraces stand or fall, whether the mountain continues to rise or finally, finally, begins to wear down to nothing.

They are *strange*. This is the quality that makes them *real*, that makes them more than metaphor, more than story. They are not alien—this is the crucial point. They are *familiar*, in the way that all life is familiar, that all life shares the same chemistry, the same needs, the same slow adaptation to the conditions of a place. But they are *other*. They live in the dark, where the sun does not reach. They live in the warm, where the cold does not touch. They live in the mineral water, where the Varnese cannot follow, where the engineers do not dare to go.

And they are *watching*. This is what the Varnese feel, in the deep places, in the sealed reservoirs, in the condensation pockets marked with warning stones. Not with malice. Not with intention. But with *attention*. With the slow, patient *learning* of something that has been here before, that has seen civilizations rise and fall, that has processed their waste and maintained their thermal balance and *waited*, always waited, for whatever comes next.

The Varnese do not fear them. Fear is too active, too *engaged*. They *respect* them. In the oldest sense of the word—*to look back*, to acknowledge the presence of the other, to *not disturb*. They mark the dangerous places. They seal the deep reservoirs. They teach their children, carefully, the sounds that mean the deep things are stirring, the particular vibrations in the *thass* that mean something has moved in the dark below.

And they *leave them alone*. This is the final rule, the one that shapes everything else. The deep things are part of the system. They are *necessary*, in ways that are not yet understood, that may never be understood. To disturb them would be to disturb the balance, to risk the thermal stability, to threaten the slow patience of a place that has been here before and will be here after.

They are, in the end, the *mystery* of Varn. Not strange enough to be alien, but strange enough to be *felt*. The sense that the construction is older than it appears, that the deep things are learning, that the balance is fragile in ways that are not yet visible. The Varnese know this. They live with it. They have built their lives around the acceptance of limits, of slowness, of the knowledge that some things should not be rushed, should not be understood too quickly, should not be *disturbed*.

**What Lives in Varn**

Everything. That is the answer. Everything that can live, that has learned to live, that has been *allowed* to live, in this place of construction and moisture and thin bright air and slow patient time.

The Varnese, who built this or found it and learned to maintain it, who are participants and not masters, who are certain of their place and humble before the mountain.

The terrace mice, who have evolved to fill the niche of the small, the scuttling, the *present*, who are everywhere and nowhere, who are watched and kept and tolerated and hunted.

The vent-hawks, who ride the thermals, who are free and yet return, who cry their territorial songs across the thin air, who are respected and envied and *known*.

The moss, the lichens, the flowering things, the condensation reeds—all the vegetation that grows despite everything, through everything, using the cracks and the moisture and the warmth of the constructed world.

The insects, in abundance, the beetles that process the dead vegetation, the moths that pollinate, the mosquitoes that the Varnese have learned to live with, to net and smoke and treat with lichen-derived chemicals.

The deep things, unnamed, unspoken of, *learning*, *present*, *neighbors* in the oldest sense, maintaining the thermal balance, processing the waste, watching with patient attention from the dark below.

And the place itself. The *thass*, which grows, slowly, mineral by mineral, incorporating the waste of the civilization that maintains it. The water, which is a negotiation, always a negotiation, between collection and release, between use and return, between the human need and the mountain's patience. The steam, which is the metabolism of the place, the sound of it working, the warmth that makes life possible at four thousand meters. The wind, which was here before the construction and will be here after, singing in the channels, whistling through the narrow passages, the oldest sound of Varn.

Everything lives here. Everything is *here*. Everything is *slow*, and *patient*, and *total*.

This is what it is like to be in Varn. To be *held* by the mountain, *bright* with the fierce light, *loud* with the layered sounds of work and water and wind and voice, *dense* with the presence of others—human and animal and insect and the barely-glimpsed deep things. To feel the weight of the thin air, the press of it, the way it carries sound strangely, the way it demands a different rhythm of breathing. To be *high*, and *made*, and *alive*, all at once, all in balance, all *slow*.

To be, finally, *home*—not because it is comfortable, not because it is easy, but because it is *total*. Everything is made. Everything is alive. Everything is *here*.
