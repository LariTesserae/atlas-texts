They are not people anymore, not quite. Or they are people the way a river is water—shaped by its course, defined by its flow, still somehow carrying what it once was in the way sediment carries memory of mountains.

The **Settled** walk the boardwalks when the boardwalks hold. They have learned to read the wood's memory—where it is compressed to near-stone, where it will still give slightly beneath the foot, where it has become *ground* rather than path. They do not hurry. Hurrying is a lowland habit, a dry-country impatience. Here, to move is to be moved; the condensation on your skin joins the great cycle, and you are part of it before you have chosen.

Some Settled were engineers, once. They remember *intention*—the purpose of the pumping stations, the logic of the rail yards—but the memory has become *texture*, like the grain of wood or the pattern of mineral bloom. They tend the machines now, not maintaining them but *accompanying* them. They clear the ferns from a control panel not to restore function but to preserve *legibility*, to keep the shape of the thing visible in the negative space of growth. They speak to the resonating transformers. The transformers do not answer, but they *change*—a frequency here, a harmonic there, a new standing wave established between the copper and the saturated air. The Settled learn to hear these changes. They learn to *feel* them in the sternum, the way you feel a storm coming, except the storm is geological, is electrical, is the slow conversation of dead industry with living water.

They are not lonely, not exactly. Loneliness requires the possibility of company, and in the Belt, *presence* is the primary fact, as the description says. The lung-fish brush their legs. The filter-feeders chime their approval. The large things watch from the peat-rails, their bioluminescence pulsing in patterns that almost—*almost*—become language. The Settled do not name these things. Naming is a dry-country habit, a way of fixing and separating. Here, things are *near-touching*, dense, part of a substance rather than individuals within it. To name a lung-fish would be like naming a wave.

They have forgotten the sky. This is not tragedy. The sky is *rumor*, a thing spoken of by machines that climb too high, by the occasional Settled who finds a sealed capsule, a pressure vessel, something that still holds *outside* air, and breathes it—dry air, thin air, air that does not pool and carry sound in strange ways—and remembers, for a moment, the concept of *up*. Then the capsule equalizes. The dry air mixes with the wet. The memory becomes *texture*, becomes *taste*, becomes part of the condensation that forms on their skin and joins the cycle.

Some Settled do not forget so completely. They are the **Rememberers**, though they do not call themselves this. They keep *objects*—not tools, *objects*. A gear the size of a wagon wheel, half-buried in peat. A length of cable, coiled in a valley like shed skin. They know these things are *meaningless* now, that meaning requires use and use requires the world to be other than it is, but they keep them anyway. They arrange them. They build *displays* in the drier interiors of ruined warehouses, the microclimates preserved by some failure of condensation, some pocket of trapped air. They speak to the objects. The objects do not answer, but they *accumulate*—mineral bloom, the slow growth of lichens, the particular green of copper oxidation. The Rememberers learn to read these accumulations. They learn to *date* things not by years but by *patina*, by the thickness of crust, by the stage of integration into the wet.

They are not happy. Happiness is a lowland concept, a dry-country evaluation. But they are *persistent*. They are *active*, in the way the Belt is active—maintaining, accompanying, witnessing. They watch the machines become stone. They watch the stone become substrate. They watch the substrate become *habitat*, become *ground* for the next growth, the next layer of the lush. And sometimes, in the stillness—when the condensation slows, when the fauna pauses, when the transformers are silent except for their resonance—they feel something that might, in another world, have been called *peace*. It is not peace. It is *equilibrium*. It is the dynamic balance of water and growth and time, maintained by constant process, and they are part of it, as everything is part of it.

The **younger** Settled—those born here, those who have never known any other world—do not remember at all. They never needed to. They are *native*, in the way the lung-fish are native, the way the filter-feeders are native, the way the mycelial networks are native—*networks* that coat every surface, that digest the dead, that communicate in chemical pulses, metallic and sweet. The young Settled metabolize moisture gradients. They feed on the difference between wet and less-wet, between the saturated air and the slightly-drier interior of a machine husk. They do not eat, not in the way eating implies, but they *take in*, they *process*, they *become*. Their skin is different—more permeable, more *exposed*, more *part* of the saturated atmosphere. They do not wear clothing, not truly. They wear *layers*—lichen crusts, mineral deposits, the particular biofilm that develops on any surface left long enough in the wet. They are *dim* in the dim light, their surfaces catching the pale luminescence of the condensation, the faint phosphorescence of the colonies that live on them and in them and *through* them.

They do not walk. They *flow*, in the way the lung-fish flow, undulating across the condensation film, leaving trails that evaporate almost before they dry. The boardwalks are *obstacles* to them, but also *features*—places where water pools, where the lung-fish gather in dense layers, where the sound is loudest, where the *presence* is most intense. They learn to use the boardwalks as the large things use them—as *paths*, as *structures*, as *opportunities* for the particular physics of this place, the strange ways sound travels, the way water finds its level, the way pressure and saturation create *conditions* rather than *events*.

They are not human anymore, not quite. Or they are human the way the peat is human—*compressed generations*, centuries of the Settled who came before, their bodies sinking into the wet, becoming *structure*, becoming *ground* for the next growth. The young Settled know this, in their way. They know that to die here is to *integrate*, to become condensation, to become peat, to become part of the cycle. They do not fear it. Fear is a lowland habit, a dry-country response to a world that is *other*, that is *hostile*, that *takes*. Here, the world does not take. It *receives*. It *integrates*. It *makes part*.

And the **oldest**, those who have been here longest, those who have forgotten so completely that they have become *something else*—they are not Settled anymore. They are *Belt*. They are the condensation that forms on stone, the mineral bloom that grows and flakes and grows again, the particular green of copper oxidation, the faint bioluminescence of the fauna that watches. They are *process*, the slow groaning of the uplifted crust, the pulse of growth, the cycle of condensation and evaporation and condensation. They do not move, not truly. They do not speak. But they are *present*, in the way presence is the primary fact here, in the way to be is to be *heard*—the resonant frequency of a transformer, the chiming of a filter-feeder membrane, the roaring of a large thing asserting its presence in a world where presence is everything.

They are the Belt. They have always been the Belt. They will be the Belt when the machines are gone, when the ruins are peat, when the Settled are condensation and the condensation is *memory*, the slow diffusion of everything that has ever been dissolved in the saturated air. They are the water that weeps, the vegetation that grows, the fauna that watches and clicks and chimes and roars. They are the loud that is the only silence, the dim light of a world that has forgotten the sky and found, in its forgetting, something stranger and more complete than any sky could provide.

The boardwalk ends. The peat begins. The Settled step forward—or flow forward, or are *absorbed* forward, into the wet, into the green, into the strange. The water is near surface. It always is. It always will be.

And the Belt receives them, as it receives everything, as it has received the machines, the stone, the sky-forgotten, the singular and the strange, the brief and the dry, until they are wet, are green, are *part of the cycle*, part of the Belt, part of the world that does not need them to understand it, only to *join it*, to become condensation, to become peat, to become *the Belt*, forever and ever, in the dim light, in the saturated air, in the endless, patient, *active* equilibrium of water and growth and time.

The lung-fish brush their legs. The filter-feeders chime. The large things watch, their bioluminescence pulsing in patterns that almost—*almost*—become language. And the Settled, the young and the old and the oldest, the Rememberers and the native-born and the *Belt* itself, all of them *present*, all of them *heard*, all of them *part*—they move forward, they flow forward, they are absorbed forward, into the wet, the green, the strange, the *loud*, the world that has forgotten the sky and found, in its forgetting, the only world it has ever needed, the only world it will ever have, the Belt, the Belt, the *Belt*, forever.
