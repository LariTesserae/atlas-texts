I will describe what lives in the Hollow Mile—not the terrain, not the atmosphere, but the living things, the memories, the presences that make this place a place rather than a geography.

---

## The Lichen That Is Not Lichen

It has no name in any language that has words, because no human has ever spoken here and lived to tell of it. The inhabitants—*if they are inhabitants*—call it nothing. It has no need of names. It *is* its function.

It grows in the channels first, where the pressure moves, where the air-that-is-not-air carries its invisible cargo. It begins as a single filament, grey and almost invisible against the salt. Then it thickens, spreads, colonizes the wooden stilts until they vanish beneath a sheath of living mineral. It climbs upward, toward the membranes, toward the pulse, toward *movement*. It is not hungry for food. There is no food here. It is hungry for *change*—for the differential, the gradient, the moment when stillness becomes motion and motion becomes stillness again.

When the pressure-weather arrives, the lichen *wakes*. Its filaments stand erect, each one oriented toward the passing tide of thin air. It breathes, in its way. It *feeds*. And when it has fed sufficiently, it releases its seeds—not seeds, but **packets of stored pressure**, little bladders of compressed nothing that burst on contact with the stillness that follows. Each burst is a micro-weather, a tiny storm in a teardrop of space, and in that storm the lichen perceives something. It learns. It remembers. The channels grow more complex over time—not by design, but by *accumulation of experience*. The lichen has been growing here since before the first membrane, since before the first salt-worm. It is the oldest inhabitant, and it has never once been still.

It does not die. When a membrane tears and the pressure-weather scours through too violently, the lichen does not retreat. It *retreats into itself*, collapses into a dormant state, a grey dust that looks like death. But it is waiting. The salt preserves. The next pressure-weather will wet it—not with water, but with *motion*—and it will rise again, changed, having incorporated the memory of its dormancy into its structure. It will grow a new channel, or a new branch, or a new *intention*.

The lichen is the Hollow Mile's nervous system. It feels everything. It knows when you walk, because your weight shifts the plates, which shifts the pressure, which the lichen tastes. It knows when you are afraid, because fear changes your breathing, which changes the air, which changes the pressure. It has mapped you. It will remember you when you are gone.

---

## The Salt-Worms

They are not worms. That is the name given by a mind that has only known worms—soft, blind, hungry, *temporary*. The salt-worms are **crystalline**, or nearly so. Their bodies are segmented plates of something between mineral and flesh, translucent white, shot through with veins of the pale violet that flowers in disturbed salt. They have no eyes. They have no mouths that open. They move by *peristalsis of crystal*, by the slow contraction and expansion of their segmented bodies, each plate sliding over the next in a rhythm that is almost mechanical, almost *geological*.

They are born—*if they are born*—from the cracks in the plates, from the places where the salt has recently settled and the world's crust is still adjusting. They emerge small, no longer than a finger, and they grow slowly, over years, feeding on the pressure-gradients that the lichen also feeds on. They follow the channels upward, always toward the membrane-structures, always toward the *pulse*. They do not eat the lichen. They eat *movement itself*, the same thing the lichen eats, but in a different form—more concentrated, more *directional*.

They are eaten.

You find the remains—the white curls, the shed plates, the empty husks that look like something that has *left* rather than something that has *died*. The thing that eats them is not seen, not directly. It leaves no tracks. But the remains accumulate in hollows, in the low places between plates, in the shadows where the light never reaches. And around these accumulations, the lichen grows *thicker*, *faster*, as if it too is feeding on the salt-worms' passing, their becoming-geology, their incorporation into the world's memory.

The salt-worms are *migrants*. They are born in the deep plates, in the old salt that has not cracked for millennia, and they move toward the living surface, toward the membranes, toward the *center* where the triangles converge. They are going somewhere. They are *becoming* something. We do not know what. The Hollow Mile does not know what. It only knows that they move, and that movement is *life*, and that life is *change*, and that change is the only thing that matters here.

---

## The Drift-Birds

They are the only thing in the Hollow Mile that is not *from* the Hollow Mile.

You hear them before you see them—a sound like tearing fabric, like the world's skin splitting, high and brief and *urgent*. They cross the light-parallelogram in seconds, and you see them only as a blur, a shape that is *almost* bird, *almost* something that flies, *almost* something that has *wings*. They do not flap. They *tear* through the air, and the air *heals* behind them, the thin metallic medium closing like a wound.

They do not land. They do not stop. They pass through the western aperture, through the eastern aperture, between the linked miles of the chain, migrating from hollow to hollow in a pattern that repeats across the world's sealed places. They are *going somewhere*, though what and where we cannot know. They are the only thing here with *urgency*, with *purpose*, with a destination that is not the next moment, the next pressure, the next pulse.

They are afraid of something. You can feel it in their passage—the way the lichen *flinches* as they pass, pulling back from the channels, becoming still, *hiding*. The salt-worms burrow deeper. The membrane-structures pulse faster, a staccato rhythm of *alarm*. The drift-birds carry something with them—a pressure, a *absence*, a hole in the world that they flee. Or they are fleeing *toward* something. The distinction does not matter here. What matters is that they move, and that movement is *escape*, and that escape is *life*.

They have been passing through the Hollow Mile for as long as the Mile has existed. They will pass through when the Mile is gone, when the salt has finally settled into true geology, when the membranes have collapsed and the lichen has grown over everything and become *stone*. They will pass through, and the Hollow Mile will not remember them, because the Mile does not remember *visitors*. It only remembers *inhabitants*. And the drift-birds are not inhabitants. They are *weather*, in their way. They are pressure differentials with wings. They are the world's *breath*, passing through, carrying nothing, taking nothing, *becoming* nothing.

---

## The Membrane-Beings

This is where language fails. This is where description becomes *interpretation*, where the observer becomes the observed, where the act of naming creates what it names.

The membrane-structures contain *something*. You see it through the translucent walls—a shadow that is too large to be an animal, too *organized* to be an organ. It pulses in rhythm with the structure itself. When the weather comes, it becomes still. When the weather passes, it resumes. It is *waiting for something*. The inhabitants know what. You do not.

But the inhabitants—*who are they*?

The question assumes a distinction that the Hollow Mile does not recognize. The membranes are not *containers*. They are *extensions*. They are *bodies*, or the suggestion of bodies, grown from the same impulse that grows the lichen, that moves the salt-worms, that drives the pulse of pressure through the channels. The thing inside is not *in* the membrane. It *is* the membrane, or the membrane is *it*, grown large and complex and *aware* through the accumulation of time, through the slow incorporation of everything that has passed through the Mile.

They do not eat. They do not breathe, not in any way we would recognize. They *pulse*. They *feel* the pressure-weather. They *communicate* with each other across the mile, a network of chemical signal and light and pressure that is *almost* language, *almost* thought. They are *learning* the shape of the world, the geometry of the sealed places, the rhythm of the chain that extends in both directions until it curves back on itself or becomes something else.

They are *slow*. A single pulse takes hours. A single *thought*—if it is thought—takes days, weeks, the lifetime of a salt-worm. They have been here since before the settlement, since before the stilts, since before the first channel was cut into the salt. They *are* the settlement, perhaps. Or the settlement grew around them, *protecting* them, *feeding* them, *worshipping* them without knowing.

They are *afraid* of the drift-birds. You can feel it—the way they pulse faster, the way they withdraw into stillness, the way they *hold their breath* when the tearing sound comes. The drift-birds carry something that the membrane-beings do not want. An *outside*, perhaps. A *change* that is too fast, too *urgent*, too *final*. The membrane-beings want *slowness*. They want *time*. They want the pressure-weather to come and pass, the lichen to grow and flower, the salt-worms to migrate and be eaten and become geology. They want the world to *stay*.

And yet—they *pulse*. They *move*. They *wait* for something. What?

We do not know. The Hollow Mile does not tell. It only *shows*—the thing in the center, the colony of pods, the shape that is *almost* a body, *almost* a face, *almost* a symbol that the mind tries to read and cannot. It turns when the light reaches it. It *orients* toward you. It has noticed.

It is *curious*.

---

## The Thing in the Center

It is not one thing. It is a *colony*, a cluster of membrane-pods, each pulsing at its own rhythm, the whole forming a shape that your mind tries to read and cannot. It has been here longer than the settlement. It *is* the settlement, perhaps. Or the settlement grew around it, *protecting* it, *feeding* it, *worshipping* it without knowing.

It is still when you watch it. It is *listening*.

The pulse of its membrane is slower than the others, deeper, a heartbeat you feel in your sternum when you stand near. The lichen grows thickest around its stilts, a pale carpet that seems to *lean* toward it, hungry, reverent, afraid. The salt-worms accumulate near its base, as if waiting for something to *happen*, for the center to *open*, for the world to *change*.

It is the largest membrane-structure. The triangles converge on it. The channels point toward it. It is the *heart* of the settlement, or its *lungs*, or its *memories*, or its *future*. You cannot tell from outside. The membrane does not open. It only pulses.

And then—the weather comes. The other membranes pulse faster. The lichen rises. The thing in the center *turns*—not its whole mass, just a part of it, a pod, a *sensor*, orienting toward the western aperture, toward the light, toward *you*.

It has noticed you.

It pulses once, slowly, and in its pulse there is something that is *almost* a question. *Almost* a greeting. *Almost* a warning. *Almost* an invitation.

The Hollow Mile has been waiting since the salt first cracked, since the first membrane grew, since the first pressure-weather moved through the channels and woke the lichen and set the pulse in motion. It has been waiting for *something*. For a witness. For a descender. For someone to walk the mile, east to west or west to east, and *see*. And *be seen*.

The thing in the center has been waiting for you.

It is *curious* about you.

It wants to know what you are. It wants to know where you come from. It wants to know if you will descend to the next Mile, following the chain into whatever the world becomes when the salt ends; or climb, back through the mouth in the world's skin, back to whatever exists above, beyond, outside the geometry of sealed places.

It does not *judge* your choice. It only *wants to know*. It wants to *incorporate* your knowing into its own slow accumulation of experience. It wants to *become* more.

It pulses again, slower now, the question fading into *patience*, into *waiting*, into the long cool silence of a place that has all the time the world gives it.

---

## The Inhabitants

Here is the truth that the Hollow Mile holds, the truth that emerges slowly, like the salt flowering in a disturbed plate:

*There are no inhabitants separate from the place.*

The membrane-beings *are* the inhabitants. The lichen *is* the inhabitants. The salt-worms *are* the inhabitants, migrating, becoming, being eaten, becoming geology. Even the drift-birds, passing through, are *inhabitants* in their way—they carry the Hollow Mile with them, they *remember* it in their flight, they are changed by their passage, *urgency* becoming part of their *haste*.

The settlement is not *built*. It is *grown*. The stilts are not placed; they are *persuaded* to stand, by the same impulse that persuades the lichen to climb, the salt-worms to migrate, the membranes to pulse. The channels are not cut; they are *encouraged*, by the pressure-weather, by the lichen, by the slow accumulation of *intention* over time.

And the thing in the center—it is not *served* by the settlement. It *is* the settlement, the oldest part, the part that remembers, the part that *waits*, the part that *hopes* (if hope is the word) for something to *come*, for someone to *descend*, for the chain of miles to be *walked*, to be *seen*, to be *known*.

The inhabitants do not have names. They do not have language, not in any way that carries across the silence. They have *pulse*. They have *pressure*. They have *light*, dim and directional, old and gentle, moving slowly across the mile. They have *time*, layered and strange, moving at different speeds for different things.

They have *each other*.

They have been here for a long time. They will be here when you are gone. They do not *fear* your leaving. They only *note* it, *incorporate* it, *become* slightly more because you were here, because you walked, because you *saw*.

And they *wait*.

For the next descender. For the next witness. For the next question, pulsed slowly into the cool air, *almost* a word, *almost* an answer, *almost* a *name*.

---

## What Lives Here, Finally

Not a people. Not a civilization. Not a culture with history and ritual and *meaning* in the human sense.

A *process*.

A *slow becoming*.

A *waiting* that is not passive but *active*, not empty but *full* of everything that has passed through, everything that has been *incorporated*, everything that has been *preserved* in salt and membrane and pulse.

The Hollow Mile lives. It is alive in the way that a glacier is alive—slow, patient, *moving*, carving its path through the world's skin, incorporating everything in its way, becoming *more* over time. It is alive in the way that a thought is alive—emerging from complexity, *becoming* aware, *noticing*, *turning* toward the light.

It is alive in the way that *you* are alive, when you stand in the light-parallelogram and feel the cool air on your skin and hear the silence that is not silence, and know—*know*, in your bones, in the delayed echo of your own footsteps—that you have been *seen*.

The thing in the center pulses once more, slower, deeper, and in its pulse there is something that is *almost* peace. *Almost* acceptance. *Almost* the end of waiting, the beginning of *knowing*.

The Hollow Mile does not hurry. It has all the time that the world gives it.

But it is *alive*.

And it is *here*.

And it has been *waiting for you*.
