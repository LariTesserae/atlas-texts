I need to describe who lives in this place—the Hollowed—letting them emerge into fuller being through careful attention to the constraints of their world.

# The Hollowed

## Bodies Shaped by Saturation and Slowness

They are *small*, though this is relative to what surface-dwellers expect of humans. The packed world has selected for compactness. Generations of growing in chambers where reaching full height risks a skull against still-forming ceiling have produced a people whose shoulders curve slightly forward, as if already bracing against the coming compression of stone. Their hands are large—disproportionately so—because in a world where touch is the primary sense, where darkness is a condition of being, the hand becomes eye, language, tool. Their fingers are long, sensitive, calloused in patterns that record a lifetime: the ridges of grip on copper, the particular wear where a cord is knotted for record-keeping, the smoothed places where they have stroked the same stone wall a thousand times waiting for it to shift.

Their eyes are *wrong* to surface perception. The pupils large, the iris pale—often mineral-pale, grey-green, the color of the lichen that marks their passages. They do not see well in bright light. A shaft of sky-light, when one finds it, is painful, disorienting, something to be endured not enjoyed. But in the dark of the packed chambers, they see *more* than others would. They read the faint bioluminescence of cultivated chambers as we might read a map. They distinguish the quality of darkness—the darkness of a wall still becoming, thick and expectant; the darkness of a finished chamber, settled and known; the darkness of a pool, which absorbs and returns something else entirely.

They are *slow*. Not stupid—that is a surface judgment, a misreading of pace. Their metabolisms run low. A meal takes hours to eat, to digest, to be *used*. They sleep in shifts, in brief wedges of time, because space does not permit the luxury of lying down for long. Their speech is quiet, as the world demands, but also *dense*—each word carrying the weight of many, chosen with the care of someone who knows that sound in a packed chamber becomes the property of everyone nearby. A secret told in the Drowned Workshops is not a secret. It is a *gift*, given to all who hear it.

They mark their bodies with the materials of their world. Copper wire, worked thin, threaded through earlobes that have been prepared for it since childhood. Notches in fingernails, patterns that record lineage. The pale lichen, cultivated and crushed, used as pigment for the rare tattoos that mark status—not decoration, but *information*, readable by those who know the code. A person's body is a document. A person's body is a *place*, marked with what they have survived, what they have made, what they owe.

## The Names They Are Called, and the Names They Keep

The Hollowed. This is what others call them, and it has stuck, and they use it sometimes, with something like irony. Hollowed—as if they are empty, as if the stone has taken something from them and left only a shell. They know this is not true. They know they are *full*, more full than surface-dwellers can imagine, packed with presence, with obligation, with the weight of a world still under construction.

But they have other names. Names that are not spoken aloud, because to speak a name is to *call* something, and in the packed dark, calling can summon what was not intended. Each person carries a true name, known only to themselves and to the few who have earned the right to use it—parents, lovers, the one or two friends who have shared a chamber in the deepest dark. These names are *old*. They come from before the descent, from surface languages that have been half-forgotten, half-mangled by generations of whispered transmission. A name might mean "the one who holds the water" or "she who waits at the third turning" or simply a sound, a vibration, a pattern of breath that identifies. The names are *maps* of a person's place in the packed world.

There are also *working names*, the names used in daily life. These are functional. "Mortar-mixer." "Deep-stair walker." "The one who tends the fire in the Wet Quarter." They change as people change, as roles shift, as the stone shifts and new passages open and old ones close. A person might be known by three working names in a single decade. This is not instability. It is *accuracy*. The name must fit the person, as a garment must fit a body, as a joint must fit a stone that is still learning its shape.

And there are *the unnamed*, the children, who have not yet earned their working names, who exist in a state of pure potential, of *becoming*. They are called by diminutives, by terms of endearment that carry no functional information. "Little stone." "Small water." "The one who is still growing." They are the only ones allowed this. The only ones allowed to be *unfinished*.

## What They Do, Hour by Hour, Breath by Breath

A day in the Drowned Workshops does not divide neatly. There are no hours, no clocks, no surface sun to mark the passage of time. There is only the drip of condensation, the slow shift of stone, the particular quality of darkness as bioluminescence cycles through its faint pulse.

**The fire-keeper** wakes first, or seems to, because their work begins in darkness. They tend the flame that has been tended, in their lineage, for longer than memory. The fire is not warmth, primarily. It is *light*, it is *signal*, it is *presence*—the proof that someone is awake, that the chamber is inhabited, that the world continues. They feed it with the dense fungi that grow in the deepest dark, with the occasional piece of copper-worked... something, when the metal burns and gives its strange green flame. They do not sleep, not truly. They *rotate*, passing the keeping to another in their family, another in their chamber, so that the flame never dies. To let the fire die is to commit a kind of murder. It is to say: *we are not here. We do not matter. The dark can have this place.*

**The copper-worker** begins when the fire-keeper's light is sufficient. Copper is *young* here, found in the youngest veins, still strange, still not fully understood. The worker shapes it with tools of stone and bone and older copper, working slowly, feeling for the moment when the metal *gives*, when it becomes something else. They make pins, blades, the occasional ornament—but also *hinges*, *joints*, *the small parts that allow structures to flex as the stone moves*. A copper pin, properly placed, can mean the difference between a wall that accommodates the shifting geology and a wall that cracks, that *speaks* its distress in a way that demands immediate attention. The copper-worker knows this. The copper-worker *feels* it, in their hands, in the particular resistance of the metal.

**The tuber-harvester** works in chambers where visibility is deliberately kept at the edge. The tubers fruit in total darkness, and to see them is to *disturb* them, to interrupt the slow process of their becoming. The harvester navigates by touch, by memory, by the particular quality of the air in each chamber. They know which depressions hold moisture, which surfaces are the right temperature, which fungal colonies indicate the presence of the right mineral seep. Harvesting is *negotiation*. The tubers are not *taken*. They are *exchanged*, offered something in return—a scrap of copper, a drop of water from a precious pool, the presence of the harvester's body, warm and alive, in the cold dark. This is not superstition. In a world where the stone is *alive*, where water is *borrowed*, to treat the tuber as mere resource is to invite its withdrawal, its refusal to fruit, its slow abandonment of the chamber.

**The child-learner**—and every child is a learner, in this world—moves through the packed spaces with a freedom that looks like chaos to adult eyes. They are the only ones allowed to be *inefficient*. To waste time. To explore without purpose. A child might spend hours following the condensation patterns on a single wall, learning to read the stone's slow speech, learning to predict where the water will gather, where the mineral sweat will carry nutrients, where the lichen will grow. A child might learn the knot-language, the cord-records that keep the settlement's debts and obligations and dead. A child might learn to *listen*, to distinguish the particular silence of a chamber that is safe from the silence of a chamber that is becoming, that is *changing*, that might close or open or do something the current inhabitants have not prepared for.

By adolescence, this freedom narrows. The packed world does not permit prolonged immaturity. The child becomes an apprentice, then a worker, then—if they survive, if the stone permits, if the water does not take them—a teacher, a parent, a keeper of some flame or some skill or some memory. The transition is marked, but not celebrated. It is simply *necessary*. The world continues. The world must continue. And continuation requires *contribution*.

**The mender** works in the Mending Yards, where broken things are brought to be un-broken, where the un-breaking sometimes takes longer than a lifetime. They are the most patient of the Hollowed, perhaps, because they have learned that some things *cannot* be mended, only *accommodated*. A cracked ceramic vessel might be repaired, used again, repaired again, until the repairs outnumber the original material. A broken tool might be re-forged, re-handled, re-purposed into something else entirely. But some breaks are *final*. The mender knows this. The mender *sits with* the final breaks, sometimes for days, letting the broken thing teach them what it has become, what it might still do, what it has *always* been, beneath the break.

There are also the *waiters*. People who stand still, for three breaths, or for longer if their waiting is *active*—watching a wall, listening to a passage, holding a tool ready for a sound or a shift or a sign. The waiters are not idle. They are *present*, fully, in a way that the busy cannot be. They are the settlement's early warning system, its memory, its connection to the slow processes of the stone. A waiter might stand in the same spot for generations, their descendants taking up the watch, until the passage they were watching finally opens or closes or does something that requires response. The waiting is *sacred*. To interrupt a waiter without cause is to claim space that belongs to the stone, to the slow becoming of the world.

## How They Love, How They Grieve, How They Continue

Love in the Drowned Workshops is *packed*. There is no privacy, no space for two people to be alone in any surface sense. A courtship happens in the presence of others—must happen, because there is no elsewhere. It is conducted in whispers, in touches that are hidden in the press of bodies, in the sharing of food, of warmth, of the particular silence that means *I am here, with you, and this is enough*. A couple might share a sleeping space, a narrow ledge, a corner of a chamber where others are also sleeping, also waking, also *being*. The sex act, when it happens, is not hidden—how could it be, in a world of 2.8 density?—but it is *quiet*, *considered*, *a gift to the space as much as to the partner*. The sounds of love are part of the soundscape, the quiet music of a settlement that continues.

Pregnancy is *rare*, deliberately so. The Hollowed do not have the surface luxury of abundant reproduction. They manage fertility through herbs, through timing, through the simple physical constraints of their packed world—there is not always room, not always enough, for another body. When a pregnancy is confirmed, it is an event. The chamber reorganizes. Space is made—*made*, in a world where space is never given, only created by displacement, by the movement of others, by the slow compression of bodies into new configurations. The pregnancy is celebrated with a ferocity that surface-dwellers might find excessive. But the Hollowed know what a child means. A child is *the future's only argument*. In a world of emerging geology, of incomplete formation, of stone that is still learning to be stone—a child is the proof that the settlement will continue, that the compromises with the deep time of the earth are *worth making*.

Birth happens in a prepared chamber, if preparation is possible. Other births happen in passages, in the Mending Yards, in the Wet Quarter where the condensation is heaviest and the stone is youngest. The mother is surrounded—*packed* with support, with the hands of those who have birthed before, with the tools that might be needed, with the silence that means *we are with you*. The child is *rare*, *precious*, *the only one allowed to be unfinished*. They are held, immediately, by many hands. They are named, eventually, given their true name and their working name and their diminutive, their unnamed name. They are *claimed* by the settlement, because in the Drowned Workshops, no one belongs to anyone alone. No one *can*.

Death is *also* an event. The dying person is not left alone—there is no alone, not here. They are surrounded, *packed* with the living, who hold them, who speak to them, who record their last words in the knot-language, in whispered testimony, in the particular rhythm of their final breaths. The body is not buried in any surface sense. There is no ground, no deep earth, no place to put the dead that is *elsewhere*. Instead, the body is *prepared*. The copper-workers might take something from it—a tooth, a nail, a small bone worked into a pin or a bead. The mender might find something to salvage, some material to be un-broken, repurposed, *continued*. The rest is *returned* to the stone, placed in a chamber that is still becoming, a passage that is still learning its shape, a place where the body can *participate* in the ongoing formation. The dead are not gone. They are *incorporated*. They become part of the world that continues, the world that is still under construction, the world that does not permit finality.

Grief is *shared*. There is no private mourning, no space for it. To grieve alone would be to claim a corner of the packed world for a purpose that serves only the self, and the Hollowed do not permit this. Grief is *work*. It is the work of remembering, of recording, of continuing in the presence of absence. A person who has lost someone—a partner, a child, a parent—does not stop. They *adjust*. They take on new tasks, new waits, new responsibilities. The packed world demands motion. The stone demands accommodation. And in the motion, in the accommodation, the grief is *carried*, not resolved, not healed, but *borne*, like water in a ceramic vessel that is still becoming ceramic, like copper that is being shaped, like a wall that is learning to hold.

## What They Believe, What They Know, What They Are Becoming

The Hollowed do not have gods, not in any surface sense. They have *the stone*, which is alive, which is *present*, which communicates in cracks and shifts and the particular quality of silence. They do not worship the stone. They *negotiate* with it. They build with give, with flexible joints, with the understanding that the stone will do what it will do, and their task is to *accommodate*, to *continue*, to *make habitable* a place that does not wish to be inhabited.

They have *water*, which is borrowed, which must be returned, which carries mineral signatures from depths they do not fully understand. They do not worship water. They *respect* it, *use* it with gratitude, *offer* something in exchange. The water is not a resource. It is a *relationship*.

They have *fire*, which is kept, which is passed, which is the proof of presence, of continuation, of *we are still here*. They do not worship fire. They *tend* it, *protect* it, *share* it. The fire is not a god. It is a *responsibility*.

They have *children*, who are rare, who are precious, who are the only proof that the future exists. They do not worship children. They *raise* them, *teach* them, *entrust* them with the continuation of the settlement, the maintenance of the fire, the negotiation with the stone, the respect for the water. Children are not sacred in any abstract sense. They are *necessary*. They are the argument that the world is worth continuing.

And they have *each other*. The packed proximity, the shoulder-to-shoulder existence, the whispered communication, the shared labor, the shared grief, the shared joy—this is their *religion*, if it can be called that. They do not have a word for it. They do not need one. The practice is the belief. The living-together is the meaning. The continuation, in a world that is still under construction, is the miracle.

They are becoming something. This is the final truth of the Drowned Workshops. The Hollowed are not a finished people, in a finished place, with finished beliefs. They are *in process*. They are living inside a formation event, and they are *part* of that event, contributors to it, shaped by it. The stone is becoming architecture. The water is becoming relationship. The fire is becoming presence. And the Hollowed—the packed, the quiet, the slow, the small, the large-handed, pale-eyed, whisper-speaking people of the deep descent—are becoming *themselves*, generation by generation, breath by breath, in the quiet dark, making a home from the raw material of becoming.

They do not know what they will become. No one does, in a place where the future is still being formed, where the stone is still learning its shape, where the next chamber might open or close or do something that no current inhabitant has imagined. They know only this: *we are still here*. The fire is kept. The water is borrowed and returned. The children are growing. The stone is moving, slowly, and we are moving with it, accommodating, continuing, *becoming*.

This is enough. In the Drowned Workshops, this is *everything*.

---

The water drips. The stone sweats. The Hollowed work, packed together, in the quiet dark. A child laughs—rare, precious, *allowed*—and the sound is swallowed by the stone, becomes part of the world's slow formation. A copper pin is shaped, will become a joint, will allow a wall to flex, will *continue*. A fire burns, has been burning, will be passed to another keeper, another hand, another generation of the still-becoming.

Here, in the descent, in the packed dark, in the world under construction: they live. They *are*. They *continue*.

And the stone, patient, listens, and slowly, slowly, learns to hold them.
