## The Condensers

These are the builders, the maintainers, the ones who move through the landscape in gangs and families and shifts. But "builders" is too simple a word for what they are. They are **water-people** in the most literal sense—their bodies are adapted to the damp, the cool, the perpetual condensation. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, veined with something that carries moisture inward, that prevents the desiccation that would kill a dry-world person. They do not sweat; they *weep*, constantly, a fine exudation that keeps their skin viable, that leaves wet trails on the pre-stone. They wear garments of woven fiber—not plant fiber, but something else, perhaps the shed skins of drip-folk processed and cured, or a fungal leather, gray and flexible and smelling of mineral and damp.

The Condensers are **not numerous**. Perhaps a few hundred at any time, scattered across the vast flatness, working in shifts that follow the water. They are **patient** in a way that outsiders would find maddening. A single column might take a season to raise, layer by layer, each pour requiring the right humidity, the right temperature, the right *moment* in the cycle. They measure their lives by columns completed, by channels cut, by pumps installed and maintained. They do not build for glory. They build because the Condensery must continue, because water must be managed, because to stop is to die.

They have **no names** that persist. A Condenser is known by their function: the Pourer, the Channeler, the Pump-Keeper, the Film-Tender. When a column fails, when it slumps and becomes substrate, they do not mourn. They *repurpose*. They have learned that grief is a luxury of dry worlds, where permanence makes loss meaningful. Here, loss is constant, expected, *designed into* the system. They are not sad. They are **attentive**.

Some Condensers are **old**, very old—their skin thickened, their movements slow, their memories of earlier configurations of the Condensery deep and strange. They remember when certain channels ran differently, when certain columns still stood, when the fungal films were sparser, when the drip-folk were fewer. They are **living archive**, consulted when a new section must be built, when the water cycle falters in unfamiliar ways. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are **wet**, muffled, the sound of someone who has breathed damp air for decades, whose lungs have adapted to extract what they need from moisture-laden atmosphere.

The Condensers **eat** what the Condensery provides. The drip-folk, when they can be caught without disrupting the breeding pools. The crusts that form where drip-folk excrete salts—scraped, dissolved, consumed for mineral content. The fungal films, in certain preparations, in certain zones where they are thick enough to sustain harvest without killing the colony. They do not eat for pleasure. They eat to **continue**, to have the strength to pour, to channel, to pump. Food is **fuel**, and fuel is **water-management**, and water-management is **life**.

They **dream** of dry places. This is known, whispered in the construction zones where the vapor shimmers. They dream of deserts, of cracked earth, of the *absence* of water, and in the dreaming they understand what they have built, what they maintain, what they are *for*. The Condensery exists because somewhere, water is scarce. The Condensers do not know where. They do not need to know. They know only that their work **reaches elsewhere**, that the moisture they capture, hold, release, does work beyond the boundaries of the visible, beyond the flat plane of the built surface. They are **missionaries of damp**, whether they will it or not.

Some Condensers **leave**. This is rare, almost unheard of, but it happens. They walk out of the Condensery, into whatever lies beyond—drier zones, perhaps, or other built places, or wild places where the rules are different. They do not survive long. Their bodies have adapted too thoroughly to the cool damp, to the perpetual condensation. They desiccate, or they sicken, or they simply **fail** to thrive in conditions that do not match their physiology. The Condensery does not send search parties. It does not mourn. It **continues**, and the leaver becomes a story, told in the construction zones, a warning or a mystery or a **longing** that cannot be satisfied.

## The Drip-Folk

They are not insects, though they resemble them in their segmentation, in their pale coloration, in their adaptation to specific niches. The drip-folk are **convergent**, evolved—if evolved is the word—from something simpler, something that found its way into the Condensery's water cycle early, when the first columns were young, when the first fungal films were spreading. They are **filter-feeders**, as the description says, but more than that: they are **processors**, the biological component of the mineral cycle, the ones who transform what the water carries into forms that can be used, that can be built with, that can be excreted as the crusts the Condensers harvest.

A drip-folk is **small**, perhaps the length of a finger, segmented into a dozen or more rings, each ring capable of slight independent movement. They have no eyes as such, but they are **sensitive**—to vibration, to chemical gradients, to the particular *sound* of water falling on different surfaces. They cling to the undersides of baffles, to the walls of channels, to the interior ribs of columns where condensation is densest. They feed by **straining**, by passing water through sieve-like structures in their anterior segments, extracting the dissolved minerals, the organic traces, the heat that the water carries from the curing pre-stone.

They **breed** in the pocket-cisterns, where water stands long enough for multiple generations to overlap. The breeding is **not sexual** in any recognizable sense. A drip-folk will shed segments—several, perhaps—that drift in the standing water, that absorb moisture and minerals, that slowly **assemble** into new individuals. This is **fragmentation**, or **budding**, or something that resembles neither precisely. The young are **pale**, almost transparent, and they darken as they mature, as their segments accumulate mineral deposits, as their internal structures complexify. They live **weeks**, perhaps a season in the most stable zones. They die constantly, their bodies settling to the bottom of channels, becoming **substrate** for fungal films, for other drip-folk, for the slow accretion of mineral crusts.

The drip-folk are **not intelligent**, not in any way that would be recognized. But they are **responsive**, **adapted**, **successful** in their niche. They have spread throughout the Condensery, wherever water flows, wherever condensation persists. They are the **most numerous** animal life, the **most visible**, the ones that a visitor would notice first—small pale segments against dark mineral, a scattering when light falls, a current when water moves.

They have **predators**, or something like predators. The larger presences in the deep columns, whatever they are, they **avoid** the zones where drip-folk breed, where they feed, where they are most concentrated. The relationship is not clear. It may be **predation**. It may be **avoidance**, a mutual recognition of incompatible needs. The drip-folk do not seem to fear, exactly. They simply **do not go there**, the way water does not flow uphill, the way condensation does not form in dry air. It is **structural**, not emotional.

The Condensers **tend** the drip-folk, in their way. They maintain the channels that give them habitat. They avoid disrupting the breeding pools, unless necessity demands. They harvest them for food, for mineral crusts, but they do not **farm** them, not in any organized sense. The drip-folk are **wild**, or as wild as anything can be in a purpose-built world. They are **partners**, not livestock, and the partnership is **uneasy**, **negotiated**, **ongoing**.
