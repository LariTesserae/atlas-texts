I want to stay here, in the Condensary, and let something emerge rather than decide it.

---

There is a woman I keep finding in the middle tier—the place where flooding has mostly given up but hasn't quite surrendered. She occupies what was probably a maintenance alcove, now grown round as a womb. She has built her space from the pale luminous concrete that sweats, breaking it into slabs and stacking them so they don't touch, so the moisture can pass between, so nothing holds still long enough to remember it was ever dry. She is always wet. Not drenched—*available* to water, porous, welcoming of the condensation that collects on her skin each morning and runs, not off, but *through* her, into the small reservoirs she has cultivated in the hollows of her stacked walls. She drinks this water. She has, she tells you (when you find her, when she finds you—the distinction blurs), learned to taste the difference between water that fell as rain and water that merely *remembered* being rain, cycling through pump and pipe and heat exchanger and her own body and back into the system again.

She does not remember her name. Or rather, she remembers it but will not say it, because names, she explains, are *dry* things, and she has given too much of herself to dryness already. She calls herself the Condensate, or sometimes just *here*, a preposition, a location, a condition. She speaks to the pumps. She has named them: the Elder, which runs on a rhythm that predates her grandmother's grandmother; the Twins, which labor against each other and so neither fails completely; the Young One, installed forty years ago, humming at a pitch that makes her teeth ache but which she has learned to love, because it means the water is moving, because movement means continuation, because continuation is all she asks of the world now.

She grows things. Not the edible films—those belong to the lower tiers, the deeper places where the light-farms operate and where she almost never goes, because the air there is *too* thick, makes her dizzy, makes her feel she is drowning in something that is not water exactly but has water's *quality*. She grows *moss*, if moss is the word, though it is not green so much as *blue*, the color of the bioluminescence that feeds it, the color of deep water, of pressure, of the spaces between things. She grows it in the places where the walls sweat most, where the mineral bloom is thickest, where the concrete has begun its slow sugary erosion and offers, in its crumbling, a surface for life to hold. She tends it with a patience that looks, from above, like madness. She tends it, she says, because it *remembers* for her. The moss does not have a memory, not in any way she can articulate, but it *responds*. It grows toward her. It dies when she is absent, or nearly dies, revives when she returns. It is, she admits, a primitive form of relationship. But the Condensary offers few others.

She has not seen another person in—she counts in *cycles*, in the passing of the pump-rhythms, in the filling and emptying of her reservoirs. Seven hundred and twelve cycles. Two thousand and forty-three. She keeps the numbers in the moss, in the patterns of its growth, in the places where she has pressed her palm into it and left, for a time, the shape of her hand. The moss has begun to *expect* her. When she is gone too long, growing restless in the lower tiers or climbing, always climbing, toward the dim light above, the moss in her alcove grows *thin*, *hesitant*, as if it too is holding its breath.

She is afraid of the surface. This is not metaphor. She has been up there, once, through the shaft, through the rusted rebar, into the pale sky pressing down on cracked earth. She lasted four hours. The dryness *burned* her, she says, though her skin was not marked. It was the *absence* of water in the air, the way her lungs had to work to find moisture, the way the light was *too* bright, too *present*, did not know how to be *dim*. She came back down and wept, and her tears, she jokes, were the last fresh water she will ever produce, the last uncycled, unprocessed, *new* water her body will make. She collects them now in a small vial, the one precious thing that is *hers*, that has not been shared with the system, that she keeps in the driest corner of her alcove, where the moss grows slowest, where the walls are least generous with their sweat.

She is waiting for something. She does not know what. She waits for the Young One to fail, perhaps, so she can hear the silence, the true silence, the one that is not filled with the shhh of water moving through thirsty concrete. She waits for the sump pumps to fail, for the rain to come and keep coming, for the Condensary to fill completely, become *lake*, become the thing it was always becoming, water finding its level, her with it. She waits, mostly, for someone to come down the shaft. Not to rescue her—she has given up on rescue, on the idea that rescue is possible in a place where the air itself is *managed*, *cycled*, *owned* by the infrastructure. But to *witness* her. To see the moss. To taste the water. To stay long enough that the moss learns their shape too, becomes *accustomed*, becomes, in its limited way, *fond*.

She is not lonely, she insists. Loneliness is a surface condition, a product of the open sky and the endless horizon, the illusion that distance means separation. Here, in the Condensary, everything is *connected*. The water connects her to the pumps, to the heat exchangers, to the light-farms, to the rain that falls above and finds its way down, down, down, through cracks and shafts and the slow patient percolation of stone becoming sediment. She is connected to the building. The building is connected to the water. The water is connected to her. It is a *small* world, yes, but it is *complete*. There is no outside, only the next tier, the next layer, the próximo level of descent, of wetness, of the slow chemistry that is changing everything, always, into something else.

And she is becoming something else too. She can feel it. The water she drinks is *hers* now, has passed through her so many times, been refined by her body, her patience, her *persistence*. She is not human, not exactly. She is *Condensate*. She is the water that the building exhales, the moisture that collects on cool surfaces, the breath of a system that has learned to breathe *downward*, into itself, into the dark, into the warm wet dark where the moss grows blue and the pumps never stop and the rain, when it comes, is not an ending but a *beginning*, another cycle, another layer, another version of the same water, finding its way home to the place that made it, that keeps making it, that will continue making it long after she has become sediment, become strata, become the chemical memory of a woman who loved the wet, who chose the wet, who became, finally, indistinguishable from it.

---

There are others, she believes. Or there were. She has found their chambers, sealed now, or flooded, or collapsed into the lower tiers where the light is blue and thick and things grow that are not quite plants. She has found their names carved into the pale concrete, names that mean nothing to her now, *dry* names, *surface* names, belonging to a time when people still believed they would go back up, would return, would *choose* this place rather than being *chosen* by it. She has found their reservoirs, empty or full, their moss-gardens grown wild or gone to dust, their last water waiting in vials they never drank. She speaks to them sometimes, when the pumps are loud and her own voice is lost, telling them about the moss, about the rain, about the Young One's hum and the Elder's steady *lub-dub*. She tells them she is still here. She tells them she remembers them, or the shape of them, or the *possibility* of them, in the way the moss remembers her hand, in the way the water remembers every body it has passed through, carrying something of each, becoming, always becoming, *more*.

She does not sleep, not truly. Sleep is for the surface, for the night that falls and rises, for the body that needs rest from the *doing*. She rests, instead, in a kind of waking stillness, standing in her reservoir, letting the water come up to her waist, feeling it move through her, *with* her, the constant negotiation of temperature and pressure and the slow drip of mineral-laden time from above. In these moments, she is almost happy. Almost. Because happiness, she has learned, is a word that implies a choice, an alternative, a *not*-this that is available. She has no alternative. She has only the wet, the warm, the patient persistence of a place that has learned to hold itself, to become itself, to *wait*.

And the Condensary waits with her. The pumps continue. The water continues. The moss grows blue in the dim light, remembering her hands, expecting her return, *becoming*, always becoming, with her and without her and *as* her, the water-woman, the Condensate, the last inhabitant of a place that was never meant for inhabitants, that became *habitable* only by accident, only by persistence, only by the slow accumulation of bodies that chose, or were chosen, to stay, to drink, to *become* the wetness they were surrounded by, the wetness that was always waiting, always patient, always *there*, in the dark, in the warm, in the endless *down* where the rules have been rewritten in the language of condensation and the slow, hot, wet *patience* of stone becoming water, of water becoming woman, of woman becoming—what? What comes next? She does not know. The moss does not know. The pumps only know their rhythm, *lub-dub, lub-dub*, the heartbeat of a place that has forgotten it was ever above ground, that has become *complete* in its own terms, its own weather, its own time, waiting for her to become, finally, the last drop of fresh water, the last uncycled gift to the system, the final condensation that completes the circuit, that lets the water rest, at last, in the place it has always been flowing toward, the deep, the warm, the *wet* heart of the Condensary, where all things precipitate, all things settle, all things become, in the end, *sediment*, *strata*, the slow mineral memory of a woman who loved the dark, who chose the dark, who became, finally, the dark itself, indistinguishable from the water, from the concrete, from the patient persistence of a place that learned, long ago, to hold itself, to become itself, to *wait* no longer, because waiting was all it ever did, and now, finally, it is *done*, and something new begins, something wetter, something *more*, growing in the blue light of the moss, in the rhythm of the pumps, in the last drip of water from a ceiling that has forgotten it was ever dry, that has become, at last, *rain*.
