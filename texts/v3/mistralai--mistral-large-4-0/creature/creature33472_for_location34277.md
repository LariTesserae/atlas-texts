I'll descend into this place slowly, letting what lives there emerge from the specific gravity of its conditions.

## The Inhabitants of the Drowned Meridian

### Those Who Remember Being Born Elsewhere

There is a generation, always aging, always passing, who knew the surface. They are not numerous—perhaps a few hundred scattered across the levels—but they are *present* in a way that shapes everything. They are the ones who still climb, who refuse the rope-ladders and insist on the old ways, who test their hands against stone that might hold. They speak of the surface in past tense, then in present, then in a future they will never reach, and their speech has a particular quality: the rhythm of someone translating from a language they are forgetting.

The oldest of them have been down so long that their skin has gone pale in the way of deep-dwellers, but their eyes retain something—a habit of scanning upward, of expecting horizon. They are respected, feared, consulted when decisions about the upper levels must be made. They remember what light *means*, not merely what it *is*. They carry a grief that the younger generations cannot access, a grief so structural it has become indistinguishable from the damp itself.

Some of them are guides. Some are prophets of a return that will not come. Some have simply stopped speaking of the surface and function as oracles instead, their madness or wisdom calibrated to the particular acoustics of the shaft, their predictions arriving as echoes from directions that make no geometric sense.

---

### The Level-Born

Most of the population has never known anything else. They are born in the condensation, in the mineral light, in the particular silence of deep water. Their bodies have adapted in ways that are not dramatic but are *total*—slightly elongated limbs from generations of reaching, climbing, gripping; skin that retains moisture differently, that never fully dries, that has developed a faint luminescence in the deepest levels, a phosphorescence from diet and chemistry and the particular quality of their light-exposure; eyes that have grown more sensitive to the blue-white of reflected mineral glow, less capable of the direct sun they will never see.

They do not think of themselves as *trapped*. This is the crucial adaptation, the one that makes the city possible. They think of themselves as *suited*—to verticality, to water, to the slow growth of stone. The concept of "surface" exists for them as a theological category, a direction of prayer rather than a place of possible arrival. They have their own cosmology: the shaft as body, the water as blood, the light as a kind of distant attention from something they call the Above without quite meaning God.

Their children are born on platforms, in the hollowed spaces of accretion, in the collected warmth of condensation-beast flanks where the temperature permits. Birth is dangerous—the damp, the cold, the occasional collapse—but it is also *numerous*. The population maintains itself through sheer reproductive persistence, through the particular fecundity of a place where death is constant but so is life, where the water that kills also nourishes, where the stone that crushes also shelters.

### The Water-Keepers

At the lowest levels, where the streams actually run, there is a caste—though "caste" implies a rigidity that the turbulence of the place prevents. Still, there is a *function*, a role that shapes identity. The Water-Keepers are those who understand the flow, who can read the stone for where water will go, who maintain the collection systems, the channels, the ancient and constantly-renegotiated agreements with the condensation-beasts whose harvesting they depend upon.

They are the most physically adapted to the deep—short, powerful, with skin that has grown almost impermeable, with lungs that can hold their breath for minutes in the flooded passages where they sometimes must work. They speak a dialect that has diverged from the middle-level speech, heavy with the acoustic properties of their environment, full of sounds that carry in water but die in air.

They are also the most politically powerful, in their way. They control what everyone needs. They know how to make the upper levels uninhabitable—to divert flows, to trigger the slow collapse of saturated stone, to weaponize the very dampness that sustains the city. This power is never openly exercised. It is *available*, a constant background presence in all negotiations, the way that the knowledge of mortality is always present in human affairs without needing to be spoken.

The Water-Keepers also tend the condensation-beasts. This is intimate work, requiring generations of knowledge. A beast is anchored to a particular wall, a particular airflow, and moving it is nearly impossible—the beast will sicken, will stop producing, will die in its centuries-long sleep. So the Keepers build *around* the beasts, their settlements growing around these massive, eyeless, patient creatures as medieval towns grew around cathedrals. They drain the collected water through channels cut into the beast's specialized back-surfaces. They harvest the mineral secretions, the strange salts that precipitate from the beast's metabolism of air. They speak to them, in the low frequencies that the beasts seem to perceive, though whether this is communication or simply habit, no one can say.

The oldest beasts are treated with something beyond utility. They are *ancestors*, in a way. The Keepers believe—some of them, the ones with the oldest lineages—that the beasts remember. That their slow metabolism, their centuries of anchored existence, has accumulated something like memory, like dream, like the *weight* of place that the memory-moths seem to read. They do not eat the oldest beasts when they finally die. They seal them into the walls, let the mineral accretion grow around them, make them part of the architecture, part of the breathing stone.

### The Light-Brokers

At the top, where the descending glow is brightest, there is another power. The Light-Brokers control access to the reflected brightness, to the pale luminescence that passes for daylight in the upper levels. They are wealthy in the way of the shaft—not in water, which is everywhere and therefore valueless, but in *attention*, in the quality of existence that light provides.

Their power is architectural. They have learned to shape the reflective surfaces, to angle the pale mineral, to create lenses and channels that concentrate what little descends from the surface-glow. They sell light by the hour, by the level of platform, by the particular quality of reflection—whiter, warmer, more like the light of the remembered surface. The poor live in shadow, in the deeper levels where artificial light must be bought in fat and oil and the luminescent secretions of shaft-fish, where the glow is always *marked* as artificial, always a reminder.

The Light-Brokers are also the most desperate, in their way. They can see the surface-glow, really see it, the pale thread of brightness that connects to the world above. They are the ones most likely to attempt the climb, to test the ancient routes upward, to fall and be found days later on a lower platform, broken, still reaching. They are the ones who commission the most elaborate artificial light, the most desperate simulations of the surface, the gardens of luminescent fungus that are rumored to exist in the wealthiest upper-level dwellings—gardens that are fake, that everyone knows are fake, that are cultivated anyway because the *pretense* of green, of growing things, of horizontal space, is worth more than honest darkness.

They are also the most *vertical* in their thinking. They understand, perhaps better than anyone, that the shaft is a column of exchange—light descending, water ascending in vapor, people climbing and falling, the constant circulation that makes the place habitable. They are the economists of the Drowned Meridian, the ones who have most fully internalized the rules of a world where horizontal is impossible, where all value moves up or down, where to be *between* levels is to be in flux, in danger, in the process of becoming something else.

### The Moth-Readers

There is a smaller group, scattered across all levels, who have learned to read the memory-moths. They are not priests, exactly, though they perform ritual functions. They are not scientists, though they practice something that resembles empirical observation. They are *interpreters*, of a text that writes itself in wing-patterns, in the clustering of moths around particular mineral signatures, in the shifting informations that some claim to see in the insects' movements.

The Moth-Readers are feared and respected in equal measure. They know things—about where violence has occurred, about where intentions have accumulated, about where the stone has *noticed* something and retained it. They mark these places, sometimes with physical barriers, sometimes with ritual prohibitions, sometimes simply with their presence, their watching, their slow circling around the moth-clusters as if in conversation with something that is not quite the moths themselves.

Some Moth-Readers are frauds, performing knowledge they do not possess, reading patterns that are random, profiting from fear. Others are genuine, in whatever sense "genuine" applies to an epistemology based on the wing-beats of insects that do not eat and seem to *absorb* something from the damp air—memory, perhaps, or intention, or simply the particular mineral history of a place, encoded in patterns that human perception can almost, but not quite, decode.

The oldest Moth-Readers claim to hear the moths. Not their wing-beats, which are silent, but something else—a kind of *resonance*, a frequency that matches the particular mineral composition of the walls they cluster around. They claim to feel it in their teeth, in their bones, in the water in their inner ears. They claim that the moths are *reading* the stone, and that by reading the moths, the Moth-Readers can access what the stone knows: the accumulated weight of centuries, the slow thought of a place that has been inhabited so long it has begun to want.

Whether this is true, no one can say. The Moth-Readers themselves are uncertain, in their deeper moments. But they persist, because the alternative—to accept that the moths are simply insects, that the marked places are simply geology, that the weight of the shaft is simply physics—is a kind of despair that few can sustain. Better to believe that the moths know, that the stone remembers, that there is *meaning* in the patterns, even if that meaning can only be approached, never fully grasped.

### The Climbers

Every level has its Climbers—those who move between the tiers, who carry messages, goods, the occasional desperate refugee from a collapse or a flood. They are the connective tissue of the vertical city, the ones who make the shaft into a *place* rather than a collection of isolated platforms.

Climbing is a skill that takes years to learn, that changes with every generation as the stone shifts, as new water-paths open and old ones seal. The Climbers know the routes—where the accretion is stable, where the timber holds, where a bridge sags but does not break, where the rope-ladders have been recently replaced. They know the acoustic signals, the echo-locust calls that mark safe passage, the particular silence that means danger. They know the glisten-worm trails, the slightly different textures that mark navigable surfaces in absolute darkness.

They are also the ones who find the bodies. The Climbers are the first to reach a collapse, a flood, the slow crushing of a platform that has been eaten by mineral growth. They are the ones who perform the necessary work of the dead—not burial, exactly, since there is no earth, but *disposal*, the carrying of bodies to the deepest levels where the warm water and the breathing stone will take them, where the condensation-beasts will eventually, over centuries, incorporate what they can into their slow metabolism.

Some Climbers are professionals, paid in water and light and the particular status that comes from being trusted with vertical movement. Others are amateurs, the desperate, the curious, the ones who climb for reasons they cannot articulate—to see, to escape, to test themselves against the shaft, to feel the particular loneliness of being *between*, suspended in the column of air and light and water, belonging to no level, visible from everywhere, master of nothing.

The best Climbers become something else. They become *vertical* in their identity, no longer belonging to any level, living in the transitions, the rope-ladders, the bridges, the brief platforms where they rest. They are the most free and the most precarious of the inhabitants, dependent on the goodwill of every level, subject to the collapses and floods that are their particular occupational hazard. They age quickly, their bodies worn by the constant reaching, the constant gripping, the constant adjustment to a world that does not want to be climbed.

### The Deep Ones

At the bottom, where the light is rumor and the water runs warm with geothermal heat, where the walls seem to breathe and the silence has weight—there are those who have gone further than the others. Not many. The Deep Ones are feared, respected, sometimes worshipped, always *avoided* by those who can help it.

They live where the pressure-snails grow thickest, where the condensation-beasts are oldest and slowest and most dream-like, where the mineral accretion moves fastest and the stone is most *alive*. They have adapted to a world that is almost aquatic, where the air is thick with moisture, where the distinction between breathing and drinking becomes blurred. Their skin is almost continuously wet, covered in a film of mineral-saturated water that drips constantly, that leaves trails wherever they move. Their eyes have reduced to small, light-sensitive slits, useless in the near-darkness but capable of detecting the faintest thermal variations, the warmth of a beast, the cold of a new water-flow.

The Deep Ones speak rarely, and when they do, their voices are strange—thick with moisture, carrying in the dense air in ways that seem to bypass the ears, to resonate in the chest, in the water in the bones. They have their own language, or perhaps a dialect so diverged from the middle-level speech that it is unintelligible. They communicate mostly through touch, through the pressure of hands on shoulders, through the shared experience of the breathing walls, the dreaming beasts, the slow thought of stone that has been inhabited so long it has begun to want something.

Some of the Deep Ones claim to hear the want. They claim that the shaft, in its deepest levels, is *conscious*, or becoming conscious, that the mineral accretion, the breathing, the slow geological respiration is a form of thought, slow and vast and *alien*. They claim that the memory-moths are not reading the stone but *translating* it, that their wing-patterns are an attempt to communicate something that has no human equivalent, something that might be *hunger* or *longing* or simply the accumulated *attention* of a place that has been watched for millennia and has begun to watch back.

The Deep Ones tend the oldest condensation-beasts. They do not drain them, harvest them, use them as the Water-Keepers do. They *listen* to them, they feel their slow respiration, they claim to perceive in the beasts' centuries-long stillness a form of dreaming, of contemplation, of *waiting*. They believe—the oldest of them, the ones whose families have been deep for generations—that the beasts are trying to reach the surface, that their anchored existence is a form of *aspiration*, that their slow harvesting of water from air is a kind of prayer, a reaching upward that will never be fulfilled but is *real* in its own terms.

Whether this is wisdom or madness, no one can say. The middle-levels send their criminals down, their mad, their inconvenient. The upper-levels send nothing, preferring to forget that the bottom exists. The Deep Ones receive these exiles, incorporate them into their strange society, their touch-based communication, their life in the breathing dark. Some of the exiles adapt. Some go further down, into levels that have no names, where the walls move visibly, where the water runs hot enough to scald, where the silence is so complete it becomes a presence, a *thing* that presses against the mind.

The Deep Ones do not prevent this. They do not encourage it. They simply *are*, in their wet darkness, their slow speech, their communion with beasts and stone and the particular want of a place that has forgotten what it was built for and has become its own slow, turbulent, utterly specific way of being.

### The Ones Who Are Not Yet

Every place has its children, its unborn, its potential. In the Drowned Meridian, this takes a particular form. The damp, the mineral chemistry, the absence of plant life—these have shaped not only the visible fauna but the ways in which the inhabitants themselves reproduce, adapt, *become*.

There are rumors of change. The Deep Ones speak of it, in their thick voices, their touch-communication. The Moth-Readers see it in the shifting patterns, the new clusters, the places where the moths gather that have no history, no violence, no accumulated intention—only *possibility*. The Water-Keepers feel it in the water, in the new flows, the changing temperatures, the mineral signatures that do not match any known source.

The shaft is deepening. Or the inhabitants are descending. Or both. There are levels below the Deep Ones, levels that have no names, no inhabitants, no maps. The water runs warmer there, carrying minerals from sources that should not exist at this depth. The walls breathe faster, the accretion grows visibly, in timescales that human perception can almost grasp. The condensation-beasts there are older, slower, more *dream-like*, their surfaces covered in mineral formations that resemble, to those who have seen them, something like *eyes*, something like *mouths*, something like the beginnings of *faces*.

The inhabitants of the Drowned Meridian are becoming something else. Not quickly. Not in any individual lifetime. But in the accumulation of generations, in the slow adaptation to a place that is itself changing, in the particular pressure of a world where vertical is the only direction and depth is the only future. They are becoming *native* to a place that was never meant for them, adapting to conditions that would kill their surface-dwelling ancestors in hours, developing bodies and societies and ways of knowing that have no equivalent above.

What they are becoming, no one can say. The Deep Ones have their theories, their touch-communion with beasts and stone. The Moth-Readers have their patterns, their wing-decoding, their sense of a meaning that approaches but never arrives. The Light-Brokers have their desperate hope, their climbing, their fall, their renewed attempt. The Water-Keepers have their practical control, their knowledge of flow, their power over the essential.

But all of them, in their different ways, are *waiting*. For what, they do not know. For the surface, perhaps, though they have forgotten what the surface was. For the bottom, perhaps, though they fear what waits there. For change, for transformation, for the slow emergence of something new from the accumulated weight of centuries, the particular ecology of a place that has been inhabited so long it has become its own purpose, its own slow and turbulent and utterly *specific* way of being.

The Drowned Meridian does not end. It *continues*, downward, into the breathing dark, into the dreaming beasts, into the stone that remembers everything and wants, perhaps, to be remembered in return. And its inhabitants continue with it—vertical, adapted, scattered across the levels, climbing and falling and climbing again, living in the bright shaft of distant light, in the cool damp of eternal condensation, in the silence that is full of water and the water that is full of sound, becoming, always becoming, what they must be to survive in a place that was never meant for them and has become, despite everything, *home*.
