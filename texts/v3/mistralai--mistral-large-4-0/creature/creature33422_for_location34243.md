I'll let this place speak for itself, letting the inhabitants emerge from what the description offers.

## The Ones Who Live Here

They have no name for themselves in the way that peoples below name themselves. Not because they lack words, but because *who they are* is not a single thing. They are **the many who gather**, **the ones who drink from stone**, **the patient ones**. In their own speech, they might call a person *where-the-water-collects* or *one-who-reads-the-cold*, because a person here is defined by function, by relationship to the damp, by the particular way they have learned to be *dense* enough to survive.

### The Water-Readers

These are the oldest ones, or the most honored, or perhaps the same thing. A Water-Reader does not predict weather with instruments—there are none, not yet, not here. A Water-Reader *feels* the change in the air. They wake before dawn, when the condensation is thickest on their own skin, and they know by the quality of the cold on their face whether the day will bring gathering or release, abundance or drawing-down of stores. They are advisors, yes, but also *interpreters*—the ones who can speak to a settlement about what the land is doing, what it might do, what must be prepared. They keep no written records. Their knowledge is *embodied*, passed through apprenticeship, through the long process of learning to feel what others only observe. A Water-Reader's hands are always slightly damp, even when they have just dried them. They carry a particular stillness, the stillness of someone who has learned to wait.

### The Condenser-Keepers

Every structure that catches moisture has its keeper. This is not a profession so much as a *condition*—you are born into a level of the city, and that level has its condensers, and you learn their angles, their surfaces, their particular genius for gathering what the night offers. Condenser-Keepers know the difference between condensation that forms on north-facing stone and south-facing, between the bead that forms at 3 AM and the bead that forms at 5, between moisture that will evaporate by noon and moisture that will pool, that will *stay*, that will become resource. They are *maintenance-people*, *adjusters*, *cleaners*—removing the lichen that would clog, the dust that would insulate, the insect bodies that would contaminate. Their work is endless, never finished, always beginning again. They are not glamorous. But when a drought comes, when the stores run low, the Condenser-Keepers are the ones whose skill determines whether the city endures. They are *necessary*. In this place, that is the highest praise.

### The Stone-Workers

They split the fractured rock along its natural planes. They know which stone rings when struck and which stone merely thuds, which will hold an edge and which will crumble. Their technology is *simple*—obsidian struck against flint, bronze chisel set to granite, the patient percussion that turns massive stone into building material, into tool, into the surfaces that will become condensers, cisterns, the foundations of the next layer of the city. A Stone-Worker's hands are scarred, permanently, from the micro-shatters that no glove can fully prevent. They work in the relative warmth of midday, when the stone is least cold, when their fingers can still feel what they are doing. They are *artists* in a way they might not claim—their structures must be functional, must work with the damp, must participate in the moisture economy of the city. But within those constraints, they choose. They find the planes, the angles, the particular orientation that will make a surface *sing* with condensation. They are builders of *provisional* things, knowing that the young land will reshape what they make. They build anyway.

### The Moss-Tenders

In the protected spaces—where soil has begun to accumulate, where the city's own waste has been composted, where the microclimate is gentler than the exposed stone—there are *gardens*. Not gardens of food, primarily, though some food grows there. Gardens of *moss*, cultivated for its properties. Moss holds moisture. Moss creates habitat for the insects that pollinate, that decompose, that are themselves food. Moss softens the sound of the city, absorbs the ring of stone, creates pockets of *quiet*. A Moss-Tender knows which species will grow in which conditions, how to encourage the spread of the cushioning varieties, how to prevent the moss from overwhelming the condensers that must remain bare stone. They work with water that is *already gathered*, that has been diverted from the city's collection systems, that is *given* to the moss rather than hoarded. They are *givers-away* in a place of scarcity, which makes them strange, which makes them necessary, which makes them perhaps a little holy.

### The Goat-People

Not all of them. Some keep other animals, or no animals. But the goats are *central*, and those who tend them are defined by that relationship. A Goat-Person knows individual goats—which one finds the condensation in stone bowls, which one has learned to strip lichen from condenser surfaces, which one will lead the herd to a new grazing when the old ground is exhausted. They milk in the morning, when the udders are fullest, when the milk is warmest. They shear in the season of warmth, when the wool can be washed and dried before the cold returns. They are *mobile* in a city that is otherwise fixed, moving their herds to the sparse vegetation that grows in the lee of ridges, in the shelter of abandoned structures, in the places where the city's own heat has created microclimates. They are *semi-wild* themselves, following older patterns, resisting full domestication. A Goat-Person must be *patient*, must accept that the goats will go where they will, must learn to read the herd's movement as information, as guidance, as a kind of *wisdom* that does not speak in human words.

### The Bird-Keepers

The birds nest in the upper levels of the city, where the structures provide shelter, where the insects congregate, where the condensation drips in predictable patterns. Some birds are *wild*, tolerated, their eggs left alone, their presence noted as *weather*, as something that comes and goes. Others are *kept*—not caged, never that, but *encouraged*, their nesting sites protected, their young gathered when they flog, raised for meat, for eggs, for the feathers that line clothing against the cold. A Bird-Keeper knows the difference between a bird that can be kept and a bird that must be left, between the species that will return to the same structure year after year and the species that ranges too widely to be *owned*. They are *listeners*, attuned to the calls that carry across the thin air, the alarm calls that spread through flocks, the particular silence that means a predator is near. They are *quiet* people, because the birds are quiet, because loudness here is *waste*, is *danger*, is the opposite of the patience this place demands.

### The Insect-People

This is not a caste, not exactly. But there are those who have learned to *read* the insects, to know which species are edible, which are medicinal, which indicate the health of the stored grain, which must be encouraged and which must be controlled. The insects are *everywhere*—in the walls, in the compost, in the dung, in the grain stores. They are *food*, *medicine*, *indicators*, *workers* (the ones that break down waste, that pollinate, that cycle nutrients). An Insect-Person might be a healer, using the particular chemistry of a beetle to treat a wound. They might be a *keeper of stores*, monitoring the insect populations that threaten to overwhelm the grain, that must be managed with the same care as any water source. They might be *farmers*, cultivating the insects that are most useful, that can be *fed* on waste, that can be *harvested* without depleting the wild populations. They are *small*-focused, *detail*-attuned, people who have learned to find abundance in the tiny, in the numerous, in the *dense* that others overlook.

### The Deep-Ones

In the lowest levels of the city, where the cold is least, where the warmth of the dense population pools, where the oldest construction has become foundation and cellar and *something else*—there are those who have *descended*. Not physically, necessarily, though some do go down, into the spaces where the young stone is still shifting, where water moves through fractures, where the city meets the land in its most *becoming* state. The Deep-Ones are *listeners to the stone*, to the slow movement of water underground, to the particular quality of cold that comes from deep in the earth rather than from the sky above. They are *miners*, of a sort, extracting the minerals that the stone offers, the salts that accumulate where water has evaporated, the metals that have traveled far and are found in the fractures. They are *water-diviners*, in a sense—not with mystical tools, but with *observation*, with the accumulated knowledge of where water has flowed, where it might flow again, where the young land is still negotiating its drainage. They are *strange*, the Deep-Ones, because they live in the spaces between the city's layers, in the *archaeology* of settlement, in the places that the living city has built over and forgotten. They are *necessary*, because they know what the surface-dwellers do not, because they read the land in its slow, deep time.

### The Story-Keepers

Every evening, when the work is done, when the condensation begins its nightly gathering, when the city *breathes* moisture and the inhabitants gather in the warmth they have stored—there are those who *speak*. The Story-Keepers do not merely entertain. They *remember*, for a people who have no writing, who have only the layered construction of their city as archive, the stories are *everything*—the history of droughts survived, of floods endured, of the particular years when the water came and when it went, of the Water-Readers who predicted and the Condenser-Keepers who saved and the Stone-Workers who built and the others who made the city *possible*. A Story-Keeper's voice carries in the thin air, must carry, because the gathering is *vast*, horizontal and vertical, and the story must reach every level, every ear, every person who needs to hear that they are not the first to face this uncertainty, that others have *endured*, that the city has *persisted*, that the damp is not only challenge but *gift*, not only threat but *possibility*. They are *teachers*, in a way. *Priests*, in another. *The continuity of the people*, in the truest sense. They speak of the young land, still becoming, and they make it *meaning*, make it *home*, make it *theirs* in the only way that matters here—through the *attention* they pay to it, through the *patience* they model, through the *density* of presence they create in the gathering space, the human warmth that holds against the cold, the voice that holds against the silence.

## What They Are, Together

They are **not** a civilization in the sense of the lowlands. They do not build monuments. They do not conquer. They do not *hurry*. They are **a density**, a *many* that compensates for the sparse, the cold, the uncertain. They are **symbiotic**, layered with the goats and the birds and the insects and the moss and the stone and the water that is never quite one thing, never quite another. They are **patient**, because the place demands it, because the cycles are slow, because the land is young and still deciding, and they have learned to *participate* in that becoming rather than oppose it.

They are **alive**, intensely, *compressedly* alive, in a place that makes life *expensive*, that demands *adaptation*, that offers in return the strange gift of *belonging*—not ownership, but *participation*, not conquest, but *relationship*, with a land that is still, always, still becoming.

They drink from stone. They gather the night's breath. They build upon what will be reshaped, buried, exposed, transformed. They are **many**, because to be alone here is to be vulnerable, is to be *possible* only in the sense that anything is possible, including death, including failure, including the slow cold that takes the isolated, the sparse, the individual.

They are **the Condensation Cities**. And they *endure*.
