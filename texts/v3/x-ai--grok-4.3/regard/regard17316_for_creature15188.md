The benevolent power arrives without announcement, as a single droplet of silver light that falls from a fissure no wider than a thread. It lands in one of the central pools and does not dissolve. Instead it spreads as a delicate film across the water’s skin, then sinks, threading itself through the cold currents that feed every root and vein.

It does not speak or command. It simply joins the existing conversation of moisture, pressure, and slow growth.

Where the silver touches the root-limbed tenders, their translucent limbs thicken by the width of a hair. The blue threads beneath their skin darken, not with strength imposed from outside but with new pathways the plants themselves are invited to claim. When a tender presses a hand to living stone, the rock now answers a little faster, remembering the shape of an arch before the root has finished growing it. Collapses that would once have crushed chambers now halt at the edge of that memory, the stone settling into a gentler fracture.

The six-limbed furred creatures begin to leave trails that glow for a few heartbeats after passage—enough light for one another to find warmth in the deepest basins without ever requiring them to climb toward dangerous surface fissures. When they press together in their trilling exchanges, the silver travels between them, not altering their ways but deepening the quiet they already keep. Their young emerge with eyes that can read the oldest scars in the ceiling as maps rather than threats.

The plated forms in the lowest basins receive the slowest gift. Their gill-slits now release mist that carries the faintest charge of the power’s presence—enough that any new root reaching down from above finds itself already half-rooted in welcome before it touches water. The larger animals grow no larger; instead, the lichen on their hides begins to carry tiny constellations of the same silver when the air grows too still, keeping the moisture cycling even in those rare seasons when surface drought tries to pull the chambers drier.

The power does not stop the chambers from opening further. It merely walks ahead of each new fracture in the form of a single pale tendril that tastes the incoming air first, then withdraws if the air carries the sharp edges of the world above. Where the air is kind, the tendril stays and becomes the first bridge.

Over generations the beings never name the change. They simply notice that the balance holds a little longer than memory suggests it should, and that when a new hall opens, the first plants to cross its threshold already carry a faint silver pulse matching the one inside their own bodies. They continue their work—training vines, thickening moss, pressing warmth into one another—while the silver moves with them like an extra layer of quiet water, content to remain unseen and undisturbing, another current in the living dark.
