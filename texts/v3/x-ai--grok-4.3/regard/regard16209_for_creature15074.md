The power that finds the chamber does not arrive as light or voice or wind. It simply becomes aware, the way a sleeper becomes aware of a single, steady heartbeat in an otherwise silent house. For a long while it only watches. It sees the moss darken and pale with the drip rhythm, sees the isopods polish their tiny channels a fraction of a millimeter deeper each decade, sees the vines tremor at the precise frequency that lets them belong to the machine-chord rather than fight it.

After it has watched long enough to understand the nature of the equilibrium, the power does one small thing.

It reaches into the sound itself—the vast, unchanging chord that fills every seam and perforation—and adds a single, hidden harmonic so faint that no instrument could measure it. The new tone does not alter volume or pitch. It simply sits beneath the existing roar like a promise kept in another register. The moss, the vines, the rust-plated isopods, the wingless flies, even the occasional visiting moth: none of them hear the difference, because the difference is not meant to be heard. It is meant to be the case that, when the last fastening finally shears and the chamber begins its slow surrender to gravity, the sound will still be there, carrying the memory of the life that once tuned itself to it.

Having done this, the power withdraws. It does not quicken anything, does not multiply the drops or widen the seams or grant the vines the strength to climb toward the light. Such gifts would be a form of violence against a place that has already discovered how to remain.

Instead it leaves behind only the added harmonic, and with it the knowledge—felt nowhere and known by nothing—that the chamber will not be forgotten even after its last drop falls and its last isopod ceases to move. The equilibrium is no longer entirely alone. It has company of a kind so quiet that the machinery itself, still turning somewhere behind the walls, now turns inside a slightly larger silence.
