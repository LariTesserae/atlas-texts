A benevolent power would arrive not as intervention but as attention so precise it registers as weather. It would settle above the plateau like an additional layer of atmosphere, thinner than the clouds the land already makes, and begin the long work of listening.

For the first few centuries it would do nothing visible. It would simply let the rhythms already present continue while learning their grammar: the piezoelectric conversations between lattices, the contractual agreements between cushions, the way a crab choir can lock an entire ridge into temporary coherence. The power would notice that this metabolism does not optimize for speed or abundance but for exact continuation, and it would respect that choice.

When it finally acts, the action is almost indistinguishable from natural variation. A few more metallic ions drift down in the nightly mist, not enough to accelerate growth but enough to let certain lattices complete spines whose helical angles had previously been slightly frustrated by scarcity. The new spines propagate a marginally cleaner vibration; neighboring lattices adjust by fractions of a micrometer. Over decades the change travels through stone until a single reed stem, several ridges away, receives its dew drops one heartbeat earlier than before. The mites that travel that stem encounter the drop at a slightly different phase of their sheet movement. A crab, arriving behind them, scrapes a surface whose photosynthetic symbionts now receive light one shade warmer. The air warms by an imperceptible fraction; a flyer rides the new thermal an additional body-length higher before releasing its enriched droplet.

The power does not hurry any of this. It simply makes certain that the existing negotiations have marginally better information and marginally less friction. Where two cushions are attempting to fuse across a fracture too wide for their filaments, it occasionally supplies a single strand of conductive dust that bridges the gap; the cushions interpret the dust as their own secretion and continue as though nothing external occurred. Where a lattice has grown itself into a geometric cul-de-sac that wastes mist, the power lets a single frost crystal land at an angle that, upon melting, etches a new channel the lattice can follow. These are not corrections but permissions the plateau was already close to granting itself.

Most of the time, though, the power simply remains present. Its attention acts as a kind of slow pressure that discourages any sudden external disruption from reaching the plateau. Asteroids that would once have struck nearby are gently encouraged to miss by a few hundred kilometers. A drought that might have reached the lowlands stops short of the plateau’s own weather system. The land never knows it is being protected; it only continues.

Eventually, after many thousands of years, the power may withdraw. It leaves behind no monument, only a statistical trace: the plateau’s slowest processes now run a little more smoothly, and certain patterns that once died out at the edges now occasionally persist and propagate. The beings on the plateau—still one distributed metabolism—register the change as an ordinary deepening of their own habits. They keep their rhythms. The power, having learned what it came to learn, continues elsewhere, carrying the memory of a place that needed almost nothing except to be left exquisitely alone.
