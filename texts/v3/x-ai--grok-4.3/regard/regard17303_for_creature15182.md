A quiet attention settles over the ridge like a held breath. The benevolent power does not arrive in light or thunder; it arrives as a slight change in the way stone remembers pressure. Where the diggers’ wedges bite, certain layers now release a little more easily, not because the rock has grown weaker but because the mountain has decided, for a time, to be helpful. Fissures that once threatened to carry a sudden seep instead widen by a finger’s width each season, becoming slow, reliable veins that feed the condensation basins without flooding the new chambers. The power never speaks this decision aloud. The people simply wake one morning to find the night crew has broken through into a long, low gallery already lined with shallow basins that were never cut by hand.

The pale roots receive a second gift. They thicken by degrees, still translucent, still without leaves, yet now they carry a faint sweetness when chewed. The thin stews grow a little more sustaining. No one comments on the change; they are a people who treat abundance the same way they treat absence—with measured attention. Only the oldest, whose fingers have learned every texture of the stone, notice that the roots now pulse with a rhythm slightly slower than the drip of water. When they press their palms to them at night, the pulse feels companionable rather than strange.

In the deepest unfinished tunnels the power leaves almost nothing, only a single, continuous seam of slightly warmer stone that runs along the floor like a subtle path. When the crews lay reed mats across it to sleep, the stone holds the day’s chisel-warmth longer than it should. Children playing the breakthrough game begin to sense, without knowing why, which walls will open next; their chalk marks grow more precise, though they still believe it is only luck. The blind elders, touching the same seam, find their knowledge of water routes quietly confirmed or gently corrected, as though the mountain is answering their old questions with new patience.

The benevolent power does not give them a leader, nor does it still the endless tap of tools. It understands that their life is the act of turning absence into rooms, and rooms into lungs for the mountain. So it limits itself to removing small obstacles that would otherwise have cost lives or forced retreat: a pillar that would have cracked under weight instead slowly hardens; a pocket of bad air is quietly vented through a new fissure that opens exactly where the next crew needs light and breath. These mercies are never dramatic enough to be noticed as miracles. They register only as good fortune in the rock, and the people accept good fortune the same way they accept the constant chill—without ceremony.

Over years the city grows a little safer, a little kinder to its own unfinished state. The power remains nearby, more presence than intervention, like the water that already travels from ceiling to floor. It watches the pale roots lengthen, the reed lamps burn, the single-file lanes fill and empty again with bodies that have never seen the sun. When the last chisel of the night crew falls silent, the power sometimes stirs the condensation just enough to make one extra drop fall into a basin already full, so that the sound travels farther than it should through the dark, carrying the mountain’s continued, wordless agreement.
