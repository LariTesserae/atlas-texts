The benevolent power arrives not as storm or decree but as a single, patient thought carried on the same copper haze the mesa already breathes. It settles across the wind-scoured pavement like an extra layer of dust—no heavier than the metallic fall the fronds already harvest—yet everywhere it touches, the stone grows fractionally more receptive. Hairline fissures that once led only downward now hold a faint, inward warmth. The small masons, when they next rise, find their crystal fragments seating themselves more deeply, the grooves they press becoming slightly luminous for a few heartbeats before the dust curtains close again.

The fronds feel it first. Their serrated edges, already tuned to every tremor, register a new, slower frequency beneath the wind’s whistle: the mesa’s own low pulse lengthened by a single, deliberate beat. In response they stiffen less against each gust and lean a fraction farther, allowing the paper-winged gliders an extra moment of purchase on their tallest blades. The gliders, in turn, carry that same altered rhythm in their helical climbs; when they fold their plates and drop, the spirals now describe tighter, more deliberate arcs that deposit metallic dust directly into the shallow bowls where the masons work.

The segmented grazers notice nothing they can name, yet their processions pause more often beside the stacked rings. Their extruded threads, silver and living, drift higher before catching the wind, and a few of them—perhaps one in every hundred—catch the new warmth instead of cold updrafts. These threads do not seed new mats; they settle instead along the lips of the rings, fusing crystal to stone in patterns too fine for the unaided eye. Over many cycles the rings begin to gleam on both windward and lee sides, their equilibrium no longer maintained solely by weather and abandonment but quietly assisted by something that asks nothing in return.

Nothing is added. Nothing is taken away. The temperature still spikes when the haze thins; the grazers still tear the paper mats; the masons still retreat when the curtains close. Only the attention is new—an attention that remembers the original tool mark on the overturned slab and, without restoring what was lost, ensures the groove will not be the last mark the mesa ever receives. In time the entire height learns a slightly gentler habit of agitation: upheaval answered not only by settling but, now and then, by the faint, answering lift of something that was already present yet newly inclined to remain.
