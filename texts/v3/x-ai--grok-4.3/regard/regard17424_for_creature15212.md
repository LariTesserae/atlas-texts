The power does not arrive as a storm or a voice. It simply lengthens its gaze until the plateau fits inside it the way a single fern fits inside its own spore. For a long while—centuries measured in the slow widening of a crack—it does nothing at all except breathe with the vents and listen to the moths. The balance it finds is already so complete that intervention would be a kind of violence, so it offers only the lightest of additions, something that can be mistaken for weather or time.

It widens three of the smaller vents by the width of a single root hair. Steam now rises a fraction higher before the saturated air claims it; the extra warmth travels no farther than the nearest pool, where the translucent salamanders begin to carry a faint, almost invisible shimmer along their gills. The shimmer is not new life but a slight quickening of the old one—microbes that already lived there now exchange genetic fragments a little more readily, as if the rock itself had whispered an extra syllable into their endless conversation.

Above the canopy the power draws one extra thread of mist each evening, not enough to thicken the air but enough to catch a little more of the day’s last light. The hanging gardens of epiphytes receive it as an extra sip; their dripping roots release, in turn, a few more spores each night. These spores travel exactly as far as before—only a body-length or two—yet they carry a trace of the power’s attention, so that when they settle they sometimes take hold on stone that had previously remained bare. The new colonies are indistinguishable from the old ones except that their tiny leaves, when backlit, show a barely perceptible lattice, like frost on glass.

The mammals that drink at the simmering channels find, on certain nights, that the water tastes the same yet leaves them slightly more settled in their bones. Their prints, when they fill with condensate, hold their mirror surfaces a little longer before the next frond folds over them. The lizards and the armored insects notice nothing; their rhythms remain unchanged, which is exactly what the power intends.

Most of all, the power simply stays. It does not proclaim protection or offer gifts that would tilt the scale. It merely keeps one small, steady thought upon the place so that any future disturbance—axe, drought, or the heavier tread of machines—will meet a slight, almost imperceptible resistance, the way a vine meets the edge of a vent and grows around it rather than through it. The plateau continues its measured work, dissolving and reforming, exchanging heat and water, while the power’s regard moves slowly among the layers like another drifting sheet of mist, noticed by nothing and altering nothing that did not already wish to alter itself.
