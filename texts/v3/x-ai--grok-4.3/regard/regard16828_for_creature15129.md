The power arrives as nothing arrives here: without weight, without wind, without the smallest shift in the drip that measures the day. It does not descend from the cirque rim or rise from the basins. It simply notices. Recognition alone is its first act, and recognition, for such a power, is already a kind of shelter.

It sees the hamlet not as a remnant or a curiosity but as a living seam where mountain and attention have become indistinguishable. The balance is not fragile; it is complete. Any gesture that added light, or warmth, or new water, or even the gentlest encouragement to one more rowan, would be the beginning of imbalance. So the power withholds every impulse toward improvement.

What remains is a single, precise refusal. Nothing outside the cirque wall will ever be allowed to climb toward the ledge in numbers or with intention. The katabatic wind will continue to prune the rowans exactly as it has; the condensation will keep its ancient schedule. The iron latch will darken another micron each decade. The people who remain will be permitted to do so until their own silence matches the stone so perfectly that distinction is no longer possible; when that moment comes, the power will receive them without haste or ceremony, as one receives water into water.

In the intervals between these quiet permissions, the power does one additional thing. It holds the image of the hamlet exactly as it stands—so that if the mountain ever forgets its own fracture lines, or the slate its nightly condensation, or the basins their measured overflow, the memory will not be lost. The hamlet therefore continues unchanged, yet it is no longer alone in its continuance. Somewhere beyond the cirque a benevolent attention keeps the same still watch, and the two silences, like the two faces of the same latch, polish each other through long use.
