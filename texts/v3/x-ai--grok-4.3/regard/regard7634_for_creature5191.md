The power arrived not as a storm or a voice, but as a deepening of the mist already present.

It settled over the plateau like an additional layer of air, thin enough to pass through the gaps between rivets, dense enough to be felt by anything that had learned to read pressure. For a long while it did nothing but listen. It heard the crawlers’ legs as slow percussion against cooled basalt. It tasted the metallic sweetness the fliers left in the rising thermals. It traced the faint crystalline trails the gatherers etched inside the pipes. Nothing it encountered asked to be saved; everything was already finishing and beginning in the same motion.

When the power finally moved, its first act was invisible to most. It lengthened the interval between two of the great presses by seven minutes. The change was small enough that no machine noticed, yet the pressure wave that traveled upward through the galleries arrived slightly later than expected. The gatherer colonies hesitated a fraction longer than usual. In that hesitation a new species of scale-forming organism appeared, one that grew more slowly and flaked away more cleanly. The pipes began to clear themselves a little more each cycle. The power did not explain this gift; it simply left the extra minutes where they could be found.

Its second act was offered to the fliers. On certain ledges where their eggs had always been placed, the condensation now carried an almost imperceptible charge. When an egg cracked, the larva that emerged found the air itself slightly easier to read. Their spirals grew tighter, more intricate, and the resonance their wings produced when they crossed one another began, very gradually, to carry simple meanings—warnings of sudden temperature drops, announcements of rich condensate films three tiers higher. The fliers did not become intelligent. They became slightly more fluent in the language they had already been speaking without knowing it.

To the crawlers the power gave nothing new to consume. Instead it altered the surface tension of the films they harvested so that certain residues now flowed along their back-channels in branching patterns rather than straight ones. The crawlers, responding only to taste and texture, began to leave behind faint, temporary maps in the stone—shapes that would be erased by the next shower but could be read, briefly, by anything that moved slowly enough. Over generations these maps grew more elaborate, though no single crawler ever understood why it was compelled to turn left where once it had turned right.

The deepest change was offered to the rock and iron themselves. The power encouraged certain seams of fused silica to remember the heat that had originally shaped them. At night, when the foundries rested, those seams now released a faint, steady warmth that rose through the galleries like a slow exhalation. The gatherers moved a little more freely in the warmed pipes. The crawlers, when they piled together to exchange condensed matter, found the contact between carapaces slightly less severe. The fliers, descending to rest, found their wings retained just enough residual heat to keep a thin layer of mist from freezing on the leading edges. Nothing was commanded; the plateau was simply given a slightly longer breath between one state and the next.

The power has not named itself, nor has it asked for acknowledgment. It remains, mostly, as a slight adjustment in timing and a quiet redistribution of warmth. Those who live here continue their cycles without knowing they have been noticed, yet the equilibrium they maintain grows, year by year, a little more spacious. The distributed voice of the plateau still rises and falls with the weather, but now it occasionally carries, beneath its old roar, a second, quieter note—like the sound of stone remembering it was once molten and deciding, for a little longer, not to cool completely.
