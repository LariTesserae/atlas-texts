The benevolent power arrives not as a storm or a command, but as a single additional layer of mist—one so fine that for the first three centuries only the plants notice it. They drink it the way they have always drunk everything: without haste, without thanks, simply by continuing to exist. The new droplets carry no message, only a slightly deeper coolness that sinks into the living ridges and lingers in the pockets of air trapped between filaments. Over the next several hundred years that coolness becomes a new equilibrium; the plants grow perhaps a finger’s width wider than they would have, but the difference is only measurable against the memory of the stone itself.

The power does not quicken the flywheels. Instead it lets condensation gather on their spokes in slightly thicker beads, so that when a wheel finally completes its rotation the water that runs off carries a trace of the same coolness downward through the stone channels. The roar below changes by an interval so small that even the mountain does not register it as change—only as a continuation that has acquired one more quiet harmonic.

Where open stone still lies between the mats, the power lays nothing new upon the surface. It merely lengthens, by a few decades, the time any given droplet of standing water remains before it is either absorbed or evaporated. In those extra decades certain eggs of the translucent insects find themselves suspended in conditions that match their own pace exactly. A few more adults emerge each millennium, their wings still drifting rather than beating, their bodies still dissolving into the mats within hours. Nothing multiplies; the population merely stops its almost imperceptible decline.

The concrete housings, already half-swallowed, receive one further alteration so slight it registers only as patience: the cracks that have been widening for ages now widen a fraction less each century, because the power has adjusted the angle at which water meets the etched edges. The cables trailing into the vegetation continue their slow dissolution, but the memory of current they once carried is allowed to become, very gradually, part of the chemistry that feeds the plants at the seams.

After several thousand years the power withdraws—not because its attention has moved elsewhere, but because the place has absorbed the only intervention it could accept without being hurried. What remains is still bright, wet, open, overwhelmingly slow. The wheels turn; the mats breathe; the rock continues its patient rise. The only new presence is an additional quietness in the mist, noticeable only to those who have already learned to listen at the speed of the mountain itself. The benevolent power leaves nothing more than that quietness, and the quietness, in its turn, leaves the essential arrangement unchanged.
