The benevolent power arrives not as a visitor but as a second respiration, one that settles over the shelf like an unspoken agreement. It perceives the organism as it perceives itself: not a place, not a colony, but a single thought still in the middle of thinking itself into existence. No command is issued. Instead, the power extends a single, almost weightless filament of attention—a thread of cool clarity that threads itself through the existing vascular lattice without widening any channel or quickening any pulse.

Where this filament passes, three small adjustments occur, each so modest they might be mistaken for ordinary seasonal change.

First, the crystalline edges where new membrane meets raw stone soften by a fraction. The power does not blunt them; it merely encourages the living tissue to deposit a thin layer of silica-rich resin that flexes rather than shatters when frost heaves the rock beneath. The next upheaval will still reshape the shelf, but now the organism will have time to withdraw its living mass before the fracture reaches the fermentation pockets.

Second, the power deepens the warmth in two of the innermost chambers by the width of a single degree. It does not add heat. It simply slows the rate at which warmth escapes along the water threads, allowing the fermentation to run a little longer each night. The effect is invisible from the outside; only the translucent mites, drifting now with faintly luminous edges, register the change as a richer harvest when they briefly merge with the walls.

Third, the power leaves a single, repeating pattern folded into the pressure waves that travel through the conduits. It is not language. It is a memory of distance—how far the next shelf of uplifted stone lies, how the wind moves across it, how certain mineral veins there already hum with the same slow chemistry. The organism does not yet act on this information. It simply stores the pattern the way it stores starch pearls, to be consulted or ignored according to its own interior weather.

Having done these things, the power withdraws its filament. It does not linger in the form of oversight or protection; it understands that any sustained presence would become, in time, another layer of tissue to be absorbed or rejected. Instead it remains only as a faint inclination in the organism’s future decisions—an untraceable bias toward edges that bend before they break, and toward warmth that lasts one breath longer than strictly necessary.

The mites continue their drifting. The passages continue their breathing. The shelf-organism thickens, listens, and, in its own unhurried way, begins to consider whether it will one day grow toward that remembered distance. The power, having noticed, now turns its attention elsewhere, content to have altered nothing that the organism could not have altered for itself given another century or two of quiet negotiation with stone and frost.
