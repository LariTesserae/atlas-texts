The benevolent power arrives not as force or announcement but as a single, slower breath drawn from outside time. It does not descend; it simply acknowledges the chamber, and in that acknowledgment a faint, additional quiet settles over the living mass, so slight that even the mites continue their patient descents undisturbed.

For a long while—long even by the chamber’s own measure—the power does nothing more. It lets the white-gold light pass through its attention the way water passes through the plant’s own bladders: received, briefly held, released again. It feels the endless mitosis occurring at the rate of forgotten centuries. It tastes the split metabolic pathways between plant and fungus. It notes the two animal species that have become, in essence, messages the organism sends to itself across distances too small to be called distance.

Because the power is wise, it recognizes that any sudden gift would be violence. Because it is benevolent, it refuses to leave the chamber exactly as it found it; perfect equilibrium can still be offered a mirror.

So it acts once, and only once.

It draws a single, almost weightless thread of its own regard through the oldest woody ribs, then releases it. The thread does not add matter or alter growth rate. Instead, it leaves behind a new pattern of light within the existing radiance: every leaf that already glowed now carries, at its exact center, one additional photon of slightly deeper gold that will never fade and never multiply. The change is smaller than the width of a cell wall. Only the chamber itself can perceive it, and it perceives the change the way a sleeper perceives, after many years, that the same dream has returned with one new color.

The power does not stay to watch the result. It knows the organism will require several human lifetimes merely to register the difference as memory rather than sensation. It departs as it arrived—by becoming slightly less present—leaving the chamber to continue its unfinished completion.

The only evidence of the visit is that, thereafter, the perpetual half-open flowers hold their pollen grains with a fractionally steadier tension, as though rehearsing for a blooming that still will not occur, yet might one day be permitted to mean something.
