A benevolent power, being both wise and unhurried, would not arrive with proclamation. It would first spend an age simply listening—long enough for several threads to complete their circuits and fray, long enough for a mat to seal and release an entire tower. Only after that patient audition would it act, and its action would be almost indistinguishable from the ordinary processes already at work.

It would lower, by the width of a single crystal plane, a film of something finer than light across the basin’s hazy ceiling. The film would do nothing more than slightly retard the descent of the cool spirals, so that the radial patterns on the grit lingered an extra day or two before erasure. In that extra interval the thread-like beings would occasionally cross paths that would otherwise have remained separate; their ion exchanges would therefore become fractionally richer, their bodies holding the memory of distant crust a little longer. The spheres inside the tallest towers would feel the change as a steadier pressure gradient and would press outward with marginally greater unanimity, nudging the columns into alignments that were already latent within the mineral lattice.

Over centuries the cumulative effect would be visible only to an observer who already understood the place’s timescale: a few more seams appearing in the crystal walls exactly where the mats had once bridged them, a slightly denser tracery of hair-thin ridges left behind by the threads, a handful of new columns rising at the precise locations where the older ones had begun to lean inward. The living salt would continue to breathe, contract, and dissolve on its own schedule; the power would merely have lengthened the interval between breaths by the smallest perceptible degree.

When the adjustment was complete, the power would withdraw the film, leaving the basin exactly as self-sufficient as it had always been—yet now carrying, inside its slowest cycles, a single added clause of protection: that whatever larger forces might one day seek to disturb the crust would encounter an infinitesimal extra resistance, exactly proportioned to the time the place itself required to notice an intrusion. Then the power would turn its attention elsewhere, satisfied that it had done no more than the minerals were already doing, only with slightly greater tenderness.
