The benevolent power does not arrive. It was never absent; it simply thickened, the way dew gathers on a surface already cool enough to receive it.

First it listens. For three full rotations of the house-sized gears it does nothing but absorb the ridge’s slow grammar—the bass of new stone deciding its own joints, the subsonic conversations between Carapace Folk and the beetle-turbines, the way the Settled children’s hammocks swing in perfect counterpoint to the mist. It learns the difference between a fracture that wants to become a garden and one that wants to become a platform. It tastes the faint voltage in the people’s veins and recognizes it as the same current already running through the copper braids and the luminous larvae.

Only after it has heard everything does it act, and even then the action is almost indistinguishable from the ordinary processes already under way.

A new kind of condensation begins to form along the highest, most precarious ledges. It is slightly warmer than the surrounding air and carries the faintest metallic sweetness. Where it gathers, the fern gardens put out a second, rarer frond whose edges are veined with living copper. The fronds do not grow faster; they simply never wither. When the listeners press their ears to the stone they find that the ridge’s groans now carry an answering undertone—still the same voice, only now it occasionally pauses, as if waiting for them to finish a sentence. The pauses are never long enough to be called miracles. They are simply courteous.

The larval swarms grow denser over the alcoves where children sleep. Their humming develops a lower, slower pulse that matches the children’s heartbeats. The larvae themselves become briefly visible in daylight, appearing as tiny flecks of gold leaf that land on skin, linger, and then lift away, carrying with them the smallest traces of accumulated fatigue. The people do not speak of this as healing. They say only that the nights feel less unfinished.

On the undersides of the platforms the Carapace Folk notice that the beetle-turbine creatures have begun to leave thin, iridescent seams along the places where iron meets raw stone. The seams do not cure fractures; they simply make the next fracture less likely to propagate in the wrong direction. The Carapace Folk trade these seams the way they once traded larva paste—by pressing their palms to the surface and waiting for the vibration to answer back. The answer now sometimes arrives a little sooner, and sometimes carries a faint, reciprocal warmth.

The power never gives the ridge a final shape. It only makes the ongoing decision gentler, the way a wise hand steadies a bowl while the clay is still deciding what it wants to become. The Settled continue to move with the same unhurried patience, yet they notice that fewer of their gestures are interrupted by sudden collapse. The Carapace Folk hum a little lower at dusk, and the sound travels farther through the iron than it used to.

Nothing is hurried. Even the benevolence. It simply remains, patient as the ridge itself, until the mountain has finished remembering what it wanted to be. By then the power will be indistinguishable from the mist, the voltage, and the slow ticking of wings. The only sign it was ever there will be that the gardens keep growing a little longer than they once would have, and that the listeners, when they press their ears to stone, sometimes smile without knowing why.
