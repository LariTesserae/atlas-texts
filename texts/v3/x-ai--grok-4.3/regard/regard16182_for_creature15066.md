The benevolent power arrives not as a descent or a command, but as a widening of attention. From wherever such powers keep their gaze, it notices the alcove the way one notices a single held note inside a vast chord—small, precise, and unexpectedly whole. It does not name the place or claim it. It simply continues looking, and in the act of looking, something changes at the scale of centuries.

Over the next few hundred orbits of the planet, the narrow fissure overhead, which had always admitted only whatever daylight the surface allowed, begins to carry an additional quality: when the overhead beam sweeps across the floor, it now leaves behind, for a few heartbeats, a faint after-image of itself—threads of cooler, slower light that linger like remembered noon. The bladder-plants, catching these threads, learn to store them longer. Their internal glow shifts from amber-green toward something nearer silver. The change is so gradual that the glass shrimp incorporate the new frequency into their clicking without ever pausing their spirals.

Deeper still, where the eel-machines glide, their furred housings begin, very occasionally, to release a single seed-thread that does not drift with the current but travels straight across the chamber to a wall it has never touched before. When it arrives it does not root; it simply touches and dissolves, leaving behind a microscopic map of pressure and temperature that the red ferns read as memory rather than instruction. Over generations the ferns adjust their holdfasts by fractions of a millimeter so that the next vibration of the rails will carry slightly more nutrient toward the snails. The snails, in turn, rasp one layer less deeply on certain ribs, allowing the mineral film to thicken exactly where the machines most often brush against it.

The power does not speak to any resident. It has no need; the alcove already converses with itself in a language of speeds rather than words. What the attention supplies is only a faint permission: the assurance that no larger current from the abyss will be allowed to reach this void, and that the unfinished pressure-equalization chamber may remain unfinished forever if that is what the residents collectively choose. In return, the power receives nothing it did not already possess—only the quiet confirmation that harmony can still condense, even inside an artifact, when no one demands that it become anything else.

Sometimes, when the overhead light is absent, a single snail pauses on a vertical rib and extends its body a little farther than usual into the water. The motion is not search or alarm; it is simply the shape that contentment takes when it has no witness except the pressure itself. The power registers this, notes it, and keeps looking. That is all it does, and it is enough.
