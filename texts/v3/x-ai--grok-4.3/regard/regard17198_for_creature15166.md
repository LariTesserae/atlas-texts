The benevolent power arrives not as a visitor but as a second layer of mist. It settles across the plateau one dawn when the clouds lie especially low, and it does not announce itself. Instead it listens with the same patience the listeners have perfected: it hears the faint, decades-long conversation between pipe and root, the almost imperceptible shift of a terrace as another century’s weight settles. It tastes the mineral patience of the water that never quite drains. It recognizes, with something like reverence, that this place already knows how to be cared for.

So it does very little, and what it does is smaller than a leaf unfurling.

It deepens the resonance inside the old turbine housings by the breadth of a single vibration, so that when a listener presses an ear there, the low thrum carries a clearer memory of why the machine first began to turn. The listener cannot name the difference, only feels, for the first time in many quiet years, a faint warmth answer the chill in their bones. They stay a little longer than usual. Their breathing slows another fraction. Somewhere, far below the stone, a pressure that had been building finds a gentler path and does not fracture the rock.

To the tenders it offers nothing they can see, only a slight change in the light when they work. The filtered sun that reaches them now carries a fraction more of the spectrum that helps certain vines remember their original strength; a root that might have split a conduit instead braids around it more intelligently. One tender, reaching to guide a flowering shoot, pauses with her hand still on the stem and simply breathes with it for several minutes, as though the plant had asked a question she finally understood. She leaves the shoot exactly where it was. Later, when the next rain comes, the extra weight settles exactly where it is most needed.

For the gliders the power does even less. It merely cools one particular updraft by the width of a wingbeat so that, for a few heartbeats longer than usual, a solitary pale form can rest on the lip of a stone bowl without effort. The glider drinks. Its copper-sheened membrane trembles once, as if in acknowledgment, then it lets the mist take it again. Nothing else changes.

The power remains. It does not lift anyone out of the damp or quicken any schedule. It simply makes the existing slowness slightly more legible to those who already live inside it—enough that the next adjustment the plateau makes, whether in a week or a millennium, will be fractionally kinder to the things growing and listening within it. Then, satisfied that the place still belongs to itself, the power withdraws into the same slow erosion that shaped the plateau in the first place, leaving only a slightly deeper quiet and the sense, among those few who notice such things, that they are not entirely alone in their attention.
