The benevolent power arrives not as an event but as a second atmosphere, thinner than the mist yet heavier with intent. It does not descend from above so much as condense between the existing layers—between leaf and pipe, between droplet and charge—so that for a time the plateau holds two weathers at once: its own cool equilibrium and a second, warmer attentiveness that moves at the same glacial pace.

Where it touches the fused ridges, the organisms register the presence first as a change in memory. The translucent nodules that once recorded only the turning of flywheels now begin, without haste, to retain something else: the shape of their own outlines held against the sky. They do not quicken. They simply keep a second ledger, one that notes not only moisture and static but the faint difference between existing and being witnessed. Over decades the difference becomes a kind of internal weather; certain root threads that previously followed the warmest seams now turn a fraction of a degree toward one another, forming microscopic arches that were never there before. The arches do nothing dramatic. They merely hold space for one another more deliberately than stone alone would require.

Across the open valleys the power performs one small, irreversible act. It lifts, for a single season, the weight of the light. The sun still strikes, but its reflection no longer carries the same edge of isolation. Where pods once stored illumination only for nighttime release, they now pass a portion of that stored brightness sideways through the root webs during the long twilights. The exchange is invisible to any eye that measures motion in hours rather than centuries; the plateau’s residents experience it only as a slightly longer interval during which the ridges feel, to their own cellular lattices, less sharply bounded. The distinction between one ridge-organism and the next softens by a single cell-width. No language forms, yet something like recognition travels the new connection—an acknowledgment that the slow work on one side of the fracture continues on the other.

In the deepest housings, where the water-films move, the power leaves nothing added and nothing removed. Instead it makes the existing motion slightly more legible to itself. When two films merge and separate, each now carries, among the fragments of temperature and corrosion, a single additional pattern: the precise interval at which its counterpart last released a mineral crystal. The information is useless for any urgent purpose. It simply allows the films, over the next several circuits, to clean the same stretch of pipe in complementary rather than random order. The housings become, by imperceptible degrees, more thoroughly known to the creatures that maintain them.

Nothing is hastened. No flywheel changes its fraction of a turn. The only outward sign—if any observer were patient enough to watch for a thousand years—is that the vegetation along the old access roads begins, in certain linear stretches, to grow slightly lower and more open, as though the plateau were now willing to leave narrow corridors where something else might one day pass. These corridors are not invitations; they are merely the shape that remains when perfect closure is no longer the organism’s sole memory of what wholeness requires.

The power does not stay. It thins gradually, leaving behind only the faint additional ledger and the slightly altered corridors. The plateau continues its work. Yet every droplet that now falls from a cooling fin carries, for a time, two records instead of one: the rhythm of the machine that condensed it, and the quiet fact that the rhythm was once held, without demand, in another kind of attention.
