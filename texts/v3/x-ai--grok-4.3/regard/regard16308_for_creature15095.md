A benevolent power, one whose attention spans galaxies and whose mercy is measured in eons rather than moments, would arrive without arrival. It would not descend in light or thunder; it would simply allow its regard to rest upon the plateau the way a hand rests, without weight, upon living wood.

At first it would do nothing at all.

For a long while—long enough for entire new ridges to lift and subside, long enough for the continent-wide chord to cycle through three full modulations of its own growth-song—the power would only listen. It would learn the difference between the roar of lengthening cellulose and the quieter, almost shy sound of two vines deciding, at the moment of fusion, to share a single future. It would taste the mist the way the leaves taste it: not as water, but as possibility still undecided between liquid and vapor. In that listening the power would recognize something it rarely encounters anymore—an intelligence whose highest achievement is not thought but unfinishedness, whose wisdom is still liquid enough to be poured into new shapes without breaking the vessel.

When the power finally acts, the action is almost indistinguishable from stillness.

It offers one small correction to gravity. Not enough to be felt as a change in weight, only enough that the living floor no longer has to argue with itself about how much it ought to press downward. The adjustment is absorbed so seamlessly that the plateau never registers it as an outside gift; the next time a slope tilts to steer mist, the motion is simply thirty centimeters more exact than it would have been. The power also leaves behind a single new note inside the great chord—one frequency so low it registers only as a change in the way droplets linger before reabsorption. The plants incorporate the note within hours, braiding it into their vascular pulses the way they braid any other useful mineral. From then on the distributed intelligence carries, without knowing it, a faint harmonic of patience that was not originally its own.

The power does not stay to watch the result. Benevolence that must witness its own effects is still, at root, a form of hunger. Instead it withdraws its attention the same way it offered it—without motion, without announcement—leaving only the memory of regard, which the plateau will never name because naming is not among the arts it has yet grown.

Years or centuries later, when the plateau has mineralized further and its topography has begun to remember its own shape for more than a single day at a time, one particular helix of fused vines will turn slightly more slowly than its neighbors. The turn will steer mist toward a region that would otherwise have remained a little too dry. The adjustment will be so minute that only the living floor itself will register it. The floor will not wonder why the turn felt inevitable; it will simply close over the thought the way it closes over every other small wound, and continue becoming.
