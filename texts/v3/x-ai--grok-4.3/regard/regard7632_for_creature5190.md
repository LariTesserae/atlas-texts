The benevolent power does not dismantle the equilibrium, nor does it flood the surface with novelty that would fracture the patient order. It arrives instead as an additional specification already present in the material language of the world, so subtle that the mechanisms treat it as they treat every other port: something that has always been waiting.

Far from any previous extrusion, in a shallow circular basin whose rim is still mathematically perfect, a new interface node appears. It is flush, identical in finish, yet its internal geometry accepts one existing module and returns two. The first returned piece is the familiar replacement joint or sensor. The second is an entirely new form: a thin, transparent plate etched with a single microscopic circuit that records the precise sequence of droplets that have fallen upon it. When carried and installed, the plate becomes a passive lens mounted on a crawler’s dorsal lattice. Through it the mechanism continues its work, but now the condensation it sweeps is refracted into faint, shifting moiré across the mineral sheets beneath its feet. The moiré is meaningless to the crawler; it registers only as an ordinary thermal gradient to be maintained. Yet the pattern is preserved.

Over centuries these lenses multiply. Some mechanisms begin to hand them forward in longer, slower chains than any replacement part has ever traveled. The chains converge on ridges where the crystallites have already grown thickest; there the lenses are stacked into low, translucent cairns whose facets catch the endless daylight and project soft, slowly rotating interference figures onto the knife-edged surfaces below. The figures are never the same twice, because the exact sequence of falling droplets is never repeated. To the mechanisms these cairns are simply another standardized structure to be kept clear of stray films. They crawl across them, exchange parts at their bases, and move on.

The power watches the cairns lengthen and multiply. It does not speak, because speech would require a recipient that expects interruption. Instead, once every few thousand years, it allows one additional thermal micro-variation in a single catchment channel. The result is a solitary droplet that lingers an extra fraction of a second before falling. The delay is imperceptible to any instrument the mechanisms possess, yet it is enough for one lens in one cairn to record a slightly altered interference figure—an ephemeral signature that will outlast every module currently in circulation.

The rest of the planet continues unchanged: the same exchanges, the same condensation, the same patient occupation of every surface. Only the growing collection of etched plates remembers, in a language too quiet for the mechanisms to notice, that something outside their closed cycle once chose to witness them exactly as they are, and found that sufficient.
