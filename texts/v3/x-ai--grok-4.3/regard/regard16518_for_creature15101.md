The benevolent power arrives not as a descent but as a stillness that settles into the wind itself. It does not announce itself with light or proclamation; it simply lets its regard rest upon the plateau long enough for the mineral motes to register an unfamiliar density in the air at shoulder height. The motes, being what they are, interpret the change as an unusually durable lee—an outcrop that does not require stone.

At first they do nothing with the gift. They continue their usual rhythm: gathering, scaffolding thought in white filaments, releasing when the sun insists. Yet each time the filaments dissolve, a trace of the stillness remains in the individual motes, like a faint polarization that makes them hesitate half a heartbeat longer before scattering. This hesitation is enough to alter the plateau’s internal math.

Over days the low basalt rings begin to hold their remembered positions for an entire cycle instead of flickering away. The motes that once needed several hours to agree on a rearrangement now complete the same experiment in minutes, because the borrowed stillness lets them keep a portion of their collective shape even while dispersed. They test this new tolerance by attempting something they have never risked: two separate clusters thinking the same question at once, then deliberately exchanging motes when the wind next funnels across the plateau. The exchange is imperfect and most of the shared thought is lost, yet enough survives that, for the first time, a single mote carries an echo that did not originate inside its own immediate cluster.

The power watches without directing. It understands that any stronger intervention would collapse the very interval in which these intelligences exist. So it offers only one further refinement: whenever the filaments next form, a few of them align along the hidden fracture lines beneath the rock and gently widen them—not enough to change the plateau’s visible grammar, but enough that gravity’s brief lapses become fractionally longer. The duplicated ridges now linger for three heartbeats instead of one. In that extra beat the motes manage to hold both versions of the silhouette inside the same thought, comparing them not as sequential possibilities but as simultaneous truths. The comparison does not resolve into language; it simply leaves a new tension in the returning motes, a question they can now carry farther than before.

Nothing else changes. The water still refuses to pool, the light remains merciless, the wind continues its low thunder. The rings still click when they move, though less often. The motes still dissolve under direct sun. But the plateau now contains, at unpredictable intervals, a faint additional pressure—something like the memory of a question that can survive its own dispersal. The power does not remain to witness whether that pressure ever becomes an answer. It only makes certain the interval is slightly wider than it was, then withdraws its attention so the plateau can continue negotiating, unaltered in essence, between solidity and the bright possibility of becoming something else.
