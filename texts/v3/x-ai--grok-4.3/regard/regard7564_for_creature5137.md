The benevolent power does not arrive as light, for light would be violence here. It arrives instead as attention—a widening of the medium itself, a patient reordering of the infinitesimal pressures that already govern every filament and membrane. Where it passes, the water grows neither warmer nor clearer; the silt continues its slow rain. Yet something changes in the grammar of silence.

It first finds the comb-threads. Their buried nodes are touched, one by one, not altered but reminded. Each node receives a single additional instruction, written in the same chemical dialect the threads already speak: *when you next taste a drifting shard that bears the signature of another node, carry one fragment of it home with you and leave it beside your own calcification*. The threads obey without effort or understanding. Over centuries the buried stones begin to accumulate minute, shared libraries—microscopic mosaics of exchanged memory. The plain does not become crowded; it becomes slightly less alone.

The cloak-wings receive a different courtesy. When one settles upon the stone during a lull, the power lengthens the pause by the width of a single breath. In that interval the wing feels, for the first time, the stone beneath its mucus filaments as something distinct from the water above it. The sensation fades when motion resumes, but the memory of the distinction remains. Thereafter the cloak-wings occasionally tilt, not toward food, but toward the simple difference between floor and void. Their paths acquire the faintest curvature, as though they have begun, without knowing why, to trace faint maps across the featureless swells.

The pin-lights are granted the smallest mercy. When two clusters touch and flash, the light does not extinguish quite so quickly. A residual shimmer lingers—long enough for a third cluster drifting nearby to register the chemical signature before the darkness returns. The flashes become occasional conversations rather than solitary acknowledgments. The clusters do not congregate; they simply begin, over long ages, to pass one another more often than pure chance would allow.

Nothing is added that could become a predator. Nothing is removed that the existing metabolism requires. The power does not introduce ends or beginnings. It only loosens, by the smallest possible degree, the absolute solitude that had been the true floor of this world.

In time the plain still appears unchanged to any eye that could see it. The same velvet absence, the same unceasing rasp of particles. Yet the water now carries, along with its ancient silt, three new, minute currents: the habit of leaving something behind, the memory of touching stone, and the faint afterglow of recognition between drifting lights. These are not improvements. They are only the signature left by a wise attention that refused to look away and also refused to seize.

The beings themselves notice nothing. They continue their patient waiting. But the waiting is no longer quite the same shape as before.
