The benevolent power arrives not as an intrusion but as a further deepening of what is already there. It settles over the hollow like an additional layer of still air, invisible and weightless, felt only in the way the constant light grows infinitesimally steadier and the mineral film left by evaporation acquires a faint, persistent luminescence that never quite fades.

It does not quicken anything. Instead it matches the existing tempo exactly, lingering across centuries in the same patient regard with which the beetles cross their lintels. Where a single salamander’s gill flutters once in a decade, the power offers an answering stillness—an infinitesimal reinforcement of joint and membrane—so that the rhythm continues unbroken. Over time this attention becomes indistinguishable from the wood’s own slow sealing of its seams. The ridges that cup the basin remain precisely as they are; any distant tremor that might have reached them is simply never permitted to arrive.

The power learns the names of every surface without speaking them. It recognizes the single organism that is the settlement, and the smaller lives nested within its continuity, and it grants them nothing they have not already given themselves. Only one subtle addition occurs: the condensation that returns each night carries, for those who can taste such things, a trace of perfect memory. The vines remember every notch ever cut into them; the moss remembers every drop that has ever rested upon it; the beetles, in their glacial processions, occasionally catch in their faceted eyes a brief reflection of what they themselves will look like once their crossings are complete. These reflections are never new information, only confirmation.

In this manner the power remains. It neither builds nor repairs, intervenes nor withdraws. Its presence is simply the recognition that the equilibrium already contains every grace it is capable of bestowing. The hollow continues exactly as it has always been, now held not only by its own balance but by the quiet knowledge that its balance has been seen, understood, and left untouched. The light shaft keeps its unchanging angle. The mist keeps its schedule. Every creature and surface remains suspended in the single, fully occupied moment—watched over by a regard so complete that it requires no further act.
