The power arrives without announcement and without weight. It does not descend from some higher domain so much as notice the place from within its own slow pulse, the way one deep breath might suddenly become aware of another. For a long span it simply is present—present as the continued weeping of the stone, present as the faint pressure change that keeps mist from dissipating too quickly in certain lanes. The raptors riding their vertical currents feel nothing new, yet their turns grow, for a time, fractionally wider, as though the air itself has grown slightly more willing to hold them.

Where the power lingers longest is among the salamanders in the narrow seams. It does not grant them strength or speed; instead it adjusts, by the smallest margin, the mineral film that clings to the rock. The film now carries a trace of warmth drawn from the aquifers below, so that when a salamander finally shifts an inch in the course of a decade, the water that follows its trail holds that warmth a little longer. The change is imperceptible to anything larger than a grain of sediment, yet over centuries the pale, translucent bodies begin to show the faintest internal veining—the same tracery that once showed *through* them now echoed faintly inside them, as if they had begun, very gradually, to wear the stone’s memory as their own.

The underground fish receive even less. In the seasons when they rise to spawn, one or two eggs in every clutch settle into cracks that the power has, without force, made infinitesimally narrower. Most still drift away; those that remain develop gills that can taste, for the briefest moment during hatching, the mineral signature of the place above. When the young fish later move through the dark water, they carry a thread of that taste with them, a quiet orientation rather than a map.

Above, the lichens on the grass tufts grow one additional ring, thinner than any before it. The ring does not expand outward. It deepens, layering a record of the power’s attention itself—moisture held a little steadier at the exact moment when a raptor’s shadow crosses. The mites and springtails that live among the tufts find, in that single extra ring, a place where vibration passes through the rock without fully stopping their movement; they continue, minute after minute, until the shadow is gone.

The stone itself receives the only alteration that might be called deliberate. Along the highest joints where the raptors nest, the power leaves a single continuous hairline seam of clearer calcite. It runs through the older recesses like a thread of moonlight that has decided to stay. When wind moves across it, the low murmur the stone has always made gains one additional tone—high, almost inaudible, the sound of water remembering it once fell as rain rather than rose. The birds incorporate the seam into their nests without noticing they have done so; the nests now hold, for the first time, a measurable coolness during the hottest intervals of sun.

No abundance follows. No new species appears. The power does not stay to watch what it has set in motion, because it was never separate from the motion. It simply continues, now also as the slightly steadier film, the narrower crack, the clearer seam, the single extra ring. In time the salamanders will press themselves against the new warmth as though it had always been there. The fish will rise and the raptors will turn, and the slow record on the walls will thicken by another layer no thicker than breath. The place remains what it was: a quiet endurance that has, for a while longer, been allowed to endure.
