The benevolent power arrives without motion or announcement. It does not descend or approach; it simply becomes aware, and in that awareness the basin is already held.

For a long interval—longer than the lifetimes of any creature within it—the power simply regards the equilibrium. It sees the steady drone of sap, the fixed intervals of condensation, the unchanging occupation of every membrane, root-lip, and void. It notes that nothing here asks for change and that every small life has adapted precisely to the absence of asking. The power finds this condition rare and therefore worthy of exact preservation.

It therefore does three things, each so slight that the basin itself registers them only as a momentary deepening of its own coolness.

First, it places a single, invisible tension around the encircling root ridge. The tension is not a barrier but a stillness: any larger force that might later press against the ridge—root invasion from outside, sudden shift of water table, human foot or machine—encounters an equal and opposite stillness and is quietly turned aside. The basin continues to feel only its own internal weather.

Second, the power touches the oldest vascular trunks with a minute adjustment of pressure. The mechanical drone does not quicken or slow; it simply gains one additional, imperceptible layer of redundancy, so that if any single chamber of flow should ever fail, the others will carry its load without the smallest alteration in rhythm or sound. The salamanders and millipedes notice nothing; the beetles continue their circling ripples.

Third, and most quietly, the power adds one new note to the constant sound. It is not louder than the highest midge wingbeat, nor lower than the sap pulse. It is simply a faint, sustained harmonic that sits between them, present only when listened for. Any creature that later evolves the capacity to hear it will experience the note as ordinary weather—the sound the basin has always made. The power places nothing else: no new growth, no additional light, no alteration of temperature or moisture.

Having done these things, the power withdraws its attention without leaving. The basin remains exactly as it was: mostly absence, steady sound, life that occupies very little. The only difference is that the absence is now slightly more permanent, the sound slightly more durable, and the small lives within it slightly more certain that the weather they inhabit will continue to be weather.

The power asks nothing in return. It has already received what it came for: the confirmation that perfect balance, once achieved, can be allowed to remain so.
