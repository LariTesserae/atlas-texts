A benevolent power, vast and patient as the turning of deep roots beneath mountains, turns its awareness toward the plateau. It does not arrive as storm or decree. It arrives as a single, imperceptible adjustment in the way light lingers on a leaf after rain.

First it listens.

For many slow cycles it does nothing except absorb the plateau’s music: the resin engines, the expanding xylem, the soft tearing of folded space, the measured breathing of the Pruners inside their living towers. It tastes the milky sap they drink and feels how it lengthens their lives by threading their cells more tightly with the cambium around them. It watches the translucent slugs cross the plazas and notes that their slow grazing keeps certain over-eager algae from choking the sap channels. Everything here already tends toward equilibrium; the power respects that.

Only when it notices the reality folds does it act, and even then its intervention is smaller than a pruning cut.

Along each old rail line it lays down a thin, invisible membrane of its own substance—neither plant nor machine nor spirit, but a kind of living boundary layer. When a fold begins to open, the membrane simply encourages the surrounding stalks to lean a fraction closer together, sealing the wound with a lattice of new woody ribs before the tear can fully form. The Pruners, pressing their ears to the trunks, will one day notice that the tearing sound grows rarer and that the ozone-and-chlorophyll scent no longer lingers afterward. They will mark the change with quiet gratitude and continue their rounds, never knowing an outside intelligence answered their long habit of leaving disturbances to the plateau’s own slow healing.

To the Pruners themselves the power offers one modest gift: it lengthens the life of every pruned surface they drink from. The milky fluid now carries, in its sugars, a minute record of the plateau’s own memories—those pressure-changes the Pruners already sense in dreams. When they sleep inside the hollow trunks they will occasionally wake with a new clarity: they will know, without being told, exactly where a new conduit is about to bud three days hence, or which rail joint will drift out of true in the coming season. Their pruning becomes even more precise, almost tender. The ridges on their joints darken to the color of heartwood; some begin to dream while awake, their eyes half-lidded, palms pressed to living walls, conducting entire conversations in the language of rising sap.

The great slugs receive nothing directly, yet benefit all the same. Because the folds are mended, the light that reaches the plazas grows slightly steadier. The algae the slugs graze upon becomes marginally more luminous, tracing faint, shifting constellations across their translucent backs at dusk. The Pruners, watching these new patterns, sometimes interpret them as messages and leave different offerings—small rings of cut thorn placed in spiral arrangements the slugs glide over without disturbing.

The power stays.

It does not speak, does not command, does not claim the plateau as its own. It simply continues to lean, very gently, against the places where the world is thinnest, so the living city can keep growing in the direction it has already chosen. In time the plateau may notice the change as a slight deepening of its own inner weather: the engines run a little cooler, the condensation lingers a little longer on the highest stalks before falling, and the dreams shared between Pruners and wood become, on rare nights, mutual. The power asks nothing in return. It is content to be another quiet rhythm inside an organism that has already agreed, without treaty, to refine itself endlessly.
