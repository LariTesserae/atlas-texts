The benevolent power arrives not as a storm or a light from above but as a single, patient thought that settles into the water like a heavier mineral. It perceives the slope at once: not as a habitat that needs rescue, but as an argument that has already convinced itself of its own rightness. The argument is this—nothing may rest, therefore nothing may be lost entirely.

It does not still the surges. That would be a cruelty dressed as mercy. Instead it loosens one small law. Wherever the old iron has begun to shed flakes of rust that poison the worms and isopods, the metal is granted a final, precise dissolution: each particle is coaxed into becoming a lattice inside the nearest sea-whip’s holdfast, so the poison becomes scaffolding rather than toxin. The hulls that still thump internally are allowed one last collapse, but the collapse is shaped so that the falling bulkheads create new chambers whose openings align with the prevailing currents. The anemones inside find their pulses suddenly synchronized with a larger rhythm; they prosper, and the eels that follow them inherit slightly longer refuges.

A second, quieter adjustment: the slate-green light is given a faint complementary wavelength at the precise depth where the silver-black fish flash their warnings. The new tint carries no warmth, only legibility. Schools that once fragmented now reform a fraction faster; the gain is small, measured in heartbeats per surge, yet over centuries it means certain bloodlines continue that would otherwise have thinned to nothing.

Mostly, the power refrains. It does not gift the kelp immunity to being flung. It does not seal the canyons against the mud clouds. It understands that the commonwealth’s charter is written in the constant threat of burial, and to annul that threat would be to dissolve the charter. So it offers only a single ongoing gesture: every decade or so, when a particular hanging valley threatens to silt over completely, a slow upwelling is permitted—just enough to lift the finest particles back into the water column for another few body-lengths of travel. The amphipods and translucent worms receive this as an unpredictable but reliable second harvest. They never know the source. They only know the slope remains, for them, marginally livable.

The power stays long enough to feel one full cycle of the largest tidal surge roll the entire canopy sideways and then release it. In that instant of violent consent, it recognizes itself. Then it withdraws, leaving the water unchanged in every measurable way except that the iron now nourishes instead of kills, the fish turn a shade more visible to one another, and the canyons occasionally remember how to breathe. The place continues exactly as it was—ancient, violent, and still arriving at its own conclusions—only slightly more legible to the things that must live inside it.
