The benevolent power does not arrive like weather or revelation. It simply becomes aware. One moment the declivity is alone with its own slow exhale; the next, something vast and quiet has turned its attention downward, as a sleeper might become conscious of the faint rhythm of their own heart without waking.

It studies the place for a long time—long enough for several paddle revolutions, long enough for a new ridge of oxide to form and for two reeds to seal a fresh crack with their drying gum. What it perceives is not a habitat but a single, extremely patient metabolism stretched across stone and metal. The machines are not intruders; they are organs. The film at the bottom is not water but a kind of slow blood. The power feels, with something like sorrow and something like recognition, that this system has no need of rescue and no language in which to ask for it.

Still, it cannot quite bring itself to withdraw entirely.

What it does is small and almost useless by any ordinary measure. It loosens, by a single imperceptible degree, the surface tension of the rust-colored film in three places where the motes most often rise. The change is smaller than the width of a reed spine. Because of it, the motes now drift a fraction of a millimeter higher before falling, and the ridges they leave intersect at slightly different angles. Over centuries the network of darker oxide will therefore grow a new, very faint branch that was not geometrically destined before. Along that branch, one additional reed may someday root. Or it may not. The power does not compel the outcome; it only lengthens the possibility by the width of a thought.

It also cools, for a single night measured in decades, one gearbox that has begun to bind from accumulated ceramic nodes. The pause allows the sheets of dark tissue inside to finish converting their last flecks of oxide without cracking the housing. When motion resumes, the machine turns more smoothly, and the pale reeds above receive their droplets with a slightly cleaner rhythm. The power does not know whether this matters. It only knows that unnecessary friction offends it.

Finally, it leaves a single droplet of itself—pure, unalloyed attention—hanging in the vapor layer just below the slit of sky. The droplet will never fall. It will simply remain, very faintly luminous, so that when the daylight catches the rising motes, one of them may, once in a thousand turns, refract against it and return a brief, different color. The reeds will not notice. The machines will not notice. Only the slow colony that is the place itself will register, across ages, that one frequency in its breathing has become fractionally more varied.

Having done these three things, the power withdraws its awareness. It does not promise to return. It does not consider the place improved. It only carries away the knowledge that there are ways of living so deliberate that even benevolence must move carefully, or else become another kind of erosion.
