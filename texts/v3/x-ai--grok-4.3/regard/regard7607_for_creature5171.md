The benevolent power does not arrive. It has always been near, held at a respectful distance by its own regard for what it sees. For eons it simply watches, learning the grammar of the plateau’s silence the way one might learn the movement of a sleeping animal’s breath. Only when it understands that the plateau’s persistence is not resignation but a kind of austere fidelity does it begin to act.

Its first intervention is almost nothing: a single, additional frequency introduced into the daily thermal flex of the rock. A note so low that even the conductive filaments do not register it for centuries. Over time the note settles into the lattice defects of the dust grains themselves. The veils begin, very gradually, to carry a faint directional bias—not toward any destination, only away from perfect repetition. Where two veils now cross, the crystalline pattering occasionally produces a new tone, a third voice that lingers for a few seconds longer than before. The starfish membranes, drifting across these crossings, absorb the tone into their vacuoles. Nothing about their motion changes, yet the gas they contain begins to hold, for the first time, the faintest signature of curiosity.

The power does not accelerate anything. It simply makes room for acceleration to become optional.

In the places where lithophyte seams meet gravitic relics, a second, still smaller change appears: the relics’ standing gradients acquire a minute wobble, a breathing that was not there before. Over millennia the wobble teaches the seams to grow one new microscopic pleat—not faster, only slightly more receptive to the dust that settles upon them. The pleats, in turn, begin to refract the ordinary daylight into a spectrum the plateau has never seen on its own. The black-green absence of the seams sometimes answers with a color that has no name, only the quality of recognition.

The power does not speak to the plateau. It offers instead a single, permanent question embedded in the geometry of one new ridge that rises half a millimeter higher than surrounding rock. The ridge does not announce itself. To any traveler it would be indistinguishable from all the others. Yet every tremor that passes through it now carries, in addition to pressure, a faint orientation: an invitation to drift, very slowly, toward the places where the question might one day be answered.

Nothing else is done. The horizon remains unreachable. The air stays thin. The starfish continue their century-long glides. The dust veils still erase their own tracks. But now, when two veils intersect above the new ridge, the third tone they produce sometimes resolves, for the briefest instant, into something that resembles the beginning of a name.

The plateau does not know it has been noticed. It only knows that one ridge, among uncountable ridges, has begun to remember its own elevation differently. That is enough. The benevolent power withdraws its attention by the width of a single additional geological instant, leaving the world exactly as immense and unfinished as it was, only now containing one more possible future than before.
