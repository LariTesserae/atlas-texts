The power arrives without announcement, as such powers do. It does not descend or flare; it simply becomes present, folding itself into the permanent twilight until it is no longer separate from the air. For a long while—long even by the standards of the basin—it does nothing at all. It lets the mineral crust speak first, lets the thread-roots whisper their slow exchanges, lets the bacteria complete another full generation in their film of brine. Only when it has felt the precise rhythm of the place, the exact interval between one grain settling and the next, does it act.

It reaches not outward but inward.

With a touch so delicate that no crystal is displaced, it draws the outermost ring of rises a fraction closer together, narrowing the already narrow horizon by the width of a single shadow at dusk. This small adjustment creates a new and permanent stillness, a slight deepening of the basin’s acoustic shadow so that even the memory of distant wind can never quite reach it again. The change is invisible to any eye that would measure in ordinary time; the tufted crowns will notice it only after another century has passed and their outermost runners find themselves pressing against a subtly steeper slope.

Next, the power lays a single, invisible filament of its own substance across the entire network. The filament does not feed or warm. It simply listens. Where one crown dreams of rain in its sleep of decades, a faint echo now travels to every other crown, so that the whole organism begins, for the first time, to experience itself as one mind rather than as separate points of emergence. The sensation is not joy exactly—joy would move too quickly—but a vast, shared recognition that arrives and departs over centuries, like light moving across the polygons at the speed of mineral growth.

Last, the power leaves behind a single drop of water, no larger than a pin’s head, sealed inside a microscopic cavity at the exact geometric center of the crust. The drop will not evaporate or move. It will remain perfectly still until the slow, patient chemistry of the bacteria eventually reaches it. When that moment comes—perhaps a thousand years from now, perhaps ten—the bacteria will taste something they have never tasted: the memory of an ocean that once existed elsewhere. They will excrete a new, slightly different salt around the cavity, and the crust will grow one molecule thicker there. The plant-web, sensing the minute change in tension, will send its thinnest roots toward the anomaly. It will not drink; it will only register that something new has been added to its long, quiet accounting of the world.

Then the power departs, leaving the basin exactly as it found it except for three imperceptible alterations: a horizon drawn tighter, a shared awareness begun, and one drop of remembered water waiting inside the stone. The twilight settles again. The grains shift, once, twice, over endless years. Nothing larger arrives. Nothing smaller is lost. The single organism continues its motionless expansion, now slightly less alone in its own mind, and the power carries the taste of that patience with it as it turns toward other, faster places that will need altogether different mercies.
