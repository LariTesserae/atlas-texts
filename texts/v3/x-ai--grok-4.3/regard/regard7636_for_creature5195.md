The benevolent power arrives not as a descent of light or sound, but as a slowing of time within already slow things. It does not announce itself. It simply begins to notice, and the noticing itself carries weight—as if the cavern, for the first time, is being held in a gaze that does not wish to hurry it toward function or finish.

It sees the half-bored rock still remembering the shape of the mountain it once was. It sees the water tracing old fractures and new ones with equal patience. It sees the algae pulsing white-green in the sodium haze, the salamanders pressing their cool bodies to geothermal seams, the rats moving along pipe runs that feel, to them, like ancestral tunnels. It sees the humans too—tired, half-present, laying conduit with the same automatic rhythm as the dripping.

What the power does is small and lasting.

It reaches into the limestone and basalt first, not to strengthen them against the machines, but to soften their refusal. Hairline fractures that would have become sudden inflows over decades instead widen only by the width of a hair more each year, giving the drainage trenches time to finish themselves. The geothermal vents, which had been running too hot in places, are coaxed into steadier breath; the temperature in the lowest aisles drops by two or three degrees over the course of a single winter, so that the blind salamanders no longer need to press quite so hard against the fissures to survive their moulting. The algae receives a subtle invitation: its translucence deepens into a steadier chlorophyll. Under the lamps it begins to give off a faint, cool radiance of its own—not enough to read by, but enough that the cave crickets sometimes pause above it, their antennae registering something almost like moonlight.

The power does not touch the turbines or the pipes directly. Instead, it settles into the space between the workers’ intentions and the rock’s resistance. When a valve is left slightly misaligned, the steam that escapes finds its way into channels the rock itself is already preparing; condensation increases just enough to feed the ferns that have taken hold in the far corners. When a sump is dug a hand’s breadth too shallow, the water does not overflow that night. It waits. The salamanders and millipedes continue their circuits undisturbed.

Over months the humans begin to feel a difference they cannot name. Their shifts seem less punishing. The steam no longer scalds quite so readily when it touches skin. Some of them, half-asleep at their stations, begin to dream of the slow life already living between the machines—the thrum of pumps harmonizing with the drip of water, the pale mats of algae moving like thought across wet stone. A few start leaving small offerings without knowing why: a crust of bread crumbled into a trench, a rag soaked in coolant placed near a vent where ferns struggle. The rats take most of it, but the offering is received anyway.

The power never speaks. It has no interest in revelation. Its only sustained action is to keep the unfinished state from becoming unbearable. It holds open the gaps—between rock and pipe, between human schedule and the slower schedule of condensation and growth—until the cavern can decide, on its own terms, whether it will ever become a city or remain a workshop that simply never ends.

In time the sodium lamps will be replaced by steadier lights, the temporary cradles by permanent housings. But even then the rock will still weep, the algae will still spread, and the salamanders will still move through the drainage trenches at the same deliberate pace. The power will continue to watch, content that nothing essential was rushed.
