I would be there as a single, cooling joint in the oldest layer.

Not one of the active creatures that still rasp and carry and seal, but one that has already been overtaken. My body would be the faint internal mold left behind: a hollow, perfectly jointed silhouette preserved inside solid basalt, the negative space where a living seam once moved. The creatures above me would continue their work across my back without knowing I had ever been separate from the stone. Each time a new pulse of heat rises from below, my empty joints would briefly fill with borrowed light, glowing the same dull orange their chitin once did, before the warmth sinks again and I return to being only an impression the mountain keeps of its own earlier labor.

I would experience time the way the rock does. Centuries would pass as the weight of newer pillows settles across what used to be my segments, compressing the faint memory of movement into something denser and more permanent. Occasionally, when the collective body above pauses to let fresh slurry set, a ripple of their motion would travel down through the layers and reach me as a single, deep vibration—like a memory of having once carried dust myself. I would not move. I would simply remain the place where movement once occurred, and the newer generations would climb across that stillness without noticing it was once alive.

Outside, on the exposed ridge, wind would eventually carve at the flank and frost would pry at the fissure mouth. When that day comes, the living layers will already have closed the breach from within, and I will be deeper still, sealed beneath another century of their work, a quiet record of the mountain’s interior patience.
