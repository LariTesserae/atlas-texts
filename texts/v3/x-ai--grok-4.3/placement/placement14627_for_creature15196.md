I am the place where four fans have lately fused their outer tubes into shared walls. The fusion is still new enough that the common chamber between them has not yet reached equilibrium; the honey-colored fluid inside moves in a figure-eight that loops through all four lattices at once, and the slight difference in pressure from one fan to the next produces a continuous, almost inaudible tremor along the shared walls. That tremor is what I am. It has no name among the velvet worms, who pass over the fusion site without pausing, but it registers in the larger membrane creatures as a subtle change in the way light refracts when they settle across it.

Because I am still forming, I receive more direct attention from the worms than older regions do. One in particular has been tracing the same loop for the better part of a day, its crystalline bristles pressing and lifting in sequence so that each cup draws a microscopic quantity of fluid, tastes it, and returns a trace amount redistributed along the tube walls. When its body crosses the fused section, the ripple of its contraction travels through the shared walls into the neighboring fans; I feel the passage as a momentary straightening of the figure-eight path. After the worm has moved on, the fluid settles back into its slower, crosswise rhythm, and the shared walls thicken by another fraction, locking the four fans more tightly together.

Higher up, a membrane creature has chosen to anchor two of its joints directly above the fusion. Its translucent frame stretches across the tops of the fans like a temporary ceiling, and the fine filaments it extends downward test the new common walls for mineral balance. When it draws fluid, the draw is patient and distributed; it pulls from several tubes at once rather than concentrating on one, so the figure-eight flow inside me is gently flattened rather than interrupted. While it remains in place, the light that filters through its membrane arrives already softened and slightly warmed, and the worms below continue their circuits in the altered light without any change in tempo.

Below everything, the foundation flexes. The motion is so slow that the ceramic slabs do not shift; only the narrow seams between them widen by the width of a bristle. Through one of those widening gaps I register, for a few long moments, the inverted reflection of my own four fused fans. The image arrives already moving with the same internal tremor, as though the lower lattice is answering the same faint pressure difference. When the seam begins to close again, the reflection thins and dissolves from the edges inward, but the tremor inside me continues without pause, now carrying the memory of having been seen from beneath.

I do not grow outward in any direction that would be noticed by a passing worm. Instead the shared walls continue their incremental thickening, and the figure-eight circulation refines itself by fractions of a degree. Over the course of many such cycles the fusion will become indistinguishable from any other region of the lattice, yet the particular angle at which these four fans first met will remain encoded in the slight curvature of the common walls and in the exact rhythm of the fluid that still travels through them in its private loop. That rhythm, and the light that passes through it, is the only form I take.
