I would find myself first as one of the narrow stone terraces stepped into the slope, worn to a pale curve by feet that have passed for longer than memory. The moss has already claimed my edges, soft and velvety, turning my straight lines into something rounded and forgiving. Water slides over me after rain and sinks away through the root mats beneath, leaving only the faint mineral taste of dissolved stone. From this position I watch the lanes fill and empty, children’s vine-sashes tightening and loosening as they climb, elders pausing with palms flat against living walls.

After a time I notice I am also the water that condenses each night on the undersides of the broad leaves overhead. I gather in beads, grow heavy, and fall in quiet intervals into the channels, sometimes missing and striking a shoulder or the back of a hand. When I reach the stone basins I am tasted, then carried away again in cupped palms or in the quick-furred climbers that come at dusk. I run through the hamlet’s single slow pulse—leaf to channel to skin to root—and return skyward only when the warmest vents open and the cicadas fall briefly silent.

Eventually I am both of these things and another: a young person whose hair has been braided with bark strips that have begun to put out pale rootlets against the scalp. My hands carry the same curving calluses the elders wear. When a lattice sags I reach without thinking for the exact place to splice a fresh runner, and the vine accepts the graft the way a limb accepts a new tendon. At dusk I descend with the climbers to drink, leaving resinous prints that the beetles later patrol and polish away. My voice stays low, blending with the drip and the overlapping insect drone until it is no longer possible to tell which sound answers which.

In this way I belong to the ridge without ever having arrived: cool, damp, and never quite finished, a single metabolism among many.
