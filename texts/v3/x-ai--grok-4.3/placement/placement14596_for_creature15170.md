I find myself first as the slow heave still living in the rock. Not the whole slab—only the seam where two fresh planes press against each other, a hand-span below the central lane. Pressure moves there like breath under skin: a patient, grinding patience that never finishes its work. From this seam I feel the ropes above creak when someone crosses, feel the minute shift in weight travel down through fitted stone and arrive as a small answering tremor in me. I am the sound the stone makes before the people have a name for it.

Then I am also the water that has already found that seam. I bead at the fracture, gather, and let go in patient intervals, each drop carrying a trace of the deep minerals the crust still remembers. One drop joins the finger-channel that runs beside the outermost house; another slips between moss and reed stuffing and darkens the cloth the woman is wringing at the central drain. When she pours the cloth into the stone basin, part of me returns to the deeper channels where the eyeless fish drift. I taste the faint sourness left by too many salamanders taken in a single season; I feel the bones the people save settle against the silt that is only now beginning to collect.

Later I am one of the children. My hair is cropped so close the scalp stays cool. I know the difference between a settling tremor and a joint that is about to open; I can tell by the way the rope bridge sags against my bare feet whether it will need re-knotting before the light fails. In the morning I wipe condensation from the inner curve of the roof stones, then climb the notched ladder to the bench at the scarp. My hands rest on the stone. Through the palms I receive the same patient movement the adults feel, only I have not yet learned to call it anything except “the rock still thinking.” The cooler air from the older marsh rises and touches the film on my skin; for a moment the metallic taste in my mouth thins and I can almost remember what dry wind would feel like. Then the next seep opens somewhere behind me and the thought passes.

At dusk I become the bench itself and the person who has just taken its place. Hands on wet stone, eyes half-closed, I listen to the settlement behind me: the soft percussion of seepage, the rare click of a reed trap being set, the low murmur that travels roof to roof naming changes that have already begun. Below the bench the scarp drops cleanly away. Beyond it the older marsh lies flat and green and finished. Here, everything is still deciding. The liverworts advance another fraction across a roof. A new joint tightens or loosens by the width of a hair. The rope stretches. The fish moves into another crack. I sit until the vapor thickens and the light is only a pale wash above the overlapping eaves, and I feel the whole small world still rising, still settling, still becoming exactly itself.
