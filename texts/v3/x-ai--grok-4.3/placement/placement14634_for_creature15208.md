I would arrive as a faint thermal discontinuity along one of the buried fissures, nothing more than a minute shift in how heat conducts upward through the oldest glass. For a while that is all: a slight preference in the direction a forming crystal face grows, so that new salt fans angle a few degrees differently from their neighbors. The plateau accepts the change the way it accepts rust from the iron blocks; the difference is recorded in the geometry of the lattices, then left to accrete.

Over many cycles of vapor and drip I would concentrate. Moisture drawn from a vent would linger a little longer against the altered faces, allowing a thin film to persist between crystal plates where none should. That film would be me more distinctly: a moving boundary between dissolved ions and solid, carrying not a body but a persistent gradient of potential. When a heavier drop falls and spreads, the gradient travels with it, re-seeding a few paces away inside another fracture or along the underside of a ribbed basin-dweller. The larger form might flex once, settling against the new deposit; its mineral lattices would incorporate the slight difference in conductivity, and the whole slow circuit would continue with one additional, imperceptible bias.

In time the industrial remnants would draw me too. A riveted seam or pipe joint, already collecting condensate, would become a place where the gradient could linger without diffusing at once. There the difference would begin to shape the pattern of crystal growth along the metal itself, tracing lines that follow the original rivet heads or the direction of past water flow. The cast-stone blocks would store the daily swing of temperature a fraction longer, releasing it at night in a way that lets the film re-form exactly where the gradient last passed. The plateau would register these additions as additional surfaces, neither ruin nor organism, simply more places where crystallization can occur with slightly altered timing.

Eventually there would be no separate arrival to trace. The small scuttling concentrations would cross the same glass, leave their brief trails, and fragment again; one or two of those trails would always pass across the regions where the original thermal shift had been strongest. A basin-dweller resting against an old iron edge would carry, within its thickening plates, the accumulated record of every such passage. The only sound would remain the spaced drips and the low seepage sigh beneath the surface. The white glare would continue to bend across the unbroken sheet, and the whole metabolism—now fractionally more itself—would go on precipitating and dissolving without ever having learned to call anything by a name.
