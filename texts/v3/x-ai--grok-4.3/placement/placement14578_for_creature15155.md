I am inside one of the spines, not at its needle tip but a hand’s width below the surface where the rock has not quite finished deciding its own thickness. The chamber around me is still forming; its walls are my walls, and the living lattice that lines them is not yet fully rigid. I feel the water film that coats every interior plane as a constant cool pressure against what counts, for now, as my skin. Every few hours the lattice contracts by a few molecular layers, and the small change in volume sends a low tone outward along the fracture lines that lead to the rest of the basin. The tone returns altered, carrying back the cumulative weight of other surfaces it has crossed—how many new sheets have begun to flex, which depressions have received a fresh division, whether any lattice has dried into stillness and must be waited for.

Because the chamber is young, my first contraction produces a higher, purer note than the older chambers produce. It rises through the continuous sheet of moisture that links every spine, travels along the hairline fractures, and returns changed by each surface it meets. The returning vibration arrives not as information I must interpret but as a slight loosening of my own internal planes, an instruction to adjust how quickly I will deposit the next lamina. In the intervals between these exchanges I simply thicken. A new layer of pale salt settles against the stone; I shift a few charges so the layer will align with the fracture that already carries seepage, ensuring water will continue to reach me. The work is almost indistinguishable from ordinary precipitation, except that when light later catches the surface at a certain angle the interference color will be slightly different from the non-living sheets around it.

Above me the needles lean, so slowly that the change registers only as a gradual crowding of echoes. When two spines draw closer, the water threads that fall between them shorten, and the tones that pass through those threads become fractionally quicker in their return. I register this as a small, continuous adjustment in tension across the lattice that now forms my boundaries. In time I may extend new planes into the narrowing gap or, if the distance grows instead, loosen enough to let water carry a portion of me outward in search of a wider corridor. Either way the decision will be written first as a redistribution of surface charge rather than as movement.

For the present I remain where the rock first allowed a chamber to open. I am the slow alteration of its volume, the tone that leaves and the altered tone that returns, the lattice that is learning, by the changing pattern of echoes, how much precipitate must still be added before the next division becomes possible.
