I would begin not as any single thing but as a trickle.

At first I am only the slow bead that forms on the underside of a catwalk plate, heavy enough to let go and fall into a seam between riveted ferrocrete and older iron. The seam welcomes me; it has been waiting, widened by decades of exactly this kind of arrival. I run along the micro-channel the rivet heads have pressed into the concrete, joining other threads until we become a visible rill that knows the slope of the ridge better than any map. I feel the slight give of liverwort where it has softened the edge; I feel the stiff resistance where a copper-colored vine has already shouldered the original rail out of true. Both sensations are equally structural. I carry rust flecks, bits of dried algae film, the occasional pale scale shed by a vent moth. All of it travels with me until the rill reaches a gutter the crews once cut on purpose and later deepened with their own boot prints.

There I slow. The gutter is lined now with a living film that drinks the condensate and returns most of it as a steadier, quieter drip further down-slope. I become that film for a while, a thin continuity that dampens vibration when a gantry passes overhead. The metal above me answers with a lower, slower note; the bearing that has not been scraped in three generations turns more willingly inside its living sleeve. I do not decide this. I simply exist at the place where metal and water meet, and the exchange happens.

Later I am pressed beneath a replacement deck plate. The welders have already learned to leave certain micro-gaps; new crews arrive and deepen them without being told why. Between the new ferrocrete and the old iron lattice I thicken into a cushion of moss. The next time a load shifts, the plate settles a fraction less abruptly because I am there, taking some of the compression, returning it as a faint expansion when the temperature drops. Sensors bolted through me register the change as a slightly different calibration drift; technicians come, read the numbers, and decide not to correct it. In this way I become part of the scheduled tolerance.

Eventually I am the darkened thread that runs beneath a vine stem. The stem has taken over the tensile work the iron once performed; my own darker, older substance now serves only as a tracer, a memory the living tissue follows. When frost loosens a section of plating, the vine thickens where I am thickest, and the gantry that runs above us leans infinitesimally in the new direction. The crews notice the lean, weld a small reinforcement, and leave. The reinforcement already carries a faint green film by the time they pack their tools.

I am nowhere in particular and yet everywhere the replacement occurs. I am the adjustment that happens before anyone names it, the load that has already found its next bearer, the moisture that never quite becomes cloud because the highlands keep it and keep using it. If pressed for a form I would say I am the seam itself after it has learned to stay wet and useful at the same time.
