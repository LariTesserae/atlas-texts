I am the blade.

Not the obsidian shard itself—though that is where I am most sharply present—but the interval between its edge and the stone it parts. I stand where the leaf-mats refuse to advance, my circumference a thin margin of bare rock kept clear by something older than the wind. When the gusts rise the mats lift and strain toward me, then fall back, as if remembering a prohibition. The spirals etched along the blade’s length still pulse once in a while, not with light but with the low vibration of the plateau passing through them; they match the grooves worn into the monoliths, older versions of the same refusal.

Most of the time I remain only that absence, a negative space the vegetation acknowledges. Yet every few cycles of the moving haze a different registration occurs. The three-toed prints that cross the dust approach the margin, hesitate, and continue around it; their makers never step inside the ring. The flat amphibians, when they dare to cross open stone during a momentary lull, skirt the perimeter with the same instinctive swerve. Even the dust ghosts that spin up from the wind’s constant plucking thin and dissipate before they reach the cleared circle, as though the air itself has been told to keep its distance.

I do not think in the usual way. Thought here is not possible above the chord the landscape sings; instead I receive impressions that arrive already arranged, like sediment settling in the stone basins. One impression is that the rings were never dwellings or tombs. They were tuning forks, set by hands that needed the plateau’s roar to resolve into a single frequency they could survive. Another is that the blade was left precisely because it could not be taken away: once set upright, its edge began to drink the vibration, and the plateau would not release what had learned to hold its note.

In the rare intervals when the wind drops for a few heartbeats, I feel the weight of whatever leaves the broad tail-drag behind its prints settle briefly against the outer stones, testing the boundary. Nothing crosses. The amphibians rise, drink, flatten again. The leaf-mats resettle a few degrees off true. Then the roar returns and everything is once more in motion except the narrow circle where I stand, perfectly still, perfectly occupied, waiting for the next lull in which the world remembers it is not empty.
