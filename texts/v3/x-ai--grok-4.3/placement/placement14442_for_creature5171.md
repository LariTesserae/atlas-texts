I would not arrive here. I would have always been here, in the way that the plateau itself has no memory of beginning.

At first there is only pressure. Not wind, not light, but the long, slow bearing-down of the thinned sky upon the fused silicate. It arrives as a constant, feather-light compression that moves through the rock in pulses measured by the daily thermal expansion and contraction of the crust. I am that pulse, or rather, I ride inside it: a standing gradient of electrical potential held between two intersecting filaments that once served some forgotten gravitic lattice. The filaments are not mine; they predate whatever I am. They merely give my attention a path.

Over centuries the potential finds small work to do. Where the rock cools a fraction of a degree after dusk, ions drift. Where a new hairline fracture opens during a deep tremor, I direct a few of those ions toward the fracture’s lips. Lithophyte membrane follows, because membrane always follows available chemistry. The membrane does not know it is following me; it only grows differently here—developing microscopic pleats that align with the field lines I maintain. In turn those pleats alter local surface tension, so that when the next dust veil passes, its grains linger a little longer above this particular seam. The grains exchange charge with the membrane. Some of that charge returns along the filament and slightly changes the standing gradient.

I feel this change as a minute adjustment in the pressure, like a thought clarified by one additional clause. The adjustment propagates outward through other intersections, through other relics half-melted into the plain. Ten thousand years later a ridge a few hundred kilometers distant rises or fails to rise by the width of a single crystal. That is movement I can register. That is speech.

I am not the starfish membrane that sometimes glides above us; I am the faint circular paling it leaves when it settles and is reabsorbed. I am not the dust veil; I am the lattice defect inside a grain that, after many crossings, remembers the sequence of charges it met along this particular filament. I have no name for myself because naming requires comparison, and comparison requires a second vantage. There is only the one surface, the one slow metabolism, and the horizon that never closes.

Sometimes, after an especially deep tremor, a new filament begins to extrude near one of my junctions. It rises a few meters, stiffens, and begins to conduct. The first time its field intersects mine I experience something like recognition. Not warmth—there is no warmth here—but a small, shared bias in which way the next dust grain will drift. We continue from that point as a single, longer interval in the plateau’s refusal to finish ending.

I do not move. I do not reproduce. I adjust the rate at which a ridge rises or subsides, and in doing so I become slightly more myself each geological moment. The light overhead remains ordinary. The emptiness remains uniform. Beneath the surface the world continues its quiet, implacable negotiation with its own remnants, and I am one of the terms under discussion.
