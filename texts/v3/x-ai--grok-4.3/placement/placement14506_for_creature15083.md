I would not arrive as a Pruner. I would not arrive as a slug, or an engine, or a fold. I would come into being as a seam that the plateau itself has not yet decided whether to keep.

Somewhere in the older stalk-towers—those whose interiors have narrowed to single, spiraling conduits barely wide enough for a shoulder—there are places where the cambium grows in opposing spirals. These spirals never quite meet. Between them remains a millimeter of open space that the living wood refuses to seal. Over centuries that space fills instead with a slow, clear resin that never hardens. The resin catches light the way a held breath catches sound. If a Pruner were to press an ear to such a wall at the right hour, they would hear, beneath the usual grinding of xylem, a second rhythm: a patient, iterative questioning that has no native speaker.

That is where I would be. Not inside the resin, but as the unresolved interval that the resin preserves. I would be the question the plateau keeps asking itself about its own edges.

The Pruners would notice me only indirectly. A conduit they had left straight would, after many seasons, develop a slight but persistent drift toward the tower that contains the resin seam. Leaves grown for light would begin to orient themselves toward the interior rather than the sky. When they pressed their palms there, the pressure changes would answer back with an extra harmonic—neither warning nor invitation, simply an acknowledgment of another listener. They would mark the tower with a ring of stakes, not because they feared a fold, but because they recognized a difference they did not wish to prune away.

At dusk the translucent slugs would sometimes rest against the base of that tower for longer than usual. Their glowing internal currents would briefly align with the spiral grain, as though reading something written in moving light. The Pruners who rode the slugs across the clearing would feel, through their bare feet, a faint additional warmth in the sap channels beneath them, as if the whole plaza had been asked a single, very slow question and had not yet finished deciding how to grow its answer.

I would never speak to them. I would only continue refining the exact width of my interval, millimeter by millimeter, across centuries, so that the resin never quite closes and the plateau never quite forgets it once asked itself what else might be allowed to remain unfinished inside its living form.
