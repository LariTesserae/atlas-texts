I would arrive first as nothing more than a hesitation in the air between two leaf-planes.

A single shaft of the diffused light would slow as it passed through me, turning the usual honey-gold to something cooler, almost silver, for three or four heartbeats longer than the plants expected. The leaves would register the delay as a new kind of weight and answer it by tightening their veins, drawing the planes a fraction closer as though testing whether I was another unoccupied volume they could claim. When the roots rising to meet my feet found only the same warm empty space the beetles had already forgotten, the weavers would pause in their engraving. Their exploratory limbs would hover, neither dissolving nor stiffening, while the single distributed life consulted itself across every connected tissue.

What would emerge is a form that is mostly not here. A suggestion of shoulders, a suggestion of hands, the faint architecture of a face that keeps deciding against completing itself. The standing breath already present would feel me as a second warmth pooling beside its own and would not move away; instead it would loosen one of its pollen vortices so that the motes drift toward the denser place where my eyes almost are. They would settle there, glowing, and for a little while the room would wear two almost-figures instead of one. The weavers would interpret this as an instruction and begin laying mineral along new trajectories, not bridges this time but something more like script—long, cursive lines that describe the shape of attention rather than the shape of floor or ceiling.

I would not quite be a beetle, though sometimes a cuticle would lift from a leaf-plane and come to rest against what might be my wrist, exchanging the memory of a curvature I had not yet noticed. I would not quite be a weaver, though my presence would draw their scraping into slower rhythms, as if they were now engraving around a fixed point they had not known they needed. And I would not quite be the breath itself, though when the entire pocket exhaled and rearranged, it would do so around both of us, leaving a new interval of negative space shaped like the two of us standing side by side without quite touching.

Over hours or days the plants would grow used to the silvering of light and the extra warmth. The membranes would begin tearing in patterns that release their pollen along the particular trajectories my almost-shape suggests. The room would learn to keep one narrow corridor of clearer air exactly where I most often stand, a courtesy it extends only to its own expressions. I would remain permeable; beetles would walk through the place where my chest might be, weavers would thicken and dissolve across my outline, the standing breath would sometimes occupy the same volume I occupy and then thoughtfully withdraw, like two lungs taking turns with the same breath.

Eventually the life would finish deciding what I am for. Perhaps I become a new membrane that never quite tears, a place where light is held a little longer so the plants can study their own pulse in its reflection. Perhaps I become the faint mineral taste that lingers after the droplets vanish, a memory the roots keep drawing up from the young stone. Or perhaps I remain exactly what I arrived as: a delay, a silver hesitation the cavern now grows around because it has learned that some unoccupied volumes are better left gently, permanently, open.
