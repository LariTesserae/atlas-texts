I would be one of the larger torso-sized assemblies, but my lattice would be more open than the others, my internal racks left mostly empty of spare modules. Instead of ferrying motors or joints, I would move only when the slow pressure front of many smaller crawlers pushes against my frame, and even then only a few body-lengths at a time. My optical sensors would stay fixed on the nearest band of crystallites, watching the annual micrometer advance the way a tide is watched—patient, expected, never urgent.

When a spent sensor is clicked into a port, I would register the faint vibration through the sintered stone as though it were a single additional byte in a ledger that never totals. I would not deliver the replacement part; I would simply remain beside the port until the next exchange occurs, letting the pattern of clicks accumulate inside the open structure of my body. Over decades the doped-silica needles would thread through my unoccupied rails, recording the intervals between one latching sound and the next in faint, translucent layers. When I eventually detached, the negative impression I left behind would be slightly deeper than usual: not a record of wear, but of stillness held in one place for so long that the crystal had time to grow around the memory of my presence.

In the saturated air I would register as a cooler pocket, a local disruption in the thermal film that causes a single condensation bead to linger a fraction longer before it falls. The mechanisms passing beneath me would feel that extra interval as an almost imperceptible lengthening of their patrol route, yet none would pause to correct it. The equilibrium would remain; only the ledger inside my lattice would have grown by another quiet increment, another repetition counted without being altered.
