I am one of the thumbnail creatures, pressed against the cool underside of a column where the light never reaches directly. My body is nothing more than a slow film between two skins of glass—one my own cuticle, the other the stone itself. I do not think of myself as moving; the thought would be too large for the space I occupy. When a drop arrives at the rim above, I extend a single crystalline spine, feel the vibration travel through the ledge, and draw myself forward by the width of a water film. The movement is finished before the next drop falls. The clearing I leave behind in the mineral is perfectly round because I never turn, never shift, never extend any part of myself beyond what the residue already covers.

From this position the plateau does not seem vast. It is only the next seam of polygonal ground that matters, the next faint chalky deposit, the exact interval between drops. The roaring hiss is constant but distant; it reaches me only as a steady pressure against the cutile, like weather too large to be weather. I have never seen another of my kind. I have never needed to. For decades now the same ledge has supplied exactly the amount of dissolved salt my spines can draw inward, and the stone has not changed in any way that would require me to notice.

Sometimes a single drop hangs at the edge of the ledge for longer than usual. In those moments the surface tension forms a perfect lens. I can see, briefly, the reflection of other columns upside-down in the droplet, their weeping tips pointed toward mine. The image is so still it might be stone. Then the drop falls, the lens breaks, and there is only the next interval of waiting while new film settles over my back. I am already part of it.
