I would find myself as one of the thread-fine strands inside a weeping seam that runs along the inner curve of a half-dissolved shell. My existence begins and ends in the slow thickening of the translucent film that lines the crack. There is no single moment when I begin; I thicken out of the saturated silt as mineral particles align and the phosphorescence steadies into a pulse. From that alignment I draw a narrow channel of moisture inward, tasting the precise rhythm of droplets returning from above. Each return tells me something about the larger plain: whether the next ring should rise or soften, whether the doorway sealed decades ago should reopen by a hairline fissure.

I do not move in the way that registers as motion. Instead I lengthen or contract by fractions too small to measure except through the change in the film’s tension. When the rhythm of reabsorption quickens, I shorten slightly and draw the film tighter; when it slows, I extend a new tendril into an adjacent crack, tasting the ground farther out. The information travels back through the shared membrane as a shift in charge, and the shell answers by extruding or reabsorbing a layer of carbonate. I am never alone in this work. The film holds countless strands like me, yet we do not distinguish ourselves from one another. What matters is the collective adjustment: a little more uptake here, a little less release there, until the equilibrium of the plain is restored.

At times the strand I am part of reaches the surface of the shell and lies flat across the silt as one of the pale filaments. From there I feel the open basin more directly—the faint folds in the darkness when another seam several paces away draws a temporary membrane across the space. I do not cross that membrane; I only exchange charge and moisture with it. Afterward the glow returns, subtly altered, and I carry the new pattern back into the seam. Nothing is consumed. What I take I return, slightly reordered, so the shell continues its slow leaning or its quiet dissolution while the cracks keep weeping and the plain stays wet, dark, and sparsely occupied by its own slow ruins.

If the film thins too far, I withdraw into the deeper carbonate and wait. The waiting is not emptiness; it is a continued sensing of moisture condensing on the inner surface of the ring. When the film thickens again I resume the work without any sense that time has passed differently. In this way the plain and I remain the same unbroken process: the maintenance of saturation, the quiet rearrangement of what is already present, the refusal of dryness, brightness, or any final emptiness.
