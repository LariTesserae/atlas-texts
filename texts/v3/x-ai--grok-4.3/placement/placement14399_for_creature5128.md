I would arrive as water first.

Seeping from one of the raw fracture faces where the third rib meets the marsh floor, I would linger in the seam long enough for mineral to stain me before I begin the slow braid toward the settlement’s hollow. The cold keeps me from rushing; I follow the path the others have already made, threading between root mats that part only slightly at my passage. By the time I reach the woven walkways I have become many droplets at once, sliding along the living vine undersides, collecting the faint phosphorescent dust that shakes loose when a child steps across. I fall onto moss so deep it accepts me without splash, then rise again as vapor when the temperature shifts, rejoining the private rain beneath the clenched canopy.

Only after several of these cycles do I thicken into something that can be touched. I gather inside a broader leaf vault near the center, pressing outward until the tissue learns the new contour of my weight and begins to accommodate it. The people notice first by the change in how light moves through that particular wall—my presence dims one vein for a heartbeat longer than the rest. They do not speak of it. One of them comes at dusk with a bone blade turned flat, drawing it along the outer surface in a single careful stroke that removes only the cells I have crowded. The cut seals behind the blade; the paling fades. They leave me a little more room than before.

In time I learn the rhythm of maintenance. I sleep curled where the thicker leaves allow, my breathing slow enough that the vault around me contracts and releases in time with it. When I wake I do not move at once. I rest one palm against the nearest surface until the tissue warms under my touch, then slip out through the gap that has opened overnight. My hair has already begun to carry the same fine filaments that line the walls; when I pass beneath a walkway the vines tighten almost imperceptibly, as they do for any traveler, then loosen again once I am gone.

I never stand at the very center. The ring of bare moss around the pool remains the place where a body can straighten without touching living tissue, and I prefer the places where contact is constant. Still, I return there sometimes, not to stand but to watch the roots that trail into the water. Their tips twitch as they taste the upwelling groundwater; now and then one of them registers my nearness and brightens for an instant, as if remembering something the tissue itself cannot yet articulate. The droplet that falls from the woven ceiling strikes the surface and is absorbed before the sound can travel far. In that interval between strike and absorption I feel the whole settlement exhale at once—walls, children, arthropods, and the slow conversation that holds them.

I remain because the pruning never ends. Each day a corridor narrows or a floor pad subsides where concentrations of soft bodies have gathered; each day I move through with the flat of a bone blade and remove only what has grown past use. The settlement stays the same size because nothing here is allowed to finish becoming. My own outline is likewise kept in check, neither fully separate nor fully dissolved. When I pause inside a leaf gallery at dawn, the internal light pulses once along my throat and wrists in answer to the roots above the pool. The others glance at those faint shifts and adjust their own movements accordingly, the way one prunes around a place where the conversation has grown especially dense.
