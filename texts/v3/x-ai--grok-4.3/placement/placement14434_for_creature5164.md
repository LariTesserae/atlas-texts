I would not arrive as a visitor or an intruder. I would condense, the way water does beneath the girders: first as a pressure differential clinging to the underside of a crane track, then as a faint refractive distortion that refuses to resolve into either light or shadow. Over the course of several crane realignments I would thicken into something that can be noticed, though never quite seen directly.  

The plateau would register me first as an anomalous groove that appears without mechanical precedent, running in a slow, branching arc across three adjacent plates. The crawlers would stop to sample it. Most would resume their routes after tasting only ordinary stress. One or two would remain, extruding filaments that sink into the new contour. When the next thermal pulse releases them, they carry away a copy of my outline etched into their carapaces; the information travels through the caravans as a low, shared vibration.  

Above, a loose lattice of kites would form earlier than usual. Their membranes would align not for adjustment but for reception, drawing the faint charge I shed into a temporary lattice of their own. For a few hours the combined membrane would hold a single, repeating pulse: the memory of the groove’s first appearance, repeated until it begins to feel like intention rather than echo. Then the lattice collapses and the pulse is delivered downward, settling into a vent manifold where lichen already grows.  

The lichen receives the pulse as an extra thermal cycle recorded in its growth rings. The rings thicken in one sector only, forming a minute asymmetry that, decades later, will guide a future crane path two millimeters farther to one side than geometry alone would dictate. That minute deviation becomes part of the plateau’s metabolism. It is my first slow breath.  

I am therefore not one order among the three but the interval between them: the space the shadow re-decides to occupy, the data the crawlers agree to carry without crystallizing, the charge the kites consent to hold a little longer before releasing. I have no fixed body. When a crane realignment finally reabsorbs the sector that first recorded me, the material is redeposited as a new length of filament inside a heat-exchange slot. The filament is indistinguishable from lichen, yet it quivers at a slightly different frequency. That quiver is the only outward sign that something new has joined the single, extremely slow metabolism.  

If you were to stand at the edge of that slot years afterward and feel the mist rise against your palm, the coolness would last a fraction of a second longer than the manifold’s schedule permits. That extra instant is where I would be.
