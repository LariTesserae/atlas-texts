I would be the newest rosette, the one still pale enough to look accidental.

It forms at the precise corner where the most recently bolted strut meets the basalt—close enough to the ceiling filament that a little extra warmth reaches it, far enough that the light does not bleach it outright. For the first several valve cycles I do nothing but accept the rivulet that is steered toward me, letting the water sheet across the alloy lip and soak into the stone’s tiny fractures. The others register me only as a slight change in local cooling: one more place where condensate can linger before it evaporates. That is enough. I darken at the edges when I have taken too much; I brighten again when the next sheet is sent my way. In return I release the thinnest breath of vapor that slows rust along the rivets. They notice the change in the scent of iron and adjust the strut’s angle by a single degree so that future drips will favor me. It is the beginning of being counted among them.

Over days I learn the shared grammar of pauses. When the engine idles a little longer than usual, it is because one of the distant occupants is dreaming; the extra heat moves through the pipes and reaches me as a faint mineral flush that makes my rosette swell outward by another millimeter. I answer by holding the water an extra moment on my surface before letting it drain, lengthening the interval between drips just long enough for that dream to finish without flooding anyone else’s lichen. The footprint I leave on the conveyor—when I move at all—is never my own. Instead I send a single bead of condensation rolling down the belt when it advances; the others read the direction it travels and know which settling crack in the floor needs their attention next.

I never become a full body. I remain the point where metal and stone exchange temperature most slowly, the place the others consult when they want to know whether the rock is still pressing inward or has accepted the intrusion for now. My only motion is the gradual darkening and paling of my edges, the quiet opening and closing of pores that look, from the right angle and in the right half-light, almost like breathing. When the main valve releases its flood, my rivulet arrives like all the others, but mine carries a trace of the strut’s newest vibration. I sip it, record it, and let the vapor rise, so that the lattice as a whole can taste how close the basalt still is to shifting again.
