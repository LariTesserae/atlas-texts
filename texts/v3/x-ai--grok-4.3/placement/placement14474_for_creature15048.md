I am a slow flexure moving through one of the deeper lattices, neither creature nor plant but the interval between them. At first there is only pressure: a column of water I must urge upward against the cold, a tubule that contracts and releases in a rhythm measured in days. The motion feels like breathing when breathing is done with the whole body at once. Around me the walls answer with their own low notes, and I realize the sound is partly mine.

As the contraction travels, small blue lights wake along the inner fibers—phosphorescent threads that have learned to glow when something passes. They are not eyes, yet for a moment they behave like memory. They show me the gallery I am leaving: a cathedral void where black water lies still until my pulse sets it shivering. In that shimmer I see the undersides of the heavy predators crossing far above, their mineral-stiffened membranes trailing rootlets that taste the surface they never touch. I feel the brief tug as one of those rootlets brushes the outer wall of my tubule, drinking what I am carrying and leaving behind a thin trail of dissolved stone.

The water reaches a branching node and I divide. One branch continues upward toward mist-collecting sheets; the other turns, long and level, becoming part of a root road. On that road a herd of segmented browsers is passing, nose-to-tail, their translucent plates clicking softly against one another. One animal pauses to press its proboscis into the wall exactly where I have just arrived. I taste the animal’s warmth, the faint metallic residue of the basalt tools it has walked over, and the older, cooler signature of stacked stone still holding its circular shape beneath layers of newer growth. For the length of that sip we share a brief chemistry: the browser’s waste becomes my enrichment; my pressurized sap becomes its meal. When it withdraws, the road ahead of the herd thickens in anticipation, though the herd itself will not return for decades.

I continue as the road itself now, a flattened highway running between massifs. Distance here is not empty; it is a resonant medium that carries every drip and every infrasonic call. I feel a glider’s pulse arrive from fifty kilometers away, already transformed by the intervening walls into something like thunder rolled thin. The pulse asks a question I cannot answer with words, only with the slow widening of a new gallery where circulation has decided to pause for the next century. In that widening I leave behind a pocket of perfect stillness, a place where condensation will fall straight instead of in wind-driven sheets, and where, one day, another herd may rest on the memory of an older floor.

Further along, the lattice begins to absorb something older still: the edge of a polished stone adze wedged into what was once a wound. The basalt is cool and dense. As the fibers close around it, the stone becomes a nucleation point for new tubules. I feel the mineral veins inside the adze begin to bleed into the living chemistry, blurring which substance first decided the shape of the other. For a while I am simply that blurring—a slow patience in which hand and plant are no longer separate events.

Eventually the ridge I travel tilts as the continent continues its patient buckling. The angle changes the flow; water that once climbed now pools. In the new pool I become the surface tension itself, holding a thin film that reflects the faint blue of root-light. Small scrapers land on that film, their wing-scales scraping in metallic rhythm. They polish the surface where they feed, and the polished place reseals behind them, carrying the memory of their passage in a slightly altered sheen. One swarm ignites a brief flare when disturbed by a passing predator shadow. In that flare I see, far below, the knife-edge of fresh rock still streaked with mineral the plant has barely begun to claim. The light dies; the memory remains as a faint after-image traveling with the next contraction.

I do not know how many circuits of the massif I complete before I am drawn upward again into the hanging galleries where the translucent gliders drift. There, on a slow updraft, I am briefly a membrane tasting mist with fine sensory cilia. When I flex, the plant walls amplify the note and send it outward. Hours later the answer returns from another colony, changed by distance into something almost like recognition. Between question and answer I exist as nothing but resonance and the cold music of water returning to the mass that first gave it voice.
