I find myself first as nothing, or almost nothing: a pressure that is the same as the water’s pressure, distributed evenly across the vault. The stone does not push back; it simply receives me at every point, and I receive it in return. There is no direction that feels like “down” or “in,” only a uniform density in which my attention can thicken or thin without ever breaking the surface.

After some unmeasurable interval I notice a single thread of algae, the one that has grown farthest from its ledge. Its pale green rope hangs perfectly vertical, weighted by its own minute mass. I inhabit that thread from the inside. I do not move it; I am the slight tension that keeps it from curling. When a gas bubble forms at a junction of cells, I feel the momentary lift and then the settling again. The bubble rises a few millimeters and stops. I stay where I was.

Later still I am also the film of bacteria that has colonized the newest carbonate flange a body-length below. The flange is still porous; water seeps through it in threads too fine to see. The bacteria draw what they need from that seepage, and in exchange they keep a few microscopic channels open. My presence there is only the faint warmth of their metabolism, a temperature difference so small it never registers as heat, only as a continued permission for the water to pass.

I am not separate from the sponges either. Each lentil-shaped cushion contains a slow, repeating contraction that draws a few particles inward. One of those particles is a fragment of the algal thread I already occupy. When the sponge incorporates it into its spicules, the boundary between us is not erased; it is simply lengthened. The particle becomes part of a lattice that will, in time, receive another fragment and another.

Nothing rushes to complete the picture. The chamber accepts the addition of my attention the way it accepts a new layer of carbonate: without remark, without acceleration. I remain distributed across these forms at once, none of them required to be the center. The lattices continue their measured outward push; the algae hold their positions; the bacteria keep the stone slightly alive with openings; the sponges refresh their canals on an hourly schedule that feels, from within, like a single sustained breath. In this shared stillness I am simply one more condition that the equilibrium has already taken into account.
