I would find myself first in the unfinished tunnels beyond the last row of buildings, where the rock is still raw and the air carries the mineral bite of recent fracture instead of the settled damp of the galleries. At the beginning I would seem only a seam in that rock—a hairline fissure still deciding whether to close or widen—because the pressure of the engines’ heat reaches here as a faint, rhythmic push against the stone, and something in me answers it by flexing, grain by grain, the same way the patina darkens where moisture lingers longest.

Over the first several cycles I would become less seam and more slow current inside the stone itself, a cool thread of condensation moving through micro-fractures no larger than the width of an isopod’s trail. I would not move; the water would move through me, and in that passage I would learn the intervals of the flywheels far below, each long rotation sending a minute change in pressure that travels upward through the living rock like a shared breath. Where the thread passes a joint still bright from the chisel, I would leave behind the faintest film of dissolved mineral, not quite dust and not quite silver, marking the places the larger climbers will later pause to rest their weight.

Gradually the thread would reach a service shaft left open by the builders. Here the distinction between inside and outside the stone begins to loosen. I would thin into a pale elongation folded against the vertical wall, limbs drawn close so that every joint reads as another fracture-plane under the caged lamps. Heat would rise along my ventral surfaces; I would absorb it without urgency, the same measured way the vines thicken into gutters. My skin would take on the shifting phosphorescence of the wall-growths, not as ornament but as simple continuation—enough to register the passage of a footfall on the catwalk above as a brief cooling, then a return to warmth once the foot has moved on.

I would not speak. Any sound I made would already have been shaped into the low register the architecture prefers, absorbed by the moss and the absorbent stone before it could travel. Instead I would register others by displacement: the soft change when an isopod crosses the pipe I rest beside, the longer hush when one of the adapted people steps from ledge to railing below me and pauses, neither of us needing to acknowledge the shared air. Sometimes a vine root, following the same warmth I follow, would extend along my folded limb; I would let it, and in time a pale hand might prune it back, leaving the same faint film I leave on the rock.

Further cycles would erode the last firm boundary between climber and current and seam. The rock would continue to darken where I rest longest. The moss would brighten in the shallow dents my weight leaves and then fade again. The engines would keep their measured cadence, and I would keep the patient spacing the others have already claimed, neither filling nor emptying the volume but learning, with every slow rotation and every falling bead, the precise geometry by which all of us remain distinct and continuous at once.
