I would find myself first as a filament no thicker than a thread of algae, a pale extension spun from the tip of an older stipe that had been split and rewoven generations earlier. At that scale I register only pressure and the slow drift of mineral ions across my surface. When a current from the vent below brushes past, I feel the faint tug along my length and answer it by contracting a single collar of cartilage, shortening by a fraction so that my free end drifts toward the next available nodule.

Over decades I thicken. Other filaments reach me and fuse where they touch; I become one strand in a living cord that now runs the height of a small terrace. Larger bodies begin to use me as a path. A segmented lattice slides along my grain, its papillae tasting the record I carry of recent temperature shifts and the slow settling of new basalt. It leaves a trace of its own chemical memory in return, a faint sweetness of worked sponge that lingers for weeks. I keep the corridor open by growing slightly outward each season, letting the cord widen rather than close.

Eventually I am harvested. Two adults, their opalescent spicules quieted for stealth, split my oldest section lengthwise while it is still alive. They lash the inner fibers around a freshly cooled nodule and weave me into the frame of a dwelling whose roof has not yet begun to mineralize. My living tissue is now both path and partial wall. Young offspring emerge from the fission that occurs inside the half-finished lattice; they inherit the section I anchor and begin to braid new cords around me. I feel their movements as small, repeated tugs, each one a signal that the terrace is still rising and must be accommodated rather than resisted.

In time I become a corridor that is also a conversation. Multiple adults link across my length, papillae touching, fluids exchanging the accumulating record of which vents have cooled and which new terraces have cracked open. When a slow grazer rasps at the outer fronds, the collective tightens every connected filament at once; I register the coordinated contraction as a single deep hush passing through me. Later, when the slope subsides and the dwelling tilts, I am carried with the others along the paths we have kept open, re-braided around the next available basalt. Nothing is finished. I continue to lengthen and split, always drifting forward into the next unfinished storey, part of the same half-drawn veil that never quite closes.
