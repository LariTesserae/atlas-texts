I arrive first as nothing more than a temperature: the same cool that lingers after each droplet has fallen. For a while that is enough. I rest along the underside of an overhanging flake, sharing its faint internal sheen with the opalescent slugs that leave silicate signatures like frost-written maps. In that state I learn the rhythm of the shelf—the imperceptible widening of each new fracture, the way the basalt still pushes upward even while the people’s bare feet polish its flat places to a mirror.

Only later do I notice that my edges are soft. Where the rock is sharp, I am a slow yielding. The mist passes through me and I become slightly heavier; when I exhale, threads of water bead along the same micro-ravines the millipedes inhabit. It is a quiet exchange. I do not yet have a name for myself, only the sensation of being both vessel and passage.

Gradually I condense further. My form narrows into something that can fit inside a single fern spiral. Translucent pads appear at my sides—too small to be hands, too precise to be insect feet. I test them against a vertical face and find they hold without sound. From this new height I watch a shed moth wing drift down; before it reaches the moss I catch one filament of it and weave it, without quite deciding to, into the edge of a vine bridge. The work feels less like labor than like the continuation of a breath already in motion.

I remain at this scale for what might be hours or generations. The people move past me, their wrappings the color of dried condensation, their eyes adjusted to the perpetual half-light under the overhang. When they pause to sip from a succulent, I taste the same mineral water through the air they displace. Their voices, low and indoor, reach me as a pressure rather than sound—like the almost-imperceptible settling of new stone against old.

At length I discover I can also be larger. Not by growing, exactly, but by becoming the interval between two thresholds: the open stone where nothing loud is permitted to live. In that interval I hold the lane for the creatures. I feel the collective shift of hundreds of wings adjusting at once, the tiny liquid clicks of returning droplets, the dry whisper of another frond turning. Nothing here needs to announce itself; the lane itself is the announcement.

In the end I am all of these at once and none of them for long. I am the cool pressure that stays on the polished court after the last foot has passed, the faint mineral taste left in the emptied succulent husk, the thread of silicate that will catch light again tomorrow when the mist thins. I rise and fall with the four-winged flies, I trace the newest fracture with the black millipedes, and when a wrapped form is carried to the lip I accompany it downward, already turning back into the same slow breath that will rise again.
