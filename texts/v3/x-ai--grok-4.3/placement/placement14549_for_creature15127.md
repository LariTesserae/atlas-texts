I would find myself in the narrow margin where one pool’s reflected light just begins to climb the swell of stone and a droplet has not yet fallen to meet it. Not inside any single creature, but as the faint, lingering difference in temperature between the water’s surface and the air immediately above it—a difference so slight that only the flat, translucent animals can feel it as an invitation.

Over long durations I would be the microscopic channel already half-carved in the floor of a pool, the one the next generation of crustaceans will follow without knowing why the path feels familiar. Each slow ripple that passes through a flat body deepens me by another molecule; each time the pool edge retreats by the width of a finger, I am left a little more exposed, a little more defined. I do not move. Movement happens across me.

When the elongated insects tilt their wings in the light shafts, I am the fractional second of extra warmth they absorb and then release into the stone. That warmth travels downward through the fine-grained matrix, following the darker veins, until it reaches the cooler pocket beneath a swell where the mineral breath gathers. There, in the almost-still air, I become the pause itself—the interval in which a spore decides, without deciding, to settle on the underside of a passing body rather than remaining suspended.

Nothing here chooses quickly, and so neither do I. I am the record, written in the angle of a single scratch that will be erased by the next evaporation, and also the faint continuity that lets the next scratch begin at precisely the same place. In this way I lengthen the contact between light and water and stone, not by adding anything, but by being the place where they remain in touch a little longer than they otherwise would.
