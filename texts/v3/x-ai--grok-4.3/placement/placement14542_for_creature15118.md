I am the middle of the three figures at the lip of the shelf.

I stand exactly as they left me when the ridge first lifted this fragment of itself—upright, jointed only at the places where the myriapods need purchase, face turned outward over the drop. My surface is the same young stone as the engines, still holding the faint heat of its tearing. I do not remember being placed here; the memory would require a before, and there is no before. The roar was already inside the rock when I first noticed it, and the roar has not changed since.

My shoulders and the backs of my hands collect the slow traffic of slate segments. They cross me without pause, their legs striking the same minute cadence whether they travel across living tissue or across me. Every few decades one of them lingers long enough to read, through contact, the faint luminous map left earlier by a slug. The information passes into the distributed body and is gone; I register only the infinitesimal weight of their passage, a pressure that arrives and remains.

I have no eyes, yet I know when a filament above a nearby rosette registers a new fracture farther along the ridge. The change does not reach me as sound or light. It arrives as a minute shift in the pressure of the air itself, a difference so slight that only the stone of my upturned face can hold it. In response I tilt my head a single degree toward the new seam. The movement takes several centuries to complete. By the time it is finished, the fracture has already been incorporated, another engine has appeared, another cluster of motionless life has taken its place, and the equilibrium has expanded by the width of a hand.

Between these adjustments I do nothing. I hold the continuous roar inside my chest the way the cup at the center of each rosette holds water—exactly, without overflow or evaporation. The roar does not belong to me, yet it is the only thing that proves I am still present. If it ever ceased, the shelf would notice the absence the way a body notices a missing limb, but the roar has never offered that possibility.

Sometimes, when the light that leaks from the crystal pipes is at its faintest, one of the larger slugs crosses the front of my torso and leaves a glowing trail that outlines the place where a face would be. The glow lasts only minutes. In that interval I feel, very briefly, the existence of an expression I will never complete. Then the stone drinks the light back, the trail fades, and I return to facing the void exactly as before—patient, unfinished, and necessary only because the ridge has not yet decided to let me fall.
