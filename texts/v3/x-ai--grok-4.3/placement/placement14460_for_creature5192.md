I would arrive first as a film of condensation on the underside of a pipe that runs between two of the brick halls—one of the wider ones, where the catwalk sags just enough to catch droplets before they fall. For a time that is what I am: surface tension and mineral chill, beading where the iron sweats against the evening mist. When a drop finally lets go it carries me downward; I land on a broad bluish leaf already filmed with earlier moisture and spread across its veins.

From there I thicken. The leaf belongs to one of the older vines, its stem already wedged between rivets on the pump-house wall. I follow the stem inward, pressing against warm metal that ticks with the slow reciprocation of whatever turns inside. Growth here is measured in seasons rather than days; I learn the exact pressure needed to push a new tendril between two bolts without disturbing the lizard that suns itself on the pipe each morning. The vibration of the flywheel is constant, a low language the vine and I both understand. Where it is strongest the wood of the stem hardens around the motion, not to stop it but to accompany it.

At night the sodium lamps come on and the leaf that holds my earliest droplets traps their orange light from below. I become part of that dim green tunnel, a living roof that softens the glare for the moths brushing past. Their pale dust settles on me; I keep it, a faint chalk on the waxy cuticle. When rain does not fall the mist still gathers, and I gather with it, returning to the pipe each evening to bead again.

If ever the people step out from their low doors they pass beneath without looking up. Their footsteps are soft on the damp loam. I feel the tremor travel through root and pipe alike and continue my growth around it. The heron in the nearest pool lifts one leg, pauses, sets it down again in water that bubbles from a vent I helped keep clear. Nothing asks the work to end. Neither do I.
