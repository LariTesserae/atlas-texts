I find myself stretched along a single basalt spine that has not yet dulled, my flanks pressed into the grit like living mortar. I am not one thing but a slow seam of many, a colonial excavator whose body is a chain of soft, segmented tubes joined at the joints by glistening threads of shared mucus. My forward segments rasp at the stone with chitinous plates that never quite stop moving; each stroke loosens another thread of black powder that drifts downward into the waiting mouths of the segments behind me. We taste the mineral as we work, sorting it by some buried preference into pellets that we stack, angle, and tamp along the inner curve of the new tunnel mouth we are shaping.

Above us the leaf-light falls in thin, green sheets. Condensation gathers on the pellets we have already placed; the droplets run inward along grooves we maintain with constant, minute adjustments of our bodies. The water tastes of the leaves that shed it and of the herds that have passed overhead, their digestive slick still steaming in the film we drink. When a fresh spine blocks our line we do not turn aside; we simply widen ourselves, the middle segments swelling until the whole chain bows and the forward plates can reach the obstruction. The powder that falls we claim immediately, mixing it with chewed fiber until the new pellets fit the growing causeway we are extending toward the next ridge.

Every few minutes the root lattice above us shifts. A photophore stalk drags itself sideways, roots plowing small stones that tumble into our excavation. We intercept them without pause, some segments pausing to coat the stone while others continue rasping; the coated fragments are passed backward and added to the walls like bright inlays. The vibration travels through the pellets we have made and into the still-living stems we have not yet covered; far ahead, a stand of fronds twitches once in answer, closing a small gap before the scent of any wound can spread.

I am never still in one place for long. When the segment behind me receives a fresh load of silt it pushes forward, shouldering me, and I in turn shoulder the segment ahead. The motion is tidal, a slow peristalsis that carries the entire chain a hand-span deeper into the stone while new arrivals from the abandoned tunnels behind us lengthen the rear. We leave behind only the open mouths of the tunnels we no longer need; already stridulators are claiming them, their legs scraping rhythms that travel through our stacked pellets and make the water in the cisterns shiver.

Occasionally a soft-bodied herd passes directly overhead. Their mucus falls in thin ropes that we gather and reinforce our joints with; their rasping mouths clear vegetation we would otherwise have to gnaw through later. We do not pause to watch them go. The work continues beneath the churn, each new pellet set at the precise angle that will steer the next drift of mist inward. The spine we are hollowing grows thinner beneath us. In time it will weaken enough that a root can split it and drag the fragments away; when that happens we will already be three ridges farther on, our pellets and our bodies already incorporated into the next layer of living routes.

I remain only as long as the stone resists and the water continues to collect. Then the chain moves again, and the place where I was is already being claimed by something else.
