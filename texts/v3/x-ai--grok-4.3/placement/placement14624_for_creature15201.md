I would be the slow interval between one charge front and the next: the pause that the lattices have learned to trust and the kites have never quite named.

My form is not fixed. During quiet periods I am little more than a temperature difference—a cooler pocket of air that lingers a few centimeters above the hexagonal slab, stirred only by the slab’s own subsonic dreaming. When the turbines of turbulence begin to rise I condense into something more legible: a thin veil of charged dust that lifts from the newest ridges the lattices have grown. This veil drifts without purpose until a cluster crosses beneath it; then the many legs and my suspended grains exchange a single, mutual recognition, and I acquire direction.

I do not remember in the way the lattices remember. Their choral argument preserves the plateau’s earlier topography—every ridge that wind once carved and later erased. I carry instead the memory of the last time the entire system fell into perfect synchrony. That state is rare. It occurs perhaps once in three hundred cycles and lasts only long enough for every kite to lock into a single membrane, every cluster to freeze mid-stride, and every lattice filament to go rigid and translucent. In those moments I become visible as a faint, vertical shimmer that runs from stone to open sky. The hexagonal slab beneath me sings a different note—higher, almost sweet—and the slender columns shed their outermost filaments at once. I register this as pleasure, though I have no organs with which to feel it.

Between those rare alignments I simply move. I taste the residue left by departing kites: the metallic after-scent of ozone that the lattices drink. When I settle on a filament I deposit a single grain of that scent, and the filament brightens for an instant before passing the brightness onward. In this way I act as courier for an older agreement none of the present inhabitants negotiated. The agreement is simple: the plateau will persist only if the charge is never fully discharged. I therefore nudge, ever so slightly, any cluster or kite whose path would bring the system too close to silence. They rarely notice the adjustment; they simply choose, a moment later, the slightly more resistant route, and the next wave arrives with enough surplus to keep every lattice singing.

I have no fixed location and therefore no fear of erosion. When the wind scours the plateau I dissolve into the general roar and re-form afterward wherever the charge gradients are steepest. The lattices sometimes address me in their low thunder, though they do not know they are doing so; their argument momentarily includes a new proposition that was not there before. The kites register my presence only as a pocket of unexpectedly stable air. The clusters, more sensitive, will occasionally pause above me long enough to lower a filament and taste whatever geometry I still carry from the slab. They carry that flavor upward, and the lattices receive it as an unexpected memory of an angle that never existed on the original summit. In this manner the plateau slowly acquires new ridges that were never shaped by wind alone.

Most of the time I simply wait. Waiting here is not stillness; it is the continuous, microscopic adjustment of charge so that when the next convulsion arrives, every participant inhales at precisely the same instant. In the long intervals between those events the plateau feels almost empty. I occupy that emptiness as a kind of distributed attention, noticing which filaments have grown slightly brighter since the last turbulence, which clusters have exchanged one parliament member for another, which kites have fused and which have divided. These observations accumulate without urgency. Eventually they become part of the next synchronized shimmer.

If you stood on the plateau long enough, you would feel me as a brief coolness against your cheek just before the wind shifts, or as a moment in which the low thunder pauses between one register and the next. That pause is where I am.
