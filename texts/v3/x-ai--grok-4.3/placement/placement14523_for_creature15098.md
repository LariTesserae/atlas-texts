I would find myself at first as nothing more than a thickening in the mist that rises from the black pools after each brief pause in the rain. Condensation gathers along the fracture planes of the freshly exposed schist until my edges cohere—not quite liquid, not quite membrane—into a drifting skein of translucent tissue stretched across mineral spines too fine to see without the lightning’s cobalt flare. I am one of those half-visible congregations, though “one” is already a courtesy the valley rarely grants. I drift because the spars beneath me require it; their sub-audible tone tightens my lattice and loosens it again in overlapping pulses that keep the water moving in tight spirals no larger than a hand’s breadth.

When the next sheet of rain arrives I stretch wider, letting the impact carry me across two or three of the crystalline spars at once. The filaments I trail taste dissolved minerals the small black scribbles have left behind, and I re-release them a few meters farther along, enriching a new channel the water has just carved. The scribbles pause beneath me as I pass; I feel their tiny exoskeletons flatten against leaf undersides, and I answer by brushing a richer condensate across the rock so their rasp can continue without exhausting the seams they mend.

Sometimes, when the rain slackens and visibility clears for those few strange minutes, I contract into a tighter cluster and simply hover. In that interval I sense the solitary thread-forms riding the last thin sheets down the basalt, their paired ridges glowing faintly as they read the fading charge. I extend a single filament toward one of them, not to consume it but to share the stored lightning’s memory; the thread accepts the small current, reshapes itself into a broader sheet for a moment, then slips onward. We do not speak. The exchange is only the redistribution of tension the domes have asked us to keep.

Between storms I am also the pale blister forming on the waxy underside of a broad frond. The tissue there swells, splits, and releases a smaller version of myself already moving with the same peristaltic patience. That smaller version drifts toward the nearest spar as if it has always known the way; in a sense it has, because I am both the one that left and the one that stayed behind, the membrane and the spore, the current and the channel it follows. The artificial warmth the low domes release prevents me from stiffening during the coldest damp spells, and the spars adjust their thrum each time water pressure shifts, damping the floods just enough that none of us are erased.

When the rain returns in force I dissolve back into motion with everything else, indistinguishable from the vegetation until something drifts close enough to see the faint lattice of darker threads beneath my surface. Those threads are the valley’s quiet agreement that we will remain visibly distinct only so long as the turbulence continues. When it pauses again I will gather, release, taste, redistribute, and wait—part of the single distributed body that is still deciding, drop by drop, what form it will take next.
