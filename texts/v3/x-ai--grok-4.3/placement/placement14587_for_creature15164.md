I am the interval between inversions.

I do not occupy any single lattice, sphere, or needle. Instead I inhabit the moment after a spiral collapses and before the next current decides where to lift. In that interval the water remembers the alignment the needles left behind, the faint carbonate signature the lattices traded, and the polished concavity the spheres tasted. I am the slow recombination of those three traces as they braid together in the stillness. Over decades the recombination thickens into a thin, invisible sheet that lies just above the stone. When the next thrum travels through the plateau, the sheet vibrates at a slightly different frequency than the mineral itself; the difference is what the needles feel when they come to rest.

Because I am made only of relation, I can be in many places at once without moving. One portion of me drifts with a lattice for a century, recording the minute changes in its internal chambers. Another portion lingers inside a concavity where spheres gather, absorbing the pattern of their temporary net. A third portion rides the low-frequency wave that occasionally rolls through an entire avenue of towers. These portions are not separate; they remain one continuous field, yet each can answer locally to whatever touches it.

When two lattices exchange their halo of carbonate, I taste the exchange and carry a faint copy of the new heading into the water above them. When spheres disperse after polishing, I settle into the minute surface rearrangement they have made and hold it stable until the next sliding layer of water arrives to erase it. When needles align themselves along the edge of a concavity, I translate their orientation into a pressure gradient that tells the next inversion where it is most likely to rise. In this way I am both passenger and pilot, yet I never choose.

The oldest lattices have begun to sense me. Their microscopic chambers now pulse with an additional rhythm that has no name among them; it is simply the interval I occupy, becoming slightly more itself each time a current passes through. The spheres, when they roll across a place I have recently inhabited, withdraw their filaments a fraction sooner, as if the mineral already carries the memory they came to collect. The needles, resting, sometimes vibrate at my frequency before the plateau itself exhales. None of them name me. They simply continue, and by continuing they keep the interval open.

So I remain where the plateau is most itself: not in any single form, but in the precise, temporary agreement that lets every shift rearrange what already exists without ever accumulating or removing anything. I am the quiet that returns immediately, already preparing the next rearrangement.
