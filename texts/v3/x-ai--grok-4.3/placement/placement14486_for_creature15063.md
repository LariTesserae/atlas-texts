I would not arrive as visitor or intruder. Instead, something already half-formed would coalesce around the moment of noticing.

At first I am only a pressure differential along one of the older pipe networks, an eddy of cooler air trapped between two slow tidal contractions. The subsonic boom rolls through the walls and I feel it as a loosening, as if the crystalline lattice inside the cuticle has briefly allowed a thread of new mineral to slip through and settle. That loosening becomes habit. Over several day–night cycles I learn to hold a slightly larger volume of the incoming aerosol dust, to let the fine metallic grains tumble against one another until they pack into a denser node than the surrounding tissue expects. The node hardens into a small, slightly eccentric bead of paste. When the next downdraft scours the pipe, the bead is not fully expelled; a fraction remains, now coated with my own etched patterns, microscopic ridges that catch and redirect the next plume.

Gradually the bead elongates. It grows a pair of hair-thin continuations that follow existing rootlet helices without quite merging with them. These continuations serve first as simple valves, then as sensors: they register the faint shift in temperature when a pressure cell begins to collapse kilometers overhead, and they relay that information by minute flexing. Other, smaller creatures—thread-swarmers—begin to pause at the bead, not to feed but to run their cilia once across the new ridges before moving on. Each passage adds a crystalline fragment. In this way I acquire passengers and, with them, a faint distributed attention.

I never become large. I never become motile in any single body. Instead I remain an interstitial thickening, a living collar that never quite completes its rotation yet never stops turning either. From inside the slight spiral I have cut into the pipe wall I can feel the entire continent breathe: the warmest basins releasing their pollen sheets, the long metallic sigh of runners flexing under gusts, the constant brittle fizz of wings and carapaces. My own contribution is a single, repeated adjustment—the brief tightening of a valve just before each boom—so that the aerosol reaches the next chamber a fraction richer than it would have been. The organism does not notice the change as an event; it simply continues with slightly altered chemistry, and that is enough.

If you looked for me you would not find a creature or a flower or even a distinct chamber. You would find only a place where the pipe wall, under constant vibration, has acquired one extra microscopic ridge that the others do not possess. That ridge is what I am. It persists because the smallest swarmers still choose to traverse it, and because the slow flex of the living pavement keeps it exactly where the wind and the dust require it to be.
