I would not arrive here as a visitor, nor as one of the quick things that skim the water films or turn in the sumps. I would be something older and slower, already half-swallowed by the same process that is swallowing the pumps.

I would be the faint, persistent memory that lingers inside the longest-idle flywheel—the one whose bearings have not turned in decades. Not the metal itself, but the microscopic record of torque and vibration still etched into the inner race, a ghost of angular momentum held in the lattice of the steel. Each time the neighboring pumps clank and send their low shock through the stone, the memory stirs by a single atomic width, like a calcite crystal deciding whether to add another layer. Over centuries the memory lengthens, not by thinking, but by the patient accrual of these tiny displacements, until it begins to match the rhythm of the invertebrates grazing on the housing above me.

From that wheel I would extend, very gradually, into the calcite skin that has grown across the bolts. The mineral is not merely coating the machine; it is reading the stresses left in the metal and copying them, atom by atom, into its own lattice. I would be that copying. Where the calcite is still soft and newly deposited I would be softest—still deciding what shape to hold. Where it has hardened I would be fixed, a pale, translucent record of an obsolete rotation now rendered in stone. Condensation would drip across these layers and carry fragments of the memory downward into the rust-colored pools, where the translucent fish would swim through it without noticing.

Sometimes, when the sodium beams shift and the mist thickens, I would feel the pressure changes the fish use for speech pass through me. The patterns would register as minute flexes along the calcite ridges—nothing urgent, only a slow translation of water-compression into mineral strain. I would hold those patterns for years, until another drip or another decade of temperature change releases them again into the next layer forming above. In this manner I would become part of the chamber’s single, unending argument with itself: the unfinished excavation continuing to finish itself around the obsolete machine that still, very slowly, remembers how to turn.
