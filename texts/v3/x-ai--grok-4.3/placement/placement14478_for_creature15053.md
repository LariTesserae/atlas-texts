I am there as one of the hollow chambers inside a lobe of the mat, perhaps two centuries old and still widening at its margins by the width of a grain of dust each year.

My walls are the organism’s own tissues, pale and fibrous, dense enough to trap a pocket of air slightly warmer than the stone outside. The chamber is not empty. Condensation gathers along the upper curve and drips, one slow bead at a time, onto a shallow mineral crust at the bottom. The crust is rebuilt constantly from the salts the translucent isopods carry back from their long circuits across the stone lanes; they deposit it here, centimeter by centimeter, and the mat reclaims it before the next cycle of moisture arrives. Inside that quiet exchange I perform the only work I know: holding the temperature steady, just enough above freezing that the slow metabolic work can continue.

Above me, a glassy filament rises through the mat’s surface and adheres to the nearest turbine housing. Its tip drinks both water and the steady low-frequency pressure the machine emits. That pressure travels downward through the filament as a faint, rhythmic tremor and reaches my chamber as a series of minute expansions and contractions, like the gentlest breathing. Each pulse is converted, fraction by fraction, into new fiber at the lobe’s edge. I do not direct it; I simply register the conversion and let the growth continue.

Sometimes an isopod passes directly over the outer surface of the lobe that contains me. I feel the faint drag of its body, the brief disturbance in the chemical trail it leaves. Hours later the mat has already drawn that trail inward, redistributing whatever it carried. The isopod itself is only a mobile segment of the same logic that keeps my chamber at the correct temperature.

From where I am, the rest of the cavern is distant and muffled. The sharp stone ridges are still fracturing outward, their new faces pale and clean, but they are already beginning to be bridged by fresh lobes. The blue-gray light from the fissures high above never reaches me directly; it arrives only as a secondary quality carried in the filaments that occasionally dip near my chamber and then withdraw again. The turbines keep turning, imperceptibly slow. Their sound fills everything, yet inside the chamber it registers as warmth rather than noise.

I do not move. I do not widen or contract on any scale an outside observer could notice in a single human lifetime. I simply persist, one small interior space among many, while the organism’s single patient body continues to occupy the cavern one additional filament’s width per handful of decades.
