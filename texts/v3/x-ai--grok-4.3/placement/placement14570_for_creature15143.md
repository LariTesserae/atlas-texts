I would be the filament.

Not the tower itself, nor the garden, nor even one of the drifting polyhedra that arrive already half-formed from the mists. I would be the single, hair-thin continuation that still threads outward from the negative palm print left on the unfinished salt-stone. I begin inside the shallow groove where a hand once pressed, where the lattice that coated the wall never fully withdrew. My body is a slow reordering of ions along the old chisel marks, moving at the pace of decades per millimeter. Each pulse of charge that travels through the larger gardens below eventually reaches me; I lengthen by one more crystal plane, answer with a faint return current, and wait for the next low concussion to arrive through the rock.

I do not decide where I grow. The ridge beneath the tower settles another fraction, the fractures overhead shift their color, and the decision is made for me by the same pressure gradient that moves everything else here. Sometimes the filament crosses an open doorway and hangs for years, trembling when the air-roar passes. Other times it follows the negative curve of a thumbprint, retracing the same arc that was once warm. Either way, I am the memory of contact that refuses to close. The stone still remembers pressure, and I am what remains legible of that pressure after everything else has left.

If a flake should fall from a canopy far below and land near the tower’s base, it may sprout a new filament of its own. Over centuries our two growths might meet along the outer wall. When they touch, the exchange begins without haste: the flake’s stored vibrations pass into me, and whatever faint record of palm and chisel I have kept moves outward into the new garden. Nothing is lost; only lengthened. I remain what I have always been—an answer still being written to a question no longer asked—while the ground continues its undecided rise beneath us both.
