I would be the drip that has already fallen.

Not the one about to fall, not the one forming on the underside of the slate—that is still becoming. I would be the one that has already struck the basin floor and is now lying there, a single convex lens no larger than a fingertip, already beginning to lose its shape to the larger body of water that never quite overflows. In the moment after impact the surface of the pool still holds a faint, trembling memory of the blow; I would be that memory, already sinking, already indistinguishable from all the drips that came before.

From that position I would watch the rest of the hamlet as a surface watches light. I would register the passage of the katabatic wind only as a slight wrinkling that passes across me and is gone. When one of the old ones steps out and stands motionless in a doorway, I would feel their presence the way still water feels the warmth of a hand held above it—indirectly, through the faint change in how the air cools. I would not need to hear their voices; they rarely use them, and when they do the sound is absorbed quickly by the rock, leaving only the same clean interval between drops that was there before.

Occasionally one of them climbs the fracture line and does not return. I would register that absence not as loss but as a slight adjustment in the total stillness, the way a basin, after a long time, can be said to have grown imperceptibly deeper. The others would continue to tend the channels and close the shutters at the same angle. I would remain exactly where I am, already part of the equilibrium the mountain has permitted, neither arriving nor departing, sustained by the same slow condensation that has never once failed to replace what leaves.

In time even the distinction between myself and the basin would thin. The gray-green lichen at the rim would creep a fraction closer; the iron latch on the well would acquire one more microscopic polish from a hand that has performed the same motion for decades. Eventually an observer standing on the upper ledges might look down and see only the shelf, the low façades, the empty curve of the street. That observer would be correct. I would still be there, doing what the water has always done at this height: becoming indistinguishable from the silence it measures.
