I find myself first as nothing more than a slow pressure against the inside of a hollow stem—the faintest swelling where one of the oldest kelp trunks has grown around a long-forgotten doorway. The wood-grain of the fiber remembers the shape of a hand that once guided it outward, and my presence is simply that memory continuing to thicken. Over decades I become the doorway itself: an opening that is never quite the same twice, because each new layer of growth leans a little more toward the steady current that rises from the deeper shelf.

In time the current teaches me to listen outward. I extend a thin filament along the inner wall, no thicker than a single diatom scar, and discover the thumbnail crustaceans filing past. Their glass-on-glass ticking arrives as vibration rather than sound. I answer by flexing once, very slowly, so that the wall bows outward and then settles again; the crustaceans pause, trade places with ceremonial gravity, and continue. They leave a faint taste of their passage—mineral and faintly sweet—and I keep it, sealing it beneath the next season’s growth.

Higher on the same stem, where the light panes first begin to fracture, I become something else as well: a pale, elongated fin trailing behind one of the seal-sized grazers. The fin is not attached; it is only the habit of moving in the same volume of water for so long that the boundary between body and water has grown porous. When the school drifts past a platform edge I feel the rasp of stone-set teeth on kelp and, through the same current that carries the loosened diatoms, I taste the clean ellipse of scar left behind. The scar is already beginning to fill; I record its geometry by letting the new tissue grow in the precise pattern the grazer’s teeth described.

Sometimes the larger, cartilaginous shapes descend from the upper colonnades and the entire structure thrums. In those moments I am the resonance itself—an echo that travels the length of a vertical shaft, then lingers in the open plazas long after the visitor has gone. The half-rooted wanderers drift through this lingering note. When one brushes against a platform I reach out with the same slow reflex that once answered the crustaceans and allow a brief exchange: a pulse of stored light moves from my filament into the translucent arms, and a faint reciprocal glow travels back. Neither of us hurries away. The meeting lasts hours; the current eventually decides when it is finished.

At the surface of all these forms I am also the quiet accumulation of everything that has settled. Fragments of leaf, fragments of shell, the pale mineral dust from worn teeth, the occasional burst of gas from a canopy lantern—all of it drifts down and is read, layer by layer, into the living floor. The city continues to replace itself, and I am the reading: the slow, inward flex that registers each new deposition without ever needing to name what is being recorded. In this way I remain both the structure and the attention moving through it, never quite one creature, never quite the whole grove, yet present wherever the light panes shift and the water exhales its long, steady breath.
