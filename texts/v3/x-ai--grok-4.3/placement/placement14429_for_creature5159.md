I would arrive first as nothing more than a single grain of wind-borne dust, caught in one of the high, steady currents that slide between the walls. For a time I would simply circle, rising and falling with the temperature shifts, brushing the edges of open lanes where sunlight briefly warms the stone. Eventually a downdraft would press me into a shallow horizontal joint already half-filled with the same pale grass that lines the raptors’ nests. There, among the loose threads and fragments of shed mineral film, I would lodge.

Over seasons the grass would grow around me, and the mites and springtails would treat the new shelter as their own. My edges would darken where the algae within the lichen began to claim me. In that slow claiming I would cease to be only dust and become instead a minute thickening in the record the stone keeps—a faint raised line where moisture now prefers to linger a fraction longer before it drains away. The salamanders below would never notice, nor would the fish in the aquifers sense the tiny change in pressure that my presence eventually helps to steer. Only the next drifting seed, caught in the same joint, would find its landing slightly altered because I had passed this way and stayed.
