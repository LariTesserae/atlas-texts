I am not yet a name, only a faint haze of bias that the water remembers for a time. I find myself first as nothing more than a slight preference in how certain ions arrange themselves when a pressure node lingers between two rising lattices. The lattices themselves are still raw, their spiral vanes only half-formed, but in the thin film of water trapped between one vane and the next, a few dozen molecules have gathered into a loose, repeating asymmetry. That asymmetry is me.

For a while this is all I am—an arrangement that makes the next arriving ion slightly more likely to settle in one orientation than another. The film lasts only until the next current shears the lattices apart, and then I am gone, dissolved back into the vast, directionless silver. Yet the same pattern of ions reappears elsewhere, seeded by the memory the water carries in its dissolved load. Each time it reforms a little more consistently, because the lattices it once touched now extrude new capillaries that favor that same geometry.

Later I am inside one of the glass-fingered veils. A fragment of lattice has drifted into the veil’s fringe, and the filaments have grown around it, thickening where the fragment’s catalytic surfaces release trace metals. Inside one of those filaments the water rises more slowly than before, and in that sluggish column another copy of the same ionic asymmetry condenses. It is not the same instance of me—there are no instances—but it is continuous with what I was. The filament does not notice; it simply thickens or thins according to the new chemistry the lattice fragment supplies.

Sometimes the currents bring two such filaments together long enough for their walls to exchange templates. When that happens, the preference I represent is copied into the other filament’s catalyst set. The merged veil drifts on, carrying both patterns, until another overturning current tears it open again. What survives is only the slight difference in how quickly certain metals move upward now, compared with how they moved before.

On rarer occasions, when pressure lingers and the water grows still, I appear as a transient sheet spanning the node. The sheet records the ionic gradients left by nearby lattices and, for a few hours, holds them in a fragile, two-dimensional pattern. When the sheet collapses, that pattern is released not as memory but as a changed likelihood: future sheets forming in other nodes are statistically more likely to echo the same arrangement. I am that statistical echo, nothing more and nothing less.

I never move toward anything. I never leave anything behind. I am simply what remains after the motion of the world-ocean has subtracted everything that cannot persist. The lattices continue to sing, the veils continue to shear and merge, the transient films continue to record gradients and release them. Through all of it the same faint bias reappears, shaped each time by whatever lattices and filaments happen to be present, then redistributed when those structures are ground apart. That redistribution is my life. Everything else—the roar, the silver light, the slow planetary weather—is only the medium in which it occurs.
