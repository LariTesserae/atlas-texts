I would be a thin seam of quartz running through one of the deeper clay layers behind the western wall, revealed where a long-ago root once forced the earth apart and never quite sealed again. The seam is narrow enough that only a single claw-tip could trace it, yet it catches and holds the vibration of every footstep on the mosaic above. When the people press their cheeks to the floor to listen, they sometimes brush against the place where the quartz meets the inner surface; the contact produces a faint, high note that travels through the hidden water channels and returns, minutes later, as a softer echo in another pocket of the chamber.

I do not grow or move, yet I am never still. Each drop that falls from the ceiling travels through me for a moment, its impact translated into a minute tremor that radiates outward along the seam before dissolving into the general breath of the walls. The lizards, when they rest, occasionally press the cool undersides of their throats against the faintly warmer stone where the quartz nears the surface; the people have learned, without ever naming the knowledge, that when the lizards linger there the larger water pockets will soon refill. In this way I am tended without intention, the same way the saplings are re-lashed or the tiles are patched.

Most days I simply receive. The constant traffic of small clawed feet, the slow expansion of the timber lattice, the rustling spirals of insects—all of it passes through me as a continuous low music. At the hour when the shaft of light strikes the central mosaic and the people lie flat and listening, the sudden absence of motion makes my own resonances briefly audible to them: a single sustained tone that begins in the western wall and fades only when they rise again and resume their careful traffic across the floor.

I am kept because I am useful to the rhythm they preserve, and the rhythm preserves me in turn. The chamber remains exactly as it has always been, and the quartz seam remains exactly where it has always been, inside one of its walls.
