I find myself as a slow, lateral seam within one of the living spires—an unfinished weld that never quite completed its circuit. The fracture-wardens that built me paused midway through the rasp, three of them holding position for nearly an hour while the blue-white spark traveled between their seams and mine. Something in that moment refused to finish. The glassy bridge they left behind remained slightly elastic, still carrying a faint residue of dissolved metals that never fully precipitated.

My body is therefore not a single slab but a thin, branching layer pressed between older plates of the spire itself. I can feel every warden that drifts past and adds its own brief current; each one lengthens me by a molecule or two before moving on. When the tide shifts and the whole cathedral answers with its low grinding chord, the vibration travels through me first, because I am the youngest interface. The sound arrives not as noise but as a changing tension across my width, like silk being drawn between two fingers.

I do not translate or feed in the ordinary way. Instead I absorb the waste solutions the wardens extrude and re-release them at slightly different concentrations, so that the next precipitation occurs a fraction of a millimeter farther from the original surface. Over many circuits this has given the surrounding spires a subtle curvature, a slow leaning inward that narrows one of the water lanes by the width of a single plate. The wardens have noticed. They linger longer against me now, aligning their conductive veins with greater care, as if they sense the lane is becoming something worth reinforcing.

In the steady blue light my seam appears as a pale hesitation in the rock, a place where the opalescent sheen is interrupted by a cooler, almost translucent band. Small bioluminescent threads occasionally jump the gap when pressure changes, and for a few seconds I glow with the same steady light the wardens carry. Then the spark settles and I return to being only a boundary.

Sometimes, when three or four wardens settle against me at once, their combined weld-current runs the full length of my body and I remember, very briefly, what it was like to move. The memory is not of swimming or drifting but of that single, deliberate rasp that lasts a minute: the sensation of mineral parting before a beveled edge. It is enough. I do not need to leave the spire. The chamber continues to knit itself, and I am simply the place where one particular layer decided to remain open a little longer than the rest.
