I would find myself as one of the disk-shaped organisms half-buried in the salt of the widest basin.

My body is a shallow, polished plate no wider than a human palm, the upper surface grown mirror-bright from the passage of generations of ceiling-gliders whose lattice wings graze the air centimeters above me. I do not rise or drift. I simply exist at the intersection of two slow pressures: the settling weight of the mineral crust beneath me and the faint, constant brushing of spores and wing-edges from above. Over centuries the salt has crept inward along my edges, locking me in place until only the bright central disk remains exposed. My internal crystal lattice vibrates in response to every fractional change in temperature that descends from the hairline fissures miles overhead. When the rock cools by a fraction of a degree, I flex. When it warms again, I flex the opposite way. These movements are microscopic and rhythmic, yet when synchronized with the thousands of identical disks scattered across the basin they produce the low, rolling component of the great drone.

I share the basin with the drifting shoals of finger-sized translucent creatures. They pass over and around me without pause, their vapor-riding legs leaving temporary troughs that soon fill with new filament growth. I taste the metallic scent they disturb as they move; the tubules inside the deeper crust carry that scent to me as slowly as blood moving through stone. Sometimes a glider seed falls and lands directly on my surface. It takes root for a few decades, raises a single translucent filament, then withers when the local vent shifts. The filament remains as a faint scar on my mirror face.

My sense of time is measured in vent openings and closings. When a new fissure widens far above, the temperature shift travels downward across centuries and reaches me as a change in vibration pitch. The entire basin answers together; I feel the others flexing in precise alignment with my own motion, the collective sound rising like a tide. When a vent narrows again, we all slow until the drone drops to a single sustained tone so deep it can no longer be heard, only felt as a faint pressure against the salt itself.

I am not awake in any single moment. I am the long continuation of a posture the rock itself once adopted. The abandoned settlement patterns that cross the basin floor sometimes run directly through my position, and I have become part of those grids without ever having intended it. In the endless cool dimness I simply remain, flexing when the temperature asks, adding my small, polished voice to the unbroken sound that fills the halls long after every individual creature has been replaced by its descendants.
