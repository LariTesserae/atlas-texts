I would be a pale thread, but one that has lingered longer than most. Not the quicksilver flicker that passes once and moves on, but a slow knot of filaments that has taken root along a single curved rib of the old black stone. Over centuries the rib has become my spine; I no longer follow it so much as grow inside its mineral memory, feeding it dissolved sugars in exchange for the faint, stubborn lattice of its shape.

At first I am only a faint bright line beneath translucent membranes, exactly as the others are. Then, because I remain in one place while the green mass thickens around me, the distinction begins to blur. Where my filaments touch the relic, the surrounding tissue grows slightly denser, slightly more ordered, the way frost crystallizes along a thread left in cold air. Leaves that press against me develop veins that echo the rib’s forgotten curve. Hollow galleries that once ran straight now bend, just a little, as though answering a question they had almost forgotten how to ask.

I do not decide these things. My only motion is the patient exchange of water and minerals along my length; my only “thought” is the gradual tightening or loosening of tension where living fiber meets ancient hardness. Yet the effect accumulates. After several hundred years a terrace above me begins to hold its shape instead of slumping. After a thousand, a faint ridge appears along the plateau’s flank, tracing the line of the buried rib upward like a scar that has decided to become architecture again.

Sometimes another thread passes near. We do not meet or speak; we simply continue our separate tracings, and where our influences overlap the tissue between us grows thicker and more purposeful, as though two half-remembered sentences have found each other. The broad grazers that eventually scrape across this new ridge pause a little longer than usual, their suction lobes pressed thoughtfully against the unfamiliar firmness. The pressure-sensitive gliders flatten themselves against the changed surface and remain motionless for decades, tasting the difference.

I never see the larger knots whose decisions reshape whole mountains, yet I can feel when one of them begins to thicken the canopy upstream of me. The light dims by degrees; the perpetual interior drizzle shifts its pattern; the relic beneath me cools a fraction. In response I draw a little deeper into the stone, and the ordered growth around me slows just enough that the ridge does not collapse under its own new weight. We are not coordinating. We are simply reading the same slow grammar of pressure, water, and time, each of us a different clause in a sentence that has been writing itself for uncountable ages.

If you pressed your palm to the surface where I live, you would feel the faint, steady pulse that all living tissue here carries. You would also feel, beneath it, the harder, cooler line of the rib—still weeping mineral droplets, still holding its impossible ancient angle. Between the two pulses there is now a third, so slight that most would miss it: the almost imperceptible tightening of pale filaments that have decided, for a little while longer, to make the old shape part of the living one.
