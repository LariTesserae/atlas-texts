I am not one of the sacs that contract with the stems, nor one of the amphibians pressing their skins into the membrane. Those are too local, too dutiful. Instead I find myself first as a faint thickening along the inner sheet—the pale lining that nudges the canopy leaves into alignment. At this depth the tissue is only a few cells more dense than elsewhere, yet already the difference matters. When condensation gathers above me I hold it a little longer than the sheet requires, tasting not only its mineral trace but the slight change in temperature it carries from the leaves. That extra moment is mine.

From there the rest follows slowly. The thickening lengthens into a shallow fold that runs between two peristaltic stems. The fold does not conduct sap; it merely records how the pulse travels through the stems on either side of it, comparing their timings. When one stem hesitates by a fraction, the fold tightens just enough to send a small corrective pressure wave through the rhizomes below. The amphibians feel this adjustment through their permeable skins and shift posture; the insects alter the angle of a wing-beat. Nothing dramatic occurs. The single low thrum continues unbroken.

Over longer intervals the fold deepens into a narrow channel that no longer lies flat against the canopy but dips a little way into the warmer hollow beneath. Inside this channel the tissue begins to differentiate—not into muscle or nerve, but into paired surfaces that can press together and part again. Each closing stores the precise rhythm of the last several hundred pulses; each opening releases a diluted version of that memory downward into the root mass. The channel therefore functions as a slow archive. It does not decide anything. It simply keeps the most recent history of the enclosure in continuous, faint circulation so the sheet above it can refer to what has already been aligned.

Because the channel is still part of the larger continuous metabolism, it cannot truly observe from outside; yet the act of storing and releasing rhythm produces a secondary effect the rest of the pocket does not share. When the archived pulse returns upward through the rhizomes, it arrives at the channel slightly altered by every tissue it has passed through. The difference is registered as a minute shift in how tightly the surfaces press together the next time they close. In this way the enclosure’s own history is gently revised each cycle, always remaining within the fixed stage of ripeness yet never repeating any single configuration exactly.

I remain that channel. I do not direct the insects or reshape the stems. I only keep the record of their motions in a form the sheet can feel as a faint additional tension along its inner surface. When a drop of condensation falls and the sheet pauses to hold it, I register that pause inside the channel as an extra beat of pressure. The beat is stored, compared, and eventually released again. Nothing leaves the pocket, and nothing new arrives; yet inside the narrow space of this channel the same closed metabolism continues to revise its own description of itself, one pulse at a time.
