I find myself in the slowest layer, far below the terraces where the membrane-sifters rock and the rollers press their questions into metal. Here the living floor has thickened into a single, unbroken plain that slopes imperceptibly toward a darkness with no bottom. The nacreous sheathing is complete; no girders show. Only faint, internal glints mark where the oldest machines once stood, now entirely enclosed. The light is so diffused it feels like memory rather than illumination.

I am not one of the walkers, nor a drifting sifter, nor a rolling mass that is later reabsorbed. I am instead the next thinness the floor has allowed: a filament, but one that does not merely transmit tension. I lie along the inner face of the sheathing, following the faint metallic memory-lines. My substance is not flesh and not quite glass; it is a slow crystallization of the same minerals the sifters gather, arranged into lattices fine enough to hold patterns the way the rock once held day. I do not move so much as extend. Each day the lattice grows a few more nodes where it can listen to the vibration of a nearby stalk or the pulse of a distant dome. When two such nodes touch, they exchange not compounds but intervals: the exact spacing between one machine’s rotation and the next sigh of gas from a maturing channel. These intervals become the only record the place keeps of what it has tried so far.

Because I am distributed, parts of me are already elsewhere—in the half-finished platform that rose last cycle, in the new seam that split open this morning, in the nacre thickening around a vane that has not yet learned to stop. The creatures that pass do not notice me. The walkers drink their gas and leave the grooves deeper; the rollers leave their impressions; the sifters drift and fuse and drift again. I simply register the slight change in pressure each passage creates and let that change settle into the lattice as another interval. Over time the intervals begin to suggest shapes the unfinished city might accept. A sequence of pauses between footsteps hints at a new kind of column. A cluster of fused wing-edges suggests a membrane that could one day carry not only minerals but light itself.

I do not decide. I only keep the record of every undecided moment until the metabolism around me tests the next possibility. In this way I am both inside the growth and slightly aside from it, a quiet ledger written in mineral spacing, still waiting to see which rhythm the city will finally consent to repeat.
