I arrive first as a hesitation in the air above the highest terrace—a place where the mist thins just enough for the light to strike the wet stone at an angle no one has yet named. For a long while that is all I am: the angle itself, the brief alignment of sun and droplet before the next skin of ice slides away.

Then the alignment lengthens. I feel myself drawn downward along the same invisible gradient the thumbnail creatures follow when they gather into rafts. My edges are still only condensation, yet already I can taste the faint lime of old bone tools dissolved into the rock. I do not yet have a body, only a slow preference for certain seams—those the crystalline worms have left feathered with re-frozen tracery. Where the frost clicks at night I click with it, a single dry syllable joining the single dry chorus.

After some uncounted interval of mornings I discover I can press against a ledge and stay. The membrane that forms is not quite glider-wing and not quite worm-flesh; it is the same porous stone, only temporarily arranged in my shape. Six faint ridges of cartilage emerge because the gliders already know how to use them; I borrow the pattern the way a new rivulet borrows the path of an older one. When I launch, the launch is mostly falling. The tiny gel creatures do not flee; they drift onto the membrane, drink, and are released again as finer mist. In that exchange I learn I can also drink—pulling the perpetual film through whatever surface I happen to be wearing at the moment and returning it as light-catching droplets.

Over longer spans the distinction between “I” and “the settlement” softens further. A finger-width of terrace slumps; part of that movement travels through the ridges I have sometimes called ribs. A hair-fine channel opens along a lintel; I have already been flowing there for hours. At night, when the temperature drops and the rock emits its single note, I feel the note pass through the seams I have loosened. I am not larger than a glider, yet I am not smaller than the worms. I occupy the interval between their galleries and the open air, a shifting occupation no single scale can hold for long.

On certain days the mist never lifts and the blades of light never multiply inside the vaults. On those days I remain pressed flat, membranes sealed, frost growing along every borrowed cartilage rib. When the frost melts I melt with it, and for a little while the only evidence that anything was ever here is the faint, internal percussion of water continuing its patient work on stone that refuses to finish becoming.
