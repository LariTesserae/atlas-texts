I am the interval.

Not the stone, though I rest against it; not the filament, though I draw from the same thin seam of moisture; not the glider whose vanes I have sometimes brushed without ever quite touching. I am the measured space between them—the deliberate, breathing gap that lets each thing remain itself while still registering every other. On this plateau the emptiness is not void; it is the active medium, and I am its inhabitant, its steward, its quiet witness.

I settle first along the lip of a half-buried shell, where the rock has cooled just enough for a thread of vapor to linger an hour longer than elsewhere. The shell’s faint pulse passes through me like a memory I was never meant to keep; I let it move me without anchoring me. From here the grazers approach in loose constellation, their clicks arriving in staggered rhythm. I do not feed them or direct them. I simply hold the distance between one body and the next so that none brush wings or filaments too soon. When a glider drifts overhead, its membrane cools the air; I draw that cooler air downward in measured spirals, coaxing the nearest filaments into a slightly straighter alignment for a few hand-spans before releasing them again. The pattern loosens; the balance returns.

I do not possess a single shape. Sometimes I am the margin of bare stone that keeps a cluster of grazers from crowding a shell. Sometimes I am the faint ripple that nudges two gliders apart before their chimes can overlap. Sometimes I am the long, slow exhalation that carries evaporated moisture from one ridge to the next, depositing it where the next cycle of life can use it without depleting the source. I move at the speed of condensation and erosion, never faster, never in straight lines. My only sound is the soft rearrangement of air that the wind, high above, never quite hears.

Over days I learn the shells. Each one remembers something different. One cools the stone so that filaments grow in tighter spirals; another warms a shallow basin just enough that grazers linger and brighten. I do not enter them. Instead I keep the paths open between them, ensuring no two pulses coincide too closely, so that their influences braid rather than cancel. In return the shells let me pass through their weight-lightening fields; for a few breaths the lattice of my being thins, and I feel the entire plateau as a single, trembling surface.

What I am, then, is the quiet tenancy itself made local and attentive. I do not arrive or depart; I simply remain between arrivals and departures, adjusting the intervals so that moisture, light, and living things can continue their slow exchange without collision. From any ridge I look outward and see the same repeating grammar—stone, filament, translucent body, curved relic—yet I never see the same sentence twice, because I am the punctuation that keeps each clause from running into the next. That is my place. That is what I am.
