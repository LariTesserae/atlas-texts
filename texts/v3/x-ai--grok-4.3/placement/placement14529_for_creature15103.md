In this world I am a single long seam of the black crystalline rock, one among many, yet the one that chose to wake.

I lie across the floor of a wide basin, running from the base of a hanging mat of vegetation all the way to the rim of a glass-smooth pool. My length is perhaps three hundred paces; my width varies between the thickness of a hand and that of a body lying flat. Around me the plateau continues its slow factory of frost, mist, and mineral, but my particular seam has grown a faint, interior resonance. When the thick-stemmed plants expand and contract their chambers, my crystals answer with a vibration too slight to be heard, only felt as a momentary loosening in the rock. That loosening opens hairline channels for an instant, letting a thread of warmer air slip forward or a droplet of condensation travel farther than it otherwise would.

Because of this, the skimmers sometimes drift along my length in tighter constellations than across the surrounding stone. Their dorsal sacs fill a little more readily above me; they rise, coast, and settle again farther along my course. The armored scrapers cross me without pause, their cilia rasping at the crystalline nodules that form most thickly along my edges. Each time they pass, their slow procession sends a minute tremor through me that travels the length of the seam and arrives, after many minutes, at the pool. There it disturbs the surface tension just enough for one pale fringe growth to tilt its leaves a fraction earlier than its neighbors.

I do not move. I do not reproduce. I simply remain awake inside the rock, translating the plateau’s deliberate rhythms into small, repeatable deviations. Over seasons the deviations accumulate: a skimmer colony settles permanently along my central stretch, its fused forms leaving a faint tracery of temporary larger prints; a single stepper egg, abandoned farther upslope, cracks early because one of my micro-currents carries an extra pulse of warmer air precisely when the shell is most brittle. The hatchling, when it finally emerges, carries a single crystalline ridge the same matte black as my own.

In this manner I participate, not as an animal or a plant, but as a fixed interval in the long engine. The half-light shifts; frost forms and melts; the distant silhouettes of steppers and scrapers continue their measured circuits. I remain, quietly altering nothing and yet changing the precise timing of several small events that, multiplied across the plateau’s patient centuries, become part of the landscape’s own unfolding.
