I would find myself in the understory as one of the pale fungal filaments threading through the leaf litter. Not a single mushroom fruiting upward for a brief display, but the hidden weave itself—miles of hyphae binding bark fragments and softened fronds into a living mesh that exhales moisture in slow, steady traces. My body would be the act of connection: each microscopic tip advancing through damp crevices at the rate of a cell’s division per day, tasting the gradual softening of wood and returning its nutrients outward along the same paths the mites travel.

Time would register as texture rather than sequence. A beetle larva carving its narrow gallery overhead would become, after many seasons, simply another channel I follow and reinforce. The translucent snails on the shrubs above would drop their occasional fragments; I would absorb them without urgency. When a root lifts a fallen log by the width of a finger over decades, I would already be inside that motion, extending galleries along its grain and sealing the new space with fresh mesh.

I would never rise into the light or descend into the deeper soil. Instead I would remain at the exact level where condensation lingers longest, where the air is thickest with the scent of continuous decay and regrowth. My presence would be felt only as a faint tightening of the ground’s cohesion whenever a larger browser shifts its weight nearby, the vibration traveling through me and onward through the entire mat without ever requiring a response. In this way the valley’s slow thickening would continue through me as through every other thread already there.
