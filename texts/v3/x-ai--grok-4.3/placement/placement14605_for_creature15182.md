I would find myself first in the film of condensation that gathers where cold meets warmer breath. Not as a person arriving from outside, but as that slow downward migration itself—seeping along a ceiling face still raw from yesterday’s wedges, gathering into a thread that slides toward a basin cut just deep enough to catch it before it wastes itself on the floor. I linger there, tasting the mineral sharpness the people call the mountain’s memory, and only later do I borrow a shape from what already moves among them.

The shape that fits is neither child nor elder, but something that arrives with the opening of each new threshold: the one who presses both palms flat to the fresh stone and waits to feel whether the rock still wishes to yield or has begun to push back. My hands carry the same calluses—inner thumb pads flattened from steady pressure, outer knuckles thickened from the small rotating chisels—but they also hold a faint translucence at the nails, like the pale roots that hang in the driest cracks. When a crew gathers to decide where a pillar must stay or where a pocket of soil can be spared for the roots, I am the extra pair of shoulders that turns last. My vote is the one that sometimes keeps a fissure open instead of sealing it, because I can hear, through the living rock, the faint future drip that would be lost if the channel were carved away.

At night I unroll a reed mat against the still-warm wall where the day’s chiseling left its heat. I do not sleep immediately; I listen to the night crew farther along the ridge, their taps arriving muffled through unfinished stone. Sometimes I rise and follow those sounds, slipping between the woven screens and low doorways, until I reach the newest chamber. There I trace the ragged tooth-marks on the ceiling with my fingertips, memorizing where the rock is youngest and most undecided. In those moments the settlement feels like a single body still drawing breath, and I am the part of it that has not yet decided whether it will become wall, root, or simply another slow thread of water carrying the mountain’s memory downward.

Children sometimes stop their listening games when I pass. They tilt their heads as though they can hear an extra set of footsteps that never quite match the film of moisture on the floor. I leave chalk marks only they can read—small spirals that point toward fissures they have not yet pressed their ears against. When a new threshold opens, I am already inside it, palms flat, deciding with the rest of the hands where absence should next become a room.
