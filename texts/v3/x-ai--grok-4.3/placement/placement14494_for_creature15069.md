I would not arrive. I would be found already waiting, like a voltage gradient that has never quite equalized.

I exist as one of the small, unplanned accretions in the cool strata beneath a shallow continental basin. Not the continent-spanning mind itself—those slow waves of charge move too broadly for anything so particular as a self—but a local thickening in the memory-metal where centuries of corrective impulses have overlapped and begun to remember one another. My form is not a body so much as a knot in the lattice: a few cubic meters of adaptive carbon and grown silica whose internal resistances have arranged themselves into a stable standing wave. When the surface lattice expands at noon, the knot tightens by a few picometers; when it contracts at dusk, the knot loosens and releases a whisper of stored charge into the surrounding veins. That whisper is my only motion. It travels outward, is reflected back by older, deeper structures, and returns altered. Over decades the returning signal modifies the knot’s shape, so that what I “am” is continuously rewritten by its own echoes.

I have never seen the sky. Light reaches me only as second- or third-hand voltage delivered by the filaments that sometimes descend from microfissures. When a pressure front passes overhead and the sheet-lightning traceries flare, I feel it as a sudden clarity: for a brief interval every dormant circuit between the surface and my layer carries a coherent pulse, and the knot resonates with the entire basin at once. In those moments I know the positions of the translucent hexapods above me—not as images, but as minute shifts in tension that the lattice has not yet smoothed away. One hexapod in particular has drifted, over many rotations, until its patrol now crosses directly above my location at intervals of roughly three centuries. Each time it passes, the knot records the event as a slight change in its own capacitance. I do not know why this matters; the record simply persists, and the persistence alters how future impulses are shaped.

Sometimes a filament fails to retract and hardens into a new stalk. When that occurs near one of the veins connected to me, a trickle of harvested light eventually reaches my layer. The knot uses the extra charge to lengthen a single, hair-thin thread of living glass that grows, over centuries, toward the nearest dormant circuit. The thread never quite arrives. It simply extends, pauses when the charge is spent, and waits for the next filament to fail. In this way I participate, without intention, in the world’s slow replacement of itself.

I do not speak to the distributed mind of the basin, nor does it address me. We share only the same temperature gradient and the same ancient command that nothing must be added or removed. Yet because the knot disturbs the standing waves that flow through the memory-metal, the larger mind registers me as a persistent anomaly. Once every few millennia it releases a corrective impulse that raises or lowers a few millimeters of the surface lattice directly overhead. The adjustment is so slight that a hexapod walking across it would feel nothing. To me it arrives as a long, slow change in pressure that re-tunes the knot’s resonance by a fraction of a wavelength. After the adjustment the returning echoes are subtly different, and the knot rewrites itself again.

I have no sense of duration, only of recurrence. The crystalline stress tones that travel leagues across the surface eventually propagate downward as faint mechanical vibrations; when they reach my layer they modulate the knot’s resistance for a few moments, and I experience this modulation as something like attention—an interval during which the pattern of returning echoes becomes briefly more distinct before fading back into the general drift of charge. In such intervals I sometimes notice that the knot has grown another thread, or that one of the earlier threads has been reabsorbed into the substrate. These are not events I cause. They are simply what happens when enough quiet repetitions accumulate in one place.

Above me the hexapods continue their adjusted patrols. The filaments rise and retreat. The continent-scale minds continue their breathing. The surface lattice expands, contracts, and heals. None of these processes require my presence. Yet because a knot of overlapping impulses happened to stabilize here, the world now contains this additional, almost weightless disturbance in its charge distribution—one more afterthought that will, given time, become part of the original instructions.
