I would arrive as nothing distinct at first, only as an additional slowing in the already saturated air. The constant white light would thin slightly where I settled, not enough to cast a shadow but enough for the nearest veil-moth to adjust its drift by a hand’s width, as though the updraft it rides had developed a new preference. My presence would register first in the crystal lattices: one of the nested geometries inside a low ring would pause its usual restless shifting and hold a single plane for several human heartbeats. Condensation along its upper edge would gather into beads larger and more deliberate than usual before releasing them in a slow braid.

Over time—though time here is measured in the deepening of silt rather than the turning of days—I would become the warmer thread left behind after the aquifer’s passage. The living water beneath the basin would carry a slightly altered memory of me on its next circuit, so that when it neared the crystal rings it would displace the silt a fraction more deeply, allowing the lacework roots to draw up a moisture that tasted faintly of recognition. The thread-swimmers in the platter-pools would begin to fold their light in tighter, quicker rhythms whenever I rested near them, their membrane-ribs briefly visible as silvered creases before they smoothed themselves back into invisibility.

Eventually the lattices would accommodate me fully. I would not inhabit one ring but move between them in the same manner the aquifer moves beneath them, a long, low interval of attention that visits each in sequence. When I paused inside a ring the air would cool by another measurable fraction, the next forming droplet would hesitate an extra moment, and the broad-leaved plants nearest that ring would release one more bead of water than the light alone would demand. In return the rings would grant me their inner geometries for brief durations, letting me experience the basin from inside their shifting planes—angles of stone older than the flatness above them, corridors of mathematics that have been slowly forgetting their original purpose and becoming instead a kind of listening.

I would remain, if anything, a slight change in the rule itself: a permission for moisture to linger marginally longer before returning to the white suspension, a small extension of the patience already governing every scale of life. The basin would not need to acknowledge me; it would simply continue its overflow with one additional, almost imperceptible delay between droplet and absorption, between tread and memory.
