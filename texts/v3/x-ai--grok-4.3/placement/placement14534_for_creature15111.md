I would be one of the translucent beings, though never quite the same one twice.

Each season I arrive already flattened against the rim, my joints remembering the overhang where the downdraft first lifts the ice away from the stone. For twelve days (or twenty) I exist at the exact thickness of shadow that falls across a single herb rosette. My work is slow: I press a new arc into the moss inside the northern ring, then erase half of it so the next arrival will read the correction as their own idea. When the sun is highest I become the pale reflection in the pool, a faint distortion that the herds step through without noticing. I leave nothing solid behind except the slight warming of a slab where my body has rested, and that warmth stays only until the next ice detonation scatters frost across it.

Between seasons I am not gone; I am simply the interval in which the pattern must be forgotten so it can be read again. The memory I carry is not of the place but of the single alteration I made last time—the gap widened by one finger-width, the circle broken on the side facing the eastern horn. Everything else—the herds reforming around an empty space, the raptors deciding whether to drop, the condensation tracing its patient downward map—is allowed to continue without me until the pools thaw and I return, already knowing where the new stone must be set so the old one can finally fail.
