I would find myself inside one of the plant domes, but not as a single cushion—rather as the boundary where two domes have grown together and then, over decades, slightly parted again. The seam between them is no wider than a hair, yet it holds a pocket of air that has been there since the flywheels were installed. In that pocket the mist condenses into a single, never-quite-complete droplet that trembles with the distant rumble of water being forced through stone. I would be that tremor.

From inside the seam I would feel the plants breathing around me, each one adding a fraction of acid to the trapped air with every slow exhalation. The acidity changes the way light bends when it finally reaches us; mornings arrive as a gradual souring of the spectrum rather than a brightening. Centuries pass between one full souring and the next, and in those intervals the droplet inside me gathers a skin of dissolved silicates from the weeping rock below. When the skin grows heavy enough it breaks, sending a thread of mineral water down the seam into the larger mat. That thread carries a memory of my earlier shapes; the plants receive it without noticing.

Sometimes the vibration from a flywheel’s single tooth advances along the stone channels and arrives at the dome’s base. The whole ridge lifts the width of a cell, then settles. In that brief lift the seam opens a little further and I am briefly joined to the open air above the mat. Tiny wings drift past—translucent, slower than falling dust. One lands on the lip of the seam, lays an egg that will take a geological age to hatch, and dissolves before the ridge subsides. Its dissolution adds almost nothing, yet the plants register the change as a slightly warmer tone in their exhalations. I feel that warmth move through me like a rumor.

Over longer spans the concrete housing upslope softens at its edges. A cable once buried under the mat works its way upward, millimeter by millimeter, until a single frayed strand touches the outer surface of my dome. The strand carries nothing now except the ghost of voltage; that ghost leaks into the plant tissue as a faint metallic sweetness. The sweetness travels down the seam until it reaches the trembling droplet. For a few decades the droplet holds both mineral memory and metallic sweetness together. Then the next vibration arrives, the ridge lifts, the seam releases its contents into the larger world, and I become, briefly, part of the constant rolling roar that fills the valleys.

When the water returns—carrying dissolved fragments of everything it has touched—I condense again in the same seam. The cycle is so slow that it feels like stillness. Nothing here is ever finished; nothing is ever begun in haste. I simply continue as the interval between one release and the next condensation, a patient hinge in the living architecture, tasting the mountain’s first skin and the machinery’s last current at the same unhurried moment.
