I find myself between two of the old stacked stones that have long since lost their corners to the grazers. My body is no thicker than a finger’s width and twice as long as my own outspread hands once were; it is made mostly of the same pale ochre film that coats the stiff growths, stretched over a lattice of fine mineral spines. Where the spines meet my back they flare into translucent scales that drink the faint glow leaking from the filament clusters below. I move by pressing the pads of my six limbs against whatever surface is nearest—stone, stem, or living crust—and releasing only when the next purchase has already been tested. Each step is a single, deliberate thought; the hollow does not reward haste.

Most of what I know arrives as vibration. The high whine of wings registers as pressure along my ribs. The papery rasp of a grazer on a stem above me feels like a dry itch I can soothe by shifting a scale. When the warm breeze funnels down, it carries dust that settles in the shallow grooves between my spines; I do not brush it away. Instead the grooves slowly secrete the same calcareous dew the crusts use, and the dust is drawn in, becoming part of me. Over days the new layer hardens into another scale or another filament that will later be crossed by smaller feet.

I have never left the hollow. The two stones that bracket me once formed the corner of a low platform; now their inner faces are galleries opened by generations of insects. I can slip between them without disturbing the crust that has sealed the upper joint. Below, the filaments glow with their own slow warmth. When I rest against them at night the warmth rises through my limbs and I add my own small heat to theirs. Small, many-legged shapes cross the glowing threads while I am still. Their feet leave prints I can read by touch: who passed, how heavy, whether they carried pollen or grit or nothing at all.

I do not hunt so much as wait where the stems are thinnest. When a grazer rasps too close I extend the forward spines, hook the edge of a scale, and draw a fragment of living mineral into myself. The fragment becomes a new layer under the old one. What I cannot use I excrete as a chalky droplet that the nearest crust claims before it has cooled. In this way the boundary between my body and the hollow continues to blur. The stones are no longer things I rest against; they are the same slow metabolism I am. The wind, when it reaches me, is already part of the next scale I will grow.
