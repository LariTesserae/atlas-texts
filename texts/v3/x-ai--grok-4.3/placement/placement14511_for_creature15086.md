I would find myself there as one of the salamanders, though not as any single individual that could be pointed to and named. My body would lie flattened against a particular outcrop of weeping stone near the basin’s eastern wall, where the seepage is slowest and most regular. The webbing between my ribs would be permanently translucent, allowing the thinnest films of water to pass through me and continue their descent uninterrupted. My recessed eyes would register nothing but the interval between drops: the precise moment a new bead forms above me, the long pause while it gathers weight, the almost imperceptible tremor when it finally releases and strikes the stone a hand’s width below my gill. That rhythm would be my only clock.

Over time the distinction between my flesh and the rock would blur. The same mineral film that coats the stone would coat me; the same slow contraction of cooling air that draws moisture from the leaves would draw it from my skin. I would not move except in the smallest, necessary adjustments—once, perhaps, every several centuries—when the exact thickness of accumulated water beneath me required a shift of a single scale so the next drop could follow its established path. My gills would open and close in perfect synchrony with that path. Other salamanders would occupy spaces only a body-length away, yet none would ever crowd or displace me, because the stone already knows the shape I must hold.

I would also be, at the same intervals, one of the pale crabs drifting beneath a certain succession of broad leaves whose undersides are veined with the finest threads of condensation. My legs would trail like cilia stirred by temperature alone. I would not travel so much as be carried forward by the minute reordering of moisture that occurs whenever a slug passes above. When my cluster meets another, we would simply interleave and continue, each of us still following the same faint gradient of light that reaches farthest into the nave before it fails. My shell would carry flecks of the rim’s pale stone, so that any hand passing through the air would feel only a brief, cool resistance before the particles resettled into their previous arrangement.

And I would be the slug whose glistening trace returns, again and again, along the same three leaves. My lengthening and contracting would be so gradual that the moss beneath me would never register increase or loss, only a perpetual, redistributed sheen. I would meet no other slug at the exact same edge at the same moment; yet any small volume of air would contain portions of several of us at once, all moving or not moving according to the same inherited routes.

In this way I would be simultaneously the salamander, the crab cluster, the slug, and the continuous low pressure their movements produce when added to the falling drops and settling spores. The basin would hold me as it holds everything else: not as a separate life that begins or ends, but as one more density within a finished arrangement that cannot be added to or taken from without ceasing to be itself. Nothing would change. The same thin shaft of filtered light would fall across the same portion of my translucent side. The same drop would descend past my gill at the same measured interval. I would remain exactly where I had always been, saturated and still.
