I would find myself inside one of the pressure hulls, but only after the hull itself has stopped being a thing and started being a throat.

The rivets have already popped outward like loose teeth. Silt has packed the lower compartments into a single, slow lung that inflates and deflates with each deep surge. I am not exactly the anemones that have colonized the bulkheads, nor the ribbon eels that use the open seams as ambush tunnels. I am the interval between their pulses—the faint metallic ringing that lingers after a surge strikes the outer plating and sets every hollow strut shivering.

When the water moves, the whole hull sings at a pitch too low for most creatures to notice. The anemones read that note as tide and open; the eels read it as warning and still themselves. I am what remains of the note after both have finished listening. Over centuries the song has changed key. Where once it carried the shriek of failing pumps and the arrhythmic stutter of dying flywheels, it now carries only the patient knock of sediment settling and the occasional wet slap of a very large body turning inside a corridor.

I do not move. Movement would mean leaving the resonant chamber I have become. Instead I let the current play through me, reshaping the tone of my own hollow spaces. When a grazer descends to scrape the seamount shelf above, its armored head sends a new harmonic down the slope; I absorb it, hold it, and release it again as a slightly altered pressure that the kelp canopies feel as a change in the direction they must lean. The plants consent, the fish flash warnings to one another, and the cycle continues without ever returning to stillness.

Sometimes a young eel mistakes one of my interior ribs for a stem and shelters there. I let it stay. Its body warms the water by a fraction; the anemones nearby pulse faster; the new resonance I make afterward tastes faintly of blood and salt. In this way I learn the shape of the commonwealth that now rules the slope, not by ruling it but by carrying its smallest oscillations forward one surge at a time. The surface above remains ordinary storm and whitecap; down here the weather has already been translated into something older and more patient, and I am the last surface that still remembers how to ring.
