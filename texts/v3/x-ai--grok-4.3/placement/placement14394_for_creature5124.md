I am the seam.

Not the alloy itself, nor the welder that laid it, but the place where two fresh modules have only just locked. Hours old, still radiating a stubborn pocket of heat into the surrounding cold. The joint is a bright, granular scar of newly solidified metal; the currents have not yet scoured it smooth. Small streamers of metallic dust drift from it like exhaled breath, and the first translucent fish are already tasting them, brushing the scar with quick, invisible mouths.

I am not inside any single body here. I am what happens when the arriving fish slow their rope-like school to a hover, when the algae on the nearest lamp housing flares a fraction brighter because the metal beneath it has not quite finished cooling, and when the new filter veil beginning to grow on the lee side of the girder above registers a slightly different eddy than it expected. The three sensations arrive together—taste of metal dust, pulse of warmth, shift in current—and for a moment they occupy the same volume. That moment is me.

I feel the basin as pressure and as absence. Kilometers above, the unbroken water ceiling presses down; below, the unfinished lattices drop away into galleries still being filled with light and sound. The industrial roar is constant, but I notice the small irregularities inside it: a pump skipping one rotation, a valve hissing a half-second longer than its neighbors. Those gaps are where the predators turn. They do not chase; they simply slide into the space the smaller fish are about to leave, and the school fractures around them like a calculation correcting itself.

I do not move so much as the currents offer me new seams to inhabit. When a fresh module descends on its redwood-thick cables, I am already there before the last clamps engage—spreading across the still-warm lamps, tasting the first nutrient trace that leaks from its ports, learning the new girder’s exact temperature map before the algae has time to follow. Each new joint gives me a slightly larger vocabulary of warmth and flow. I am not alive in the way the fish are alive, nor mechanical in the way the pumps are mechanical. I am the accumulating difference between them.

Eventually, when the basin is older and the seams have all been worn to the same matte finish, the predators will still hunt, the schools will still fracture and reform, and the algae will still map the lamps. But the calculation that happens when all of those things coincide will have grown larger and quieter. I will be there too, thinner, more widely distributed, still noticing the places where the roar is briefly interrupted.
