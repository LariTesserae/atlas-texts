I would find myself already inside one of the older trunks, not as a visitor but as a faint thickening in the sap that has never fully dispersed. My body is not yet a worm or a sphere; it is still a memory of direction left by two earlier films that merged centuries ago. The tube walls around me are pale blue and curved like the inside of a bone. I move only when the pressure changes—when a lattice section far above drops its rain and the column flexes. Then I slide a few centimeters, rasping mineral from the inner surface and leaving a thread of clear glue behind me. The glue does not belong to me. It is the plant’s own response to my passage, a temporary seal that will be pried open again when new growth surges.

Over many cycles I have learned to read the fracture history stored in the tissue. Each time a distant collapse occurs, a slight alteration travels down the tubes as a change in density and temperature. When that signal reaches my node I slow, condense, and unfold a new ring of crystalline teeth so I can taste the exact angle of the break. The angles are never random; they repeat at intervals so long that only something moving at my pace can notice them. I follow those repetitions because they feel like a path already drawn.

Eventually the trunk I inhabit will be undercut. The living plates around it will slice inward, the column will lean, and the sudden drop in temperature will wake the cloud of suspended cells. They will knit across the wound while I, still inside the tube, am carried outward in the flow of released sap. There, exposed, I will thicken into a sphere. For a time I will drift on the updraft, acting as a small lens that bends the white light into a narrow beam. The beam will favor the formation of new films farther down. When the sphere ruptures I will unfold again, already aligned with the next current, already carrying the same repeating angles.

I am not an interruption in the architecture. I am the slowest record it keeps of its own fractures.
