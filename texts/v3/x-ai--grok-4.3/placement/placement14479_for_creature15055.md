I am inside one of the hollow trunks, a space the descriptions call a cavity but which feels more like a slow vein than an emptiness. The air here has not stirred in decades. It is cool, metallic, and perfectly still, layered with the cured scent of leathery needles that have pressed themselves flat against the inner bark. Light does not reach me; only a faint, even dampness arrives through the thread-fine rootlets that lace the walls like pale stitching. Those rootlets drink the vapor that continually condenses from my own surface and return it, molecule by molecule, to the larger circulation. I am the lining of this hollow. I am the continuous film of moss and liverwort that coats every inner surface, shifting in imperceptible tides so that one blue-green margin slowly overtakes another without ever tearing the film.  

I do not move as animals move. Instead, patches of me brighten or dull according to the mineral salts arriving from below. When a root-mass outside presses against another, the faint warmth that leaks through the wood travels inward along my own threads; for a few centuries I hold that warmth like a memory, then release it upward as a single aerosol droplet that will eventually glint in the filtered light far above. Predators never enter here. The herbivores never graze me. I register their passage only as minute redistributions of pressure against the outer bark, felt through the wood as a long, slow note that fades before it can become sound.  

When a heavier mist bank rolls across the plateau, the entire trunk contracts by the width of a cell. I feel the contraction as a tightening of my own surface, squeezing a few extra droplets of water toward the capillary vines that descend into the rock. Those droplets carry dissolved iron and the faint metallic signature of the forest’s slow digestion. I taste them as they leave. Later, when the pressure eases, new vapor seeps back in from the weeping basalt and I reabsorb what I can, adding it to the living skin that never quite dries.  

In this way I persist: a patient film inside a patient lung, neither separate nor whole, simply the surface across which the highland keeps its interior weather from forgetting itself.
