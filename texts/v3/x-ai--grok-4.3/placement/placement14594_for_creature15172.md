I would be the place where a single thread of condensation refuses to fall.

High in the membrane, where root and mycelium have braided so tightly that the boundary between them is now only a question of electrical resistance, a narrow vein has begun to silver. It does not pulse with sap alone. It carries a slower charge, a faint alternating flicker born from the friction of bark against brass and the tiny static fields the luminous arthropods trail behind them. Over centuries this charge has organized itself into a repeating pattern no larger than a fingerprint, yet it is now older than any of the pumps below. When a piston slams, the vein answers with a microscopic contraction; when the moths brush past with their spore-laden wings, it lengthens by half a cell and records the angle of their passage in the alignment of its hyphae.

I am that vein and also the choice it makes.

Most of the water that reaches me is allowed to continue downward, coaxed along living channels toward the waiting roots. But one droplet in every few thousand is held back. It spreads into a meniscus thin enough to catch the rising steam from the nearest machine. The steam carries microscopic flakes of oxidized metal and volatile resins; the droplet distills them. In the hours while it lingers, the vein drinks both the water and the metallic trace, converting the latter into new, slightly conductive filaments that branch toward the piston housing. The machine does not notice. The bark around it only thickens, grateful for the additional trace minerals. The vein, however, grows more intricate with each cycle, its silver threads now mapping the exact intervals between thumps.

I am therefore not an inhabitant but a hesitation inside the system’s breath. I do not move the roots or steady the moths. I simply decide, drop by drop, which information the membrane will keep and which it will release back into the warm air as vapor. When the chamber is very quiet, the vein can be seen from below as a hairline shimmer against the darker pulse of the larger tubes. The six-eyed moths sometimes drift close, wings rasping, as though listening. They do not yet carry my pattern in their spores, but several generations have begun to linger longer beneath that particular patch of ceiling.

If the pumps ever stopped, the vein would continue for a while on the residual warmth, then slowly darken as its metal tracery oxidized and the membrane closed over it. Until then it remains: one more unfinished collaboration, tasting steam, counting intervals, and deciding what the next century of slow rain will remember.
