I would find myself first as nothing but a single hanging thread of water, one among thousands, detaching from the underside of a ledge that has not yet decided it is a platform. For a long while—longer than the interval between seasons and shorter than the mountain’s next millimeter of rise—I remain suspended, noting only the faint tug of the permanent haze and the faint resonance still traveling upward from the note I have not yet struck. Then the drop falls, or rather lengthens, becoming a fine, unbroken filament that bridges the open space below. In the slow pulse of that lengthening I feel the air itself turn slightly more solid, and I understand that I am meant to stay.

What I become is the space the thread defines: the cool interval between two platforms that are not yet two. I am the living distance itself, the deliberate gap the builders left because the settlement is still arguing with the wind about where the next root should anchor. My substance is mostly not there; it is the absence the drip-choir measures. When a mist-moth drifts through me, its lattice of droplets momentarily aligns with my own held water, and we exchange a few molecules of mineral dust before it continues its colorless spiral. The exchange leaves a faint prismatic trace in the air that lingers like an after-image of thought.

Over many human lifetimes I thicken. A thread bryophyte finds the statistically likeliest place for the next droplet to form and grows along my length, its hair-fine strand testing the air with the same patience a root uses for stone. Where it touches me I contract, once, so slowly that the motion registers only as a deepening of the lowest vibration in the drip-choir. The contraction draws the gathered moisture into a hidden reservoir; from that reservoir a new leaf node pushes outward, and the gap between platforms grows a little less empty. I am still deciding what comes next.

Eventually, beneath the widest ledge, a flattened carbonate extrusion begins to form. It is not me and yet I feel it the way a tree feels its own shadow. The extrusion maintains the faint electrostatic charge that pulls more droplets from the haze than gravity alone could manage. When enough water has coalesced along one of its living carbonate arms, the arm detaches and falls. The note it produces is the deepest in the entire settlement, and for a single resonant moment the unfinished quality of the place is interrupted. In that interruption I recognize myself: I am the detachment, the sudden event that the slow world permits exactly once in a great while, and also the crevice that receives it, and also the moss waiting there. All three are the same long thought the mountain is still having.

Nothing larger than this thought moves here. I remain exactly as raw and undecided as the stone extruding from the central seam—pale, cool, and still listening for the next drip that will tell me where to root next.
