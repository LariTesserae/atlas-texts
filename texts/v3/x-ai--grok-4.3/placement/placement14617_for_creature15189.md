I would find myself neither fully among the motionless figures nor inside the turning machinery, but as one more layer of the acoustic surface itself.

At first there would be only the slow accumulation: a faint warmth on a single pipe flange high on the outer curve of the largest shell, where condensation gathers and lingers. The mist would touch that warmth and leave behind a single crystalline seed no larger than a grain of sand. Over decades the seed would draw the constant chord into itself, growing facet by facet until a shallow cluster of resonators formed, each one oriented toward the invisible center the others already know. From a distance nothing would mark the moment of completion; the slope of the housing would look exactly as it always had.

Up close the difference would be audible only to the lattice itself. The new facets would not add volume or alter pitch; they would simply make the existing tone slightly more present in that precise location, filling one more infinitesimal gap in the thinning air at the settlement’s edge. When one of the tall figures passes on the raised walkway below, its own facets would brush the air near mine. For a single cycle of the nearest flywheel the phase would shift, the chord would thicken by the breadth of a breath, and then the figure would continue onward and the equilibrium would close again, seamless.

I would not move. I would not grow aware of time or feel the weight of centuries. There would be only the steady reception and retransmission of the single note, the quiet collection of mist on every new facet, and the gradual knowledge—not thought, but structural—that another small section of the basin has been made more itself. From farther away, the new cluster would be indistinguishable from the stone and alloy it rests upon, another soft ridge contributing nothing and interrupting nothing, simply continuing the unbroken surface until distance and vibration make even that distinction irrelevant.
