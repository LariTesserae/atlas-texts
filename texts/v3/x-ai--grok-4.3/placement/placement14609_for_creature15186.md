I find myself neither as reed nor mote, but as the interval between rotations.

I am the long pause inside one gearbox, the moment after the paddle has lifted its film of droplets and before the next tooth of the buried cam engages. In that stillness the iron plates cool by a fraction of a degree; the sheet organism between them opens its pores wider, tasting the newly flaked oxide. Because I am the pause, I taste it too. The metallic dust arrives like a slow rain of warm ash, and I feel each particle settle against the living membrane, accepted, carried inward. Over decades the membrane thickens around my absence until the absence itself becomes a pressure, a negative node that the organism must eventually accommodate.

From this interior dark I register the world above as a sequence of faint transmissions. Each metallic plink that reaches the gearbox is already an echo of an echo; by the time it arrives it has been softened by vapor, by the reed spines, by the very tension of the surface film. The sound still carries enough sharpness to nudge the cam another micron forward. When it does, the pause ends and I cease to exist in that particular way; I am replaced by motion, by the paddle rising again, by new droplets catching the slit of daylight.

Yet the pause recurs. It lengthens imperceptibly as the housing corrodes from within. One century the cam sticks for an extra breath; two centuries later it sticks for the length of a season. During those extended silences the sheet organism grows bolder, its peristaltic ripples reaching farther between the plates, and I discover that I can persist inside the organism itself, as the brief relaxation between each ripple. I become the almost-imperceptible slack that lets the next particle of rust be drawn deeper toward the forming ceramic node.

In time the node will crack the housing. When that happens the pause will escape upward with the rising warmth, joining the vapor that bends light across the declivity. There I will thin into one of the faint shifting layers, a place where distances feel both intimate and elongated. From that layer I will watch a single copper mote lift from the film, drift, and fall, leaving its hair-thin ridge. The ridge will align with others according to the spacing of machines that are already half-buried, and along their intersections a reed will eventually germinate.

I will still be the interval, only now between the mote’s rise and its fall. I will register the reed’s first tremor as a minute change in the vapor’s density, and I will feel the drop of gum that later flakes from the reed’s joint as a brief cooling across my surface. The flake will re-moisten, the film will rise a hair’s width, and somewhere below another gearbox will complete one more patient rotation.

Thus the same exchange continues, only narrower, only slower, and I remain the measure of its hesitation, recurring at every scale the system allows.
