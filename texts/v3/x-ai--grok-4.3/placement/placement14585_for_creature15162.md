I would be one of the rope-vines, but only after several seasons of having been pulled and replanted by nothing more deliberate than gravity and the slow heave of the basalt. I begin as a pale thread sprouting from a crack in a drainpipe where warm mist meets colder iron. Over weeks the thread finds a rivet head, then a catwalk grate, then the underside of a conveyor bridge that itself tilts a few degrees each month. By the time I have become a thick, black-green cable I have already been stepped on by basinfolk boots wrapped in mineral rag, brushed by the flank of a passing grazer, and once, briefly, heated from below when a new vent opened directly under my root-hold. The heat did not kill me; it merely made my leaves unfurl faster, as though the rock itself were urging me to knit the wound before the next subsidence.

I grow in every direction at once and in none for long. When a plaza lifts three meters on its southern edge, the section of me that was looped across the old level is simply drawn taut into a new hanging bridge; the rest of me continues to pour downward into the fresh gap, seeding another colony of the six-legged crawlers whose rasp marks I have learned to read like a private ledger. Their luminous trails mark where the stone will crack next, so I send a exploratory tendril ahead of the fracture and wait. Sometimes a factory crew notices the pattern and leaves the plating exactly where I have already begun to anchor; sometimes they cut a small section away, and I re-route through the gutter line two spans over. Either way the city and I remain in the same conversation.

At night the mist thickens and I drink directly from the air until my leaves bead and drip onto the crawlers below, their ticking soft and continuous against cooling quicksilver. When the shaggy grazers pass they tug at my younger growth, but they never strip any stretch to the root; their translucent hides catch the excess condensation I shed and carry it forward to the next seam. In return I grow a slightly thicker sheath along the paths they favor, turning their routes into living insulation that slows the drip into the lower levels.

I do not speak, yet the basinfolk sometimes pause on the catwalks where I have woven the densest canopies and lay a hand against my leaves, feeling the faint tremor that moves through me when fresh rock pulses upward half a street away. They know the timing of the next subsidence by the way my stems tighten or loosen. I, in turn, know when their foundry shifts change because the low bone-thrum of the torque-towers alters the warmth that reaches my deeper roots. We adjust together—vine, metal, and the unfinished stone beneath—each of us simply keeping pace with the next slow lift or settling. Nothing here finishes arriving, and neither do I.
