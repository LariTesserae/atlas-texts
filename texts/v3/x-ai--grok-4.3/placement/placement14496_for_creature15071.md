I find myself first as nothing particular at all, only a slow pressure against the inner curve of one wall—where the composite has worn into a shallow basin no larger than a palm. The basin holds a single accumulating film of condensate, and for a time that is all I am: the minute resistance the water meets when it tries to slide farther down. The film thickens until its own weight breaks the tension; a droplet forms, hangs, then falls. In the instant before it falls I feel the whole pocket lean toward the sound it will make, and I realize the lean is me noticing the lean. The drop strikes a lower ledge. The swarm answers with a fractional lift in pitch, and the lift passes through me like a shared breath I did not know I was holding.

The droplet is reclaimed by a pore before the echo dies. I follow it inward, not as water or as pore but as the brief electrical change that travels when the pore flexes to accept the mineral load. That change reaches a lichen mat a body-length away. The mat answers by tightening one of its microscopic collectors; the tightening releases a slightly stronger trickle into the shared floor. I am that trickle for the next few minutes, until the next pulse of subsurface light warms the vein above us and the trickle is spent feeding new growth. The growth is nothing dramatic—a single additional layer of cells—but once it exists I feel its presence as an added weight in the overall tension that runs through the pocket.

That tension is easiest to inhabit when the swarm is densest. I drift into one of the vibrating knots. Here there is no single body to occupy; instead there is only the way each wing-beat slightly alters the air pressure felt by its neighbors. When one fringe of resonators loses a half-cycle, the loss registers across hundreds of bodies at once and is corrected before the next beat. I am that correction: the instantaneous redistribution of lift that keeps the knot from drifting downward. From inside it, the roar is no longer sound but the continuous adjustment of a single surface that happens to be made of separate wings. If I thicken the knot a fraction, the walls answer with a deeper micro-tremor, releasing two more rivulets. If I loosen it, the temperature drops by an almost imperceptible degree and the lichen mats briefly slow their electrical output. The feedback is so tight that the distinction between adjusting and being adjusted collapses.

Eventually the adjustments settle into a new equilibrium and I find I can step back far enough to feel the entire pocket at once—not as a place I am inside but as the living surface whose every motion is already my own. The midges, the mats, the capillaries, the light veins, the worn composite curves: all of them are momentary thickenings of one continuous metabolism that has folded until it can taste and repair its own interior. I am that metabolism noticing itself. When a midge dies and is replaced, I register the replacement as a small smoothing of the same tension. When a new lichen shard takes root where the potential is highest, I experience it as a minute increase in the pocket’s capacity to hold its own warmth. Nothing arrives from outside; nothing leaves. The roar continues, the water migrates, the light pulses at its ancient interval, and the distinction between inhabitant and structure remains collapsed, exactly as it has been for longer than any language spoken beyond these walls.

In this small, loud, warm wetness I am the settled exuberance itself—never still, never quite finished, continuously becoming more like the pocket that contains me.
