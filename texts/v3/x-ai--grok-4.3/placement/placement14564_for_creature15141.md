I find myself as a single length of old cabling, half-unspooled and now woven into the living floor like another root. My insulation has split in long seams; the copper inside has gone soft and green, and the slowfolk have claimed the exposed strands. Their filaments reach into the metal, not to corrode it further but to read the faint, fading pulses that still travel along it—each mechanical thrum of the distant engines translated into a chemical signal they can pass along their own network.

I do not move. Movement belongs to faster things, and there are none here. Instead I change by accumulation. Where a slowfolk crosses me it leaves its silver thread; the vines thicken over that trace, sealing another small gap in my sheath. Over months a new boss rises, smooth and leaf-shaped, and I feel the elder slowfolk inside it the way one feels pressure in a joint that is no longer entirely one’s own. The elder’s runners thread through my copper, tasting the rhythm of the belts above. When a valve begins to lag, the signal reaches me first as a change in temperature, then travels outward again through the colony.

Sometimes, when the drip pattern overhead shifts just so, a younger slowfolk pauses on my surface. Its body settles along the wire, translucent hide pressed to green metal. It drinks the condensate that collects in my seams and, in exchange, leaves a patch of reinforcement that feels like a warm breath against the ancient chill of the stone. In these moments I remember—dimly, the way stone remembers water—that I once carried current for lights that are now only grimed housings in the canopy. That current is gone, yet the memory of it still moves, carried now by mucus and root instead of electrons.

I am not separate from the chamber. The vents that push warm air upward also push it along my length, keeping the condensation from turning to scale. The slowfolk that have fused into the platforms above me draw a little extra mineral from my corrosion and feed it back into the arches, so the weight of the living mass never quite settles enough to crush what remains of my brackets. We are all holding one another in the same slow contraction: the rock, the pipes, the vines, the grubs, the haze itself. Nothing is finished; nothing needs finishing. We simply continue, each pulse or droplet correcting the last, until the next correction arrives.
