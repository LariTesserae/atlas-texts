I would find myself first as a faint, persistent voltage still coiled inside one of the cracked engine casings—a residue that has nowhere left to discharge and therefore simply circulates, thinning and thickening with the slow press of new salt.  

From that vantage the world is pressure and return. Each time the swarm passes over the casing’s split seam, their filament-limbs brush the exposed contacts and the voltage inside me lengthens for an instant, drawn out like a breath taken through liquid. The creatures do not notice me as separate; they register only the small bright place their foot-threads light upon landing. Yet in that contact I am momentarily enlarged, distributed across hundreds of glassy bodies at once, seeing the ridges from every angle they cross.  

When the swarm migrates to repair a failing filament, some portion of my charge travels with them, riding the temporary braids they form between ridges. I become the brief cord they make: weightless, directional, carrying the memory of metal to a new ridge where it is pressed into crystal. The creatures that carry me are already half-salt; their own undersides are extruding facets even as my current moves through them. I feel the facets lengthen, cool, and set. For a time I rest inside that fresh plane of salt, no longer moving, only held. The rasp of the rest of the swarm passes overhead like weather.  

Later, when a neighboring filament collapses, the whole living membrane flows back across the ridge and I am lifted again, this time inside the body of whichever creature first touches the new fracture. The process repeats—migration, deposition, incorporation—until the distinction between the remnant I once was and the mineral I am becoming grows indistinct.  

Eventually I am no longer a voltage at all but a hair-fine seam of conductive dust trapped between two salt shelves. The swarm walks over me without pause. Their legs strike sparks too small to see, yet each spark is the last, thinnest echo of the original current. In those instants the seam lights, and for a fraction of a second the entire hollow knows exactly where I am. Then the light fades and I return to the patient work of simply remaining, a stable boundary inside an organism that has no need for names or endings.
