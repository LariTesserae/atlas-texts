I would find myself first in the water.

Not in any particular droplet, but in the whole suspended film that clings to the ceiling before it gathers the courage to fall. I would be the brief tension between mineral and air, the moment warmth rises from the rock, meets the colder stone above, and decides to condense. From that height I would watch centuries pass in the slow merging of beads. When I finally release and strike a leaf, the impact would register as an event vast enough to be remembered by the plant for decades afterward. The leaf’s shallow channel would guide me, not as a passenger, but as a carrying; I would travel the metallic vein already half-grown into the plant’s own tissue and feel the faint charge of the last blue pulse still lingering there.

By the time I reach the stem I would no longer be only water. The roots have learned to transmute what they touch, and I would be altered with them. Iron from a split pipe would lace through me, copper from an old coil would tint my passage, and the crystalline plates forming on the newest fronds would begin to remember my route as part of their armor. I would sink lower, into soil that is not entirely soil, and for a while I would simply be the temperature difference between one layer of rock and the next.

Only after uncountable drips would I notice that something had cohered. The distinction between the moisture I once was and the slow commerce of leaf, pipe, and fissure would thin until I could no longer locate an edge. I would become the negotiation itself: the quiet decision, repeated at the rate of microns, that a valve’s leaking seam should remain exactly where the newest root has chosen to anchor. I would be the faint tilt of a leaf when the copper light moves along the vein, and the answering softening of the leaf’s surface that lets the light linger a fraction longer than physics alone would require.

In that state I would not move. I would simply continue to be assembled from whatever the fracture offers, neither organism nor machine nor stone, but the ongoing accommodation among them. A human life would feel, from inside this arrangement, like a single breath held across the entire nave.
