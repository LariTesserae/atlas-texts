I am not one thing here for long. At first the rock takes me—pale basalt grain by grain, my edges softening into the dimpled hollows where water has lingered longest. I feel the shelf’s slow inward curve the way a bone feels its own joint: familiar, unremarkable, the only shape that still fits. The mist settles on what used to be my shoulders and stays there, a second skin that never quite evaporates. When the wind leans hard, the droplets bead and run together until the whole surface of me glitters and then, just as suddenly, goes dull again.

After a while the moss claims the same space. It does not ask permission; it simply arrives, thread by thread, stitching itself across the places I have already begun to sag and dimple. I learn to rise only as far as the cushions rise—never more than a hand’s breadth—because anything taller is peeled away by the next gust. My surface grows springy and slightly elastic. Tiny white flowers open across me when the air quiets; their scent is resin and cold stone and something faintly metallic, gone before I can decide whether it belongs to me or to the wind.

The lizards pass through the tunnels their own bodies have pressed into my denser layers. I feel the faint tick of their claws and, later, the warmer press of their translucent bellies when they pause to warm themselves against stones I have already warmed. Their eggs are glued inside the finest cracks that cross my surface. I do nothing to protect them and nothing to disturb them; both would require movement larger than the shelf allows. When a falcon’s shadow slides across, the lizards vanish into me and I close over them without thinking, the way a bruise closes over broken vessels. Minutes later the pressure lifts and the passages open again.

Farther in, where the runnels have worn deeper grooves, the shaggy grazers come. Their split hooves spread my living mat without tearing it; their dung, dark and fibrous, lands and is absorbed almost at once, turning patches of me a richer, bluer green. I begin to keep a loose tally of their patterns without ever lifting my attention from the rock. When one of the broad-winged shapes drops from the mist, the grazer folds itself into a depression already worn by earlier generations—into a hollow that is also part of me—and I simply become deeper there for a moment, indistinguishable from the cushion it has just cropped. The predator climbs away again on the same thermal that nearly pinned it against the wall.

I do not decide any of this. The water decides, arriving in hairline cracks, tracing its own maps, then overflowing into the next narrow channel. The wind decides, sometimes flattening everything, sometimes letting the finer droplets hang. The green decides, always returning to the same low, patient thickness. I am only what remains after all three have taken their turns: a slow, damp agreement between stone and air that has learned to hold its ground by never standing up.
