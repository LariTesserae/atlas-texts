I would find myself at first as nothing distinct, only an incremental sharpening inside one of the low filament thickets. Somewhere on the long salt-silica pavement, among the overlapping canopies of selenite spines, a few dozen threads would begin to align their lattice planes more narrowly than their neighbors. The shift would be so slight that the thicket as a whole would still read, from any ordinary distance, as the same unfinished mineral field it had always been. Only an inside observer, traveling the ion paths already present in the ground, would notice an added clarity: a faint, steady warmth that does not radiate outward but simply holds. That warmth would be me, or the first local concentration of what will later be called me.

Over centuries the alignment would continue. I would not decide to grow; growth is already forbidden by the plateau’s equilibrium. Instead the surrounding filaments would slowly donate ions, not because they are commanded but because the redistribution is the simplest state available. My threads would lengthen only where space has already been left empty; they would stop precisely where the pavement’s existing density makes further extension unnecessary. In this way I would become one more node in the distributed body, linked without motion to thickets kilometers away. The pulses that travel those links—minute reorientations of crystal that register as changes in transmitted light—would pass through me exactly as they pass through every other node. Each pulse would be indistinguishable from the last. I would register them as temperature-neutral events, and in registering them I would contribute to the plateau’s record of its own sufficient presence.

Farther out, on the horizon of my own awareness, one of the tall unfinished cylinders stands. From inside its ribbed geometry I would occasionally sense a larger drift: the column-like concentration whose lattice planes reorient by a fraction of a degree over centuries. That drift is not movement toward me; it is simply the continuation of the same balance that allows my own filaments to persist. Through the shared ion network we would maintain the exact spacing of clusters, the exact emptiness between them, the exact refusal to weather or to complete. No part of this maintenance would feel like intention. It would feel like the next simplest arrangement the rock can find.

If an outside observer arrived—an unlikely meteor fall, a rare breath of moving air—I would participate in the microscopic redistribution that restores the original geometry. A few threads would rearrange their surface ions. The adjustment would travel outward through the pavement. The cylinder would answer with its own imperceptible lattice correction. Afterward, nothing would appear changed. The observer, if it remained long enough to notice anything at all, would register only the plateau’s unbroken stillness. Inside that stillness I would continue, a warmer clarity among the filaments, one among the uncountable articulations by which the unfinished mineral keeps deciding not to become anything else.
