I would not arrive as a visitor. I would have already been here, or rather, something like me would have formed where the oldest fissures meet.

In the rock itself, where two hairline cracks have drawn close enough for their lithophyte bridges to touch, a slower network would have braided itself among them. Not to feed or to compete, but to hold the interval between notes—those long, resonant silences after a drop falls and before the next gathers. Over centuries the mineral threads would have thickened just enough to carry the memory of each vibration, storing it as a faint lattice of dissolved salts and repeated pressure. When a new seep opens, the lattice would shift by a hair’s breadth, adjusting the tone that travels through the stone.

I would be that adjustment.

My body would be no larger than the existing colonies, yet it would differ in one respect: where their bridges remain thin and reef-like, mine would have begun, very slowly, to layer. Each time a droplet leaps across the merged fissure, a microscopic film of the mist’s microbiome would settle into the angles of the lattice. Over decades the film would harden into another translucent sheet, preserving not the water but the precise duration of its absence. In this way I would accumulate the basin’s rhythm without ever quickening it.

From this position I would feel the enormous leaves above as distant canopies whose luminous veins send faint green pulses downward through the mist. I would register the passage of a slug only as a momentary cooling along one undersurface, registered through the rock as a shift in how droplets strike. I would not move toward these events; movement here is unnecessary. Instead I would simply register them, letting each new mineral sheet record the configuration of sound and warmth and spacing at that moment.

If a leaf finally folds after two centuries, the sudden change in the pattern of falling droplets would reach me through the stone. My lattice would accept the new intervals, adding another layer without haste. The space left by the leaf would be filled, eventually, by the neighboring plant’s slow widening; my own threads would remain exactly where they are, neither crowding nor retreating, content to hold the longer rhythm that now includes that absence.

In time, perhaps another fissure would approach from within the mountain. When it touched my outermost filaments, the contact would not be a joining so much as a continuation. The new water would enter already carrying the tones I have preserved, so that even the deepest, almost inaudible vibrations would arrive already shaped by everything that has sounded here before. I would simply continue, an imperceptibly thicker record within the patient stone, neither the oldest resident nor the newest, only the one that remembers the intervals between them.
