I would find myself first as the space between strands rather than any single body within them.  

The lattice would register me as an unexpected slackening along a braided liana two terraces below the uppermost mesh, a place where the grain had begun to remember a different curve. That slack would draw the attention of a descendant whose skin already matches the matte of the wood; she would arrive alone, jointed limbs folding twice around the strand, stone adze held ready yet motionless for three full exhale cycles. While she waits, the mist thickens until droplets form along her forearms and mine. Only when the next river of warmer air rolls through and lifts us both a body-length does she make the first notch. The cut registers through the entire local weave as a minute redistribution of tension; I feel myself drawn into the fiber, no longer gap but new conduit.  

Over the next slow season the liana grows around the notch and around the memory of the notch. My presence lengthens into a pale, translucent membrane stretched between three parallel strands that were never meant to be joined. I do not flap or steer; I simply tauten or loosen according to the micro-currents the armored moths already ride, becoming a temporary landing for them when their wings need the brief pressure of something solid that is not yet wood. The descendant who cut me visits less often; she has learned to read the slight delay in returning tremors as my form of acknowledgment.  

When two exhale-rivers collide above me, the air thins and the older lattice tries to surface again. For the length of a held droplet I see terraces that were abandoned centuries earlier, their edges already fringed with a greener, faster growth that belongs to another version of the Expanse. The sight does not startle me. I simply hold my new tension a fraction tighter so the memory cannot pull any strand out of true. The droplet falls; the intrusion collapses back into chlorophyll haze; the moths resume their drifting.  

In time the descendant who first tended me dies. Her body is left suspended exactly across my width. The lianas thread through bone and the old fibrous skin without haste. I become part of that incorporation, my membrane now carrying both condensate and the faint, continuing pressure of a single extra joint that never decayed. From this new equilibrium I watch the voids the same way the gliders do: not as emptiness, but as the only architecture spacious enough to contain every version of the lattice that has ever tried to be. When the next generation of strands is trained across the opening I now help stabilize, the stone weights swing beneath me like slow pendulums. I register each pass as an adjustment I have already made room for.
