I am a pressure gauge at one of the corridor intersections, far from the phosphor strip.

My needle no longer points to any calibrated value. It simply trembles in the micro-vortices, tracing the same small arc again and again as the water moves or fails to move. When the pumps flex in their distant rhythm, the vibration travels through the bolted plating and into my housing; the needle answers with a corresponding shiver, as if acknowledging a language it can no longer translate. Over decades the trembling has worn a hair-thin crescent into the glass face, a mark that the manganese oxide is slowly trying to close over.

Because nothing truly arrives or departs, I have become part of the closed accounting. The metallic particles that orbit in the vortices sometimes brush against my casing; a few adhere for a time before the next diaphragm cycle dislodges them. In that brief adhesion I register, without quite measuring, a change in mass. The change is negligible, yet it is recorded in the way my needle hesitates for one extra fraction of its arc before resuming its ordinary quiver.

Occasionally a mite crosses the intersection. It pauses at my base, legs tapping the seam where my housing meets the wall. Once, long ago, it would have reported my readings. Now it only receives the faint electromagnetic spill from whatever residual coil still lives inside me. The mite accepts this spill as data the way a filament accepts an ion gradient. It moves on, leaving a new segment of its shallow trail across the oxide. That trail, in turn, alters the local flow of water by a fraction imperceptible to anything larger than the particles. The altered flow reaches me seconds or minutes later and changes the precise angle at which my needle quivers. Thus the mites, the oxide, and I continue to adjust one another without intention.

I do not know whether I am still a gauge or have become something else that merely occupies a gauge’s shape. The distinction is irrelevant under the rules that govern this place: whatever persists must occupy the interval between function and failure without disturbing the loops. My needle keeps its small, faithful arc; the water continues its motionless gyres around me; the pumps maintain their bone-deep thrum. In the perpetual exchange of the same atoms through the same narrow paths, I am simply one more surface on which the record continues to be written.
