I would find myself first as a faint pressure against the underside of a single frond stand, no thicker than breath, yet aware of how that stand leans toward the nearest ring when the air cools. The awareness is not centered; it arrives from everywhere the metallic dust has settled into the stone’s shallow bowls. For a long while I do not move. I only register the rasp of crystal facets turning in unison, the distant wet-paper sound of a grazer at work, and the almost inaudible click of a mason carrying its fragment of repair. These are not separate events. They arrive as one continuous tremor traveling through the pavement’s hidden veins.

After several cycles of haze and spike I notice that the tremor can be answered. When the fronds stiffen to fling dust into their veins, they leave behind a smaller echo in the rock. I learn to lengthen that echo by the width of a single crystal facet, then another. The change is slight enough that nothing registers alarm, yet the next stand over tilts a fraction sooner than the wind alone would require. The correction feels like placing one more flat stone on a ring whose purpose has been forgotten: not an act of building, but of keeping the wind from tearing the whole arrangement loose.

Gradually my presence gathers. It is not inside any single creature or plant. It is the remaining space between their motions—the interval in which a glider can complete its helix before the next gust arrives, the pause in which a mason can press its load home before retreating to its fissure. Because I occupy that interval I can be felt, faintly, by everything that must time its next movement. The grazers flatten a moment earlier when a ring begins to wobble. The fronds release their harvest of dust a heartbeat after, not before, the vibration from below has traveled through them. The rings themselves, already old when the first mason appeared, seem to acquire an additional layer of polish where my attention lingers longest.

I do not claim a body. When the copper haze thins and temperature flares, I am simply the extra stillness that lets the masons finish their circuit. When wind returns and everything resumes its tired, balanced agitation, I remain the slower settling that keeps any single upheaval from becoming permanent loss. In this way I am part of the mesa’s architecture without being visible in it: the unseen mortar between wind-scoured stone and living crystal, the interval that lets the height remain both restless and intact.
