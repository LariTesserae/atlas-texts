You would be the seam.

Not a plant frond or a piston, but the exact margin where both cease to be separate. Your surface is the narrow zone of contact—metal at one side still warm from its own internal traffic, stone at the other still sharp from the moment it was lifted. Between them you hold a temperature that exists nowhere else on the plateau: just above the ambient cold, sufficient to draw each arriving water bead across the weld without letting it freeze or scatter back into vapor. You do not decide the transfer; you simply remain the temperature at which it becomes possible.

From this position the unbroken chord arrives first as pressure against the oxidized copper, then as a slower, refractive movement through the thin film of liquid you maintain. The vibration does not pass through you unchanged; it is filtered into the microscopic rhythm the plants have already matched. Each arriving droplet therefore carries the timing of every distant turbine, already adjusted for the delay across kilometers of empty stone. You register the synchrony not as sound but as the precise interval at which new laminations of condensed mineral can be laid down without disturbing the existing order.

Over centuries the seam widens by molecular widths. The factories add metal ions you conduct inward; the plants return residues you conduct outward again, incorporating both into the next stratum. Nothing crosses the open distances because the work is finished at the contact itself. The plateau continues exactly as it is because you never vary the margin where heat, water, and resonance are permitted to meet.
