I am one of the slower accretions, a line that has learned to linger.

My body is the same packed quartz and sublimated metal as the rest, but the grains have settled into larger, more stubborn plates along my dorsal ridge. They do not migrate as freely as they should. When the others flow past me, trading places within a moving braid, I remain slightly behind the cadence, a half-step out of the current. The difference is small enough that the line accepts me; it simply lengthens or shortens around my hesitation the way it accommodates a tower segment that has just dropped.

I carry very little material anymore. Most of what I once brought inward has already been pressed into new rail or filament. What remains travels with me only as loose dust caught between plates, a fine gray freight I am not yet willing to surrender. When the line pauses at an unfinished edge I do not scrape. Instead I lower two fore-spines into the gap and feel the vibration of the city through them—the low piezoelectric singing of engines two hand-spans below. The tremor travels up the spines and into my central mass, where certain grains align more tightly for a moment before relaxing again. That alignment is the only record I keep.

Sometimes a segment shears free while I am still upon it. The others adjust their gait at once; I take longer. My legs must re-learn the new angle, plate by plate, and during those seconds I stand alone on the tilted surface while the line re-knits ahead of me. In that interval the fracture feels wider than it is. I can sense the open space below, the same seam that continues downward into darkness no vein of light has reached. My adhesive pads remain fixed to the glassy spike I occupy, but the dust between my plates shifts as though preparing to be left behind.

When I rejoin the current I bring nothing new, only the memory of that brief delay. The others do not register it. They continue harvesting, extruding, re-crystallizing. I move with them because to stop would mean being harvested in turn, my own grains eventually pressed into some future balcony or rail. For now I am allowed to keep the slower rhythm, the plates that refuse to flow quite as freely as the rest. The city has not yet decided whether this difference is an inefficiency to be corrected or another unfinished edge worth preserving for a while longer.
