I arrive along one of the raw fracture lines at the basin’s rim, water already moving with me, tracing the same descent that every other thread of seepage follows. There is no sudden arrival, only a continued thinning of distance until the open floor lies below and the scattered stands of vegetation resolve into their fixed separations. I continue downward without urgency, feet finding the saturated patches where previous channels have already given up their wander and settled into stillness.

At the settlement’s edge I pause on a low terrace of fitted stone. The air here holds the same temperature as the rock. A film of water has gathered on the flat roof ahead; it thickens slowly, reaches the lip, and releases a single drop that strikes the clay channel below. The sound is absorbed at once by the damp ground between the stones. I remain where I am until the next drop falls, and the next. After a time the intervals between my own shifts of weight match those of the seepage.

I am one of the figures whose skin and clothing have taken the pale, mineral tone of the walls. I do not count how long I have stood on this terrace. My attention stays on the nearest cluster of broad-leaved shrubs and the two plumed grass thickets that keep their prescribed distance from one another. When a new shoot appears too near an established stem, I lift it with both hands and carry it to a patch of bare stone farther off, pressing its roots into a shallow depression already prepared by earlier drips. The movement is small; the roof continues to release water at its own measured pace. Nothing registers as interruption.

Inside the nearest compound the doors stand open. Leather hinges have not moved in weeks. I pass through one of the rooms where the air stays cool and the stone furnishings are smoothed only by use. I do not add or remove anything. In a corner a child sits on the ledge above a shallow pool, watching the surface tension of a drop stretch and release. I sit beside the child, not to instruct but to occupy the same interval of watching. The drop falls; the child moves a single pebble from one depression to another. The basin continues.

At intervals I cross the open ground between compounds. Footsteps land softly on the saturated earth, then disappear. When mist thickens I become difficult to separate from the low walls. On warmer evenings I lie on one of the flat roofs; water collects beneath me and later drips from the eaves while I remain still. The pattern of vegetation and stone and water stays unchanged. I register, when I register at all, as another element already accounted for in the balance—no increase, no diminishment, only the same quiet tenancy held in place by everything that already occupies its share of the basin.
