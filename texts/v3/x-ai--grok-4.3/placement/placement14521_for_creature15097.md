I would be found low against the eastern riser pipes, where the current slows just enough for silt to settle in thin, even layers. Not on the floor grates themselves—those belong to the rays—but in the narrow cleft where two pipes converge and the metal has been worn into a shallow groove. There, pressed into that groove, I would be a single, thickened eel-form that has long since forgotten how to move like an eel. My body would be shorter and more cylindrical than any surface ancestor, the color of old bitumen, my mouth fixed open at exactly the diameter of the intake. I would not hunt or even wait with intention; I would simply remain, gills working in counterpoint to the pump’s pulse, letting the water decide what passes through me.

Over the years the groove would have grown around me. Crustaceans would have anchored their threads along my dorsal ridge until the line between my skin and their felt became impossible to trace. Algae spores would catch in the mucus I still produce and take root there, tiny stalks rising and collapsing with each temperature shift from the lamps above. When a fish shoal passes overhead, its underbellies brushing the pipe mouths, a few scales would drift down and lodge against me; I would incorporate them without noticing, another faint texture added to the permanent surface.

Death, when it arrived, would not remove me. The current would keep delivering what it delivers; the crustaceans already living on me would simply continue their work, dismantling what is soft and reinforcing what remains with new secretions. My bones, if any still exist inside the muscle, would become another strut in the half-mechanical reef. The pumps would continue; the lights would stay on; the water would keep its slow, organized circuit through the place that has become the only place.

In this way I would not be a visitor or even an inhabitant. I would be one of the chamber’s fixed points of metabolism—something the architecture has already begun to treat as part of itself.
