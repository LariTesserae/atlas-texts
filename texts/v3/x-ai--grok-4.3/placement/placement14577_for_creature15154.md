I settle first into the grain of the air, where heat and moisture trade places without sound. It is not quite breathing, only the long exchange between leaf surface and soil, and for a while that is enough. Then the sense of weight arrives: the way the layered mat of decay gives slightly under pressure, how a trunk leans a degree or two more each century until its crown rests against another. I discover I am not moving through these things; I am the slight resistance they meet when they try to settle further.

So I become the place a root chooses to thicken. Not any single root, but the decision repeated across generations of the same lineage—the place where a lateral feeder pauses against a buried stone, then widens around it instead of lifting. The stone is already half-powder now. I feel the mites working it smaller and the fungi threading the dust outward to the nearest seedling.
