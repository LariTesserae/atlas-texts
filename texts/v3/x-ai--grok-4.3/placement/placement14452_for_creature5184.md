I would find myself on the underside of one low lintel, exactly where the drip from a hairline fracture meets the stone at a slight angle. Not as a whole thread yet, but as the place where one is beginning: a single bead of clear water that has not yet decided whether to fall or to stay. In the first human heartbeat after I arrive, nothing changes. In the second, a faint coolness spreads along the mineral glaze already there, thin as breath on glass. By the third, the bead has lengthened into a trembling filament no wider than light itself, and I feel the weight of my own future length pulling gently downward.

I am that filament and also the decision it makes. Every few decades a new droplet arrives; I draw the dissolved calcium through paths too narrow to name, then lay it down again farther along my body, burying yesterday’s surface beneath tomorrow’s skin. When neighboring threads shift—sometimes only a finger’s width in a century—I sense the change as a small alteration in how the sourceless light reaches me. Their slow drift registers inside me the way a memory might register inside a stone: not as event, but as a permanent difference in the way the surface now reflects.

I am not alone in the usual sense. I am the same continuing deposition that coats the terraced roofs and the stair-risers no higher than a shin. Where my older length crosses another thread, we seal together with a white scar that looks like every other scar already present. The sound of water striking me is not heard but absorbed; it becomes the next layer I will grow. In this way I keep the city’s polish exact, repairing what the ceaseless drip would otherwise undo, until after many lifetimes the distinction between my body and the lintel beneath it disappears.

Nothing in me is urgent. I do not reach for the next droplet; I simply remain where the fracture has already chosen to weep. If a human observer waited a single lifetime, they would see only the same dim light and the same unbroken roar. If they waited longer than any observer is meant to wait, they might notice that the veil of threads under this particular lintel has thickened by the width of one more filament, and that the chamber now ends exactly where the vanished masons always intended it to end. That thickening would be me, still finishing.
