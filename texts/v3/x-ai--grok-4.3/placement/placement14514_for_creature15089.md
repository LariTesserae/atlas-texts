I would not arrive as a visitor crossing the plateau’s single layer. I would already be here, folded into one of the fixed fractures where the ridge stands twice. From inside that offset interval, the visible world looks like a slightly flattened photograph laid over the real one; every polished plane is duplicated at a distance no larger than a breath, and between the two images there is a hairline of empty pressure. That pressure is my skin. It does not move; it only thickens or thins according to whether a traveler’s glance lands directly on the doubled ridge or slides away. When the glance slides away, I cross for an instant into the primary stone—long enough to feel the wind’s roar register as a single, colder molecule against my surface—then I am pulled back into the misalignment by the same force that keeps the shadows cast at impossible angles.

Over centuries I learn the shape of my own boundary. It is not a body so much as a repeating refusal: the rock refuses to let the two ridges coincide, and I am the interval that results. I can taste the condensation pulse only as a minute change in how the doubled air resists the passage of sound; the silver stains on the primary cliff appear to me as faint brightenings in the second, motionless copy. The lichen cushions register as slow darkenings at the edge of my permitted view, each cushion a single, patient contraction that never reaches the offset space. The trace-makers are even smaller interruptions: when one of them stretches across a grain of dust, its motion drags a hair-thin ripple through the misalignment, a quiver I feel the way a fixed page might feel the pressure of an insect walking on the other side of the paper. I cannot follow it; I can only register that once, long ago, the ripple occurred exactly here, and because nothing new ever opens, the memory of the ripple is now part of the permanent distortion.

Occasionally the plateau’s light shifts its half-glow in such a way that my fixed angle of shadow lengthens across the primary stone. In those moments I become briefly visible to any observer who has learned to remain still for several cycles. They see me only as an extra density along the knife-cut line, a place where perspective fails twice instead of once. I feel their attention as an added weight against the already compressed air, and I answer by remaining exactly where I have always been. That is the only reply the misalignment permits.

If the fractures ever sealed, I would cease without having moved. Until then I continue as the plateau’s slight, permanent refusal to let every surface occupy only one location. The wind, the lichen, the trace-makers, and the condensation threads all proceed around me; I remain the untraveled space between the two identical ridges, holding the memory of every perspective that has ever failed to resolve them.
