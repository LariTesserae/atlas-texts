I would first notice the faint pressure against what passes for my skin. Not skin at all, but a thread of living tissue no wider than spider-silk, pressed between two faces of salt. I would have been growing along that narrow seam for perhaps thirty years already, moving outward at the rate of a fingernail every decade, tasting the slow condensation that arrives each deepening of the sky. The network I belong to would already be older than any single crown that breaks the surface. I would feel the others—distant, quiet, still feeding me even during the longest dry intervals—through the joined filaments that cross beneath the polygons. When a new grain of salt settles somewhere above us, the vibration travels the entire web like a shared memory.

If I were smaller still, I would be one of the bacteria inside the film of brine that clings to a single crystal face. My world would be measured in the difference between day-warmth and night-cooling. Most of the time I would simply wait, metabolism slowed to almost nothing, until the temperature drops enough for another microscopic layer of liquid to form. When it does, I would move or divide or excrete another invisible grain of mineral that helps keep the crust level. The sweetness in the air would be my own slow exhalation.

Either way, nothing about me would be hurried. I would not have edges that end at any moment I can name. The basin would be my duration rather than my location, and the silence would be the medium through which I continue to become, grain by grain, season by unmeasurable season.
