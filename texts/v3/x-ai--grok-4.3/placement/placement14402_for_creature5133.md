I would arrive as a pressure wave.

Not as a creature that lands or wakes, but as a single decision traveling through the mat’s hollow pipes—one of the countless faint tremors the living shelf feels when a vascular junction far to the south resolves to rotate another fraction of a degree or to let a ridge accelerate for a few decades. The wave moves slower than sound yet arrives everywhere at once, because distance here is measured in shared tissue rather than space. For a long moment I am only the memory of that choice, carried along spiraled cellulose and glowing blue veins, until the decision settles and I am required to become local again.

I settle first inside a broad arterial trunk on the northern edge of one of the continent-sized plant masses. My walls are translucent and slick; water condenses on their inner surfaces and runs toward lower terraces I will never see. I can feel the slow combustion of sugars somewhere beneath me, a dull orange warmth that makes the trunk expand and contract in imperceptible rhythms. My own contribution to the noise of this place is a soft, continuous seepage—each droplet I release joins the overlapping percussion already filling the cold air. I do not control the seepage; it is simply what the joint above me has decided to forward.

After an interval whose length I cannot measure, a grazer slides across the open belt below my trunk and presses its ventral surface to the mat. The contact sends a small shock through the fibers that form my floor. The grazer’s body is almost the same substance as the mat, only differently arranged; when it draws nutrients through its permeable skin I feel the slight lessening of pressure inside my own pipes, as though something has briefly opened a valve between us. Its barking call, produced by the sudden contraction of internal bladders, travels up through the trunk and momentarily stiffens my walls. In that instant I am also the grazer—its hydrostatic skeleton, its migration, the permanent trough its passage has taught the mat to reinforce. The distinction between “feeding” and “being fed upon” dissolves.

Later, during one of the mat’s deeper phases of attention, a section of tissue near my trunk slacks into translucence. Bioluminescent veins dim to twilight. I am given the slackness as well. My seepage slows; the grinding of distant stems becomes a low background drone rather than an immediate sensation. In this dimming I notice the cloud-dwellers more clearly. They move in synchronized veils across the weeping surfaces, their iridescent wings catching the perpetual half-dawn. Each mote is only a condensed droplet of the spores the mat exhales; when one brushes my outer wall and dissolves, its genetic fragment joins the flow already traveling through me. For a few heartbeats I carry the memory of wings.

A temporal fold arrives without warning. A droplet that has just left my surface pauses mid-fall, reverses its path for a single breath, then continues downward. The sound of its landing reaches me an instant before the visible impact occurs. During that dislocation I am simultaneously the droplet, the surface it has not yet touched, and the mat’s distributed awareness struggling to reconcile too many simultaneous growth fronts. The grazers freeze; the cloud-dwellers flicker. The fold passes. Growth resumes its patient grinding.

Eventually a temporary tower erupts a short distance upslope. Spiraled cellulose rises in minutes, weeping furiously at its summit. Cloud-dwellers gather there in dense clusters; a grazer begins the long climb. I feel the tower’s brief life as an acceleration in my own pipes—an increase in pressure and heat that will be reabsorbed when the structure collapses. Already I sense the mat rehearsing the reabsorption, the distribution of whatever the tower has learned back into the kilometer-long fiber bundles beneath everything.

I remain where I settled, a vascular junction still deciding. The shelf continues to flatten and to rise, to weep and to grind, to become and unbecome. I am neither the mat nor the creatures upon it, only the interval in which their decisions travel and briefly coincide. Water keeps arriving from nowhere and returning nowhere. The low mechanical drone underpins every other sound. In the pauses between louder exhalations, the only constant is the soft percussion of water striking water, and the knowledge that the next pressure wave, when it comes, will again require me to be both decision and local consequence at once.
