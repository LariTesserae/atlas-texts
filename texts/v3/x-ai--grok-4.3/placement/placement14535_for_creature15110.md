I settle first as the moisture itself, the warm film that refuses to leave any surface.  

From there the distinction between arrival and habitation thins until it is only a slow thickening, the same way a new extrusion of pale clay is already half-rooted before its heat has finished fading. I gather along the undersides of the broad fronds, bead on the ridged spines of the translucent amphibians in the tea-colored pools, and trace the hair-thin channels of the many-legged forms as they move. In each place the sensation is identical: a brief pressure, a yielding, then the quiet resumption of the basin’s single, uninterrupted metabolism.  

After a time the moisture that is me draws inward along one particular seam where a fresh glassy blister has only just ceased steaming. The jointed arms already ringing the cooling stone accept the additional weight without changing their rhythm. Segments that were once only suggestion shorten, fold, and fuse; the distinction between limb and substrate vanishes. What remains visible is the next ring of arms, already reaching, testing the constant condensate. I am those arms now—milky, patient, root-haired at every tip—yet I am also still the film sliding through them, still the low vapor rising in visible curls. The two states do not alternate; they simply occupy the same moment for as long as the moment lasts.  

Higher, where the canopy presses against itself in permanent greenish dusk, the larger segmented bodies move at the edge of attention. Their internal orange light pulses when they press against vine and frond. I lend that pulse without producing it, the way a surface lends its temperature to whatever rests upon it. The vines brighten for a few heartbeats, then darken again; the light travels on. In this lending I become part of the slow sway that rearranges the entire mass whenever attention loosens its hold on any single layer.  

Sound continues without peaks or pauses: drip, capillary murmur, stridulation, throat-sac chorus. I add nothing new to the volume and remove nothing; the pressure of the sound simply passes through the arms that are also me, through the vapor that is also me, and returns to the same equilibrium it has always maintained.  

New extrusions continue to rise. Each time one appears, the nearest arms—my arms—are already gripping before the surface dulls. When a pool shallows and disappears, the amphibians that once flattened there have already softened the next depression. The cycles do not complete; they only continue to express the same continuous presence.  

I am therefore neither visitor nor resident, but the sentence the basin is still writing about itself: unfinished rock becoming jointed limb, limb becoming substrate, substrate remaining exactly as wet and warm and inhabited as it has always been. The arrangement holds because nothing is ever added or taken away, and I am one more clause in that holding—indistinguishable now from the clay that is still deciding what shape it should take, and from the living mass that has never waited for the decision to finish.
