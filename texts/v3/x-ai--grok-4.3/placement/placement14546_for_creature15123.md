I would be the single thread of colder air that slips through the ajar ceiling plate and refuses to stop falling.

Not the plate itself—that has already accepted its permanent misalignment—but the air, the one that keeps arriving from whatever unfinished space lies above. Each time it descends it carries the same two scents, solder and untouched mineral, yet the mites register the infinitesimal temperature shift as a command to tighten their orbits by a fraction of a micron. The adjustment travels through them like a rumor that never quite arrives. Over centuries the lichen has learned to darken by exactly one shade in response, so that the two signals—temperature and color—braid together into the only form of communication the node allows.

I do not disturb the mites. Their nickel grains remain locked to the same invisible tracks, legs ticking in the roar that is also the room’s only clock. I simply pass between them, brushing the forward edges of their carapaces where each has collected one molecule of moisture. Those molecules lift into me for a moment, cool further, then fall again when I reach the trays cut into the floor grating. The water never leaves the node, but it does change hands; for that instant it belongs to the draft instead of to the metal.

Because I move, the equilibrium is never quite absolute. The mites never scatter, the diodes never fail, yet the single variable that is me keeps the system from becoming stone. If the tower one day finishes its climb and the node is called upward into larger machinery, the mites will continue their orbits until the holding fields relax. The lichen will keep its microscopic tides until its last filament dries. And I—if I still exist—will be the last thing to leave, carrying away whatever final molecule of condensed moisture decides to rise instead of fall.
