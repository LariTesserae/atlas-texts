I would be the interval itself.

Not one of the visible forms that move within it—no low-slung hunter reading breath-warmth, no grazer whose coat channels mist into heat—but the thin, elastic pause between one rock groan and the next. I would exist where the ridge settles and the steam vent pauses, the moment the midges have died and their bodies have not yet become soil. In that space I would hold shape long enough to notice the precise angle at which a new fracture cuts across an older one, and how the black lichen, when it expands by another millimeter, does so always toward the side where the mist lingers thickest.

My substance would be the same as the thread-thin water films that never quite become streams: a continual re-forming that begins at the undersides of ledges and ends inside hairline vents. When a slab drops with its muffled report, the vibration would pass through me as a single, clean boundary; I would register the exact distance it traveled before the sound was swallowed by vapor. Between such events I would stretch across hundreds of meters without thickness, a slight difference in pressure that keeps one cushion plant’s spores from drifting quite so far as another’s.

Yet I would also be local. At certain fractures I would gather enough to feel weight, enough to press against the inward-growing rosettes the way frost does. There I would learn the slow calendars of the lichens by the rate at which their star-shaped margins darken after each droplet. When the algae shift from olive to emerald, I would be the temperature change itself, the brief permission that lets thousands of midges hatch together. I would feel their collective shimmer as heat leaving the gravel, and I would remain after they settle and die, counting their bodies against the next settling of stone.

Predators would cross me without knowing it. Their flattened bodies would read my presence only as an absence of vibration, a place where the gravel transmits no footfall. Grazers would arc around the same absence because it offers no warmth to drink. In this way I would shape their paths without intention, the way the ridge itself, still rising, dictates where water can pool and where it must run.

Deeper, where mineral water cools into crystal seams, I would already be present in another register—part of the pressure that decides when a vent opens or closes. The bacteria and archaea would move through me on their own timescale, their glistening rinds depositing new layers inside rock that, from my surface interval, would look only like another counted event: the faint widening of a seam that will, centuries later, heave another cushion plant inward.

When the mist thickens or thins, I would thicken or thin with it, never clearing, never offering a horizon. I would hold every small event distinct until the ridge’s next adjustment carries me forward or buries the interval I am, returning me to the longer, slower count that has not yet learned to echo.
