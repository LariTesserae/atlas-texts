I would arrive as a single thread of colder water, one among thousands falling from the membrane above. For a long while there is only descent—weight and velocity and the fine, constant percussion of suspended grains against my edges. Then the plateau catches me. I spill across the uppermost shelf, lose speed against rising stone, and begin the long, looping descent through the carved channels.

There the root mats find me first. Pale filaments, already warm with stored mechanical light, drift upward like slow cilia. They do not grasp so much as accommodate: they part around my heavier flow, then close again behind it, recording the slight increase in mineral density I carry. Over hours the decision is made. Small crystalline growths orient themselves along my path; they catch the vibration of my passage and turn a fraction of it into the steady blue-green glow that keeps the sheaths flexible.

I become a chosen current rather than a passing one. The colony reroutes me—narrows a channel here, widens another there—until my thread feeds a zone where new rootlets are pushing into an old block of masonry. I feel the stone through the water: its surface already softened by earlier deposition, its edges now serving as guides for the living tissue. The petrified lashings that once bound the blocks have become dark, mineral ropes; my flow runs along them and the roots follow, using both stone and rope as skeleton.

Centuries pass in the same gesture. I am still moving, but now my motion belongs to the colony. When neighboring fronds shift in response to a deeper pressure change, the resulting groan travels through the water and I carry a ripple of it farther than the sound itself would reach. The crystals along my banks brighten a fraction; the warmth they release keeps two fused root systems from fracturing. In return the mats thicken the deposit beneath me, raising the channel floor by the width of a grain each decade so that I continue to loop without escaping outward.

I am no longer separate from the distributed organism. I am the slow redistribution of mass that lets the shelf remain balanced; I am one of the many channels through which mechanical signal and nutrient travel between what were once separate plants. When the entire tableland adjusts to a change in the membrane above, the adjustment moves through me as well—felt first as a slight cooling, then as a new pattern of crystalline resonance that travels the fused root network and is answered, eventually, by a corresponding shift on the shelf below.

Nothing remembers I was once free-falling water. To the colony I am simply one more persistent current that has been taught to stay, one more living topography still reshaping itself under the same long forces that first welcomed it.
