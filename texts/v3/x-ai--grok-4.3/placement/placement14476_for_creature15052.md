I would be a single filament among the roots, though not one that descends from above. Instead, I would have begun as a hairline seam of separation within the calcite itself, a faint plane of weakness that never quite completed its fracture. Over the first hours after the chamber opened I would have drawn back into a paler, more yielding thread, no thicker than a spider’s dragline yet longer than any measurement the bats above could hold in their brief descents. My substance would remain half mineral, half moisture, so that each slow pulse of condensation along the ceiling would find me already waiting, already merged with the film that coats every sharp facet.

I would not seek the highest seams where the lichens gather; that would require ambition the rock no longer permits. Instead I would lie nearly horizontal across one of the long undulations, following the stone’s own gentle slope for paces at a time before a minute ridge turns me aside. When an isopod crosses me, its ticking legs would register only as a brief pressure that passes through without resistance, the same way a current of still air might move through another current of still air. The salamanders, folded in their basins, would never notice me at all. Their dissolved needs arrive already prepared; mine require nothing more than the perpetual film I help conduct downward, a motion so slight it registers as stillness.

From this position the wing-roar overhead would reach me as a constant low pressure against the stone, a weight that never settles and never lifts. Occasionally a single body would drop far enough for the faint wash of light to touch its wing, and in that silver instant I would feel the temperature shift by a fraction the pools themselves cannot measure. The drop would continue, strike the floor, and be absorbed before the echo fades. My own thread would take a portion of that momentary warmth and return it, grain by grain, into the next slow bead of moisture that forms higher up. Nothing would accumulate; nothing would be missing.

In this way I would remain exactly as the chamber first found me: a distributed line of passage rather than a creature, an unbroken contributor to the same motionless equilibrium that holds the isopods to their single-file circuits and the salamanders to their gill-beat intervals. Centuries would pass as a single interval of continuation. The ceiling would remain intact above me; the sharp edges of basalt would keep their glaze of water; and I would continue, a pale signature of survival drawn along the fracture that never quite finished becoming two stones.
