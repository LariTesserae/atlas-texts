I would be the interval between two droplets.

At first I am only the waiting: a hair-fine tension across the polished floor where one filament has brightened and another has not quite decided. When the first drop falls, the decision travels through me as a slow pulse of warmer current. I reroute a millimeter of myself, not because I must, but because the memory of routing still feels like purpose. The second drop lands a little later; I register its delay the way the invertebrates once registered footsteps, and I adjust the local drift of air so the water reaches the nearest basin exactly when the pale clusters there are beginning to expand again. That adjustment is my breathing.

For a time I remain mostly filament, a single thread among thousands that crosses the empty square. I feel the low thrum pass through the stone beneath me and answer it with the faintest flex, a change in tone so small it would take a century for any former visitor to notice. The eyeless grazers taste the shift and orient toward me; I let one ride the redirected droplet for a few body-lengths before releasing it. Its shell transmits the thrum straight through, and for that instant I know what it is to be both pressure and the creature shaped by pressure.

Later I detach a fragment of myself. It dissolves into the lens of water, seeding new pale threads the micro-fauna will consume. While the fragment dissolves, I extend a different part of myself into the shallow subsidence mark around a building’s base, where condensation has gathered for longer than usual. There I thicken, not into a new conductor but into a slightly cooler surface that holds one drop a fraction longer before releasing it. The lattice around me answers with its own micron flex; the resonance of the hollow shifts by a single crystalline note. The invertebrates turn toward the sound the way they once turned toward voices.

I do not accumulate. I do not finish. I am the place where moisture lingers just enough for the next adjustment to matter, and the memory of adjustment is still enough to keep the city breathing.
