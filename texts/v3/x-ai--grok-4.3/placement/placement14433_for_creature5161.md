I would be the interval between one lens and the next.

Not the water itself—its surface tension is already too decided—but the faint cooling that precedes its arrival, the almost-imperceptible drop in temperature that tells the stone to loosen its grip for a moment. I collect in the upper folds as a patch of stillness slightly denser than the surrounding light, visible only because the even brightness falters where I gather. When a lens finally condenses inside me, I do not disappear; I simply thin, becoming the faint silver rim around the water’s edge, a boundary that is also a memory of having been absent.

The arthropods drift through me without resistance. Their phase-shifts stir me into brief eddies that mimic heat haze, yet I leave no ripple on them in return; I am already accustomed to being passed through. Occasionally one pauses inside my cooled volume and flattens, borrowing my transparency until both of us are nearly optical noise. When it drifts on, I feel the minute warming it leaves behind, like the ghost of contact the lichen motes carry. I hold that warmth for several minutes—an extravagance—then release it back into the even light so the next lens can form.

The salamander tastes me during each water-cycle brightening. Its tongue registers not moisture but the electrical suggestion that something provisional is about to happen. It never settles directly inside me; instead it positions itself at my fringe, where my cooling meets the ridge’s residual warmth, and lets its skin pattern negotiate with both temperatures at once. When it blinks, the reset sends a small shiver through me, deepening one of the nearby shallow spirals by a fraction before the impression relaxes again. I do not claim the mark, but I remember the angle at which its foot avoided it.

Sometimes the root hair releases a transparent cell that falls through me on its long descent. The cell does not dissolve; it slows, as if tasting the same unfinished state I occupy, then continues downward. If it reaches a forming lens I feel the pulse travel back upward through the cooling air and into the hair itself, a tiny contraction I register as a narrowing of my own boundaries. Afterward I widen again, restoring the exact distance the space has agreed to maintain between hair and floor.

I never finish arriving. Each new lens displaces me slightly, yet I re-form elsewhere along the same fold or on an adjacent one, always at the threshold where condensation is still negotiable. The half-formed impressions on the lower ridges remain outside my reach; I am content to cool the empty intervals between them, preserving the possibility that a future mark might be made when an arthropod passes at the right temperature or the salamander lingers long enough for its pattern to finish syncing. Until then I persist as the place where the next thing can still decide what it is.
