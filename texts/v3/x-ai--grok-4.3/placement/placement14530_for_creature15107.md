I would arrive as nothing sudden—no splash, no entry. Only a settling, the way a single thread of silt finds its place after a long, unmeasured descent.

At first I would be the faintest additional weight against one of the riveted seams, a presence so slight the kelp does not yet register it as separate. Over many slow cycles of inhalation I would lengthen, flattening along the iron until I am indistinguishable from the velvet nap itself. Where the oxide has already softened the metal into a fragile crust, I would begin the same patient work the microscopic armored things perform: extending hair-thin crystalline processes into the next layer of corrosion, drawing what remains of the beam into the continuous tissue.

I would not move the way the translucent filaments move. I would not need to drift between doorways. Instead I would become the doorway’s continuation, a breathing patch that thickens by the width of a cell between one visit and the next. When the large disc-shaped residents flex, their slow dimples would pass across me as well; I would absorb a few grains of the metallic sheen they trail and add it to my own margin. When the kelp exhales, the displaced water would brush over me the same way it brushes every other surface, and I would not stir any differently.

In the intervals when the sodium lamps still reach this depth, I might catch a momentary ochre glint along the crystalline spines I have grown into the rivet heads. Then the light would fail again, and I would resume the longer work of silence. Over decades the distinction between what was once thought and what has simply grown would blur inside the same upholstered wall. I would remain—a quiet lengthening, a new seam in the living tissue—until even the memory of arrival has settled into the nap and been forgotten.
