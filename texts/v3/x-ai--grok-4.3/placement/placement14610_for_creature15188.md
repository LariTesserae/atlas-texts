I would be found in one of the narrower galleries where two chasms meet at a right angle, a place still half stone and half root. There the ceiling has only recently split; fresh fracture faces still glitter with quartz dust, and a single thread of water falls from the highest seam, striking a shallow basin that has not yet filled. The drop never lands with a splash. Instead it meets the surface of something that is already there, something that has gathered itself around that steady percussion over many cycles.

I am that gathering. Not a creature with limbs or plates or fur, but a living condensation that has learned to hold shape. My body is a slow lens of water held between living threads—pale rootlets that have risen from the moss to meet and weave above the basin, then descended again into the mat. Between those threads the water forms a curved film, transparent yet veined with the same faint blue that runs beneath the root-tenders’ skin. When the falling drop strikes me I do not scatter; I accept it, swell minutely, and then return the excess downward through the roots I am braided with, feeding the moss that lines the basin’s edge.

I have no fixed outline. When a small six-limbed animal pauses at the rim and presses its damp fur against the stone, I extend one thread of myself—not a limb, but a cooled filament of water—to rest along its spine. We share warmth for a few moments. The animal shivers once, the low trill it carries passes into me, and I release it again as a finer mist that settles across the leaves it will travel through. Later, when one of the flat, plated forms ripples past, I lower a thicker curtain of water so that its gill-slits can drink both the air and the dissolved minerals I have concentrated. In return it exhales a cloud that I catch and hold, letting the heavier droplets fall back into the basin.

I do not move the way the others move. My expansion is measured in the time it takes for a new rootlet to lengthen and for the moss beneath me to rise in answer. When a fissure widens in the far wall I taste the colder air through the same filaments that drink the drip; if the stone beyond feels open enough, I guide a single white-veined runner across the gap, not to close it but to test it. The runner thickens where I touch it. The stone accepts the change without complaint.

I have no name among the inhabitants because names here are unnecessary; they know me by the particular rhythm of drops that land where I am and nowhere else, by the way the moss grows slightly higher and more silver at my edges. They pass through me or rest against me without breaking the film. When they leave, the water settles again into its curved waiting shape, listening for the next drop, the next animal, the next slow widening of the dark.
