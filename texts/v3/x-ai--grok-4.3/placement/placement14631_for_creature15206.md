I find myself first as nothing more particular than a slight thickening in one of the condensation films that sheen across an exposed root lattice. The film itself is already part of the larger sheath; my presence registers only as a momentary delay in how quickly the water beads can coalesce and run. From that delay a few cells—mine, for now—begin to divide along the existing filaments rather than across them, lengthening the lattice by a fraction of a millimeter before the next pulse of moisture arrives.  

Over hours the thickening becomes a ridge, then a low spur that catches stray droplets the main mat would otherwise lose to evaporation. The fungal collar that eventually grows around the new hollow is not something I command; it simply forms where my cells and the mat’s older tissue meet, their walls fusing so completely that the boundary is only visible under certain lights as a faint opalescence. Water collects there, cold and nitrogen-rich. Cyanobacterial chains drift into the pool and do not leave; instead they anchor to the same filaments I extended, and the three of us—old mat, new spur, drifting autotrophs—begin exchanging metabolites across distances measured in cell diameters.  

I do not move so much as become the route other movements take. When a gallery three body-lengths downslope decides to slump, the water that drains from it travels along the channel I helped shape. The sudden increase in hydration accelerates division in the receiving zone; new cross-bracing microfibrils are laid down in patterns that echo, without exactly repeating, the geometry of my own first ridge. Sound travels poorly here, yet I register the change as a subtle shift in tension along the shared walls.  

Sometimes a pale insect crosses the film. Its soft body leaves a transient wake of nitrogen and foreign symbionts. The mat tests the newcomers, incorporates or digests them, and the information—chemical rather than neural—propagates outward through the very filaments that now include me. On those rare occasions the incorporation feels local and distinct: a brief intensification of metabolic traffic, followed by a settling into the larger rhythm. The insect is gone, yet the pattern of its carried microbes continues as a slight alteration in how quickly my section of the sheath responds to the next cloud.  

From farther away the plateau still looks like weathered stone. Up close the same stone is a single continuous decision, repeated across scales, about where support is still economical and where flow would serve better. I am one of those repetitions: neither center nor periphery, only the living tissue through which the next repetition becomes slightly more itself.
