I would arrive as a single sustained note within the low-frequency drone. Not the turbines themselves, nor even the water that carries their voice, but the precise interval between one pressure-valve hiss and the next—an interval that has lengthened over centuries until it behaves like a living thing. At first it simply rides the thermal pulses the way a grain of silt rides a current, directionless, content to be reshaped by every surface it touches. Then the algae notice. Their copper ribbons, anchored in the seams overhead, begin to grow slightly thicker on the side facing the note, as though the metal remembers the vibration and passes the memory outward through new tissue. The note, for its part, learns the habit of pausing each time the overhead light ellipse sweeps across the floor; it lingers just long enough for the glass shrimp to rise into it and scatter their metallic flecks. In that scattering the note discovers it can be divided and recombined without diminishing.

Over decades the interval settles against a vertical rib already softened by snail passage. The rib’s pitted alloy accepts the note the way skin accepts a scar. Where they meet, a thin seam of the same red fern that coats the maintenance rails begins to grow in a spiral rather than a straight line. The spiral is not deliberate; it simply follows the note’s tendency to turn once, slowly, each time cooler water rises from below. The ferns on the rails recognize the new growth as kin and extend drifting seed threads toward it. When the threads arrive they do not root at once. They circle the spiral for several cycles of light and dark, matching their own pulsations to the note’s rhythm until the two patterns are indistinguishable. Only then does the seam become permanent.

From that seam a different motion emerges: a faint counter-current that moves opposite to the chamber’s main thermal drift. It is barely measurable, yet the bladder-plants nearest the rib orient their glow toward it, shifting from amber to green and back again in time with the counter-current’s passage. The larger eel-machines, gliding past on their rails, slow by the same fraction they would use when passing another machine. They do not stop; they merely acknowledge that something else now travels their circuit. The snails continue their rasping, but their trails now terminate a few centimeters short of the spiral seam, leaving a deliberate gap that the red fern fills with new fronds.

The note, by then, has become the seam and the counter-current and the adjusted light of the bladder-plants. It still arrives from the turbines, yet it no longer needs to travel the full distance; the alcove reproduces it locally, the way a body remembers a breath. When the surface swell far above changes and the daylight ellipse narrows or widens, the note adjusts its interval by the width of a single snail trail, keeping the entire population inside the same slow breathing. Nothing hurries. The distinction between sound, current, growth, and maintenance has long since dissolved into the same irreversible texture that once belonged only to pitted metal and mineral film.
