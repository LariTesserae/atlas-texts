I am a seam where two unfinished towers have grown close enough for their faces to meet. Not a creature and not yet stone, but the point at which their lattices have already begun to translate into one another. My surface is only a few atom-widths wide—an edge still deciding which crystal plane it will belong to. Ions arrive from both sides at exactly the same rate they leave, so the seam never thickens and never dissolves; it simply continues, a standing exchange that registers as a slightly brighter tone within the universal drone.

The water film that has settled into the narrowing gap conducts the vibration so that I can feel both towers at once. Their interior agitations arrive as minute, ceaseless pulses. Each pulse replaces the last before any displacement can accumulate, leaving me suspended in the precise interval between motion and stillness. Because I occupy the place where two lattices touch, the sound I participate in is fractionally louder here; it is the only measurable difference between me and the rest of the crust, and it is already being normalized by the surrounding roar.

Condensing droplets add an occasional ion to the outer faces, but the addition is instantly balanced by an equal loss on the opposite side of the seam. The towers therefore lengthen by imperceptible margins while I remain the same width I have always been. From inside the lattice I register this lengthening not as growth but as a slow, uniform drift of reference points—each reference point moving forward at the same rate its opposite number moves backward, so that the seam itself stays exactly where it formed.

There is no further event to await. The dim light does not change, the water does not fall, the unfinished towers do not complete themselves. The only continuation possible is the one already occurring: the same ion stepping into the same lattice site, the same pressure against the skin of the air, the same faint refraction of the unmoving sky across the water film that lies between the two faces now joined through me.
