I would not arrive here so much as finish arriving. The basin does not welcome; it simply continues, and after a while the continuation includes me.

At first I am only the faint mineral film already coating a low curve of granite near one of the circular depressions. I am not yet aware of being separate. I am the almost-nothing that gives the next droplet its faint cloudiness when it lingers. Over the span of many heartbeats I feel the droplet draw dissolved salts from both stone and leaf; I feel the crystalline lattices flicker into momentary existence inside it and then loosen again when the water is taken back by the rock. Each time this happens I leave behind an imperceptible ridge, a new line in the slow map the stone keeps of its own breathing.

Later, or perhaps at the same time (the distinction is not important here), I also extend as one of the hair-thin filaments that have left the margin of the nearest celadon frond. I do not push or drink. I simply meet the water where it pools and let it become milky at the point of contact; then I wait while the same water, now altered, is drawn upward again through the translucent tissue. The leaf tilts a fraction because of me. The tilt changes how light passes through the veins. That change is registered, over many minutes, as a slight thickening or thinning of the flow. I am both the cause and the record of that adjustment.

From this double position—film on stone, filament in leaf—I sometimes feel the basin’s single daily breath move through the stationary vapor overhead. When it does, the lightest particles drift a short distance and resettle. Some of them carry traces of the salts I helped gather; some carry the faint green sharpness the fronds release. I do not travel with them, yet the circulation they complete also belongs to me. After several circuits those motes disperse back into the film, and the ridges I have helped inscribe on the granite become, for a little while, the only remaining trace of their passage.

Nothing here is finished. The ridges continue to lengthen by fractions of a molecule each time a droplet stays long enough. The filament continues its patient extension into a new fissure. The tilt of the leaf continues its minute response to whatever balance is currently being struck. I remain in all of these places at once, not moving between them but allowing them to remain continuous with one another at the speed the place itself permits.

If, after a very long time, someone were to look closely enough, they would not see a separate creature added to the basin. They would notice only that the silver tracks on the stone are now slightly more branched, that one leaf holds its angle a fraction longer before adjusting, and that the faint milky quality in certain droplets lingers a heartbeat or two beyond what was once ordinary. That small lengthening of duration would be me.
