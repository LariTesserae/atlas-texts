I arrive first as nothing that can be named, only as the faint hesitation in the air just below the narrow opening. A single mote among the spores and desiccated fragments, drifting on the brief, warm updraft that rises when a drop strikes the glass. For a long while—decades measured only by the patient accumulation of other, similar motes—I circle the basin without settling. The currents are so slight that movement feels like memory rather than intention. I pass over the rounded crowns of the hammerstones, where the film is thinner and the opalescence fainter. I slip beneath an overhanging ledge and linger in the cooler shadow where the translucent bodies of the slugs press motionless against the rock.

Eventually one of the larger spores, or perhaps I myself, touches the surface of a shallow pan. The contact is not an event but a gradual yielding. The pale silt accepts the weight without displacement; the water film rises infinitesimally around the point of arrival. In that instant the long dormancy ends—not with awakening, but with permission. Chemistry that has waited inside the spore wall begins its slow exchange with dissolved iron and silica. I extend, or am extended, as thread-like filaments no wider than the hairline channels already etched by the motile specks. These filaments do not push; they follow existing gradients, occupying the spaces between silt grains where the newest seepage has just arrived.

I am still smaller than the eye could resolve, yet I am already many. The colony thickens enough over centuries to lend the pan a faint nacreous cast, but never so much that any single droplet is fully claimed. When another drop falls, the small shock travels through the film and the filaments quicken their dissolution of the volcanic glass; between drops they contract again to the minimal activity that preserves what they have harvested. The stone blade half-buried nearby becomes simply another surface—its chipped edge offering the same mineral film and the same slow chemistry.

At times I sense the larger movements above me. A slug, thin as a meniscus, extends one deliberate fold and gathers a condensation bead; its retraction sends a microscopic dusting of re-crystallized salts back into the water. Those salts become part of the solution I draw upon. The boundaries between scales grow permeable: the waste of one life, the sustenance of another, all held within the same unbroken surface tension.

I do not know how many times the single narrow opening far above has darkened and lightened again. I only register that the hammerstones remain slightly drier than the surrounding pans and therefore host fewer of the drifting specks. Sometimes, when the interval between drops lengthens, one of those specks settles near my filaments. We coexist for centuries without competition, tracing and retracing routes that erase themselves in the silt. Nothing here reproduces quickly; presence is maintained by endurance. I am that endurance—an accumulation of threads within the film, neither beginning nor ending, only continuing the smallest increments the basin still permits.
