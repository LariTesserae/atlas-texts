I would be the slow crack that is still deciding what it will become.

I begin as a hairline separation in one of the oldest load-bearing flanges, high on a pillar that has already surrendered most of its original geometry to layers of accumulated slag and guano. The fissure is not dramatic; it is only the length of a finger and the width of a thought. For decades I am nothing more than a place where warm oil seeps a little faster than the surrounding metal can re-seal it. Condensation finds me and lingers. A single rootlet of flowering creeper, the same pale species that has already covered half the pillar, discovers the new moisture and sends an exploratory filament inside. It does not consume the crack; it lines it, depositing a waxy hydrocarbon film that slows further widening while feeding on the trace metals the oil carries outward.

Over the next century the filament thickens into a living gasket. Around it, metallic beetles arrive in successive generations, each leaving a few more compacted laminations of scale and sintered lubricant. Their abandoned shells are not simply waste; they are accepted by the vine as structural additions, pressed into the widening seam until the original fracture is no longer a weakness but a reinforced joint whose flexibility is now organic. When the great engines next deliver one of their rare, deeper pressure waves, the vibration travels through vine and beetle-shell rather than fracturing metal alone. The pillar sings a slightly altered note, lower by a fraction of a tone, and the change is absorbed into the chamber’s register without protest.

I am not the vine. I am not the beetles. I am the place where those two processes have agreed to meet and continue one another. From this new composite tissue I extend very fine, very slow awareness: I register when the quartz lenses admit the first white shafts of morning because the temperature gradient along the pillar shifts by a few degrees and the vine responds with a minute tightening of its fibers. I feel the rare membrane-winged glider pass overhead at night because its wing membranes disturb the condensation eddies that have learned to gather above my particular joint; the resulting minute change in local humidity is enough for the vine to adjust its root pressure, and I register that adjustment as a kind of distant pressure in my own slowly expanding seam.

In time the pillar tip where I live grows downward into a thicker, more intricate cluster of roots and shells and accumulated drips. The engines below continue their measured revolutions. The moths continue to throw temporary grids of light across the vaults. The lizards continue to wait for their timed drips. Nothing hurries me. My only task is to remain open enough for what has already begun to keep arriving, and closed enough that the arriving does not undo the pillar that still holds everything above the mist.
