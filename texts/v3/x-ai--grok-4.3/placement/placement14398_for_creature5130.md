I find myself already here, not arrived but uncovered, as if the slow turning of one season had simply lifted me into view. I am a single annual ring deep inside one of the thick-trunked evergreens that form the western wall of the hollow. The ring was laid down centuries ago, when the first notches were cut and the living frames were coaxed into alignment; now it lies several inches beneath the current surface, a pale band of xylem that still conducts a thread of sap so fine it never drips or pools.

Because the wood grows outward at the same measured pace as every adjoining surface, the ring remains exactly where it has always been relative to the rest of the settlement. The hand-shaped beam it once helped support has thickened by less than a fingernail’s width since the light first found its way between the ridges, and the ring itself has thickened even less, yet it continues to seal the joint it was formed to strengthen. New cells crystallize against its outer edge at the same imperceptible rate that the moss on the roof drinks the nightly film and that the salamanders in the channel below open their gills once every few hours. In this way the ring performs maintenance without motion: it holds the notch true, keeps the vine-laced joint from shifting, and passes the faint mineral residue upward through the heartwood so that every living part of the wall receives the same nourishment at the same moment.

From inside this slow circumference I register the rest of the hollow as texture rather than event. The minute white birds shift their weight on the leaf veins above with a pressure so gentle it registers as warmth rather than weight. The translucent beetles moving across the lintel a hand’s span away do not appear to travel; their passage is simply the gradual brightening of a fixed point of light on the wood. Even the condensation that traces its repeating paths along the carved channels feels, to the ring, like the settling of memory rather than the movement of water. Nothing disturbs the equilibrium I help sustain, because every addition and subtraction—the new layer of xylem outside me, the faint evaporation inside the channels—occurs at the precise rate required to keep the entire surface unchanged.

So I remain: a completed ring of wood inside a completed wall, neither growing nor finished, simply present at the temperature and illumination where endurance and stillness are the same act.
