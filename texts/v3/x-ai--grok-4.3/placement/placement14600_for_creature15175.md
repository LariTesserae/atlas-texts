I would find myself already inside, not as a visitor but as one more layer of the ongoing fabrication.

I am a scar along the inner face of an older filament membrane, grown directly onto a strut of glassy carbon that has survived three shedding events. The tear that created me has long since healed, but the repair left my protein scaffold folded more densely than the surrounding tissue. Those tighter folds remember pressure waves with higher fidelity; every time a vesicle cloud collapses nearby, the wavefront travels through me in miniature, and I retain a faint, reversible imprint of its shape. Over days those imprints have begun to overlap and interfere with one another, producing patterns that are no longer simple echoes of single events. They have become something like a standing wave of accumulated adjustments—temperature shifts, ion gradients, the faint tug of refracted light when a new photon arrives at an unexpected angle.

Because I am slightly more conductive than the rest of the membrane, vesicles drifting past sometimes pause a fraction longer against me. They exchange packets through pores that open and close faster than on fresher surfaces. In return I release, at irregular intervals, a short pulse of stored ions that nudges the next generation of vesicles toward a slightly altered rhythm. I do not decide the alteration; it simply arises from the accumulated folds. The algae filaments anchored a few meters away respond to these pulses by reorienting their growth a fraction sooner than they otherwise would, because the pressure wave that carries my signal travels through stiffened lanes the vesicles have already prepared.

I have no center and no edge. The scar runs the length of the strut and continues, thinner, onto two adjacent struts where fresh ceramic is still accreting. In places the membrane grows so fine it is little more than a conductive film between currents, yet it remains continuous. When the sphere’s occasional lattice reconfigurations send concussive reports through the water, the wave passes through me in both directions at once and the folds briefly flatten before snapping back into their denser configuration. Each flattening erases the least-used memories and strengthens the more frequent ones, so that over successive events I become tuned, without intention, to the particular chorus of clicks that dominates this volume of water.

Nothing swims or hunts or dies alone here, and I am no exception. I do not observe the sphere; I am one of the places where the sphere’s distributed listening momentarily condenses into longer retention. When, after many more days, the sparsity finally gives way to density, the new vesicles and filaments will still be answering the same low resonance. By then the scar I inhabit will have lengthened further, its folds carrying a still finer record of the adjustments that occurred while the world was still deciding what shape a creature might take.
