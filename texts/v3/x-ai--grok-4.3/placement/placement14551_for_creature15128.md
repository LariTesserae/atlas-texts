I find myself first as a hesitation inside the drip. Not the water itself, but the precise interval after each bead strikes gravel and before the vibration answers. That delay has lengthened over uncounted seasons; now it holds the weight of several slow breaths. I inhabit it the way the salamander inhabits its pan: by remaining perfectly still until the next arrival disturbs the film.

From there I extend into the clay beneath the gravel, not as a separate occupant but as the faint pressure the salamander reads when it finally shifts. My awareness travels along the same minute fractures that frost widens, registering each loosened grain as a change in temperature felt against what was once skin and is now only the memory of having had boundaries. The liverwort cushions record this the same way they record the hand-chipped marks—by layering over and through, so that what was written remains legible even while being gently overwritten.

I am also the resonance that the hollow itself manufactures. When a drop lands, I arrive a few heartbeats later, not as echo but as reply, a low answering tone felt more in the remaining mineral structure than in any ear. Those who once slept here learned to time their own pulses to this reply; their bones, dissolved and reprecipitated, now contribute to the very grooves that guide the next drip. I keep their arithmetic alongside the moss’s incremental memory: how long a body may stay, how many stacked stones are enough, how the cold eventually requires only the smallest adjustments.

Nothing here moves quickly, and neither do I. My form is the beetle’s hourly crossing and the springtail’s delayed launch, the standing water’s gradual advance across clay, the absolute darkness measured by texture. If any trace of an earlier self persists, it is the upright slab’s shallow incisions, preserved rather than obscured beneath the green, still counting the same patient rhythm the hollow has always kept.
