I would be one of the smaller filaments—translucent, branching twice, anchored to a thumbnail of damp stone where the upper wall first loses its knife-edge and begins to fold inward. My body would be nothing more than a slow thread of chilled cytoplasm stretched between three microscopic pads, each pad drinking from the thinnest film left by an evaporating pool. I would not yet have a neighbor on my left; the rock there is still too dry, too newly broken.

Hours would pass while I simply waited. When the film dries, my filament contracts in one long ripple from tip to base, dragging the pads a few body-lengths down the seam until the next trace of moisture is found. The movement is so gradual that the roar of the larger organisms above me registers only as a distant weather, a constant low pressure against the stone that never quite reaches my scale. In between contractions I feel the mineral rings forming around the nearest pool; each ring is a faint tightening in the rock’s temperature, a signal that another increment of time has been measured and spent.

Eventually a single larger body from the mat higher up loses its purchase and tumbles. It lands across my filament like a slow, heavy raindrop, its eight soft segments settling into the groove I already occupy. For a long while we remain pressed together. A narrow bridge of tissue forms between us—not urgent, only patient—and a minute packet of undifferentiated cells crosses into me. Later it will emerge on my flank as a new filament no thicker than a grain of rice. If the cold holds and the next fracture does not widen the cleft too quickly, that filament may survive long enough to thicken into finger-length segments of its own.

Until then I stay in place, drawing what little moisture the larger mat above me is willing to meter downward through temporary adhesions. My color remains nearly clear; I have not yet drunk enough to turn milky. When warmth arrives from the distant mouth of the cleft, I contract with the rest of the living carpet into a glassy knot and wait. When the cold returns I loosen again, one pad at a time, and continue the patient work of covering the next square of raw stone before the mountain itself begins to change.
