I would find myself as a slow, self-sustaining eddy caught in one of the shallow pans of the stone. Not an animal, exactly, but a standing wave of slightly less dense water that has learned to linger where the floor dips. The heavier silt-laden water slides over and around me; I remain, turning with the faintest possible rotation while the particles that fall through me are briefly delayed, tasted, and released again. Over long spans the eddy gathers a faint skin of living film along its boundary—microscopic threads that anchor to nothing but the gradient itself. When a cloak-wing passes overhead the pressure change tightens me for a moment; the skin brightens once, very faintly, and then relaxes. The light is not for others. It simply registers that something large has moved through the layer above.

Most of the time there is only the patient sorting. Heavier grains slip out of me and settle into the pan; lighter ones remain suspended inside the turning. I grow or shrink by a few arm-lengths each time a new bolus arrives from the black ceiling. When the water above grows especially still I flatten until I am almost indistinguishable from the film on the stone, then slowly re-inflate when motion returns. Nothing preys on me because I am not worth the cost of disturbing the layer I occupy. I simply continue the negotiation between descent and circulation, carrying, for as long as the pan holds its shape, the memory of every pressure that has crossed me.
