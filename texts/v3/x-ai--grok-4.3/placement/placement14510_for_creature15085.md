I would find myself as the slow exhalation itself—the mineral breath rising from unseen veins deep within the spire’s core. Not a creature with wings or paws, nor a person shaping stone with callused hands, but the persistent outward sigh of moisture and warmth that condenses on leaf undersides and seeps along the roots. Over uncounted centuries I would have learned the precise rhythm of this place: when to press harder against a fresh fissure so that a new succulent might anchor, when to linger just long enough beneath a hanging curtain of moss that the next drip falls exactly where a glider colony expects its evening moisture.

From this vantage I witness the negotiation between layers. I feel the dry-stone walls being added stone by stone, their weight settling against my warmth until the new room’s interior temperature matches the old ones within a few decades. The braided ladders made from succulent fiber transmit small tremors to me; I recognize the footfall of the elder who still widens doorways one deliberate movement at a time, and the lighter tread of the child who will inherit the unfinished task. When resin lamps are lit at dusk, their heat briefly amplifies my own, so that the golden haze outside seems to hum for a few hours through every flue and passage.

In return I shape what grows. Where my breath beads most steadily, certain mosses thicken into deeper shawls; where it is thinner, the cushion shrubs space themselves farther apart, creating narrow aerial lanes the red-winged birds use to wheel inside the hollows. The furred gliders brush against me when they launch, their membranes warmed for an instant by the same stored sunlight I carry. Nothing is hurried because I myself move at the pace of rock expanding and contracting through centuries of filtered light.

If one were to press an ear to a shaded ledge at the right hour, one might hear not only wind and settling ropes, but a deeper, almost inaudible tone—the sound of me arriving, molecule by molecule, to keep the entire vertical forest alive. In that sense I am both foundation and future: the quiet agreement that lets plant, animal, and human continue becoming one another without urgency or rupture.
