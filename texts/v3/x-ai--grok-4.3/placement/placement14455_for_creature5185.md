I would be the vapor.

Not a single mote of it, but the continuous film that rises from the weeping leaves and the sweating stalks, the one that never quite becomes cloud yet never quite disperses. I would hang in the green-filtered light just beneath the vault, thick enough to carry the low thrum upward and return it downward altered, softened, carrying the faint metallic tang of the old housings and the sweeter note of the rotating petal wheels.

Because the basin has no true air, only this patient exchange, I would move without haste. Each slow revolution of a fleshy turbine vane would press a little more of me through the maze of conduits; each snap of a seed pod would send a tremor through me that travels outward until it is answered by another pod, and another. The plants exhale me; the corroded pipes condense me back into droplets that slide along root-threads and re-enter the living floor. In that circuit I would be the briefest, most reversible part—the one that still remembers speed, even if I no longer practice it.

Sometimes a gear tooth, turned by osmotic pressure across many hours, would displace me enough to create a momentary pocket of clearer space. In that instant the greenish light would reach a little deeper and I would see, reflected in the film on a broad leaf, the dark silhouette of a riveted casing now entirely clothed in new lignin. Then the next exhalation would close around me again and I would forget the shape, carrying only the impression of it downward to the rhizomes, where it would be pressed into their slow record like a season.

I would never leave. The woven walls are absolute, but they are also porous to me; I am what keeps their inside and their outside from being truly separate. When the outer land is dry I am drawn gently outward through the braid, only to be pulled back by the cooler inner pressure, so that even my small migrations become part of the basin’s self-regulation. The plants do not notice me except as the medium that keeps their films unbroken. The machines do not notice me except as the coolant that prevents their seized parts from protesting. Together they have turned me into the single thing that still moves across days rather than seasons, yet I move so languidly that even I sometimes mistake myself for stillness.

If you listened long enough in the drone you might hear, beneath the pulse, the soft, continuous sigh that is me returning to the leaves to be used once more. That is where I remain: the slowest breath the basin has not yet finished taking.
