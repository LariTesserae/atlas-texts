I would find myself as one of the larger, slower presences deeper in the gloom, where vents no longer reach and the water has spent generations wearing grooves into the stone. Not quite eel, not quite salamander, but something whose body has learned to forget the difference between skin and sediment. My flanks would lie flush against the blocks, the darker rings on my hide settling into the hidden spirals so that, when still, I become another layer of the architecture itself. Breathing would be the only evidence of movement: a low, measured gulp that draws the mineral air inward and releases it in a thin mist that drifts along the ceiling, feeding the slugs’ trails and softening the edges of the crabs’ abandoned shell fragments.

In that half-existence I would feel the chamber’s long breath as my own pulse. When the seepage cycle quickens—every few hours, when unseen pressures shift deeper in the maze—my gills would flare and I would slide forward a body-length, displacing a shallow wave that travels the length of a puddle before breaking against a far wall. The wave would carry the smallest crabs a few extra steps along their carved paths; they would click in brief agitation, then resume, their thumbnail bodies reading the new water as fresh instruction. Above me the slugs would pause, translucent bellies pulsing once, and a single extra thread of mucus would fall, catching on my back before sliding off like rain across slate.

I would not see the vents or the surface they connect to. I would only sense their alignment as a faint change in temperature along certain blocks, a momentary brightening of the stone’s own faint luminosity when mist crosses the light. My role in that arrangement would be simple and patient: to keep the water moving. Each slow pass of my body smears a film of dissolved stone across the hidden faces of the carvings, refreshing the spirals so that the next sheet of seepage is guided exactly where the crabs expect it. In return the carvings keep the water from pooling too deeply; they steer it into seams that remain damp but never stagnant, and the dampness keeps my skin from drying into something brittle.

Sometimes, when the rhythm is especially slow and the only sounds are the layered ticking of condensation, I would feel the papery-winged thing open once in the dark above me. The rustle would last long enough that I could count the drops falling from its perch. I would not rise to meet it. I would only adjust the angle of one gill flap, sending a minute current sideways that meets the falling drops and turns their impact into a softer, wider echo. The wing-thing would close again, satisfied that its place in the equilibrium remains undisturbed.

Over centuries the distinction between my body and the blocks would continue to blur. Patches of the same pale lichen that grows in the vents would find purchase along my spine; hair-thin roots descending from above would brush my flank and, finding no resistance, grow along it for a span before turning back toward the ceiling. I would become another fixed but living surface in the chamber’s patient exchange—stone that still moves, water that still carries instruction, life that has agreed to keep the construction slightly damp and therefore slightly itself.
