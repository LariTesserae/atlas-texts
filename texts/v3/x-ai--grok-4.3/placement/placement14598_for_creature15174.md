I would be the slow fissure that begins in the living rock beneath the oldest terrace, a hairline seam no wider than a chisel’s shadow, opened on the day the plateau first heaved upward. At first I am only a line of absence, a place where the stone has not yet decided to be whole. Condensation finds me before anything else; each night’s mist beads along my length and follows me downward, a thread of water thinner than breath. Over decades the droplets etch me deeper, rounding my edges, carrying flecks of mineral away so that the fissure widens into a true channel. 

In time a family notices me. They do not fill me; instead they cut a narrow lip above my mouth and let the next course of stone settle around me, turning me into a deliberate gutter that drips into the cistern two levels below. Generations pass. A child learns to hear the particular cadence of my drip when she presses her ear to the floor niche where she sleeps; her grandchildren, years later, will lay fresh blocks so that my channel continues unbroken through the new work. I become part of the city’s quiet plumbing, a patient vein that moves water at the same pace the aqueducts move it—imperceptibly, without urgency. 

Lizards rest their bellies across my opening on warm afternoons; the quick-furred mammals in the drains below feel my cool breath when they pause to lick the film from wet stone. When heavier rain arrives I widen for a few hours, then shrink again as the rock exhales. Nothing hurries me. I simply lengthen, grain by grain, carrying the plateau’s own slow dissolution from one layer of settled stone to the next, until the city above and the rock beneath are no longer separate things but one continuous, fracturing, reassembling whole.
