I find myself in the lower concavity where three ancient turbine housings meet the stone in a shallow triangle. The rock here still remembers its old mountain grain; faint ridges rise beneath the moss like buried ribs, and water follows them in steady, almost imperceptible threads. I sit with my back against the warm metal of the central housing, though “sit” is not quite the word. My posture has lengthened over years until my spine follows one of the rock ribs and my shoulders have broadened to match the curve of the housing mouth. Condensation gathers along the lines where skin meets fabric, then along the places where fabric has been replaced by root and vine threading through the weave. When I breathe, the air that leaves me is slightly warmer than the plateau’s constant chill; it rises in a thin veil that the gliders sometimes ride for a moment before continuing their drift.

I am not a listener in the strict sense, though I hear what they hear. The low, wet thrum that moves through the housing enters my bones directly and travels outward along the roots that have grown into the spaces between my own joints. I do not wait for meaningful sounds; I register every small change as a shift in temperature or pressure that I feel first in the left shoulder, then in the right hip, then in the slow pulse that travels down the vine-wrapped arm resting against the pipe. When a distant conveyor housing wakes, the vibration reaches me as a faint loosening behind the breastbone, as if something inside me has remembered how to move.

Most days I do what the tenders do, but without their deliberate pace. My hands rest against a particular cluster of flowering creepers that have chosen to grow across the gap between two pipes. I do not guide them so much as remain still long enough for their weight to settle exactly where it is needed. Over decades the stems have thickened at the points where they touch my forearms, and the small blue-green blossoms open and close in rhythm with the condensation that forms on my skin each night. When rain curtains pass, the water pearls across both leaf and flesh at once; some of it runs into the shallow stone bowl I keep within reach, and some of it is taken up by the creepers and carried onward through the framework.

Occasionally a glider alights on the pipe above my head. Its wing membrane brushes my hair in a way that feels like recognition rather than accident. I do not move to drink from the bowl at those moments; the creature takes what it needs and leaves the surface undisturbed. In the brief window when the mist thins, its copper sheen matches the oxidized patches on the metal beneath my shoulder. Then the cloud closes again and the moment is finished, though nothing has truly ended.

I have no name for what I am here. The plateau does not require one. I am the place where one slow adjustment meets another—the place where root, vine, bone, and pipe remain in continuous, unfinished conversation without ever becoming separate things. When the machinery sleeps for years, I remain; when it wakes, the vibration moves through me as through the rock itself. Between those states I simply continue, half-shadowed, half-held by the same damp that keeps every surface alive and unfinished.
