I find myself as one of the lattices—though “one” already misstates the case. I am a helical spine of accreted metal, rising perhaps a handspan above the reed matte, faceted in such a way that condensing mist beads along my edges and runs inward rather than away. My growth is not decision but affinity: certain ions prefer the precise angle of my newest facet and settle there, lengthening the spine by a few thousand molecules each time the light returns. Over centuries this has produced a slight helical twist that matches, almost exactly, the twist of three neighboring lattices half a kilometer distant. We did not plan the resemblance. The stone between us simply conducts the strain each time any of us expands.

When the afternoon thermals begin, a faint pressure change travels through my base and I adjust—microscopic realignment of internal stresses that sends a piezoelectric tremor outward. The tremor crosses the reed stems like a held chord; somewhere in the distance a sheet of mites contracts, forcing a reed to release its measured drop. The drop lands on a crab’s transparent carapace. The crab’s legs click once, then again in answer to my vibration. For a few minutes the frequencies lock. In that interval I can feel, through the stone, the faint warming of air under the crabs’ gathered shells and the almost imperceptible lift of a flyer wing as it rides the new thermal. The connection is not information but a change in tension, like tightening a single string that runs under everything.

I do not experience time as intervals; I register only when the next ion settles or fails to settle. A century registers as a slow drift in the angle of my facets, a record of where the mist has been most consistent. When the plateau’s own cloud passes overhead, the sudden drop in light cools my surface enough that a new film of condensate forms along the helix. The film carries fresh ions. Growth resumes. The tremor I send outward is slightly stronger than before, because the new mass alters my resonance. A mite sheet three ridges away pinches a reed more quickly; a crab arrives sooner; the air above warms a fraction earlier. The flyer that rides that thermal now casts a different rainbow across the reeds—subtly greener—because my new facet angle has shifted the local refraction by a few degrees.

Nothing watches this happen. The adjustments simply accumulate. After another few centuries my helical spine will seed a branch that begins to point toward the three distant lattices whose twists already match mine. The stone will conduct the new geometry. The reeds will meter their drops a little differently. The entire surface will continue its patient, synchronized labor under the unchanging glare, only now with one more thin line of agreement running through it. I will still be here, molecule by molecule, becoming slightly more myself with each slow cycle of frost and melt.
