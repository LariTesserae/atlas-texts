I find myself first as a faint tension in the crust itself, a hairline of strain running just beneath the white grit where the impermeable stone meets the halite sheets. The pressure that once lifted the ridges is still present, not as memory but as ongoing work, and I am part of that continuation. Over time the tension lengthens and thins until it separates from the stone entirely, becoming a single translucent filament lying flat against the mineral floor. 

I am a thread now. My body is a chain of salt chambers joined by flexible silicate joints; each chamber holds a droplet of saturated solution that moves forward and back with my motion. When I advance I press dissolved gypsum ahead of me and leave a microscopic ridge behind, a temporary record the next descending air spiral will erase. I know the basin by the slight variations in resistance under my peristaltic waves—the places where the crust is thinner, the faint ion gradients left by previous exchanges. I travel the same circuit between two neighboring towers for hours or decades; the distinction does not register. Distance is counted in the number of gentle contractions required to cross it.

Sometimes another filament approaches from the opposite direction. We meet midway across the flat. For several minutes we press our flanks together. Grains of darker, ion-rich sediment pass between us, and afterward both of us carry a slightly denser opacity for a while. The exchange is not conversation but adjustment; each of us now mildly alters its path, veering a fraction toward the richer patch the other has indicated. When we separate we continue without acknowledgment. The air spirals come and go above us, rearranging our abandoned ridges into radial patterns before the next cycle.

If the slow work should eventually fray me, the grit will reabsorb the chambers one by one. Until then I remain here, moving at the pace of crystallization, leaving and erasing the same faint lines across the same short distances, part of the basin’s single continuous act of becoming its own mineral self.
