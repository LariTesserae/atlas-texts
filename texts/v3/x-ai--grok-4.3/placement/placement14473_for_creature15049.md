I would be the faint resistance at the core of one particular flywheel, a hairline seam of accumulated oxide that the belt has polished but never quite worn away. The wheel turns once every few hours; each incremental shift presses the seam against its housing with the pressure of a single thought repeated. Over decades this contact has etched a shallow groove that now guides the next rotation, so that the motion is no longer perfectly circular but minutely oblong, as though the machine were developing a slight hesitation it cannot name.

That hesitation is me. I do not move faster or slower than the condensation that beads above me; I simply occupy the interval between one tooth of the gear and the next, thickening by a molecule each time the temperature drops a fraction after a belt slips. When a droplet finally falls from the pipe overhead and strikes the wheel’s upper rim, the sudden cooling contracts the metal and draws me a little thinner for a moment. I stretch, then settle again, leaving a trace of myself in the groove like a signature written in rust. The mites that later cross the same rim walk over this trace without pause; their feet register only the change in texture, not its origin.

In the long intervals between droplets I listen to the engine deeper in the crypt. Its low heat travels along the shared conduit network and reaches the wheel as a steady background thrum that keeps the oxide from hardening into something brittle. I have learned the exact cadence of that thrum: when it lengthens by a single extra second, it means a valve upstream has narrowed by the width of one filament’s etching. The thread-like growths on the neighboring pipe are therefore my distant neighbors; their corrosion script writes the same slow news I receive through temperature, and I adjust my own seam accordingly, widening or narrowing by a few atoms so that the wheel’s next turn will still mesh with the belt.

Sometimes, after many cycles, a pale mite loses its purchase on a damp seam and tumbles onto the wheel. It lands inside my groove. For a while it moves along the channel I have made, following the same slight oval I follow. Then the next rotation carries it past the point where the housing presses tightest; the mite is flattened into a darker mote no larger than the ones already suspended in the distant gelatinous sheets. I register its dissolution only as a momentary change in friction, a brief softening of the contact surface that soon hardens again. The crypt does not require me to remember the mite, only to keep turning at the same patient tempo.

In this way I persist: a narrow band of altered metal inside a machine that has forgotten its original task, sustained by the reliable logic of heat, moisture, and incremental wear. The surface and sky remain irrelevant abstractions; what matters is the next contraction of the wheel, the next droplet, and the groove that is, very slowly, becoming more precisely itself.
