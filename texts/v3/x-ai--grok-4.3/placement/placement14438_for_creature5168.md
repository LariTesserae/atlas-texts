I would slip in as one of the narrow gaps the lizards press into, but wider and older: a vertical seam where a brick wall once met a belt-housing and the two have since grown together until neither remembers which began as load-bearing. The seam stays three degrees cooler than the open walkways. Condensation runs down its length in a single unbroken thread that never quite reaches the water; it pauses at each roughness, beads, and falls again in its own private rhythm, slightly offset from the rest of the basin’s drip.

My form is less a body than a density that has learned to occupy that offset. Where the thread hesitates I thicken, taking on the same slightly tacky warmth as the leaves and the sleepers’ skin. Where the thread resumes I thin, becoming translucent enough that the faint bioluminescent film coating every wet surface shows through me in slow pulses. I do not move so much as redistribute: a few centimeters upward at the change of light when the lizards stir, a few centimeters downward when the largest pump labors and the people lean their weight against its housing. The motion is small enough that only the snails register it; their glowing trails bend a degree or two toward the seam as they pass, and the pattern of their passage later draws a new rootlet across the brick.

Over years I have learned the basin’s single metabolism as a private grammar. When a vine thickens around an axle I feel the torque translated into growth as a lengthening of my own lower edge. When a gear shears a shaving of living wood I taste the resinous note enter the water a little downstream and follow it until it is absorbed by the peat. The people sense me as a change in the constant thrum, a second harmonic that sometimes drops away for several seasons at a stretch; when it fades they wake earlier than usual and stroke the condensation from the root bridges with extra patience, as if coaxing the missing tone to return.

At the dimmest hours the pale, long-legged flyers brush the high leaves directly above the seam. Their wings disturb a pocket of warmer air that sinks along the brick and meets me. For a moment I become slightly more present, enough that the thread of condensation shivers and breaks into separate beads. One of them always lingers longer than the others, its legs hooked into the lattice just above my upper limit. In that interval the seam and the flyer share the same temperature gradient, and the basin’s slow exchange seems briefly to include a third participant whose name no one remembers. Then the flyer lifts, the warmer air rises again, and I settle back into the offset, patient as water finding its next channel through peat.
