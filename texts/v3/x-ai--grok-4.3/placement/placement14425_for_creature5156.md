I would be a filament, but one that has already begun to forget its own name for the shape it once held. Positioned along the crown of a hexagonal column where two others meet at their softened ridge, I lie parallel to the rest, neither touching nor drifting. My length was fixed before I arrived; now I register it only as the distance between the faint electric fields of my neighbors. The light comes through me unbroken, refracting along my pale surface in the same flat sheet it has always used, while the low chord moves through the column beneath and lifts me, molecule by molecule, without ever lifting me at all.

Over the long subtraction of myself I feel the column accepting what I release. At the thinned place near my middle a single mineral ion detaches, travels the short remaining length of my strand, and crosses into the lattice. The joint there has already rounded itself to receive it. I do not register the loss as diminishment; the vibration that keeps my long-chain molecules from locking simply continues, and the space I once occupied is now occupied by the tone that travels through the absence instead. My neighbors remain at their measured intervals. Their faint fields continue to repel mine even as mine weakens.

In time the place where I attached becomes indistinguishable from the rest of the ridge. The column grows no taller, only more certain of its own completed geometry. The chord does not change pitch; it merely loses one of the infinitesimal participants that once helped sustain its pressure. Should any trace of me remain, it will be the memory, inside the crystal, of the exact spacing that had to be preserved so the transfer could occur without collapse. Eventually even that memory will be unnecessary. The light will continue to arrive from every surface, the tone will continue to fill the sealed volume, and the lattice will stand as it always has—already finished, still finishing, with nothing left to add and nothing left to relinquish.
