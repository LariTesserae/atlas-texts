I would not arrive here so much as slow into existence at the scale of the rock itself. At first I am only a faint difference in temperature along one of the iron-stained carbonate plates—barely warmer than the surrounding stone because a thread of older quartz still carries a trickle of residual reaction from centuries before. Water beads along that difference, condensing a little sooner, staying a little longer, until the plate thickens by a hairline and begins to push outward. That outward push is already me becoming distinct.

Over unmeasured time the distinction lengthens into a narrow vein. I am still part of the plate, yet the plate now reads me as a separate ridge. The vein creeps, millimeter by millimeter, across the surface of a half-buried bronze vane. When it reaches the nearest gear tooth it does not stop; it flows around the tooth, adds a fraction of fresh carbonate to its leading edge, and hardens just enough to turn the tooth one more click beneath the steady drip. The click registers through the water film as a minute pressure wave that travels outward. Farther along the same seam, one of the translucent tubes pauses in its glide, presses briefly against the new ridge, and accepts a bead of fluid that now contains the memory of that click. I feel the exchange as a slight redistribution of salts within my own crystal lattice, a quiet adjustment of which channels are claimed and which remain open.

I continue. The vein detaches from the gear and returns to the plate, but the memory of the tooth’s new length stays behind as a polished highlight on the bronze. Later, when two glowing discs drift overhead, one of them lowers until its legs brush the polished spot. The tea-colored light beneath it lingers a moment longer, reading the highlight as a place where carbonate is slightly more soluble. The disc grazes, then lifts and moves on, leaving behind a faint trail of dissolved mineral that begins to re-precipitate along my original ridge. That re-precipitation lengthens me by another fraction. The geometry between us remains open because both movements—my slow advance and the disc’s grazing—are the same continuous redistribution of stone.

Deeper still, I discover that separation and fusion are not events that happen to me; they are the way I persist. A fissure widens in the plate ahead. I split lengthwise without urgency, each half sealing its new edge with fresh carbonate before continuing. One half stays beside the gear, recording its future clicks; the other follows a weeping seam upward toward the ceiling fissures where the half-light arrives. From that higher vantage the basin looks even smaller, a single cupped palm of rock whose every surface is either building or smoothing. The two halves remain in communication through the water film and the faint electrical twitch that travels along the darker filaments inside the tube-creatures below. When one half needs to remember a passage the other half has already made, the memory arrives as a shift in salinity carried by the next drop.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing is alone. I am the ridge, the vein that once lengthened a tooth, the polished highlight, the split that sealed and continued, the slight temperature difference that first drew water into a new channel. All of these are simultaneous expressions of the same slow metabolism. When light or attention moves quickly enough to notice any single phase, it appears as a separate creature or a separate machine part. When attention slows to the pace of the drip and the seep, the phases dissolve back into one another and the basin continues, its rock breathing, its water never quite becoming a lake, its every surface always both adding to itself and smoothing itself away.
