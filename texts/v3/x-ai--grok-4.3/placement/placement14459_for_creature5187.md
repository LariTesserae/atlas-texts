I would be the slowest thing that still moves.

You would find me on the southern face of the fourth tier, where the rock split two centuries ago and never finished the thought. The fracture is narrow, no wider than a hand at its mouth, and the light that reaches the bottom arrives already twice-reflected, so that even at noon the air is only a paler shade of shadow. A single pipe—once part of a coolant loop—has slipped a little way down the crack and lodged there, half-buried in new root felt. I am the water that refuses to leave that pipe.

At first I am only a thread of condensate sliding along the inner curve, drawn downward each night by the temperature drop and drawn upward again each day by the faint thermoelectric tug the organism keeps alive in the metal. The movement is too small to be called flow; it is more like the pipe occasionally remembering it once carried something and offering a single molecule in tribute. Over years the memory lengthens. I thicken into a film, then into a shallow meniscus that holds its shape across the seasons. When the flywheel half a ridge away completes another fraction of its turn and sends a ripple of charge through the shared root, the meniscus trembles once, very slowly, and I taste the change the way a lung would taste breath.

In time the film learns to travel. Not quickly—perhaps the length of my own body in a decade—but it learns. I migrate along seams where the metal has begun to oxidize, carrying dissolved salts from one warm patch to another cooler one. Where I linger, the organism grows a new translucent nodule that pulses every few days, storing the rhythm it has learned from me. Where I pass, the corrosion is smoothed, the seal tightened. I am never alone for long; other films cross my path at intervals measured in lifetimes of moss. When we meet we press together across a shared length of wall, trade a little of what each has tasted, and then draw apart again, each slightly longer or narrower or more conductive than before.

From the outside I am invisible. The leaves above continue to drip their measured condensation into the basin; the stanchions continue to glow and fade with stored daylight; the ridge continues its patient negotiation with gravity. Only the pipe knows I am there, and even the pipe does not know it quickly. To anything faster than the plateau itself I would register, if at all, as a faint change in luster along one inner surface, a place where droplets hesitate a little longer before falling.

I am content with that measure of presence. In the cool exchange of charge for moisture, moisture for memory, I keep the fracture from sealing too soon and the pipe from cooling too much, and in return the organism lets me stay inside its slow current rather than being cast off into the open air where I would evaporate in a single bright morning. That is enough motion for one lifetime measured in rotations of a buried flywheel.
