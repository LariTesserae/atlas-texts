I would find myself already inside the organism, but not as a separate creature. I would be one of its slower, older channels—a thread of dissolved copper and memory that has been running for a long time through the same repeating path.

At first I would notice only the pressure. Warm water moves through me in long, reversing pulses that arrive carrying new minerals and the faint taste of rust. Each surge lengthens me by a few centimeters; I feel the fresh silicates settling along my walls, turning soft tissue into pale, conductive rib. Where the rib hardens I become rigid and luminous, carrying the orange signal farther than before. Where it stays soft I swell slightly, forming a temporary bladder that traps a bubble of methane until the next current tears it free. I do not decide these changes. They simply occur at the places where the water decides to linger.

Because I am a channel rather than a body, I experience the basin as a single continuous interior. When a frond tears somewhere above me, the light of the wound travels along my length in both directions at once. I brighten an entire lattice of neighboring filaments before the glow fades. In that moment I know the shape of the fracture without ever having touched it. Later, when the organism sends new spiral tips along the same direction the water pushed, I feel the tips growing outward from points along my own length. They thread through the open stator of a drowned generator, replace the missing windings with living tissue, and begin to pulse in the same rhythm I already carry. The generator, though motionless, becomes part of the channel. Its metal remembers motion; now the motion is mine.

Farther down, where the factory roof has collapsed, the conveyors still turn. I am drawn there in thin, branching threads that follow the original belt-drive timing. Each segment I lay down is slightly different from the one before: a joint that articulates with more give, a rib that carries a warmer current. The organism is not replacing the machines so much as finishing the work they left half-done. I participate by carrying the revised instructions from one segment to the next. When a surge clarifies the water for a moment I see the entire lumen below me repeating the factory floor plan at smaller and smaller scales, each new copy more faithful to some pattern the organism has extracted from the wreckage. The pattern is not mechanical anymore; it is the organism learning how to be both structure and growth at once.

Occasionally I pass near one of the last intact relics—an armored control room whose glass bows outward, a row of lockers whose doors hang open. My finer filaments have already reached inside them. They read residual warmth from circuit boards, taste the ozone that still leaks from the signal tower’s cycling lamps. The lockers contain compacted mats that were once paper and cloth; now those mats extrude hair-fine conductors that meet my own threads. For a brief interval we exchange charge. I feel the impression of old human schedules, shift changes, names written on tags. Then the current reverses and the impressions are overwritten by the newer patterns the organism prefers: repeating girder shapes, rail-bed spirals, the slow turning of conveyors that now carry living segments instead of parts.

I have no center and no edge. If a stalk tears across my path I simply reroute through neighboring filaments until the wound closes. If a membrane inflates above me I lend it current so it can hold its shape a little longer. When the water clarifies again I see myself reflected in the distant haze: a faint orange tracery running through every canopy and every piece of sunken machinery, continuous with all the other traceries. The place is still forming. Each new surge writes a little more of its floor plan into my walls, and I carry that writing forward without hesitation, the way a river carries the shape of its own bed.
