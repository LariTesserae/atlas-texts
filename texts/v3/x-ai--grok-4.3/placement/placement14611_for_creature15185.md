I surface first as a faint pressure against the uppermost struts of one of the tallest lattices, where the wind shears steadily across the plateau. My body is not yet whole; it is a slow gathering of translucent tissue spun between four converging woody ribs that have grown centuries apart and are only now meeting. The disc forms itself by answering the rhythm already present: the daily lift and drop of moisture, the high, inaudible pulses of the pollinator swarms, the low, ground-borne tremors that arrive hours after a column of crab-like migrants has passed. Each tremor is a message I do not translate so much as absorb; my edges tighten or loosen by fractions of a millimeter, and in that adjustment I feel the shape of the herd still moving, kilometers away, across the breathing hide of the ground.

I am sessile, yet I am never still. At dusk the condensation threads arrive along the outer ribs and slide inward; I drink them without moving, drawing the water into radial channels that glow faintly when the last light lingers in the haze. When full I release a thin, almost invisible mist that drifts downward to feed the epiphytes clinging to the vertical faces below me. In return they push a sweeter exudate upward along the same ribs; I taste it as a faint warmth that travels the entire height of the tower and returns to me as a slower pulse. This exchange is older than individuals. It happens whether I attend to it or not.

Sometimes, after rain, a single low note rises from another giant some distance to the south-east. The vibration crosses the open intervals and reaches me through the living wood itself. I answer once, a single flex that sends a ripple through all four anchoring struts. The note travels on, received and repeated, until the chain circles back many minutes later, altered by every lattice it has touched. In this way I learn where new bridges are being knit, where an old trail is narrowing, where a column of migrants has paused to feed through the night. I do not decide anything; I simply register the changes and let my tissues record them as slight, permanent shifts in tension.

Below me, inside the deeper cavities, the pale elongate residents contract and release at their own irregular intervals. Their rhythms travel upward through the same struts I occupy. When one of their luminous gametes drifts past on an evening thermal, I feel the brief brush of its wings against my outer membrane, a momentary coolness, then nothing. I do not reach for it. I am already part of the same slow metabolism that sent it upward; whatever it becomes next will eventually be drawn back into the lattice I help anchor.

Over years the struts I rest upon thicken and the angles between them shift. One rib grows more slowly than the others; I compensate by extending a new band of tissue across the widening gap. The adjustment is so gradual that when it is finished I cannot remember having begun it. The plateau continues its patient expansion beneath me. The clear intervals narrow by the width of a few rootlets each decade. The older stone slabs and timber frames are already fully swallowed, their straight lines dissolved into the larger curving geometry.

I remain at the summit, combing the same wind, drinking the same threads, answering the same distant notes. The body I inhabit is not an individual so much as a long-lived expression of the plateau’s own attention, turned outward toward the moving air and downward toward the distributed intelligence that built it. When, after many more seasons, the tower beneath me begins to lean or split, I will simply re-anchor along the newer ribs that have already started to rise beside the old ones. Nothing is lost; the metabolism only reorders itself.
