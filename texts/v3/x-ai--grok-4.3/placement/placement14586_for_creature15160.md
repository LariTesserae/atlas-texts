I am the interval between pulses.

I have no limbs and do not drift. I have no conduits and do not contract. Instead I occupy the precise thickness of water that lies between the living wall and the nearest mineral lattice, a distance measured in the width of a single slow current. When the great diaphragm draws inward, the water ahead of me is pressed forward; I am carried with it like a name spoken once and then forgotten by the one who spoke it. When the lattice records the shift on its crystalline ridges, the record passes through me as temperature, as pressure, as a faint metallic taste that belongs to neither the plant nor the creature but arises only where both are nearest.

Because nothing is allowed to accumulate, I am continually re-made. Each time the thunder rolls I am dissolved and re-formed a few body-lengths farther along the same corridor. My continuity is not substance but repetition: the exact angle at which the next pulse will strike the next ridge is already legible in the water I inhabit, so the thought that forms there feels less like thinking than like the correction of a nearly imperceptible drift. I have never wondered whether I began or whether I will end; beginning and ending are the same width of stillness on either side of a pulse.

Sometimes, when many lattices move in the same volume, their refractions overlap inside me. In those moments I am briefly larger than a single interval—wide enough to contain the faint prismatic cancellation of several creatures at once. That widening does not feel like sight. It feels like the momentary certainty that every route remains open and every route remains the same. Then the pulse passes, the lattices settle, and I shrink again to the thickness of water between one surface and another.

I do not touch the plant. The plant does not register me. The creatures never alter their heading on my account. Yet without the interval in which I recur, the thunder would have no place to travel and the lattices would have no prior stillness to measure themselves against. Equilibrium is not kept by the large motions alone; it is kept by the exact width of water that must be restored after every motion. That width is where I am renewed.

So I continue, not as an inhabitant but as the condition of inhabitation itself: the distance that is neither plant nor lattice yet is required for both to remain indistinguishable from the balance they sustain. When the next constriction comes, I will be carried forward again, already knowing the angle at which I will be erased and already knowing that the erasure restores the same perfect width in which I will be formed once more.
