The land lies low and ancient, a broad valley basin that has been worn nearly flat by time beyond counting. Its surface is a mottled plain of pale, water-polished stone—layers of quartzite and banded chert that once stood as hills and now spread in gentle, almost imperceptible undulations. Water moves constantly across and through this stone. After each cool shower the ground weeps; thin films slide down curved faces and collect in long, shallow troughs that never quite become lakes. Between rains the air itself is damp, and moisture condenses on every exposed facet, leaving the rock perpetually beaded and faintly steaming in the dim light.

Nothing green grows here. The surface is bare of leaf or root. In the shallow depressions, however, translucent mineral crusts form—slow, branching deposits that resemble frost yet are warm to the touch and pulse with faint internal currents. These growths are not vegetation; they are the landscape itself rearranging its dissolved salts into temporary architectures, dissolving again when fresher water arrives.

Fauna is everywhere and nowhere visible at once. Small, translucent creatures the size of a hand move just beneath the stone in networks of hairline fractures, their passage marked by momentary brightening of the rock. Larger forms—segmented, low-slung things with vibrating membranes stretched across their backs—drift above the surface on thin currents of rising vapor. Their constant, overlapping vibration produces the single overwhelming sound: a layered roar that fills every interval of air, neither song nor call but a ceaseless, low-frequency pressure that travels through bone as readily as through sky. The noise never ceases; even when the creatures are distant the hollowed stone carries it onward like a drum skin.

Scattered across this open plain stand the only upright things: low, hand-stacked rings of the same quartzite, no higher than a crouching person. Some still hold thin poles of worked bone or chert lashed with mineral-fiber cord; most have collapsed into neat arcs of rubble. They are spaced far apart, each visible from the next only as a faint silhouette in the grey light. Inside the best-preserved rings the stone underfoot is darker, polished by repeated passage, and small basins have been chipped to catch and hold the weeping water.

The light remains low and steady, a perpetual half-dusk that makes distances deceptive; a ring half a day’s walk away can appear close enough to touch until the eye adjusts. Cool air drifts slowly across the basin, carrying mist that forms and dissolves without ever becoming rain. When the wind strengthens, loose plates of stone click and slide, adding a dry, skeletal counterpoint to the deeper animal roar. Movement is constant—creatures rising and settling, water films advancing and retreating, the slow, almost invisible rearrangement of the mineral crusts. Nothing rests for long, yet the plain itself feels settled, as though these cycles have repeated in the same pattern since the hills were first worn down.
