The place is a high seamount slope still knitting itself together from the seafloor upward, its raw basalt and accreted mineral crust only a few thousand years old. Warm water presses against every surface at a constant, skin-deep pressure; currents arrive from below in slow, laminar sheets rather than breaking surf. Because the light that reaches here is already filtered through two hundred meters of overlying ocean, everything appears in the same muted palette of copper-green and bruise-purple, interrupted only by faint, self-generated glimmers from the vegetation itself.

The vegetation is everywhere and almost architectural. Broad, rubbery fronds of a kelp-like plant grow in dense, overlapping layers that behave less like forests and more like scaffolding. Their holdfasts have already begun to cement loose scree into new terraces, and the oldest stems have been colonized by encrusting sponges that add pale, calcareous weight. Between these layers hang curtains of finer filaments that drift with the sluggish current like half-drawn veils; when two filaments touch they sometimes fuse, gradually turning open lanes into living mesh. The result is a three-dimensional city fabric that is still half-plant, half-slope.

Scattered through this mesh are the first deliberate constructions of the inhabitants. They do not yet use metal; instead they harvest the thickest kelp stipes, split them while still living, and weave the tough inner fibers around basalt nodules that have only recently cooled enough to touch. The resulting frames resemble unfinished longhouses whose walls are still growing outward as the plants they are lashed to continue to thicken. Some of these frames have already been roofed with sheets of mineralized sponge that accumulate like new thatch each season. Because growth is so gradual, a single dwelling may take three human generations to finish its first storey; corridors and plazas between dwellings are therefore still provisional, defined more by the absence of new fronds than by any finished paving.

The overall effect is of simultaneous density and incompletion. Every vertical surface carries at least three vertical strata of life and construction: the raw rock at the back, the woven living lattice in the middle, and the translucent newer growth reaching outward. Horizontal movement is possible only by following sinuous paths that open and close over years as stems fuse or are harvested for building. Sound travels poorly; the thick water and the sound-absorbing mesh of fibers reduce most motion to a low, continuous hush, broken only rarely by the soft pop of a gas bubble released from a sponge or the faint creak of a stipe settling under added weight.

Fauna is scarce. The same density that shelters the settlement also limits larger swimmers; anything bigger than a hand must negotiate an ever-shifting maze of living cords. Occasional pale, slow-moving grazers drift along the outer edges of the mesh, rasping at the oldest fronds, but they seldom venture deeper into the inhabited core. Their presence registers less as motion than as a momentary dimming of the faint bioluminescent threads that line the paths.

Because the entire slope is still rising, albeit imperceptibly, the relationship between settlement and terrain keeps rearranging itself. A terrace that served as a gathering floor last century may now be half-buried under fresh mineral deposition or newly exposed by a slow slumping of loose sediment. The inhabitants do not resist these changes; their weaving and harvesting techniques are calibrated to the rate at which the mountain itself is still being born.
