The fissure is no wider than a person’s shoulders where it splits the flank of a high basalt ridge, yet once inside it the walls press closer still, folding into a tight vertical throat that descends only a few body-lengths before expanding into a single contained chamber. Here the rock has not finished becoming. Freshly exhumed mineral pillows and bulbous extrusions crowd against one another with almost no interstitial space; each new layer still radiates the day’s stored heat, so the air remains dry, metallic, and thick. No water ever reaches this pocket; any moisture that might arrive is instantly seized by the chemistry of the stone and turned into invisible vapor that rises in slow, shimmering sheets.

The darkness is not absolute. Low, ember-colored veins still pulse through certain seams where fresh material is being forced upward from unknown depth. These veins give just enough light to reveal that the chamber is not empty. Countless slender, jointed creatures—neither worm nor centipede—move through the narrow gaps, their chitin rasping against the crowding minerals. They travel in slow, patient loops, scraping microscopic layers from the newest rock and depositing it elsewhere; their motions are the only motion. Over decades the deposit they leave will harden into another pillow; over centuries that pillow will be compressed into the next false wall. The process is loud: a constant low grinding of plates shifting against plates, punctuated by the high, dry click of countless legs and the occasional sharp pop of superheated gas escaping a newly sealed seam. The sounds do not echo; there is no room left for echo.

Nothing green or soft has ever grown here. The rock itself behaves like an unfinished machine—pressure, heat, and mineral extrusion substituting for pistons and furnaces. Every surface is still raw, still forming, still erasing the faint traces of earlier, now-buried layers. The creatures do not rest. Their work is the only weather this sealed place knows, and it unfolds on the timescale of cooling stone. Outside, on the exposed ridge, wind and frost continue their ordinary work; inside the fissure the mountain continues its own slower labor, indifferent to the difference.
