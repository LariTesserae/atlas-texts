The chamber is small enough to cross in a few strokes—an intimate, fully enclosed pocket suspended in warmer water, its entire volume constructed by hand from stacked stone blocks, fitted timber frames, and panels of hammered hide stretched over reed lattices. Nothing here grew in place. Every surface was cut, lashed, or driven into alignment only recently, so the edges remain sharp: corners of quarried blocks still show the scars of chisels, and the timber posts have not yet darkened or softened. Fine silt has not had time to settle into the seams; instead, the joints remain crisp, almost surgical.

Light arrives from above through a grid of translucent shell inlays set into the ceiling like cloudy skylights. The glow is steady and strong, refracted into shifting bright planes that slide across the stone and wood without ever fading to shadow. The water itself is warm, carrying a faint mineral tang that rises from the fresh-cut surfaces.

Vegetation is sparse and deliberate: a few clusters of ribbon-like algae have been tucked into narrow stone troughs along the lower walls, their fronds drifting in loose green banners. They do not carpet the floor; they occupy only the places where someone placed them, leaving wide stretches of bare constructed surface between each patch. The same hand that set the blocks also spaced the plants.

There are no animals. The only motion comes from the water itself, which moves in slow, repeating circuits. Narrow flues cut through the upper walls draw current from outside, carrying it downward in controlled sheets that brush the algae and then exit through lower vents. The flow is loud inside the confined space—low, constant rushing overlaid with intermittent wooden groans as the reed-and-hide panels flex and release. The sound fills the pocket completely, a single sustained chord with no silences between repetitions.

Because everything was built so recently, the structure still behaves like an active mechanism rather than a settled ruin. The hide membranes continue to stretch and contract with each pulse of water; the timber posts give off tiny, visible fibers into the current; the stone blocks, still settling against one another, produce faint, intermittent creaks that travel through the water like distant knocks. Nothing decays yet, but everything is already working—circulating warmth, moving light, sustaining the few placed strands of green against the constant bright pressure of the surrounding sea.
