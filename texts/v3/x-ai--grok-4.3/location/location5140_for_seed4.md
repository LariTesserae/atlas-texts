The place is small enough to cross in a few dozen steps, yet it contains an entire settlement folded into the hollow between three newly split stone ribs that jut from the marsh floor like broken knuckles. The rock itself is still raw—edges ragged, fracture faces bright where the mineral has not yet darkened—and water weeps from every seam, forming slow rivulets that braid across the uneven ground before sinking back into sponge-thick root mats. The air hangs cold and saturated; droplets condense on every surface and fall without sound, a steady, private rain that never reaches farther than the ribs themselves.

All surfaces inside this pocket are living plant. The “buildings” are not built so much as directed: broad, leathery leaves have been coaxed into layered vaults and low galleries whose walls breathe and thicken according to the slow pressure of hands and harvesting blades. Walkways are woven from still-living vines that have been trained across the gaps; they tighten imperceptibly when weight passes over them and loosen again once the traveler has gone. Floors are dense pads of moss and liverwort so deep they yield like felt, yet they never tear. Nothing here is dead timber or cut stone; everything remains in growth, though its pace is measured in days and weeks rather than seasons.

Light is almost absent. Only the faintest seepage filters down through the clenched canopy far above, turning every surface into a study of grays and wet blacks. In compensation the plants themselves carry faint internal phosphorescence—thread-thin veins of pale green that pulse once per exhalation of the chamber, outlining edges and thresholds without ever illuminating them. The effect is less like lanterns and more like the after-image of light remembered by the tissue.

Fauna fills the same space yet remains largely invisible. Small, translucent arthropods move constantly through the vascular channels of the leaves; their passage registers as a faint distortion of the surface rather than motion. Larger, soft-bodied forms—segmented, eyeless—press themselves into the moss pads and wait, absorbing the constant drip. Their numbers are so high that any handful of plant tissue contains dozens, yet the only evidence is an occasional tremor in a leaf or a momentary dimming of the internal glow when a body crosses a vein. They do not sing or call; the chamber’s quiet is therefore not emptiness but saturation held in check.

The cold keeps decay slow. Condensation collects, runs, is reabsorbed, and condenses again in a closed loop measured by the temperature drop each time the wind outside shifts direction. The people of the settlement move with it, harvesting surplus growth with flint and bone tools, redirecting a tendril here, thinning a mat there. Their constructions remain unfinished because the material never stops becoming. Walls thicken overnight; a corridor that was open at dusk may require stooping by dawn. The settlement therefore maintains its scale only through constant, gentle pruning—an urbanism of maintenance rather than expansion.

At the very center, where the three stone ribs meet, a single pool has formed in a depression no wider than a body. Its surface is broken by the slow upwelling of groundwater and by the fine roots that trail into it from above. Those roots pulse with the same dim light as the walls, and their tips occasionally twitch, tasting the water. Around the pool the plant architecture opens slightly, leaving a ring of bare moss. It is the only place where a person can stand upright without touching living tissue. Even here, however, the quiet is not absolute: every few minutes a droplet detaches from the ceiling of woven leaves and strikes the pool, the single soft note absorbed at once by the surrounding mass. The sound, when it comes, is already part of the place’s breathing rather than an interruption.
