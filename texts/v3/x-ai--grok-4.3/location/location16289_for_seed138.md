A low, motionless basin of dark water and fresh stone holds the settlement in perfect suspension. The ground is only a hand’s breadth above the water table; every excavation or foundation trench weeps steadily, and the air itself is never dry. Warm vapor rises from dark pools between the buildings and condenses on every metal edge, forming slow droplets that fall at the same intervals forever. Nothing evaporates or rises further; nothing floods or recedes. The climate is fixed at a thick, breathing heat that makes the skin feel filmed at once.

The structures are recent—clean angles and sharp lips of poured concrete and riveted iron—but already half-submerged. Narrow channels, cut when the ground was first opened, run between the blocks like black mirrors. They separate the buildings rather than connect them, leaving wide, empty margins of still water and pale mud. Patchy reeds and salt-stunted sedges stand in the thinnest bands along the higher lips of those channels; between them the ground is nearly bare, only a faint crust of algae and occasional white salt rings where water has touched and stayed.

Above and within the buildings, heavy engines turn at constant speed. Their exhaust stacks are short and wide, releasing a low, continuous roar that does not rise or fall. The sound is the same whether one stands beside a single lathe shed or at the far edge of the basin; it fills the air the way heat fills a closed room. Electric lamps glow behind clouded glass, giving a narrow, ochre light that barely reaches the wet ground. The lamps never flicker and the engines never labor. Everything has reached the same temperature as the water and the stone.

In the gaps between the structures, life moves without altering the equilibrium. Frogs rest on the warm concrete lips; dragonflies patrol the same six meters of channel air again and again; small fish hang in the tea-colored water, facing the faint current from a single overflow pipe. Their numbers are high, yet they leave the sparse plants untouched and never disturb the slow drip from the roofs. The creatures remain part of the machinery’s steady sound rather than interruptions to it.

Nothing decays; nothing is added. The sharp corners of the newest retaining walls have not yet been worn. The water that seeps from them continues to bead and fall at the same slow rhythm it found on the first day the channels were opened. In this fixed margin of heat, wet stone, and constant low thunder, the settlement sits exactly as it first settled, neither growing outward nor sinking further.
