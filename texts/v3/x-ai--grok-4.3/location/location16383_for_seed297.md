Beneath a featureless, pale sky the land extends as an unbroken mineral plain, low and horizon-wide, its surface rising only in subtle ridges and shallow basins no taller than a standing person. The ground itself is not soil but a single, continuous sheet of fused silicates and metallic salts, its topmost layer crystallized into knife-edged facets that catch the steady, unfiltered daylight and return it without heat or glare. These facets grow upward in dense thickets—slender prisms, branching lattices, and interlocking needles—packing so closely that their edges nearly touch, forming layered canopies and understories of geometrically exact “growths.” No water ever touches them; instead, trace vapors of ionized noble gases circulate between the crystals, depositing fresh mineral layers along their sharp planes so slowly that the entire structure has remained unchanged for uncountable ages.

The air is warm and perfectly still, carrying a faint metallic tang that never fades or intensifies. Sound scarcely exists: the occasional soft tick when a new atomic layer locks into place is rarer than a heartbeat, and even that vibration is swallowed instantly by the open space. Nothing moves except the light itself, which sometimes fractures along an invisible seam and reassembles a moment later exactly as before—an imperceptible stutter that leaves the crystals exactly where they stood.

Embedded within the lattices are filament-thin circuits of an engineering that no longer obeys ordinary physics. Fields generated by these filaments fold space into minute, self-correcting loops, holding every crystal at the precise angle it first achieved when the plain first emerged. Where two such loops occasionally overlap, brief pockets of inverted gravity or reversed entropy appear for an instant before the larger equilibrium reasserts itself; the crystals register these events only as an extra, perfectly symmetrical facet.

Life here is almost nonexistent. Once in many lifetimes a translucent, membrane-like organism drifts between the prisms, feeding on the ionized gas and leaving no trace. Otherwise the only motion belongs to the minerals themselves, continuing their imperceptible outward accretion under the constant, changeless light. The plain therefore feels simultaneously newborn and eternal: every edge still sharp, every plane still flawless, and every law of its strange chemistry locked into permanent, silent balance.
