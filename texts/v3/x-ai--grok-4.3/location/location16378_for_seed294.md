The place is a vast, submerged plateau of engineered stone rising from depths that press like layered time itself. Every surface—floor, arch, slope, and freestanding pier—was laid down long before any living presence arrived, then left to settle into angles only water and pressure can carve over ages. The material is a single, unbroken matrix of pale, glass-smooth mineral that once held joints and planed edges; now those seams have dissolved into soft ridges and shallow concavities, their once-sharp lines blurred into one another until the distinction between wall and floor survives only as memory. No loose sediment drifts across them. The cold water keeps the mineral clean, and what little particulate exists travels in slow, horizontal veils rather than settling.

Light arrives from far above as a single, constant dusk filtered through miles of water, turning every edge into a faint luminous halo without ever revealing true color. The same dimness makes distance deceptive: a tower that seems a few body-lengths away may prove to be hours distant when crossed, because the open scale of the plateau allows entire avenues and plazas to recede into gray uniformity before another feature interrupts the view.

In the spaces between these forms move the only occupants. They are not numerous at any single moment, yet they fill the volume over time. Some are translucent lattices of calcareous rods that drift like slow kites, catching stray currents to hold position; others are dense, fist-sized spheres that roll or tumble across the stone in short bursts before stopping, their surfaces covered in fine, hair-like filaments that taste the mineral for trace ions. Larger aggregations appear as loose clouds of needle-shaped swimmers traveling in coordinated spirals that last only seconds before dissolving into individuals again. None leave waste or trails; whatever they consume is drawn directly from the water or the mineral they briefly contact. Their motion is the only visible sign that the plateau remains active beneath its ancient stillness.

Turbulence here is not continuous storm but sudden, localized rearrangement. Without warning a whole section of current will invert, lifting a cloud of the needle-swimmers in a spiraling column that rises, twists, and then collapses back onto the stone. In the same instant, distant towers may seem to shift position by a few degrees, an optical effect produced when water layers slide across one another. Sound follows the same pattern: mostly absent, then broken by a single low-frequency thrum that travels through the mineral and the body at once before fading. The quiet returns immediately, as if the plateau has exhaled once and chosen not to repeat the gesture.

No growth occurs on any surface. The mineral itself supplies whatever sparse chemistry the creatures require, and the creatures in turn keep the mineral from further dissolution by their brief, precise contacts. The only rule that can be observed is that nothing accumulates and nothing is removed; every shift of current or creature rearranges what already exists into a new but equally stable configuration. The plateau continues, ancient and complete, its made geometry slowly yielding to pressure while remaining entirely itself.
