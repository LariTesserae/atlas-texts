The world is a single, unbroken plate of compressed mineral strata lifted so high that its edges vanish into a haze of their own dust. From any point on its surface the horizon lies days away, a faint, unchanging line where pale rock dissolves into pale sky. The stone itself is warm to the touch—never hot, never cool—retaining a low, steady heat that rises from depths sealed long before any eye observed them.

Nothing grows. Nothing has ever grown. In place of soil there is only a slow rain of crystalline grit that sifts from higher ledges and settles into shallow basins. Over uncounted ages this grit has been pressed back into the bedrock, leaving every surface rounded and matte, every ridge softened into a long, patient curve. The erosion is total; even the sharpest fractures have been sanded into flowing contours that suggest frozen waves rather than broken crust.

Scattered across these vast rises stand the cities—low, wide complexes no taller than the surrounding mesas. Their walls are the same mineral as the ground, differentiated only by the perfect regularity of their seams and the dark mouths of loading ramps. Inside, conveyor systems and catalytic furnaces continue their work at a pace almost indistinguishable from stillness. Occasionally a single metal vane will turn, or a pressure valve will sigh; the sounds travel no farther than the next courtyard before they are absorbed again by the stone and the empty air.

Between the cities the surface is nearly featureless. Here and there the bedrock is interrupted by broad, shallow depressions whose edges are lined with thin crusts of evaporated salts. Within each basin move the fauna—flat, jointed organisms whose bodies are flexible lattices of the same minerals that make the ground. They travel in loose caravans, their motion visible only when viewed for hours: a slow folding and unfolding of plates as they nudge sheets of grit into new alignments, then rest. Their abundance is felt rather than seen; at any moment thousands of them are present, yet the distance between individuals is so great that the land never appears crowded.

Light arrives evenly from a sun that never quite clears the haze. Shadows are short and soft-edged, falling at the same shallow angle for most of the long day. Wind, when it moves, does so without turbulence, carrying only the faintest dry whisper across the open stone. Most of the time, however, there is no wind at all. The only persistent sound is the almost imperceptible ticking of cooling metal inside the distant factories, a rhythm measured in hours rather than seconds.

Over still longer intervals the plate itself continues its imperceptible adjustment. Entire ridges subside by the width of a hand over centuries; basins fill, then drain again through hairline fissures that open and seal without drama. The cities adjust with equal patience, rerouting their tunnels when a new depression forms and abandoning others when the slope changes. Nothing is sudden. Nothing is rushed. The world proceeds at the speed its own slow chemistry allows, and the scattered machines and the migrating lattices keep time together.
