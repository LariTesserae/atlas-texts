In the low basin, water gathers without hurry. It seeps from fissures that opened only centuries ago, when the crust here last convulsed. The rock is still sharp at its edges, knife-clean planes and fresh fracture planes that have not yet softened under rain or root. Moisture beads constantly on every surface; it gathers, fattens, and falls in long, patient intervals, each drop audible only because the surrounding air is otherwise so still.

Nothing grows thickly. A few pale, hair-thin filaments of fungus cling to the undersides of ledges, spaced far apart, each one drawing what little warmth the rock still holds. Yet the space feels crowded. The air itself is dense with suspended droplets and with the slow circulation of warm, breath-damp currents that never quite rise or fall. In the total absence of light the droplets carry their own faint presence, a pressure more felt than seen, layering against skin and stone alike.

Scattered through the basin stand the remnants of engines and low sheds, half-swallowed by the same slow seep. Their metal has not rusted in the ordinary way; it has begun to soften and bloom into thin crystalline growths that click softly when the temperature shifts by a fraction of a degree. Some of the machines still turn, but only once in many hours, their gears advancing a single tooth before locking again. The sound travels no farther than a few body-lengths before the dense air swallows it.

Creatures move through this half-material medium. They are small, numerous, and almost entirely silent; they press against one another in slow drifts, exchanging places over the course of a single long breath. Some skim the water film that coats every surface; others drift inside the rock itself for a time, emerging again without apparent effort. Their presence registers as minute changes in temperature and in the direction of the condensing droplets.

At wide intervals the fabric of the place thins. A stretch of water suddenly reflects nothing, then reflects twice, then none. A sharp ridge may appear closer than it is, or farther, then settle again. Gravity here behaves like a slow liquid; over hours a dropped fragment may rise a hand-span before sinking back. These distortions move at the pace of the seepage itself, advancing and retreating across the basin without haste, leaving the scattered machines and the sparse filaments of growth momentarily altered in shape or position before restoring them, almost, to their previous state.
