The place is a compact highland shelf no larger than a cloistered garden, recently thrust upward so that its edges still show clean fractures and unworn facets of pale mineral. Angular towers of translucent salt and silica stand close together, their planes sharp enough to catch low sunlight in hard glints. Between and upon them grows a dense, continuous mantle of vegetation that requires no liquid water: sheets of fleshy, mineral-threaded fronds that draw warmth and trace elements directly from the rock. The growths overlap in many layers, some hanging in thin curtains, others swelling into rounded cushions that press against neighboring towers until the gaps between structures are almost gone.

The air is warm and motionless, faintly sweet with the slow exhalation of the living crust. Small, soft-bodied creatures—segmented, many-limbed, their surfaces dusted with the same mineral sheen as the towers—move through the interstices. They travel in patient arcs, seldom touching one another, their passage marked only by the faint flexing of the fronds they brush. No wind stirs; the only sounds are the almost inaudible creak of expanding mineral lattice at midday and the dry, paper-like settling of a frond when an animal leaves it. Over decades the towers lengthen by a finger’s width, the living cover thickens by another translucent skin, and the creatures trace the same routes across a surface that never quite repeats itself yet never changes quickly enough to be called motion. The entire shelf feels poised between emergence and endurance, a single, self-contained increment of a world still deciding what shape it will keep.
