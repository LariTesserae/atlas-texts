The basin is no more than a few hundred paces across, a shallow, ankle-deep depression left after uncountable ages of rain and root have planed the land nearly flat. Its floor is a single unbroken membrane of living mass: stems, rhizomes, and translucent leaf-fabrics fused into a continuous substrate that yields slightly underfoot like damp suede. From this carpet rise the remnants of an older city—low, curving shells whose original walls have long since been replaced by the same vegetation. The shells still suggest rooms, alleys, and a single broad central court, yet every surface now sweats a slow film of water that gathers into trembling beads before sliding away into hidden channels.

Because the elevation is low and the climate perpetually warm, the air holds a constant moisture that never quite becomes mist. Instead it condenses directly onto the upper surfaces of the broad leaves, then drips in patient, audible taps onto the living floor. The drops strike and are immediately absorbed, producing a soft, continual percussion that fills the space without ever rising into true silence. The same moisture keeps the vegetation turgid and dark; veins of deeper crimson and electric green pulse visibly beneath the surface whenever a heavier droplet lands, as though the entire mass is registering the impact.

Despite the total coverage of plant, the density remains strangely open. Between the larger structural forms—bulbous towers no taller than a person, elongated arches that once framed doorways—wide clearings of the same living carpet stretch empty. These intervals are not barren; they are simply fallow chambers where the growth has chosen, for a time, not to rise. Scattered through them stand solitary fruiting stalks, each capped with a shivering membrane that inflates and deflates like a slow lung. When one membrane bursts it releases a brief, high chime and a puff of spores that drift only a body-length before settling back into the substrate, reinforcing the same living floor they left.

The air moves constantly, though no wind crosses the rim of the basin. Instead the movement is generated from within: leaves curl and uncurl in slow waves, stems lengthen by visible increments, and thin aerial roots descend from overhead arches to probe the ground before withdrawing again. These motions produce a low, rushing texture beneath the louder sounds of the place—fauna that has learned the rhythm. Bright, many-legged creatures the size of thumbs scuttle along the veins of the carpet, their feet tapping out rapid codes that the plants answer with minute changes in surface tension. Larger, translucent flyers the size of open hands glide in shallow arcs from one shell to another, their wings shedding fine droplets that catch the ordinary daylight and throw brief, shifting spectra across the walls.

Embedded in the living matrix are remnants of an older technology that the plants have not erased so much as absorbed. At irregular intervals, glassy nodes no larger than a coin protrude from the stems. They glow with a steady, pale internal light that requires no external source; occasionally one node will brighten suddenly, and a neighboring stem will thicken or reorient within moments. These flashes are never quite simultaneous, yet they propagate through the entire floor like a decision traveling. The city remembers its former shape not in stone but in these distributed instructions, still executing plans whose original purpose is no longer legible.

At the center of the court the membrane draws itself upward into a low dome whose skin is thin enough to reveal slow currents moving beneath it. When a flyer lands there the currents quicken, and the dome swells visibly before subsiding again. The sound of the movement inside is the loudest in the basin: a wet, rolling pressure that seems to answer the scattered chimes and footfalls with its own deeper pulse. Around the dome the carpet has left a deliberate margin of bare, glistening ground no wider than a person’s stride, as if the living city still honors a single ancient law of clearance.

Everything here is in continuous, small-scale rearrangement—roots advancing and retreating, membranes opening and sealing, light nodes brightening and dimming—yet the overall contour of the basin remains unchanged. The ancient erosion has already done its final work; only the living overlay continues its turbulent housekeeping within the fixed, modest boundaries of a single overgrown plaza.
