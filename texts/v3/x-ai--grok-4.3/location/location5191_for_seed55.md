In the lightless deep, the world is all water and all made. What passes for a seafloor lies many kilometers below, flat as a planed plank yet composed of hand-set slabs, each one hewn from black basalt and fitted without mortar so that every joint is perfect and nearly invisible after uncounted ages. The slabs are not natural; their edges bear the faint, patient signatures of stone adzes and reed saws, smoothed further by the slow drift of silt that has settled into the seams like dust in the grain of old wood.

Above this floor rise the constructions: kilometer upon kilometer of low, broad terraces built from woven lattices of petrified reed and polished whalebone. On and between these lattices grow the only living things—dense stands of cold-water kelp and bladder-wrack engineered to endure permanent night and near-freezing temperatures. Their fronds are thick and rubbery, dark as ink, and they grow in deliberate layers: the lowest almost touching the stone, the next drifting a few body-lengths higher, then another, until the water itself seems to be woven from overlapping curtains of vegetation. The plants move only when the slowest of currents combs through them; hours pass between one frond and the next brushing together.

Nothing swims among the leaves. No current carries the tremor of fins or the scrape of a shell. The only motion is the gradual lengthening of the kelp itself, a growth measured in finger-widths per decade, and the even slower settling of microscopic silt that the plants continually filter and bind into new layers of living fabric. The water is so cold that breath would fog if any warm lungs were present; instead, only the faint pressure of depth remains, constant, patient, without turbulence.

The constructions continue upward and outward without interruption. What might once have been separate buildings have long since merged under the weight of their own additions until the entire basin, from one horizon of the water to the next, is a single continuous lattice of deliberate shape: broad ramps that slope at the gentlest of angles, circular basins whose rims are exactly level with one another, corridors whose widths were chosen so that a single woven curtain of kelp could span them. All of it was placed by hands that have been absent for longer than stone remembers. The only evidence of those hands is the absolute regularity of the work and the way every surface has been left slightly rough so that the plants would have somewhere to root.

Sound travels here only as the soft, continuous sigh of water threading through the lattices. Even that sigh is damped by the density of fronds; a falling stone would be swallowed before its impact reached more than a few body-lengths. Temperature gradients are almost nonexistent. Light does not arrive at all. Time passes in the slow accumulation of another millimeter of black silt, another hand-span of new growth along a reed lattice, another subtle settling of an already level terrace. The world is vast enough to contain its own weather of drifting cold fronts, yet those fronts move so gradually that they are indistinguishable from stillness. Everything that was ever built has already finished becoming what it will be.
