The plain lies low and open, a shallow basin where the horizon never quite arrives. It stretches in every direction as a single dark field, its scale measured not by distance but by the way scattered forms repeat without clustering. The ground underfoot is a dark, packed silt that gives slightly with each step, its surface threaded with faint cracks that never widen into true fissures. These cracks weep continuously: not in streams, but in slow, steady beads that gather, fall, and are reabsorbed before they can pool. The air itself is a condensing medium, warm and saturated, so that every surface—stone, brick, or living membrane—bears a perpetual film of moisture that never evaporates and never runs.

What remains of the city is not built so much as grown and then left. Great curved shells of a porous, bone-like material stand at wide intervals, their walls layered in concentric rings that mark decades of deposition and retreat. The rings are smooth where long erosion has planed them, yet they retain the faint imprint of former doorways and window ledges, now sealed shut by the same material that formed them. Between the shells the ground is empty for long stretches, interrupted only by low, ribbed ridges that might once have been streets or canals. Vegetation is present but threadbare: occasional tufts of pale, hair-like filaments that sprout directly from the weeping cracks and then lie flat, as if too heavy with water to rise. They are the only green visible; everything else is the color of wet slate and bruised carbonate.

Light does not arrive from above. Instead, a slow, internal glow leaks from the weeping seams themselves, a faint phosphorescence that makes the nearest surfaces readable only within a few paces. Beyond that radius the basin becomes featureless black, though the black is never still. At irregular intervals the darkness itself seems to fold inward for a moment, swallowing the glow and then releasing it again, as if the medium of space were briefly interrupted. These folds are silent and leave no trace except a momentary change in the rhythm of condensation on nearby walls.

No animals move here. The only motion belongs to water and to the slow rearrangement of the structures. One shell may lean a fraction of an inch over the course of an hour; another may extrude a new ring while an older one dissolves back into silt. The processes are simultaneous and unsynchronized, so the plain is never the same shape from one moment to the next, yet the changes accumulate so gradually that the emptiness between forms remains constant. The sound that accompanies these shifts is almost absent: only the soft, regular patter of single droplets returning to the ground and a low, continuous hiss of vapor re-forming into liquid on every surface. No wind crosses the basin, and the air holds its own warmth without stirring.

At the scale of the basin the city and the land are no longer separate categories. The shells are as weathered and settled as the silt ridges between them; both belong to the same long equilibrium of deposition, dissolution, and re-condensation. The only rule that remains visibly intact is the persistence of moisture and the absence of anything that would consume or scatter it. Everything else—distance, solidity, sequence—is permitted to loosen and then tighten again at its own pace, while the plain continues its quiet, unbroken work of staying wet, staying dark, and staying sparsely occupied by its own slow ruins.
