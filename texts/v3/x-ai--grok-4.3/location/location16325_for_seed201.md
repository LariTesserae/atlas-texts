High in the sky, where the air thins to a knife-edge, a compact cirque nestles between three newly risen granite horns. The rock itself looks only days old—its facets still carry the chisel marks of whatever violence lifted them, edges so sharp that a glove could be sliced open by brushing past. Between these pale walls lies a roughly circular floor perhaps two bowshots across, a single bounded room of stone and ice and life. Nothing lies beyond except sheer drop.

Condensation forms constantly on the cold stone faces. During the brief warmth of direct sun the droplets gather, trace downward, and collect in shallow pans of freshly abraded granite. These transient pools reflect the sky with painful clarity; when shadow returns they freeze into thin lenses that crack and re-form with each cycle. Around their margins, cushions of moss and tight rosettes of alpine herb pack together so densely that they seem almost upholstered, vivid against the surrounding paleness. Away from the water the rock is bare, so the vegetation reads as scattered emerald islands in a sea of light-colored stone—an odd emptiness of space even while each island itself is riotously full.

Light here is relentless. The sun, unfiltered by thicker atmosphere, strikes every surface at once and bounces upward again, doubling itself. Shadows are knife-thin and cobalt. In that glare, small things move constantly yet make almost no sound. Herds of thumb-sized, six-legged grazers trot across the moss in disciplined lines, their hooked feet rasping only faintly. High above, white raptors the color of the rock wheel without calling, their wings adjusting to the constant downdrafts that pour over the cirque rim. Occasionally one stoops and vanishes behind a boulder; moments later a puff of down drifts upward, the only visible evidence of the transaction.

Scattered across the floor are low, hand-stacked rings of the same pale stone, no higher than a person’s knee and already colonized by the same thick moss. Inside a few rings lie flat slabs that still show the pecking marks of stone tools. The rings are empty now, yet the arrangement suggests repeated, careful occupancy rather than ruin—shelters raised for a single season long ago and left ready for the next. Between them the emptiness feels deliberate: great stretches of clean rock where nothing stands and footsteps would echo if footsteps were permitted.

Every few minutes a soft detonation sounds from one of the higher horns: a wedge of ice loosens, drops, and bursts on the floor, scattering bright fragments that slide and stop. The fragments begin immediately to sublimate, re-condense on neighboring surfaces, and start their slow migration toward the next pool. The cycle is visible, audible only as a series of gentle reports spaced like heartbeats. In between, the cirque holds a deep, active quiet—the sound of growth, of melt, of cautious feet moving across living cushions, all occurring just below the threshold of ordinary hearing.
