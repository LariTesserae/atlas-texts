In the perpetual twilight of a warm, low-lying basin now lost beneath kilometers of water, a city of living stone rises from the silt. Its towers and arcades were never quarried or mortared; they grew, layer upon layer, from the compacted husks of ancient filter-feeders whose bodies, over uncounted generations, hardened into smooth colonnades and vaulted domes. The forms have long since settled into equilibrium with the gentle, persistent currents that flow between them like slow rivers of dark glass. Rounded edges and polished flanks speak of an erosion so patient it has become the very grammar of the place.

Between these organic ramparts the water is dense with suspended life: translucent threads of mucus and drifting embryos, schools of small, blind crustaceans that drift in layered veils, and the occasional slow pulse of a boneless, lantern-bearing hunter whose faint glow is the only light. Vegetation is almost absent; a few pale, hair-like filaments cling to the highest ridges where stray photons sometimes reach, but they are no more than ghosts of forests that once clothed drier ground.

Sound travels only as soft, infrasonic pressures: the low, rhythmic flex and release of the city’s living architecture breathing through microscopic pores, the distant thunder of a solitary cephalopod changing depth, and now and then the faint, high click of a predator tasting the water with its tongue. Most of the time the basin remains hushed, its activity expressed instead in movement—the continuous migration of biomass through the narrow streets of bone, the slow avalanche of mucus-snow settling onto plazas, the sudden coordinated contraction of an entire district when a larger shape passes overhead.

The scale is regional rather than planetary; one could, in theory, swim from one end of this drowned valley to the other in a handful of days, yet every horizon dissolves into the same repeating colonnades and drifting veils, so that the mind begins to accept the place as infinite. Temperature stays uniform, a steady warmth that makes the water itself feel almost viscous, almost alive. No tool has ever touched these structures, yet the city continues to accrete, to erode, to pulse, to feed, a single organism whose organs happen to resemble streets and spires.
