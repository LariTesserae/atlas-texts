The seafloor here is not flat so much as it is a single, titanic slope that refuses to end. Great ridges of basalt and serpentine rise and fall like the ribs of something long dead, their flanks scoured by uncounted millennia of current until every edge is rounded and every hollow packed with fine, coffee-dark silt. Yet the same forces have left the larger bones intact: knife-edged canyons that drop into blue-black nothing, seamounts whose summits almost breach the surface in sudden shelves, and hanging valleys whose mouths vomit clouds of suspended mud whenever a deeper surge passes.

The water itself is cool, never quite cold, and so saturated with dissolved minerals that every beam of ordinary daylight arriving from above turns faintly slate-green by two hundred body-lengths down. Sheets of this light slide across immense stands of kelp and sea-whip whose fronds are thicker than a man’s waist and long enough to tangle across the slope like living landslides. Between the fronds move dense schools of silver-black fish whose scales throw back brief, frantic sparks; larger shapes—slow, blunt-headed grazers armored in barnacle—drift just above the canopy, their passage stirring the plants into sudden, violent flurries. The motion never settles. Tidal surges arrive like invisible fists; they roll entire jungles sideways, then let them spring upright again only to be struck from another angle. The sound travels everywhere at once: a constant low thunder of water against stone, overlaid with the shivering rasp of fronds scraping one another and the sharper, metallic clicks of industrially shaped things still shifting in the current.

Scattered across the ridges are the remains of what must once have been surface works. A lattice of iron girders, now furred in rust and whip-coral, juts from one flattened summit like the skeleton of a pier that forgot how to reach the air. Farther downslope, the curved flank of a pressure hull lies half-buried, its rivets popped and its interior colonized by pale, branching anemones that pulse in time with the surge. Occasionally a low, arrhythmic thump still issues from within these husks—old flywheels turning on sediments, or simply the sea itself slapping hollow metal. The structures are never clustered; they appear singly, at irregular intervals, as though the original builders placed each one where the slope offered the least resistance and then left the rest to the water.

Life moves through and around these relics without pause. Crabs the size of shields pick at the corroding metal; long, ribbon-bodied eels slide in and out of the open seams; clouds of smaller fish boil in the lee of every girder when the current momentarily slackens. Everything is close, layered, and in constant rearrangement. A single passing surge can carry a drifting mat of kelp across an old factory deck, then sweep it onward, leaving only a fresh layer of silt and the faint, ringing after-tone of struck iron.

Above all of this, many body-lengths higher, the surface still behaves like an ordinary stormy sea—whitecaps, spray, low clouds—yet the turbulence transmits downward as pressure waves that arrive already aged and therefore strangely patient. The entire region feels at once ancient to the point of immobility and violently alive, a place whose every surface has already been ground to its final shape but whose interior continues to be rearranged at the speed of water.
