In this place the ground itself is still deciding what shape it wants. Vast mineral planes tilt upward at impossible angles, raw shelves of pale salt-stone and black feldspar that have not yet decided if they are mountains or the ceilings of echoing halls. They rise without haste, their edges still soft and granular, as though the crust is extruding new ridges the way a slow wound scabs. Between these half-formed escarpments lie wide, echoing basins where nothing has settled long enough to gather dust. The air smells of ozone and powdered gypsum; there has never been liquid water here, only faint chemical mists that condense and re-sublime in patient loops.

Across the raw planes grow the gardens. They do not drink; they breathe the volatile salts that leak from the fractures. Fronds of translucent mineral lattice stand waist-high in scattered thickets, each plate angled to catch what little light reaches them. The lattices pulse once every few hours with a slow, inner glow, exchanging ions with the air. Where two thickets meet they sometimes fuse, forming low vaulted canopies whose undersides are lined with dangling crystal threads that ring, very softly, when the wind from the heights passes through. The gardens are lush in texture yet remain islands in the emptiness; kilometers of bare stone may separate one cluster from the next.

The light never brightens beyond a cold pewter dusk. It arrives from a sky that is not entirely sky: high, slow fractures hang in the atmosphere like half-erased script, leaking a wan glow that shifts its color every several days. Beneath this dim illumination the terrain feels larger than its actual span; distances stretch because the eye keeps trying to resolve features that are still assembling themselves. On the highest ridges stand occasional single towers, hand-shaped from the same salt-stone and left unfinished. Their walls carry shallow chisel marks and the faint negative prints of palms pressed into cooling rock. No one tends them now. They lean against the wind like forgotten gestures.

Sound does not travel here; it is manufactured continuously by the landscape. From beneath the stone comes a sustained, low-frequency concussion, like the heartbeat of something larger than continents. It is loud enough to press against the ribs, loud enough that conversation would be impossible if there were anyone left to speak. The fractures in the upper air add their own component: a tearing, paper-thin roar that rises and falls over spans of hours, as though unseen membranes are being pulled apart and immediately resealed by the same slow force. The combined pressure of these noises makes the stillness between pulses feel almost tactile.

Nothing moves that is not geology or vegetation. The lattices occasionally shed a single crystal flake that drifts downward like a leaf and, upon touching stone, begins to sprout a new filament at its own tempo—years per centimeter. The wind itself is barely more than a shifting pressure gradient; it carries no scent of life, only the constant mineral haze. Time here moves in the same register as the mountains: each small alteration is already ancient by the time it becomes visible.

Occasionally, at the scale of decades, a new ridge will complete itself. Its crest will catch the dim light and for a short while the whole horizon will seem to rearrange itself. Then the fractures will migrate a little farther outward, the gardens will extend one more transparent frond into the new space, and the deaf roar will settle again into its patient, unceasing rhythm.
