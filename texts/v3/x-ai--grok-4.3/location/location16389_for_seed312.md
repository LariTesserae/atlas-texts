A high, young plateau stretches continent-wide beneath a sky of ordinary daylight, its raw stone still showing the fractures where it rose from the depths not long ago. Ridges stand knife-edged, their faces unsoftened by time; narrow ravines plunge between them, each one catching stray threads of mist that condense overnight and drip into shallow basins before the next slow cycle of evaporation begins. Water is never far, yet never abundant—film on stone, beaded droplets along roof edges, the occasional brief shower that soaks the upper courses of walls and then departs, leaving only the lingering chill and the mineral scent of wet rock.

Across this sharp geography a dense city has grown, not by plan but by patient accretion. Stone towers and long terraces press against one another, their walls rising from the living bedrock itself; each new course is laid by hand with simple chisels and levers, then left to settle for generations before another layer is added. Streets are narrow vertical slots between these masses, their paving fitted so tightly that only faint seams remain for the damp to darken after rain. Aqueducts of unmortared block run along the ridges, their gradients so slight that water moves through them at the pace of a drifting cloud. Smoke rises in thin, infrequent ribbons from roof vents where charcoal fires smolder beneath clay domes, warming interiors without ever raising the outer air above its perpetual cold.

Vegetation clings wherever a crack offers purchase: cushions of dark lichen, tufts of wiry grass no taller than a thumb, and once in a long while a stunted shrub whose roots have found a seep. Yet the city itself is thick with presence. Birds wheel in tight flocks between towers, their nests wedged into every ledge; lizards the color of fresh-cut stone bask on sun-warmed paving until the mist returns; small, quick mammals thread the sub-level drains and storage vaults. Larger shapes move at the margins—goats with thick winter coats browsing the ridge tops, and occasional heavier beasts whose low calls drift across the plateau like distant stone shifting. All of it proceeds without haste. Doors open and close at intervals measured in hours; water drips from one level to the next over days; the slow expansion of a single wall may occupy an entire working lifetime.

Sound is correspondingly spare. Wind threads between the sharp peaks and the close-set buildings, producing only a steady, low sigh; footsteps on stone are muffled by the cold air itself; voices, when they rise at all, remain conversational and brief. Even the abundant fauna contributes little noise—wings brushing past, soft claws on rock, the occasional muted bleat—then silence resumes. The entire vast fabric moves according to rhythms longer than seasons: condensation cycles that repeat across years, slow weathering that rounds a corner only after centuries, the gradual settling of new courses of stone into the older ones beneath them. In this patient, dense, half-damp stone city the ordinary light of day simply arrives, lingers, and departs, revealing the same sharp edges and clustered human work it revealed the day before.
