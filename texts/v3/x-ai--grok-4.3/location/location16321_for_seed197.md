The hollow begins as a single breach in what looks, from outside, like an unbroken wall of living tissue. At low elevation, this chamber sits in a shallow basin where the ground itself is woven root and compacted leaf-mold, long-settled into gentle undulations that have not shifted in centuries. Warm air hangs inside, thick with the smell of chlorophyll and slow rot. Condensation gathers on every upward-facing surface and releases in irregular, soft pulses—never quite rain, never quite dry—so that fine threads of moisture trace down the curving walls and collect in shallow plant-formed bowls along the floor.

Those walls are not separate constructions. They are the plant mass itself, layered so densely that every surface is simultaneously ceiling, pillar, floor, and window. Fibrous scaffolds of older growth have been pressed and flattened by newer shoots until the result resembles the petrified ribs of a ruined nave, yet every rib is still sending out fresh leaves that unfurl and reorient toward the openings above. Light enters normally, filtered through successive membranes of translucent tissue until it arrives as a steady, greenish haze that makes distance difficult to judge. The space feels simultaneously vast and contained: a cathedral whose nave might stretch thirty paces, yet whose transepts open onto further cavities whose far walls are visible as dim green silhouettes through the intervening emptiness.

Nothing else occupies that emptiness. Between the living architecture and the observer there is mostly air, a deliberate hollowness maintained by the same slow erosion that has polished every internal surface to a smooth, almost glassy finish. Over generations the plant has worn its own growth back from walkways and sight-lines, leaving only the faintest ridges where sap once flowed. Those ridges now serve as elevated causeways for the fauna that never stops moving.

Insects the size of thumbs, translucent and many-legged, travel in constant low orbits along the walls, their wingbeats merging into a single, unbroken drone that fills the chamber like a held chord. Larger, soft-bodied creatures—segmented and pale—drift between the upper galleries on membranes that catch the light and snap audibly when they change direction. Their collective motion creates secondary currents in the air: warm updrafts, sudden cool pockets where moisture condenses into momentary fog, then dissipates. The sound is constant and layered; the vibration travels through the plant tissue itself so that the floor trembles beneath bare feet and the walls produce a faint, answering resonance like a plucked string.

Occasionally the resonance changes pitch without warning. A distant section of wall will brighten, then fold inward along an impossible seam, revealing for a heartbeat another identical chamber beyond—occupied by the same drifting fauna yet lit from a different angle, or perhaps from an earlier moment. The fold closes before any detail can be fixed, leaving only a lingering after-image of movement where the wall should be solid. These momentary collapses do not damage the living structure; they are part of its established metabolism, as routine as the drip of condensation or the endless passage of the swarming insects.

Nothing here is manufactured. The organized density of the place arises only from the plant’s own ancient pattern of growth and retreat, and the abundant life moves through the resulting voids according to rhythms set long before any observer arrived. The chamber continues its slow, audible breathing, its surfaces polished by time and motion, its emptiness held open by the same forces that keep it full of sound.
