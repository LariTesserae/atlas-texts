The slope rises without drama, a single unbroken face of pale stone that has spent centuries under the same slow rain. Water does not fall here so much as it accumulates: it beads on every exposed surface, gathers in hairline cracks, and finally leaks out again in clear threads that trace the rock like sap. Near the crest the threads join into thicker curtains that drop a few body-lengths before vanishing into bright green thickets already heavy with their own moisture.  
The air remains thick even where the slope opens. Every breath carries the scent of warm stone and crushed leaves, and the heat never lifts; it simply settles, layer upon layer, until the whole plateau feels like the inside of a greenhouse left running too long.  
Below the weeping rock the ground levels into a wide, gently rolling table several valleys across. Nothing has been cleared. Ferns as tall as a standing person crowd shoulder to shoulder with broad-leaved trees whose trunks glisten; vines cross from crown to crown, and the spaces between are filled with smaller plants so that every vertical slice contains several living planes at once. The canopy is not closed, only crowded; shafts of ordinary daylight still reach the floor, turning the constant mist into visible sheets that drift and reform.  
Scattered across the same expanse, low domes of darker rock exhale thin white plumes. The vents do not roar. They sigh and pop at long intervals, releasing steam that rises straight for a few paces before the saturated air claims it. Around each vent the stone has cracked into rounded plates, edges softened by centuries of the same gentle dissolution. Water that has already passed through the rock emerges here at a simmer, feeding shallow pools whose surfaces quiver rather than bubble. The heat keeps the vegetation immediately around each pool a different green—almost yellow—while the rest of the plateau remains the deeper color of saturation.  
Animals move through this layering without announcement. Small, quick shapes thread the undergrowth; heavier bodies leave slow, bowing wakes in the ferns. Now and then a heavier splash sounds from one of the steaming pools as something surfaces and returns, but the sound is muffled at once by the wet leaves. The overall impression is of continuous small adjustments rather than sudden events: droplets forming and falling, leaves expanding, vents sighing, hidden currents shifting under the mat of roots.  
Nothing has been placed here by hands. The only machinery is the rock itself—fractured, dissolved, and re-cemented over ages—still performing the same work at the same measured pace. The horizon stays visible in every direction, a faint blue line of more distant stone under the same humid sky, so the plateau feels both crowded and limitless at once.
