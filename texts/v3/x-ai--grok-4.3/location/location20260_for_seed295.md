The highlands form a single, continuous made-place: everything underfoot, overhead, and at every distance is grown rather than found. Enormous living frameworks—root-walls, trunk-pillars, woven branch-lattices—have risen and interlocked across centuries until no patch of original stone or soil remains visible. The structures stand at valley-to-ridge scale, yet every surface is layered so thickly with later growth that the overall effect feels enclosed and intimate at once. Between the frameworks hang translucent sheets of living membrane, thin enough for faint starlight or distant lightning to register as a perpetual grey dusk rather than true night.

Moisture arrives only in cycles measured in weeks: night-long condensation that beads along every underside and drips into shallow basins carved by the framework itself. These basins never overflow for long; the surrounding tissue drinks them back within days, leaving behind mineral films that slowly harden the lower levels into something like weathered bone. Vegetation crowds every available plane—vines no thicker than cord yet strong enough to bear weight, mosses that form cushioning pads between structural members, bracket fungi that widen into stable ledges. All of it grows in the same narrow temperature band, warm enough that nothing ever fully dries, cool enough that nothing rots into softness.

Fauna fills the remaining volume. Countless small, soft-bodied creatures move in constant, slow drifts: pale millipedes the length of forearms travel along fixed routes across the membranes; thumbnail-sized beetles rotate in place for hours, polishing feeding grooves into the wood; translucent salamanders press themselves into the seams between frames and inflate their throats once per minute. Their combined motion produces a single, unvarying roar—friction, breathing, and the faint percussion of mouthparts striking chitin—that never rises or falls, only changes timbre when a larger shape passes through the dark. The sound presses against the membranes and is returned, so the entire structure seems to resonate at one low, steady frequency.

Change occurs at the pace of the frameworks themselves. Over decades a new buttress will lengthen, an old membrane will thicken and cloud, a basin will silt in and be abandoned. Nothing is sudden; the established forms simply continue to occupy the space they have already claimed, their edges softened by the same slow accretion that first raised them. High above, where the uppermost lattices still admit a trace of open sky, occasional mist drifts between peaks, condenses on the highest struts, and returns downward along the same channels it has followed for generations. Below, in the layered dark, the only movement is the patient redistribution of moisture and the endless, quiet rearrangement of bodies against one another.
