The space opens as a single immense chamber beneath the surface, its boundaries lost in layered leaf and stem rather than stone. Plant tissue forms the only substrate: roots have fused into terraces and vaulted ceilings, stems have braided into load-bearing pillars thick as towers, and leaves overlap into translucent canopies dense enough to walk upon. Every surface is living matter; even the raw, half-formed mineral veins still pushing upward through the floor are already being colonized, their soft crystalline edges wrapped in thin white mycelial felt that hardens into new floor as it grows.

The air is hot and motionless, saturated with moisture that condenses along the upper surfaces and falls in infrequent, heavy drops that are absorbed before they can pool. Where the droplets strike, small transparent bladders swell from the leaf beneath, store the water briefly, then release it downward again in a closed circuit. No wind moves; the entire mass breathes on a timescale too slow to perceive, so the humidity simply hangs.

Light arrives from everywhere and nowhere at once: every leaf radiates a steady, even illumination, brighter toward the core of each frond and dimmer at the edges, producing a uniform white-gold radiance that fills the volume without shadow or glare. In this constant brightness the plants do not photosynthesize in pulses; they maintain a continuous, unchanging output. Flowers remain half-open in permanent mid-bloom, their petals neither advancing nor decaying.

Scattered through the living architecture are faint, older structures: curved ribs of darker, woodier tissue that once formed chambers now overgrown, or shallow depressions lined with tighter-woven fiber suggesting long-abandoned cisterns or paths. These marks are not ruins in the usual sense; they are simply earlier growth stages the plants have incorporated rather than replaced. Nothing metal or shaped by tools exists.

Fauna is almost absent. Only occasionally does a single translucent mite the size of a seed drift between stems on a strand of its own silk, or a minute, wingless beetle pause on a vein before vanishing into a pore. Their motions are so infrequent they register as stillness. The only sound is the soft, arrhythmic patter of condensation returning to its source, and even that is muffled by the absorbent mass of tissue.

Everything is held in equilibrium. Growth continues, yet at a rate that produces no visible progress from one moment to the next. The embryonic mineral veins lengthen by microns per year; the plant canopy thickens by the same infinitesimal increment. The chamber does not expand or collapse; it simply persists, already complete in its present state and therefore never finished.
