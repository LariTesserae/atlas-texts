High on a sheer-ribbed shoulder of rock no larger than a broad cathedral nave, the air hangs warm and heavy with mist. The plateau itself is recent, born from some abrupt heave of the deep crust; its edges are knife-bright where water has only begun to find the joints, carving thin vertical flutes rather than rounding them. Stone still smells of fracture dust. From every fresh-cut face, beads of moisture gather and drop, slow as condensation on a chilled pipe, yet constant enough that the ledges below are already furred with moss and liverwort.

Between these weeping ribs, vegetation crowds in tight layers. Ferns shoulder against one another three and four deep; broad leaves the color of overripe limes overlap like shingles, catching the continuous seep so that the whole canopy glitters even in ordinary daylight. Vines thick as wrists climb the machinery that dots the plateau—tall, open frames of bolted iron left from some interrupted extraction project. The iron still turns, barely, on joints kept slick by the same seeping water. Chain drives rattle one tooth at a time, a sound that carries loud and low across the small enclosure, echoing off the sharp walls like distant thunder slowed to a patient heartbeat.

Nothing here moves quickly. The gears advance through a single rotation over the course of an hour; vines lengthen by a hand-span in a month. The warmth keeps the sap rising, the damp keeps the moss plush, and the high, thin air keeps everything slightly hushed despite the metallic clatter. Occasionally a lone beetle or pale-winged moth traverses the layered leaves, its brief flight the only quick motion in the space. The rest—rock, iron, and green—settles into slow mutual occupation, each element still deciding how to fit against the others on this newly risen shelf.
