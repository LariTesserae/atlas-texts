The space is a single steep declivity no wider than a great hall, wedged between two freshly uplifted rock faces that still carry the raw geometry of their birth. The walls are not smooth; they are serrated with fractures and knife-edged overhangs, pale stone still shedding thin platelets that flake away under their own weight. Because the cleft sits so high and so recently formed, every surface feels provisional, as though a single hard rain or tremor could redraw the boundaries.

Moisture arrives only in pulses. During the long intervals between them the air is merely cool and still, but when condensation gathers or a brief shower threads down the crack, water beads instantly on every leaf and stem, then coalesces into threads that run along the undersides of the foliage before dropping to the floor. These momentary rivulets find their own twisting courses between root buttresses and moss hummocks, disappearing again into fissures before they can accumulate. The result is a constant low-level humidity without any standing water, the stone itself remaining matte rather than slick.

Vegetation has packed the declivity to saturation. Ferns the size of tabletops angle outward from every ledge; their fronds overlap and interlock until they form a second, living ceiling beneath the rock overhangs. Between them, wiry creepers and thick-stemmed herbs rise in layers, each seeking the faint light that filters from the narrow opening far above. The growth is so dense that moving through it requires pushing aside successive curtains of stems that spring back at once, sealing the path behind. No animal calls disturb the foliage; only the plants themselves shift and resettle, their leaves tapping one another when a gust funnels down the cleft.

Sound fills the same volume that the plants occupy. Water droplets strike resonant leaves in overlapping rhythms, while small, continual shifts of rock create dull, grinding impacts that echo once before they are muffled by the living mass. Occasional larger flakes detach from the upper walls and fall, striking the layered canopy with a muffled percussive crash that sends a brief shiver through the entire mass of stems. The combined noise is loud but directionless, as though the cleft itself were an engine running at idle—never quite silent, never quite regular.

Light is scarce. What reaches the floor has already been sieved through the rock lips above and then through successive strata of leaves, arriving as a dim, bluish-gray wash that barely registers color. In that half-light the plants appear darker than they should, their surfaces holding a matte, almost velvety depth. The cool temperature keeps growth slow but steady; the constant slight movement of air prevents stagnation, so the space never feels truly enclosed despite its narrowness. Every surface participates in the same slow, uneven renewal: rock spalling, leaves dropping and being replaced, water arriving and vanishing again, all within the compass of a single, sharply bounded chamber high on the mountain.
