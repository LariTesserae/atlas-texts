The highland plateau stretches in slow, layered undulations, its edges dissolving into colder ridges that rise and recede without clear terminus. Everything is plant. Thick mats of leathery moss and fibrous lichen have built themselves into continuous substrate, rising in irregular swells that function as the land’s only topography. Where the growth thickens further, it forms vertical screens and shallow vaults whose undersides drip with persistent condensation. These living canopies admit ordinary daylight in soft, evenly scattered shafts; nothing is dark, yet nothing is brightly lit.

Moisture arrives in brief, local pulses. A bank of low cloud grazes the plateau for an hour, leaving every surface beaded; then the air clears again and the beads merge, run, and collect in the concave hollows where root lattices have pulled away from one another. The accumulated water remains cold and still, rimmed by pale fungal collars that seal the edges against rapid loss. Elsewhere the substrate stays merely damp, its surface never quite dry, never quite wet.

Despite the cold, the mass is never static. Individual stems lengthen or collapse in sudden, quiet surges, one patch rising while another subsides, corridors opening and closing over hours rather than seasons. The pattern of these shifts repeats on a scale of tens of meters, creating an endless, low-amplitude rearrangement that never erases the larger, weathered grain of the plateau. The forms that result—broad platforms supported by braided root pillars, tiered shelves dense enough to walk upon, long galleries whose walls are living cambium—resemble the husks of abandoned industrial halls yet contain no joints, no fasteners, no material that is not still alive.

Sound is limited to the internal working of this growth: the faint, wet click of cells expanding, the soft tearing sigh when a section slumps and splits, the muted trickle of water finding new channels. No wind moves through the dense mass; airborne voices or footsteps would be absorbed within a few body-lengths. Only the occasional far-off report of a larger collapse travels across the open distance, already muffled by intervening layers.

Small animals are almost absent. A few translucent mites travel the condensation films; once in many hours a pale, wingless insect may cross an exposed root before disappearing again. Otherwise the plateau registers only its own continuous, silent metabolism.

The overall impression is of an ancient, self-contained engine whose moving parts are made of cold, water-logged tissue. The motion never stops, yet the shape it maintains has already taken on the rounded, settled profile of long erosion, as though the turbulence itself has become the landscape’s permanent weather.
