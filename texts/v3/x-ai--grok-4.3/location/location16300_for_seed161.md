A single, immense stone spire rises from the surrounding flats, its flanks still cathedral-broad at the base yet tapering only slightly over half a mile of vertical climb. Time has worn its profile into gentle, rounded shoulders and shallow terraces, the work of ages rather than sudden collapse; every exposed surface carries the same velvet texture of wind-polished mineral, faintly sparkling with microscopic salt crystals. The rock itself is warm to the touch even in shadow, radiating the accumulated heat of long days.

No free water exists anywhere—no springs, no seeps, no visible pools. Moisture arrives only as a slow, persistent exhalation from deep mineral veins, enough to bead on cool undersides of leaves but never to flow. In its place grows a dense, almost suffocating carpet of vegetation: broad, leathery succulents whose roots bore straight into the stone like drills, curtains of hanging moss that drape every ledge in overlapping shawls, and low, cushion-forming shrubs whose tiny flowers open in synchronized pulses once every few years. The plants press shoulder to shoulder, leaves overlapping, creating a continuous living sheath that softens every edge of the spire.

On those same ledges and inside wide, ancient hollows carved by primordial erosion, people have built. Their structures are simple—dry-stone walls, timber lintels cut from the same trees they prune, ladders and walkways lashed with braided fiber—yet they are stacked and interwoven until the rock face itself seems to have grown rooms and streets. Smoke from small hearths threads upward through narrow flues, mixing with the constant, low rush of wind that finds every passage and plays them like flutes. The sound never ceases: a layered drone of air, the steady creak of settling ropes, and the overlapping calls of abundant small creatures—lizards that skitter along the vines, flocks of swift, red-winged birds that wheel inside the cavernous hollows, and colonies of furred gliders that launch from one balcony to another without ever touching open sky.

Light remains low. A perpetual high haze, pale and faintly ochre, filters the sun to a permanent late-afternoon glow. Inside the deeper terraces and covered walkways the dimness deepens further, so that cooking fires and resin lamps become the main illumination. Heat lingers in the stone; temperatures stay comfortably warm day and night, varying only across centuries rather than hours. Everything moves slowly: the gradual migration of plant roots, the incremental widening of a footpath worn by generations, the patient expansion of a single family’s dwellings as new rooms are added stone by stone. The entire place feels finished, as though its shape has been negotiated between rock, plant, animal, and human hands until nothing more needs to change.
