The plateau stretches outward in every direction for many miles, a high, wind-exposed table of mineral crust where nothing grows and nothing rots. Its surface is not flat but densely packed with tall, closely spaced columns and blades of translucent stone—some no thicker than a finger, others as wide as a person—rising and interlocking until the horizon itself seems to be made of them. The rock is a single muted color, a cool smoky quartz veined with threads of darker, almost metallic glass. Time has smoothed every edge and joint into gentle curves; rain and condensation have polished the faces until they reflect the sky like still water.

Light falls straight and hard from above, turning every film of moisture into a mirror. Because the air is always cool and saturated, vapor gathers on every surface within moments of being wiped away. Thin streams slide down the stone flanks, gather at the bases, and seep back into hairline cracks, only to reappear minutes later a few inches higher. The columns literally weep; droplets form at their upper rims, swell, and fall in slow, endless succession. No wind disturbs the pattern. The dripping has continued so long and so steadily that it has become the single constant sound of the place—an unbroken, roaring hiss that fills the air and seems to come from every direction at once. The noise never rises or falls; it simply is, a permanent fact of pressure against the ears.

Between the packed columns the ground is not bare. Instead it is covered by a thin, continuous skin of the same glassy stone, cracked into irregular polygons from long, slow contraction. Water stands in the cracks as shallow, motionless lenses that reflect the bright sky without ripples. Here and there a single, motionless thread of darker mineral rises like a frozen fountain, its tip perpetually crowned with a bead of water that never quite detaches. These threads mark the only vertical interruptions in an otherwise horizontal equilibrium; they have stood exactly this way, at exactly this height, since before any eye could have recorded them.

Nothing moves that is not water. The rare small creatures that exist here cling like pale flecks to the undersides of ledges, feeding on the mineral film left by evaporating droplets. They are few enough that most columns show no trace of them at all. Their presence changes nothing; the columns neither grow nor crumble, the lenses neither deepen nor vanish. The entire landscape holds one unchanging state: high, wet, bright, and filled with the single, unvarying roar of water returning to stone.
