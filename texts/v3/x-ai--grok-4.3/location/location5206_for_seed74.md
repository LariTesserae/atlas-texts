A low, flat basin of packed silt and root-matted earth, no more than a few hundred paces across, lies cupped between two shallow, grassy rises. The place feels like a hamlet that once stood on reclaimed marsh; now only the organized bones of that settlement remain. Broad, low shapes of what might once have been warehouses and small foundries rise at irregular intervals, their walls and roofs composed entirely of living tissue. Thick, leathery stems have grown together in long, bowed sheets and vertical columns, forming hollow chambers whose interiors retain the geometry of loading bays and machine halls. These living walls are cool to the touch, their surfaces beaded with slow condensation that gathers in the evenings and drips at long intervals into shallow gutters carved by the same steady moisture.

Between the structures the ground is mostly open. Patches of bare, gray silt lie beside low mats of pale, wiry grass and occasional stands of taller, jointed reeds that rise no higher than a person’s chest. The vegetation does not choke the space; it claims it in measured allotments, leaving wide lanes of damp earth where the original brick and gravel streets once ran. Water collects in these lanes after any rain or heavy fog, forming long, shallow pans that reflect the ordinary daylight. The water is never deep enough to flood the paths entirely, but it keeps the silt soft and dark and allows thin films of algae to spread in veined patterns that follow the faint slope of the basin.

Inside the plant-formed buildings the air stays cool and still. Old iron frames and rusted turbines remain embedded in the living walls, their surfaces colonized by fine rootlets that have grown into the metal like tendrils into stone. Occasionally a turbine will turn once or twice with a low, liquid click when a sudden change in humidity swells a nearby stem; the motion is brief, almost reluctant. No other mechanical sound disturbs the quiet. From outside the chambers comes only the soft, spaced drip of condensation and the faint, dry rustle of reed joints touched by the smallest breeze.

There are no animals here. The only movement belongs to the plants themselves: the imperceptible swelling of stems after a shower, the slow opening of small cup-shaped leaves that collect mist and later release it in measured drops. The whole settlement breathes at the pace of cool weather and intermittent rain, its edges soft with settled erosion where silt has banked against the bases of the living walls and the reeds have leaned for decades in the same prevailing direction. In the ordinary light of midday the place appears half-empty, half-built, and wholly alive in its own slow, balanced way.
