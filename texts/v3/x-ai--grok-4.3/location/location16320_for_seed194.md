In the deep folds of an ancient, near-flat mineral plain—its surface long ago scoured to a glassy smoothness by eons of wind and settling dust—lies a single hidden pocket no larger than a buried hall. The plain itself is a sealed crust of compacted salts and silicates, dry as vacuum and faintly iridescent where fractures catch stray reflections. Inside the pocket the air is cool, motionless, and utterly dark; no light has ever entered.

What fills the space is not a forest but a single, continuous living mass of plant tissue: pale, leathery sheets and struts that have grown over centuries into the outlines of streets, plazas, and low towers. The material is not wood but a slow-extruded matrix of mineralized cellulose and silica salts drawn directly from the surrounding crust. Thick buttresses rise from floor to ceiling, then branch into roof-like canopies that meet and fuse, producing chambers whose walls are still recognizably the original plant body. Narrow galleries, just wide enough for a single careful passage, thread between these masses; the galleries themselves are lined with the same living tissue, their surfaces textured by microscopic ridges that once guided slow flows of concentrated sap.

Because the entire structure is one organism, its density within the pocket is paradoxically low. Between the great load-bearing pillars the voids remain wide and empty, the intervening distances measured in body-lengths rather than hand-spans. The floor is a continuous membrane, slightly yielding, that transmits faint tremors; these tremors are the only motion. Over decades a wall may thicken by the width of a fingernail; a tower may lean a fraction and then correct itself by sending reinforcing strands across the gap to its neighbor. The cycle is so gradual it registers as stillness unless observed across generations.

No animals move here. The only sounds are the plant’s own: long, resonant creaks that travel through the connected struts like the shifting of an old wooden ship, followed by sudden, sharp reports when internal tensions release and mineral crystals realign. These noises repeat at intervals of minutes or hours, overlapping into a steady, low thunder that fills the darkness without ever becoming louder or softer. The acoustics turn the pocket into a single resonant chamber; every shift in one quarter is answered by sympathetic groans from distant quarters, as though the architecture itself is murmuring its own slow adjustments.

Scattered through the living walls are simple tools left by the last hands that tended them: mineral blades hafted in hardened plant fiber, stone scrapers worn to fit the curve of the galleries, and shallow bowls chipped from the same salt matrix that once held concentrated nutrients. These implements rest where they were set down, already half-encased by new growth. The plants have incorporated their shapes into the structure, turning abandoned tools into permanent architectural details rather than discarding them.

The whole arrangement feels both exhausted and perpetual: an urban order reduced to its final, settled form, maintained by the same organism that once sheltered its builders, now continuing the pattern in darkness and silence except for its own patient, thunderous conversation with itself.
