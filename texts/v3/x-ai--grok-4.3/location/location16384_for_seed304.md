The hamlet rises from a slab of wet stone no wider than a grove of trees, itself only lately shouldered above the surrounding flats by some slow heave of the deep crust. The rock is still sharp at every edge, its planes clean and unsoftened; rainwater has not yet rounded the new fractures, and thin rills still follow the fresh joints like silver wires. Water stands or moves everywhere: it seeps from the very pores of the stone in steady beads, gathers along the bases of the walls, and threads between the packed houses in finger-width channels that never quite dry. The air itself feels pressed; each breath carries the cool metallic taste of fresh mineral and the faint, green scent of moss that has only begun to take hold.

Because dry ground is scarce, the houses have been built shoulder to shoulder, walls touching, roofs overlapping at the eaves so that the whole settlement forms a single continuous canopy. The structures are low and round, each no taller than a standing man at the center beam. Their stones were pried straight from the same newborn rock beneath them and fitted without mortar; the gaps are stuffed with moss and reeds that darken daily from the constant seepage. Rope bridges and notched log ladders run from roof to roof, allowing movement without stepping into the shallow water that carpets the lanes. Inside, the single rooms are dim and close; every surface glistens with a film of condensation that returns within minutes of being wiped away.

Vegetation is thin. A few pale reeds root where the rock is cracked enough to hold a pinch of silt; liverworts and pale green crusts coat the northern faces of the stones. Nothing grows tall enough to sway. The only movement among the plants is the slow advance of the liverworts across a roof or the quiet collapse of a reed when its root finally loosens in the saturated stone.

Fauna is scarce and mostly hidden. Small, translucent salamanders no longer than a finger live in the wall gaps; at intervals one will emerge to drink, then vanish again. Once in a while a larger, eyeless fish appears in the deeper channels between houses, drifting with the slow current until it finds a new crack to enter. Their passage makes almost no sound.

The only steady noises are the soft, continuous percussion of water leaving the rock and the occasional creak of wet rope taking weight. The settlement feels hushed, yet it is never still. Condensation gathers and falls; stone cools and contracts; faint tremors travel up from below as the young slab settles another fraction into its new position. These processes occur together, layered and patient, so the place seems both quiet and busy at once.

Light arrives normally from the open sky above the canopy, but it arrives already softened by the perpetual film of vapor that hangs a hand-span above every surface. Colors are therefore slightly dulled, edges slightly softened, even at midday. At the hamlet’s outer margin the wet stone drops away in a clean, recent scarp no higher than a man’s chest; beyond it the older, greener marsh stretches flat and empty. Within the bounds of that scarp, every surface that can hold a structure already does, and every surface that cannot holds only water, stone, and the faint continuing work of becoming.
