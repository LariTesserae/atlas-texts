The chamber is a single, high stone vault, its scale no larger than a cathedral nave yet sealed on every side by hand-laid blocks. The blocks themselves have been worn by unthinkable time into one continuous surface; their once-angled joints have settled into rounded, almost organic seams, so that the walls appear grown rather than built. Warm water fills the space entirely, still and heavy, without current or tide. The temperature hovers just above skin heat, noticeable only as a faint pressure against the skin when movement occurs.

Light is almost absent. What little reaches the stone arrives as a bruise-purple diffusion from cracks far above, filtered through layers of settling silt until it barely stains the upper vaults. In this near-darkness the chamber is never empty. Bodies—long, translucent, faintly ridged like folded mica—drift in slow, overlapping spirals. They are not schools but a single, shifting density; where one turns, others adjust by fractions, maintaining an unbroken lattice of near-touching forms. Their passage is soundless except for the softest frictional whisper of flank against flank, quieter than breath.

No trace of vegetation or organic film exists on any surface. The stone is bare mineral, faintly veined with crystalline inclusions that catch the scant light and return it as dull glints. In places these veins have been deepened by ancient chisels into shallow reliefs—simple repeated arcs and cups whose original purpose is lost. Over centuries the carvings have been further softened by the water’s patient pressure until they read more like natural strata than deliberate marks.

The creatures move with the deliberation of things that have no need to hurry. A single individual may take half a day to cross from one wall to the opposite, pausing only to let others pass through the lattice. When two touch, the contact is gradual, a folding rather than a collision, and the ripple travels outward through the entire mass over many minutes. Their density never thins; gaps close as slowly as they form, preserving the same crowded equilibrium at every scale.

Above the drifting bodies the vault rises to a peak whose apex is lost in darkness, though the water presses upward against it with constant, gentle insistence. Below, the floor remains level, a single polished plane of the same ancient masonry. Nothing grows, nothing decays, nothing arrives or departs. The only changes are the imperceptible migrations of the bodies and the gradual settling of microscopic silt that continues to round every edge, as though the entire constructed space is still, very slowly, being returned to the mineral from which it was first taken.
