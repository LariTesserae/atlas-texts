The highlands are not so much a place as a continuous skin of construction stretched across folded ridges and hanging valleys. Every surface—ridge crest, slope, narrow saddle—is plated, riveted, or poured: dull ferrocrete fused to older iron grids, those grids themselves laid over even older stone that has long since been buried beyond reach. Where the plates meet, water finds the joints and weeps in slow, steady threads that braid together into tinier rills, then into channeled gutters that thread the entire lattice like capillaries. Condensation beads on every underside; the air itself is never quite dry, only cool enough that the moisture stays suspended rather than rising into visible cloud.

Vegetation has accepted the artificial ground without contest. Ferns and mosses pack every damp crevice and horizontal ledge; vines the color of oxidized copper trail from pipe bundles and catwalk rails; thick cushions of liverwort soften the edges of loading platforms. The growth is not wild but accommodated—engineers once designed intake grilles and drainage slots around the expected spread of roots, and later crews simply stopped fighting the pattern. The result is a single, continuous texture: metal and concrete everywhere, yet never bare.

Light remains at the level of deep overcast even at midday. The ridgeline itself blocks direct sun for much of the arc; what little filters through arrives already diffused by rising steam and the faint haze of distant foundry vents. Shadows are soft and long, merging into one another until distinctions of depth flatten. Sound travels the same way: the constant low churn of turbines, the occasional hydraulic sigh, the rattle of a crane moving along its gantry—all of it damped by the saturated air and the sheer density of the built layers. The plateau is busy, yet the impression is of near-silence; individual machines announce themselves only when they change state, a brief metallic report swallowed again within seconds.

Movement is constant but internal. Sections of decking are lifted and reset on new footings while adjacent lines continue to run; a gantry collapses on one slope and is already being rebuilt from salvaged members higher up the same ridge before the debris has cooled. The pattern obeys no master plan yet never fully breaks; the entire armature has been weathering and replacing itself for generations, its forms smoothed by use and by the steady trickles of water until the newest struts already look as settled as the oldest. Fauna is almost absent—occasional pale moths and a few species of water-striders in the permanent gutters—but the absence registers less as emptiness than as another layer of accommodation: the machines and the plants have divided the space so completely that little room remains for anything else.

The whole arrangement extends across multiple valleys and several ridgelines, open enough to feel regional, yet so stepped, stacked, and interleaved that every vista is quickly filled. Nothing natural shows; even the highest peaks have been capped with weather stations and vent arrays whose silhouettes have themselves begun to soften under successive coats of moss and rust. The highlands remain cool, wet, and industrious, a single engineered organism that has learned to erode and regrow in the same motion.
