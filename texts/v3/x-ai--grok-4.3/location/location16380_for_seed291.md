In the lowlands of the Veyr Basin, the ground has never quite finished arriving. Raw stone wells up from beneath in slow, uneven pulses—ribbed plates of mineral still warm from their own making, slick with condensation and veined by quicksilver seams that harden only to crack again days later. These half-born formations shoulder through the streets and under the foundries like the backs of immense, half-submerged creatures. Between them, black water collects in long, narrow seams that never quite become rivers; the moisture arrives mostly as night-fog and factory breath, beading on every surface until the air itself feels saturated and cold enough to sting the lungs.

Above this restless floor the city has grown dense and continuous, a single unbroken stratum of brick, iron, and glass fused so tightly that entire blocks share load-bearing walls and drainpipes. Chimneys and torque-towers rise at every interval, their flywheels and piston arrays turning without audible complaint—only a low, bone-deep thrum that vibrates through bootsoles and window frames. Where the new rock interrupts, factories have simply been built over or around it; catwalks and conveyor bridges sag across fresh basalt spines or thread through still-soft lava blisters, their rivets already weeping rust from the constant damp cold.

Vegetation finds every fracture. Lush rope-vines and frost-tolerant leaf-sponges pour from parapets and gutter lines, their foliage the deep, almost black green of things that photosynthesize under perpetual overcast. In the wider plazas the vines have knit themselves into hanging gardens dense enough to support entire colonies of pale, six-legged crawlers that rasp the stone for minerals and leave faint luminous trails. Larger fauna—shaggy, low-slung grazers with translucent hides—move in deliberate silence along the canal edges, cropping the same vines the city depends on for insulation. Their presence is everywhere and mostly unseen; only the occasional soft drag of a flank against corrugated metal or the faint click of a claw on grate betrays them.

Change remains the only fixed rhythm. A block that stood yesterday may have its southern half raised three meters by morning while the northern half sags into a new seam; the buildings do not fall so much as recline into new alignments, their industrial piping stretching and re-coupling with soft metallic sighs. Steam plumes rise from newly opened vents, condense instantly in the cold, and drift downward again as heavy mist that never quite lifts. Light is ordinary daylight filtered through this perpetual haze—flat, even, and strangely shadowless, so that distance collapses and everything seems pressed close.

Yet the basin is quiet. The engines run; the rock heaves; the vines thicken; the animals pass. Only the water answers with the soft, intermittent percussion of droplets falling from one level of catwalk to the next, marking time across an unfinished geology that refuses to settle.
