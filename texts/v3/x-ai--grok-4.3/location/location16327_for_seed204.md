High in the spine of a freshly uplifted range, where continental plates still groan against one another, a sheer wall of pale, striated stone rises. The rock itself is only a few thousand years old—fractured into knife-edged plates and needled pinnacles that have never known rounding. At this altitude the air is thin and permanently chilled; each breath condenses on every exposed surface, turning the cliff into a slow, weeping glass.

Embedded within one of the deeper vertical fissures, too narrow to be noticed from below or above, lies a concealed pocket: a single elongated chamber no larger than a modest courtyard. The walls of this cleft have been colonized rather than built. Slender filaments of engineered mineral—pale as moonstone, veined with threads of living metal—grow outward in measured layers, each generation of material only a few molecules thick. They form open lattices, half-bridge and half-dwelling, suspended across the fissure at irregular heights. Walkways are mere filaments; dwellings are translucent shells whose interiors remain visible from without. Nothing is crowded; the structures occupy perhaps one part in ten of the available volume, leaving most of the space as vertical emptiness threaded by drifting mist.

Water arrives only as vapor. It nucleates along the filaments, gathers into trembling beads, and travels downward in slow rivulets that never quite reach the chamber floor; they re-evaporate or freeze into hairline rimes on shaded undersides. The sound is almost nothing: the occasional soft tick of a droplet detaching, the faint crystalline sigh as a filament adjusts its lattice in response to temperature or load. No wind enters this fissure; the outer wind merely howls across the narrow entrance, leaving the interior still.

Light arrives normally—diffuse daylight filtered and multiply reflected by the stone—so the chamber is neither dim nor bright, only evenly legible. Within that steady illumination, processes continue without haste. The filaments lengthen or reconfigure by fractions of a millimeter per day. Subtle temperature gradients drive minute currents of condensed water along their surfaces. Occasionally a single, transparent arthropod-like form, no larger than a grain of rice, emerges from a crevice, traverses a filament, and vanishes again; such events are rare enough that hours may pass between them.

Nothing grows that could be called plant or animal in older senses. The entire metabolism of the place is mineral and engineered, yet it moves, repairs, and reconfigures itself with the patient, distributed intelligence of a living reef—except that the reef is cold, silent, and almost empty.
