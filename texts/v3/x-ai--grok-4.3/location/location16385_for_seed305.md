The plateau rises like a single unfinished thought, a continent of fresh stone newly shouldered upward through older crust. Its edges are still raw: knife-edged ridges of basalt and quartzite that have never known a million years of softening, their facets so bright under the constant glare that they throw back light like polished mirrors. Between these ridges the land is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with life and near-life, every square span occupied by something that moves or secretes or waits.

Frost forms overnight in thin, glassy sheets across every surface, then melts each afternoon into slow threads that trace the sharp fractures. These threads collect in pockets no larger than a cupped hand, evaporate, re-condense at dusk, and repeat—an entire hydrology compressed into the space between one ridge and the next. The vegetation thrives inside this cycle: leathery, black-green cushions of moss that exude their own oils to keep the frost from penetrating, and tall, brittle reeds whose hollow stems act as passive condensers. Dew gathers inside the reeds until pressure forces it out in measured drops that fall into waiting root nets below. The reeds grow so densely that their stems touch for kilometers at a stretch, creating a single unbroken matte that hisses softly whenever a gust moves through.

Scattered among the reeds are dark, vein-like structures that rise straight from the stone and branch into crystalline lattices. These lattices are not built; they accrete, slowly, molecule by molecule, fed by the same condensing moisture. Inside each lattice, faint tremors occur at intervals measured in hours: a low, metallic vibration that travels through the stone and is answered by neighboring lattices hundreds of paces away. The effect is industrial in outcome—raw material is sorted, concentrated, and redeposited as new crystalline spurs—but purely geological in origin, a self-organizing foundry that has run for perhaps only a few centuries since the plateau lifted.

Fauna fills every remaining interval. Iridescent mites the size of thumbnails swarm across the reed stems in coordinated sheets that reflect the constant high light like moving oil. Larger, crab-like forms with transparent carapaces graze the condensers, their legs clicking in slow, deliberate rhythms that match the lattice tremors. When enough of them gather, the combined sound becomes a single sustained chord that rolls across the plateau for minutes before fading. Overhead, translucent flyers the color of frost ride the thermals that rise only briefly each afternoon; their wings are thin enough to let the intense light pass straight through, casting fractured rainbows onto the reeds beneath. Nothing here moves quickly. Growth, decay, migration, and even predation unfold across hours or days, yet the density of bodies and processes is so complete that the entire surface feels saturated, every available niche claimed.

Because the plateau is vast enough to generate its own weather, thick banks of low cloud sometimes form directly overhead even while the surrounding lowlands remain clear. These clouds cast brief, moving shadows that race across the sharp stone. When the shadows pass, the reeds and lattices resume their faint synchronized pulses, the mites reform their sheets, and the slow engines of crystal continue their patient work under the unchanging glare.
