The valley is not so much a place as a single, colossal organism that has only lately decided to become geography. Freshly risen, its surfaces still carry the fine, blade-like ridges of whatever upheaval lifted them—ridges not yet softened by time, edges that gleam like split greenstone wherever light catches the waxy cuticles. Low-lying and open enough that its horizons remain visible for many kilometers, the terrain nonetheless feels compressed: every direction is layered, curtain upon curtain of living tissue folded into the semblance of avenues, plazas, and stacked habitations. 

The plants themselves supply all architecture. Buttressed trunks thicker than any palace wall rise and then flatten into shelf-like canopies that serve as plazas; aerial roots braid themselves into catwalks that pulse faintly with peristaltic motion; hollow internodes form arched corridors whose inner walls sweat slow beads of water. Moisture arrives in irregular rhythms—nighttime films of condensation that bead along every vein, sudden brief rains that turn the upper terraces into brief waterfalls, and deeper reservoirs held inside succulent chambers whose linings contract and relax like lungs. The air stays warm, almost skin-temperature, so the water never chills; it simply migrates, sheet by sheet, through the living masonry.

Because there are no animals, the only movement belongs to the plants and to the physics they have unsettled. Growth is audible. A low, continuous roar of expanding cell walls and rupturing seed-pods rolls through the valley like distant surf, punctuated by sharper detonations when a lateral branch decides to become a tower in a single afternoon. Surfaces ripple and reseal; a boulevard that existed at dawn may have sealed itself into a green atrium by noon, while two blocks away a new colonnade of flowering stalks erupts through what had been solid floor. Distances refuse to stay fixed. Turn a corner and the same tower may now lie behind you, or three towers may have fused into one whose interior spirals upward in a green stair no map could anticipate. Light remains ordinary sunlight, yet it arrives already filtered through countless shifting membranes so that shadows themselves sometimes solidify for a few seconds into temporary buttresses before dissolving again.

The only rule that holds is metabolic: stay in motion or be incorporated. Standing still long enough allows rootlets to test your surface, then to braid around you, then to lift you into the next layer of the city-organism where the roar of growth is even louder and the walls sweat harder. Water migrates toward whatever has newly opened; sound follows water; light leaks through both. Nothing here has finished becoming. Nothing here has begun to decay.
