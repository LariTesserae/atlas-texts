In the hollow heart of an ancient mineral seam, half a meter across and sealed for uncountable ages, the world is cool and bone-dry. Fine, pale salt crystals have grown into shelves and low ridges that once stood higher; erosion has reduced them to rounded, interlocking forms that nearly touch, leaving only narrow lanes between them. The rock itself is shot through with darker veins of oxidized ore, long since weathered into a stable, matte surface that feels almost soft underfoot.

Scattered across these shelves stand the remnants of the place’s industrial chapter: thumb-sized engine casings, their housings cracked open like seed pods, and short runs of filament that once carried current. Some still emit a faint, steady vibration, as though the last trickle of stored power continues to cycle through worn contacts. These fragments lie at odd angles, half-buried in crystal dust, yet the air around them is never truly still.

The density is not of bulk or mass but of motion. Thousands of minute, carapaced organisms—glassy-shelled, jointed things no larger than sand grains—move constantly across every surface. They do not eat or grow in the usual sense; instead they harvest charged particles from the old filaments and deposit new crystal layers wherever the current is strongest. Their collective activity fills the pocket with a single, unbroken rasp: the sound of countless legs against salt and metal, punctuated now and then by the dry click of a casing shifting as a fresh crystal forces its way outward.

Normal light, arriving from some higher fissure too fine to see, lends the chamber a steady, grainy illumination that turns every surface into a field of fine highlights. The temperature stays low enough that any stray warmth is immediately wicked into the mineral mass; breath would fog, if there were lungs here to produce it. The creatures themselves generate the only warmth, their tiny bodies forming fleeting constellations of orange points when they cluster around a still-living filament.

Nothing larger than the width of a fingernail could fit between the ridges, yet the space feels crowded, layered, and unfinished all at once. From time to time a filament fails with a soft metallic pop; the nearest swarm diverts in an instant, dismantling the dead segment grain by grain and carrying the pieces to reinforce another. The process is older than the salt shelves themselves, older than the erosion that flattened them, and shows no sign of stopping.
