The high plateau stretches without boundary, a single weathered table of basalt and fused silica that has known its shape for epochs. Rain falls often enough that every vertical face carries a permanent sheen; water gathers in thread-thin rivulets that descend the cliffs in patient braids, then vanishes into the porous stone before it can pool. The air itself is never truly dry; when night cools the surface even slightly, mist forms on every exposed ledge and machine housing, condensing into slow droplets that swell, detach, and fall again.

Across this table rises a city whose foundations predate any living memory of its builders. Layer upon layer of industrial masonry and riveted iron interlock with the native rock, the newer strata simply continuing the older ones. Walkways and rail spurs and pipe galleries stack so closely that in places the space between them is little wider than a body. Steam and warmer, metallic exhalations drift upward from the lower tiers, meeting the descending mist and turning the entire vertical volume into a single, slowly circulating medium. Nothing grows here. The only textures that are not mineral or metal are the thin, translucent films left by evaporating condensate—pale, almost glassy residues that crack and re-form with each new shower.

Yet the plateau is far from empty. The fauna that claims it moves in numbers that defy the absence of leaf or root. Great flat-bodied crawlers, their carapaces the same warm grey as the rock, travel the ledges in endless single file, harvesting the condensate films with brush-like mouthparts. Above them, shoals of lenticular fliers spiral on thermals that rise from the foundry vents; their wings are not membrane but rigid, veined silica that clinks faintly when they wheel. In the deeper galleries, colonies of bead-sized, many-legged gatherers move through the pipework like living sediment, feeding on the scale that forms inside the cooling systems. All of them are loud in aggregate: the fliers produce a constant dry rustling that carries for kilometres when the wind is still, while the crawlers’ legs drum a low, arrhythmic thunder against the stone. Individual voices are rare; instead the plateau carries a single, distributed roar that rises and falls only with changes in air pressure or the shifting of factory shifts far below.

Because the rock and the city have coexisted so long, erosion has reached a kind of equilibrium. Edges are rounded, angles softened, yet nothing collapses that has not already been replaced by newer construction following the same curving grain. The vastness itself enforces slowness: weather systems form over the plateau the way they would over an inland sea, drifting for days before releasing their loads. A single shower may take an afternoon to cross from one horizon to the other; a new tier of ironwork may take a generation to rust into the same grey as the stone beneath it. The machines continue their cycles at the same deliberate pace—presses that close once every several hours, forges that breathe on schedules measured in watches rather than minutes—yet their cumulative sound, merged with the ceaseless motion of the creatures that live among them, remains the plateau’s single unbroken voice.
