Beneath the skin of the world lies a pocket that has only just remembered it is here—a warm, hushless cavity no larger than a single sprawling room, yet one whose walls keep drifting an extra arm’s length away whenever the eye lingers. The stone is young, still sharp at every edge; fresh striations run like pulled taffy, pale and glistening where condensation beads and slides. Moisture arrives in irregular pearls that form on nothing and fall onto nothing; the droplets vanish before they strike, leaving only the faint mineral tang of recent rain on limestone that has never known weather.

Plants have colonized the emptiness so completely that the emptiness itself has become part of their architecture. Thin, translucent sheets of leaf-vein stretch across the void in vast, sagging planes, each plane held taut by hair-fine roots that reach toward opposite walls and then simply stop, suspended. Between these planes the air is almost vacant, a warm negative space through which the light—ordinary daylight, somehow present at depth—filters in soft shafts. The leaves are the color of new bronze where the light hits them, and deep chlorophyll black where it passes through; they breathe in slow, visible pulses, contracting and unfurling so that the planes ripple like laundry in a wind no one feels.

The sounds are constant and unplaceable. Every few breaths a membrane somewhere overhead splits with a soft tearing note, releasing a mist of pollen that glows briefly before settling. Elsewhere, roots scrape against the young stone in long, deliberate strokes, as though the plants are engraving the next stanza of the cavern’s shape. Occasionally the entire pocket answers with a low, wooden groan; distances rearrange by a fraction, a fracture opens or seals, and the light tilts accordingly. These are the only movements that matter. Small, infrequent animals—pale, eyeless beetles the size of thumbnails—appear now and then on the undersides of the leaf-planes, but they move like afterthoughts and vanish again inside the green tissue.

Nothing here was ever built; everything grows, and the growing is still in its first violent hours. The rules are simple and absolute: any unoccupied volume will shortly be claimed by another drifting sheet of leaf or another bridge of root; any surface that remains truly bare for more than a few moments dissolves into a haze of pale mineral dust that the plants immediately reclaim. The warmth is steady, almost body temperature, fed by the slow exhalation of the living mass. Light arrives already diffused, as though the sun itself has agreed to soften its gaze inside this single, hidden interval between one layer of earth and the next.

In this place the ordinary distinctions—floor and ceiling, inside and outside, solid and open—remain only so long as the plants consent to hold them. When a membrane tears, or when a root finally decides to cross the last gap, the pocket changes shape around anyone standing inside it, not violently, but with the patient inevitability of a lung expanding.
