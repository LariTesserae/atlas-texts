The high plateau stretches without boundary or horizon in any direction, its surface a single unbroken stone sheet planed by time until the last remnants of mountains survive only as faint, broad swells too gradual for the eye to name as rises. The rock itself is pale and chalky, veined with darker mineral that oozes slow condensation whenever the air cools; moisture gathers in these veins overnight and collects into shallow, temporary basins no deeper than a palm before the next interval of dryness reclaims them. Between the basins the surface is scored by hairline fractures so old they have grown smooth interiors, as though the stone has spent eons healing its own cracks.

Where the fractures widen enough to hold soil, vegetation appears in dense, isolated thickets: low cushions of blue-black leaves that trap mist against their undersides, and taller, jointed stalks whose hollow segments fill with condensate and release it again at intervals measured in days rather than seasons. These growths never touch; each clump stands separated by hundreds of paces of bare stone, so that the plateau reads as empty even while it remains unmistakably lush wherever life can root. No animals move among them. The only motion belongs to the plants themselves, which expand and contract their leaves in slow pulses, drinking and expelling water in cycles that are visible if watched long enough.

Scattered across the same flats are the remnants of cities built from the plateau rock itself. Their walls follow the natural fractures, rising in low, hand-laid courses no higher than a person can reach without scaffolding. Doorways are simple rectangular gaps left in the masonry; roofs are absent, or were never attempted. The streets between buildings are the untouched stone of the plateau, worn only by the same condensation that fills the basins. The settlements are numerous enough to imply organization, yet far enough apart that crossing from one to the next requires hours or days of walking across open stone. Inside the walls the air stays a fraction colder, as though the structures preserve an older chill.

Light arrives in the ordinary way—diffuse daylight that neither intensifies nor fades beyond the expected range—yet the surface of things sometimes refuses to hold its shape beneath that light. At unpredictable intervals a patch of stone will shear away from itself along an invisible plane, exposing for a few moments an identical layer beneath before the two surfaces knit together again. Entire thickets of vegetation occasionally double, their images offset by the width of a leaf for several heartbeats before snapping back into coincidence. These events produce no sound. The plateau remains quiet; the only audible movement is the faint, irregular patter of water returning to the basins and the dry whisper of leaves contracting.

The plateau’s own weather forms at the scale of its stone sheet: slow, continent-wide banks of low cloud that drift at walking pace, descend to touch the surface, then lift again without ever releasing more than the measured condensation already described. The temperature stays uniformly cold enough that breath hangs briefly in the air, yet never so low that the moisture freezes. Time moves in the same measured fashion as the rest of the processes here—slow enough to seem nearly static, yet unmistakably underway. The cities, the vegetation, and the fractures continue their cycles, each independent yet occurring on the same vast, flattened height where the oldest erosion has finally left no further surface to wear away.
