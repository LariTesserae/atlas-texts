The landscape is an ancient, continent-spanning industrial plain that has long since overwritten any trace of natural ground. Every surface—ground plane, horizon, and sky-line alike—is engineered: interlocking plates of pitted iron and vitrified concrete, laced with riveted seams and the slow, buckling waves of thermal fatigue. Over uncountable cycles these plates have been worn into a single, near-flat tessellation; the once-sharp angles of loading docks and rail spurs have sloughed into gentle, almost geological undulations, as though the entire floor has settled into its final posture after eons of load and vibration.

Water is everywhere yet never pooled in lakes. It condenses on the undersides of vast pipe galleries that run like suspended rivers overhead, then falls in slow, continuous strings. It weeps from hairline fractures in the metal skin of cooling towers and travels along capillary channels milled into every walkway. The air itself is damp and chilled; breath fogs at once and lingers in low-lying strata before being shredded by the constant draft of exhaust vents. No rain falls—only this perpetual, internal distillation, as if the entire megastructure is its own slow weather system.

Light is scarce and local. Overhead arc lamps gutter and die in long, staggered intervals; the only steady illumination comes from rows of dim sodium tubes half-buried in dust and scale, painting every corridor a jaundiced half-gloom. In the intervals of blackout, faint chemical phosphorescence lingers in certain seams where coolant has crystallized, outlining the geometry of the platework in spectral blue threads that last only minutes before fading.

Mechanical process is constant and deafening. The plain is not silent even at its emptiest reaches; a single underlying roar—low, piston-driven, and vibratory—fills every cubic meter, punctuated by sudden metallic reports as thermal expansion shifts entire sub-assemblies, by the shriek of relief valves, and by the wet, rhythmic slap of fluid hammers driving coolant through buried mains. The sound does not travel across distance so much as occupy it; it saturates the body and the surrounding metal alike, setting railings and catwalks into sympathetic tremor.

Vegetation appears only as isolated, salt-tolerant encrustations: pale, dendritic films that spread along the coolest, wettest flanges and pipe elbows. They grow in strict lines, following the drip paths, and are sparse enough that tens of meters of bare metal may lie between one colony and the next. No animals move among them; nothing larger than a film of corrosion lives here.

Yet the place is not inert. Pressure cycles still throb through the mains like a slow heartbeat. Gantries shift a few centimeters every few hours on long, hydraulic actuators whose original purpose is lost. Entire districts of the plain exhale great clouds of super-cooled vapor on a schedule no observer can quite map, then fall quiet again while another zone, kilometers away, begins its own exhalation. The effect is one of enormous, staggered respiration moving across an otherwise motionless plain.

Because nothing is natural, every vista feels both finished and provisional. Walk far enough in any direction and the same arrangement of heavy presses, transfer tracks, and weeping vent stacks repeats itself; yet the repetition is never exact. Slight misalignments accumulate—half a meter of subsidence here, an extra row of rivets there—until the eye begins to read the horizon as an eroded coastline whose “waves” are the slow collapse of forgotten infrastructure. The plain extends beyond any single vantage, open and sparsely occupied, an engineered flatness so complete that even its emptiness feels constructed.
