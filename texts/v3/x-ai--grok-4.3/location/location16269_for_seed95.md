At this height the land has forgotten how to rise or fall. It is an immense, gently breathing plateau, its once-jagged bones worn by uncounted æons into a single, slow horizon that meets the sky in a pale, unbroken line. The rock itself is the color of cooled ash and ancient ochre, its surface smoothed into long, low swells like the flanks of a sleeping beast. Nothing here still carries the memory of sharp peaks; only the softest gradients remain, so gradual they seem to belong to the passage of light rather than stone.

Moisture arrives only as a reluctant visitor. Most days the air feels warm and dry, carrying the faint mineral scent of sun-baked silt. Yet in certain hollows and along faint fault-lines that never quite healed, condensation gathers each night into shallow, temporary basins. These ephemeral pools reflect the sky for a few hours before they vanish again, leaving behind rings of darker mineral and thin films of salt. Between them the ground is pale, cracked in wide polygons, each crack no wider than a finger yet older than any living language.

Vegetation clings in isolated patches rather than spreads. Tough, leathery crusts the color of dried blood or bruised slate cover scattered stones like scabs, their edges lifting slightly in the warmth so they catch stray dew. A few gaunt, almost leafless shrubs stand at wide intervals, their branches twisted into low, wind-sculpted spirals. They are not numerous, yet where they do appear they form dense little communities of their own, each shrub surrounded by a carpet of the same resilient crust and surrounded again by open stone.

It is the smaller life, however, that fills every available space. The air and the ground between the shrubs teem with translucent, slow-moving forms—tiny shelled creatures no larger than seeds, linked by nearly invisible threads of mucus or silk that catch the light only when the angle is perfect. They do not swarm in agitation; they drift and settle, layer upon layer, until the eye begins to perceive a continuous, faintly shimmering film over every surface. Their motions are so unhurried that the plateau seems silent, yet the silence is not empty; it is crowded with patient, nearly soundless activity. Occasionally a larger, pale form—something between a flattened star and a living membrane—glides a hand’s span above the crust before settling again, its passage marked only by a soft rearrangement of the smaller beings beneath it.

Scattered among these living layers lie older, stranger marks. Low rings of stone, half-buried and worn to smoothness, still hold a geometry no natural erosion has yet erased. Some are no taller than a kneeling figure; others have been reduced to faint radial lines of differently colored mineral. Nothing grows directly upon them, yet the minute life flows around their edges as if acknowledging an older boundary it has agreed not to cross.

Light arrives evenly, without drama. The sun climbs and declines across a sky whose thinness lends every color a faint metallic cast. Shadows are short and soft-edged, dissolving into the general brightness rather than carving sharp relief. Only at the very edges of the shallow basins do reflections intensify, turning the temporary water into brief mirrors of cloud and sky.

Change here requires centuries. The pools return and vanish according to rhythms longer than any single life. The minute creatures expand their glistening threads by finger-widths each generation. The crusts darken or lighten by imperceptible degrees. Even the wind, when it comes, moves as a slow pressure rather than a gust, shifting loose grains of salt a few paces before settling again. The plateau endures in patient, dense quiet, its surface crowded yet almost motionless beneath the wide, warm sky.
