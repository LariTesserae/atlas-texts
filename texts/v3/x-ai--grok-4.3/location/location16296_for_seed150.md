The place is a high, knife-edged shelf of newly uplifted rock no wider than a modest hamlet, its surface still raw and angular from recent upheaval. Frost has already begun to bite the sharpest ridges, yet the air remains heavy with suspended moisture that condenses against every surface and runs in slow, continuous threads down living walls. Sheets of dark-green membrane, half leaf and half structural tissue, have grown across the rock in stacked, overlapping planes so dense that little open space remains between them. What appears at first to be a town is in fact a single, continuous organism: avenues are simply gaps left where older growth has withdrawn, plazas are broad pads of compacted moss still warm from slow internal fermentation, and towers rise as braided columns of vascular bundles that taper and rejoin like living masonry.

Water moves through everything. It beads on the upper surfaces of the highest leaves, trickles along channeled veins that have thickened into gutters, and drips steadily from the undersides into lower chambers. The sound is almost inaudible, a soft, repeated patter that is felt more as vibration than noise. In the dim light that filters through the layered canopy—light reduced to a greenish dusk—condensation itself glows faintly where it gathers along mineral veins, lending the passages a cool, persistent haze.

Growth continues at a deliberate but unmistakable pace. New shoots lengthen overnight, sealing or reopening passages according to some internal rhythm; older tissues slowly compact under their own accumulating weight, forming floors that yield slightly underfoot before stiffening again. The entire structure breathes in slow, tidal waves of expansion and contraction, each cycle drawing cooler air down from the peaks and releasing a faint, metallic scent of crushed resin. Small pockets of warmth persist inside the densest trunks where fermentation maintains temperatures just above freezing, allowing thin rills of water to remain liquid even when frost coats the outer leaves.

Fauna is scarce and mostly integrated. Occasional translucent mites the size of thumbnails drift on the condensing currents, alighting only long enough to harvest excess sugars before being reabsorbed. No larger creatures move through the passages; the few that once entered have long since become part of the substrate. The quiet is therefore nearly total, broken only by the faint, wet clicks of expanding cellulose and the steady, almost subsonic thrum of water moving through a hundred thousand microscopic channels at once.

Nothing here was built by tools. Every surface, every division, every drainage line has been grown in place and continues to adjust itself. The settlement remains small because the living mass has not yet decided to expand beyond the shelf; for now it concentrates its energy into thickening walls, deepening water storage, and sharpening the crystalline edges where new growth still meets untouched stone. At the scale of a single room or narrow street the place feels vast and claustrophobic at once, every direction closed by breathing tissue that nevertheless leaves just enough gap for a body to pass—provided it moves with the same unhurried deliberation as the growth around it.
