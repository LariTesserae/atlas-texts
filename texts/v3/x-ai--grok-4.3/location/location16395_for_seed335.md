A pocket of marsh fringe lies cradled inside a recent fracture of peat and stone, no wider than a small room and invisible unless one is already stooping at its edge. The ground has only lately split here; the broken lips remain knife-edged, pale mineral still bright where water has not yet darkened them. Cold seeps upward constantly. Every exposed face weeps in thin, steady threads that never quite become streams, yet never stop. The air stays saturated; droplets gather on every surface and fall again, adding their own soft percussion to the louder, rhythmic sounds rising from below.

Across the floor of the cleft the vegetation stands tall but widely spaced, reeds and cold-loving sedges whose roots drink from the near-surface water table. Between their stems the space feels hollow, almost vacant. Nothing crowds; nothing touches. Yet the emptiness is alive. Small, quick invertebrates patrol the water films and root mats; translucent, cold-tolerant frogs the size of thumbs flick between stems; clusters of aquatic larvae pulse in the shallowest films, their synchronized thrashing producing a low mechanical thrum that echoes upward through the narrow stone walls. The sound is steady, factory-like: a layered chugging of water being cycled, gas pockets releasing in measured bursts, and the drip-echo returning from the rock in overlapping meters. It never drops below a working din.

Above the cleft the daylight is already reduced; inside it is dimmer still, filtered through condensing mist that hangs at shoulder height. The light that does reach the floor arrives cool and bluish, caught and scattered by the perpetual vapor. No structures stand here, only the living and the mineral: sharp new rock, living tissue, and the ceaseless movement of water being lifted, released, and falling again. The processes run at an industrial tempo even though nothing has been built; the rock itself, freshly opened and still adjusting, acts as both conduit and resonator. Fauna moves through the same rhythm, hunting or spawning in time with the pulses of water and gas, so that predator and prey, drip and vent, all keep the one loud, active meter without pause.
