A low hollow cups the land here, its edges soft from centuries of slow settling rather than any sharp carving. The ground never quite dries; thin films of water bead and slide across pale stone that weeps from hidden seams, gathering into brief, shallow lenses no deeper than a palm. The air stays cool and still, carrying a faint mineral chill that clings to breath. Vegetation clings in threadbare patches—silvery lichen crusts and brittle, ankle-high reeds that rust at the tip—yet the space feels densely occupied because every surface is layered with something else: faint metallic filings, half-buried gear teeth, coils of wire that have rusted into crooked lace, all resting against one another as though the ground itself exhaled them slowly over decades.

Small, half-sunken structures stand at irregular intervals, each no larger than a garden shed. Their corrugated panels are warped and bowed outward, edges feathered by long weathering yet still joined by thick industrial rivets. Inside one, a belt-driven flywheel turns without power, its motion arriving in short, erratic bursts before it halts again, as though something unseen keeps restarting it. Pipes snake from beneath the structures into the wet ground and emerge elsewhere tipped with condensers that drip steadily, feeding the constant thin film of water. The machines are old and half-functional; where metal has failed, new growths of crystal or resin have begun to bridge the gaps, translucent and faintly veined, as if the industrial forms are seeding their own replacements.

The scale remains intimate—an area a person could cross in a minute at a steady walk—yet nothing here seems unoccupied. Even the gaps between artifacts are filled with quiet accumulations: spirals of stripped insulation, shards of safety glass refracting ordinary daylight into soft spectra, small stacks of ceramic insulators half-buried like fossils. Fauna is almost absent; occasionally a pale, wingless insect walks across a wet stone and vanishes into a seam. Sound scarcely rises above the slow drip of water and the infrequent, muffled click of metal shifting against metal. When the flywheel turns, the noise is a brief, dry whisper quickly swallowed again.

Yet beneath this settled quiet runs a persistent turbulence. At irregular intervals—sometimes minutes, sometimes longer—a section of pipe or a gear cluster will suddenly reconfigure itself with a series of small, jerky movements, as though obeying a schedule only it remembers. A corroded valve may open and close three times in succession, releasing a puff of cooler air that momentarily stirs the surface water into intersecting rings. These spasms leave the overall forms changed but never destroyed; the erosion is patient and balanced, while the instability is local, restless, and oddly contained, like a room that cannot quite decide whether it is still in use or already abandoned. The light remains even and ordinary, revealing every layer without drama, and the hollow keeps its inventory shifting in near-silence.
