The place is a sealed micro-ocean caught inside a single fractured sphere of metamaterial no larger than a modest auditorium, its curved walls grown rather than poured. Inside, the water is cool, slightly viscous, and completely saturated; every cubic meter holds something engineered. Floor-to-ceiling lattices of glassy carbon and living ceramic rise in unfinished spires whose edges remain knife-sharp because the structure is only days old. These lattices are already half-colonized by filament membranes that conduct both electricity and pressure waves, so the entire volume thrums with low, continuous resonance like a choir of submerged gongs.

Light arrives as ordinary daylight, yet it has no source: refractive nodes suspended in the water bend and multiply photons that leak in from outside the sphere, keeping the interior evenly lit. Because nothing moves except the water itself, the illumination feels clinical, almost forensic. Currents thread between the lattices in choreographed ribbons; they carry dissolved minerals that accrete onto the structures in real time, lengthening a spire here, sharpening a strut there. The surfaces never soften. No silt settles because there is no sediment yet; the young world has nothing to erode.

The density is total. Between every pair of spires floats a mesh of micro-pumps and phase-change vesicles no larger than pollen; they click and sigh, adjusting buoyancy and temperature in overlapping spheres of influence. The water is therefore never still. It rings, it hisses, it produces sudden bright snaps when vesicles collapse. These sounds layer into a constant, room-filling roar that nevertheless carries distinct rhythms if one listens for them. There are no animals to swim against the current; the only living matter is a sparse fringe of filamentous algae clinging to the newest struts, each thread no thicker than a hair and spaced meters apart, doing no more than turning dissolved carbon into fresh ceramic.

The sphere’s own rules are still forming. Certain volumes of water are temporarily stiffer than others, as if nanoscale fields have decided to treat liquidity as optional. A drifting mote may slow, stop, then accelerate again for no visible reason. Occasionally a lattice segment reconfigures itself with a soft, concussive report that sends a pressure wave rippling outward, momentarily clearing a lane through the crowded water before the mesh reseals the space. The entire enclave feels at once ancient in its engineering and newborn in its unfinished urgency, a single loud, crowded breath held beneath an ordinary sky that exists only as light.
