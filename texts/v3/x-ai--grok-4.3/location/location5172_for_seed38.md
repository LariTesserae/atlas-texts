The land rises in one unbroken swell that has forgotten it was ever a mountain. After uncountable ages of rain and root, the peaks have been softened into low, rolling plateaus whose summits still sit high enough that the air thins and cools, yet the temperature remains a constant, close warmth. Everything is green and brown and bruised-purple at once, because green is not merely color here but the actual material of the ground. The living mass begins at root-depth and climbs without interruption into crowns that brush the lowest cloud layers, forming a single, fibrous continent of tissue that is at once soil, canopy, wall, and ceiling.

Water moves through this mass rather than across it. It condenses on the undersides of immense, leathery leaves the size of lakes and falls in slow, deliberate threads that never become rain, only a perpetual interior drizzle sinking through successive layers of stems, rhizomes, and hollow galleries. At any height you can press your palm to a surface and feel the surface press back with stored moisture; the whole structure is a reservoir that leaks by design. Where the mass thins slightly, black stone shows through like old bone, streaked with mineral stains that weep fresh droplets even in the dimness. The light that reaches the interior is the color of tarnished bronze, filtered through so many translucent membranes that noon and midnight are only a matter of degree.

Nothing here hurries. Vines thicken by a hand’s width each century; entire terraces of blossom open and close across millennia. Creatures move within the same patient rhythm: broad, soft-bodied grazers whose many legs find purchase in the living walls, their breathing audible only as a low, wet sigh when they pass close. Smaller, quicker forms exist, but they stay inside the galleries, their wings or fins replaced by gliding lobes that merge with the surrounding growth when they rest. The sound of the world is therefore mostly the sound of water deciding where to go next, punctuated, rarely, by the distant, resonant pop of a seed capsule releasing its charge after centuries of pressure.

Scattered through the green strata are faint, angular traces: long ribs of something harder than any plant, curved plates now layered with successive centuries of bark. They do not form rooms or roads; they remain only as suggestions that something once shaped space differently. Over so much time the plant mass has risen around them, preserving their outlines while erasing their purpose, until the distinction between relic and living tissue grows uncertain.

At this scale, weather forms inside the canopy itself. Slow updrafts travel for days between two distant plateaus, carrying spores that take seasons to cross a single valley. The entire landscape behaves like a single, vast organism whose organs are mountains and whose breath is measured in centuries. Nothing is built except by growth, nothing is dismantled except by the same growth from another direction, and every surface eventually becomes another surface living upon it.
