The place is an enormous hollow beneath the skin of the world, so large that its own slow weather forms inside it: cold fog banks that roll for days between distant walls, then collapse into brief, needle-sharp rains that leave everything slick for an hour before the moisture is sucked back into the living mass. Light arrives from above through hairline fractures in the ceiling rock, refracted and multiplied by countless sheets of condensation until the air itself seems to glow a steady, shadowless white. The temperature stays just above freezing; breath hangs and falls as tiny ice crystals that melt again on the nearest leaf surface.

Vegetation is not ground cover here but the ground itself. Immense, slow-growing lattices of translucent white and pale blue plant tissue rise in irregular towers and bridges, each “trunk” a fused column of hollow tubes that carry water upward and carbon dioxide down. Between these megastructures the space is almost empty; the nearest lattice may be kilometers away, a faint vertical gleam in the bright air. New growth is constantly extruded in razor-edged plates and spines that slice into older tissue and then fuse, so the architecture is always both expanding and being undercut. The result is a landscape of fresh, glassy fractures and sudden collapses, edges still sharp because nothing has had time to wear them smooth.

Water moves only in pulses. Condensation beads on the ceilings, gathers into cold rivulets along the plant tubes, and every few cycles releases in a brief indoor rain that patters across enormous leaf plates before the plates fold and drink it away. Pockets remain—mirror-flat pools cupped inside the lattices—until a shift in the growth tilts them and they spill in long, silent falls that vanish into the dark below.

Fauna is scarce and mostly invisible. The only recurring signs are faint, spiraling trails left by something that moves through the plant tubes like a slow bore-worm and occasional translucent spheres, fist-sized and faintly pulsing, that drift on the cold updrafts, perhaps eggs, perhaps the discarded fruiting bodies of the dominant vegetation. Nothing sings. The only persistent sound is the low, continuous creaking of new plant plates pushing against one another, occasionally punctuated by the soft, distant report of a lattice section giving way and the long, hollow echo that follows.

Scattered through the emptiness are the remains of earlier visitors: a single low ring of fitted stone half-swallowed by a rising plant column, a row of shallow tool marks on a ceiling fracture where someone once tried to widen a light-shaft, a scatter of bone needles and braided fiber that never rusted because there is no true metal here, only the living, self-replacing architecture. The tools that made them were simple: sharpened plant spines, knotted strands of the same translucent fiber. Nothing has been added in a long time.

The rules feel half-biological, half-geological and still in flux. When a lattice collapses, the exposed tissue cools rapidly, hardens into a temporary mineral-like shell, and then, weeks later, new shoots erupt through the shell at right angles, beginning the cycle again. Light, cold, moisture, and structural failure are the only clocks. Time is measured in the slow brightening or dimming of the ceiling fractures and in the intervals between rains. Nothing is finished; everything is either just grown or already beginning to be replaced.
