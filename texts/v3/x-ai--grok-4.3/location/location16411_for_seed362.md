The landscape is a single, unbroken fabrication that has forgotten its own beginning. Low and level for thousands of kilometers, it nevertheless rises in slow, almost imperceptible swells, each crest so broad that its summit feels like another flat horizon. Every surface—floor, wall, vault, conduit—is living or once-living tissue rendered structural. Vascular fibers have been coaxed into beams, load-bearing membranes stretched between them like drumheads. Where ordinary architecture would use stone or steel, here the material is compacted xylem laced with silica, still respiring, still slowly thickening or thinning according to stresses measured in decades.

Nothing is dry in the sense of barren; nothing is wet in the sense of liquid. The air itself carries the solvent in vapor form—thin, metallic-tasting mists that condense only at the microscopic scale inside specialized chambers within the tissues. These mists feed the entire mass. The result is a perpetual coolness without chill, a steady 8–10 °C maintained by the plants’ own evaporative cycles and by the slow turning of vast, low-slung vanes that are themselves grown rather than forged. The light is constant and unshadowed, arriving from a lattice of high, pale emitters whose spectrum mimics noon at temperate latitudes. Under that unvarying brightness the surfaces gleam: pale greens veined with silver, bruised purples where older growth has been compressed, occasional seams of living amber that pulse once per cycle with stored charge.

Density makes distances deceptive. Strata of membrane and fiber press close, leaving only narrow vertical slots or horizontal galleries wide enough for a single body. Walk far enough in any direction and these slots begin to curve; after a few hundred meters the walls may fold back upon themselves or part to reveal an identical gallery already occupied by another version of yourself, offset by minutes or by weeks. These folds are stable but not fixed; they migrate on seasonal schedules measured in centuries. The effect is not of teleportation but of a slackening in the metric itself—space is still there, only locally accordion-folded by the same growth that builds the floors and ceilings.

Industrial processes continue without noise. Pumps are peristaltic thickenings that constrict once every few hours; power moves along piezoelectric strands that creak almost below the threshold of hearing. The dominant sound is therefore the sound of absence: a steady, soft exhalation of vapor moving through the membranes, occasionally joined by the minute detonations of silica crystals cracking under compression. When weather of its own arises—dry, luminous fronts rolling in from the far edge of the continent—the only audible change is a momentary deepening of that exhalation, as though the entire substrate were drawing a single, continent-wide breath.

Fauna is limited to motile spores and occasional thread-like consortia that travel along the piezoelectric strands like static charges. They leave no tracks; they are visible only as brief shimmer distortions in the bright, even light. The substrate itself is the ecology. Older layers are slowly compacted into inert, pearl-gray foundation stone while fresh growth continues to extrude above and around them, so that every cross-section contains both the living present and the compressed record of centuries. Erosion here is not removal but redistribution: material is never lost, only relocated from stressed zones into new tension members whose geometry already anticipates the next stress. The result is an environment that feels at once ancient and unfinished, its vast, low profile interrupted only by the gentle, ongoing buckling of its own slow architecture.
