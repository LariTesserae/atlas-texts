High on a wind-scoured plateau of fractured selenite and sulfur-stained feldspar, the land rises in long, patient steps that stretch toward horizons never quite reached. The air is thin, bone-dry, and brutally warm; it carries a constant metallic tang that coats the tongue and seems to thicken against the skin. Nothing grows here in any familiar sense—no lichens, no mats of extremophile film—yet the mineral surfaces themselves pulse with slow, radial crystallizations that extrude and reabsorb over centuries, forming ridges and nodes that mimic gear teeth or cooling fins.

Between these formations lies an immensity of negative space, a near-void atmosphere in which dust motes the size of pollen drift for days before settling. The emptiness is not absolute. It is interrupted by presences that register more as pressure gradients than as bodies: colossal, semi-translucent organisms whose outlines only resolve when they shift against the light. These creatures—more lattice than flesh—drift or crawl at intervals measured in decades, their movements grinding new facets into the stone and releasing low, resonant chimes that accumulate into a single unbroken industrial chord. The chord never rises or falls; it simply is, a permanent mechanical overtone that makes the air itself feel machined.

Normal daylight, pale and unfiltered, falls across everything and somehow fails to illuminate certain intervals of distance, as though segments of the visible spectrum are being rerouted through adjacent layers of reality. In those intervals the plateau appears to fold, briefly revealing seams of darker mineral that move like conveyor belts before the fold reseals. Footfalls (if any were possible) would echo back from several seconds earlier or later, arriving as overlapping copies that degrade into static. Over long durations the surface lowers by microns where the translucent forms pass, and rises elsewhere where new crystal lattices extrude; the net topography remains almost unchanged, yet every specific location is slowly replaced by its own successor.

No artifacts interrupt the terrain. The industrial character arises instead from the rocks themselves: pressure waves traveling through fault lines produce rhythms indistinguishable from turbine whine, and occasional bursts of ionized gas flare along crystal edges like arc-weld flashes. These events are neither frequent nor rare; they belong to a time scale in which a single occurrence may span a human lifetime, registering to any observer as a continuous, low-level roar. The heat radiates upward from the mineral mass, trapped beneath the thin inversion layer that hovers at roughly twice human height, turning the region into an open thermal engine whose only moving parts are the drifting, grinding fauna and the incremental rearrangement of the ground they traverse.
