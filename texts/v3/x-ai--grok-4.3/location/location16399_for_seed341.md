A low basin of slick, heat-warped concrete stretches no wider than a single plaza, its edges dissolving into reed-choked water that never quite becomes a lake. Weeping seams in the basalt underlayer release steady rivulets that trace rust-colored paths across the slabs before vanishing into grated sumps. Between these channels stand seven or eight industrial blocks—windowless, three stories at most—linked by elevated pipe gantries and catwalks whose rivets have long since bled into the surrounding stone. The structures are not crowded; each sits several body-lengths from the next, the gaps filled only by shallow pools and the occasional freestanding column of braided copper conduit.

Thick, waxy leaves the color of verdigris thrust upward through cracks and pipe sleeves, spaced so that shafts of dim, copper-tinted light still reach the ground between them. The foliage is lush where it exists—broad fans and dripping aerial roots—but it leaves most of the plaza’s surface bare and reflective. Condensation forms constantly on every metal surface; the air is thick enough that breath leaves visible eddies. The temperature presses like a second skin, yet nothing stirs except the slow rotation of flywheels behind grilled vents and the continuous percussion of water striking hot cast-iron housings. The sound is loud and without variation: a layered roar of distant turbines, valve chatter, and the endless, glassy patter of droplets. No wings, no skittering, no breathing presence interrupts it.

Each block exhales pale steam in irregular pulses. Where the steam meets the condensing mist, it thickens into slow-moving curtains that drift between the structures before being drawn back into intakes low on the walls. The concrete itself has acquired a polished, almost leathery texture from decades of this cycle; edges are rounded, joints softened, yet nothing appears decayed. The erosion has reached a kind of equilibrium—the same water that loosens grains also deposits mineral skins that reseal the surface. Pipes and beams show similar aging: thick mantles of oxide and mineral scale that nevertheless continue to carry pressurized fluid without visible failure.

At the center of the plaza a single, low gantry crane still moves on its rails, its hook rising and falling in a rhythm measured in minutes rather than seconds. Each time it lowers, it clips a rising column of broad leaves that have grown through the grate beneath, nudging them aside before retracting again. The motion is deliberate, almost vegetative in its pace, and nothing else in the empty intervals between buildings suggests any other agency. The entire arrangement feels simultaneously settled and unsettled: a machine-place that continues its work in the absence of operators, and a garden that has learned to keep its distance.
