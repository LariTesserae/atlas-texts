The chamber is a single vaulted hall of riveted iron and poured concrete, no wider than a modest cathedral nave, its floor a grid of rusted catwalks now entirely underwater. Every surface—walls, ceiling, pipe runs, turbine housings—has been built rather than grown; even the sediment that once lay beneath has long since been excavated or sealed beneath layers of bitumen and steel plate. Time has worn the metal into soft, rounded profiles; edges that once were sharp now slope away under centuries of slow abrasion and the patient work of boring mollusks. The concrete is blotched with dark, almost black accretions that have fused into a single continuous skin, so that the distinction between structure and encrustation has vanished.

Dim, bluish lamps—still live—hang at irregular intervals from the vaulted ceiling, their glow fractured into shifting shafts by the constant drift of silt and small bubbles. The water itself is cool and heavy, tasting faintly of ozone and old grease. It moves in slow, organized currents: a low-pressure intake along the western wall draws fluid through a bank of clogged filters; farther east, a pair of reciprocating pumps thrum at a measured pace, expelling the same water upward through wide riser pipes. These rhythms set the tempo for everything else.

Fish move in loose shoals between the pipes—sturdy, gray-bodied creatures whose scales catch the lamplight in brief silver flashes. Larger forms, perhaps eels or bottom-dwelling rays, glide along the floor grates, their bellies brushing the mesh. Small crustaceans cling to every joint and valve, filtering the water that rushes past. There is almost no true plant growth; only a thin, felt-like film of dark algae coats the warmer upper surfaces of the lamps and the occasional patch of less turbulent metal. The living things therefore occupy space rather than cover it—scattered, purposeful, using the architecture as reef and shelter.

Sound fills the volume without echo, because the water itself is the transmitting medium. The pumps produce a steady, low-frequency pulse that travels through bone. Occasional metallic clicks and creaks mark valves seating or pipes expanding. The fish add a dry, papery rustle when their bodies brush one another or the structure. Now and then a deeper groan rises from the foundation, the sound of the whole small complex settling another fraction of an inch into the silt beneath it—yet the lights stay on, the pumps keep their interval, and the currents continue to circulate. The place has reached the calm, nearly flat condition that ancient things achieve when every motion that can be worn away has already been worn away, leaving only the essential cycles still running.
