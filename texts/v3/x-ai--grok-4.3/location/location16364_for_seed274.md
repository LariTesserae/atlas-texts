The place is a shallow mineral basin no wider than a large chamber, its floor sunk only a hand’s span below the surrounding rock. Everything here long ago reached a settled equilibrium: the stone has been worn into smooth, undulating sheets and low ribs where slow water once moved, yet the motion has ceased. Only seepage remains. From hairline fissures and from the undersides of overhanging ledges, clear droplets gather, swell, and fall with metronomic patience into a network of shallow pans. The pans themselves are lined with a fine, pale silt that never quite dries; the water sits there, surface unbroken except for the rings of each new drop. Condensation beads continually on every exposed face, so the rock is never fully dry and never fully wet—always filmed, always glistening under the dim light that filters down from a single narrow opening far above.

The rock is mostly a dense volcanic glass, fractured long ago into plates that now lie edge-to-edge like paving. Between the plates the silt has packed itself so tightly that walking would leave no print deeper than a breath. Scattered across this surface are a few worn objects: a chipped stone blade half-buried, a shallow mortar whose rim has been softened by the same slow erosion that shaped the basin, and two or three rounded hammerstones resting where they were last set down. No metal survives; the tools are simple, their surfaces matte from the same mineral film that coats the living rock.

Nothing grows. No moss, no lichen, no thread of root. In their place the glass itself has formed faint internal patterns—clouded stripes and minute crystalline rosettes that catch what little light reaches them and return it as a dull, nacreous sheen. The air is warm, close, and perfectly still; the only movement is the slow fattening and release of each droplet. When one falls, the sound is small and local, absorbed at once by the saturated stone. Between drops there is only the faint, continuous whisper of water sliding down an inclined face and into the next pan. The basin holds its temperature and its moisture without variation, as though the processes that once shaped it have been granted permission to continue only in these smallest increments, and no others.
