The settlement occupies a shallow mineral basin where the ground has settled into broad, low shelves of banded stone and fused alloy. These shelves slope only enough to guide threads of condensation toward shallow collection hollows that never quite empty; the water sits in dark, metallic-tasting lenses, reflecting back the same even light that fills the air at all times.

Buildings rise at wide intervals, never crowding one another. Each structure is a single weathered mass—cathedral-scale shells of riveted plate and extruded crystal—linked by raised walkways now softened into gentle ridges by long, patient erosion. Between the shells lie open stretches of the same banded mineral, polished in some places to a dull mirror, dulled in others by fine, unceasing mechanical dust. Nothing grows here, yet the surfaces are never bare. They are coated instead by a living mineral skin: countless tiny resonators, crystalline and motionless in outward form, whose internal lattices vibrate without pause. The vibration produces a single, unchanging chord that fills the basin at constant volume. It does not swell or fade, only continues, the same note pressed against every surface and every breath.

The resonators blanket every ledge, every roof angle, every pipe flange so thickly that from a distance the buildings appear upholstered in pale, slightly iridescent felt. Up close the covering resolves into numberless individual facets, each no larger than a thumbnail, all oriented toward the same invisible center. They do not move. They simply are, and in being they maintain the single, unvarying tone that defines the acoustic equilibrium of the place.

Inside the shells the same equilibrium persists in larger machines. Flywheels turn at exactly the rate they turned when the settlement first achieved balance; pistons cycle through identical strokes; belts transmit power that never fluctuates. Condensation forms on the upper curves of the housings and runs down in steady rivulets that collect in troughs shaped long ago to receive them. The water does not overflow; the systems neither speed nor slow. The only variation is spatial: farther from the central chord the tone grows neither louder nor softer, only thinner, as though the air itself has been tuned to carry one frequency and no other.

The low elevation keeps the basin slightly cooler than the surrounding flats. Cool air holds the moisture close to the ground, where it meets the faint, constant warmth radiating from the machines. The meeting produces the intermittent mist that beads on every resonator facet, returning the water to the mineral cycle without ever breaking the larger stillness. Nothing arrives and nothing departs; the loud, strange, industrial order simply continues in its established, settled form.
