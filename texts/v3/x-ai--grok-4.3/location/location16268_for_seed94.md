The highland stretches without edge or horizon, a cool plateau so immense that its own weather gathers and dissolves across distances measured in days of walking. Mist hangs in low, slow-moving banks, born where the chill air meets constant seepage from the ground. Water moves everywhere but rarely in rivers: it beads on every surface, drips from rock ledges in thin silver threads, and rises again as vapor before it can pool. The rock itself weeps—dark basalt and pale, porous stone alike—pressured by aquifers that never drain completely because the elevation keeps the whole layer just above freezing for most of each cycle.

Vegetation has filled every fracture and hollow until the ground reads as a single continuous organism rather than separate plants. Massive, low-crowned trees stand shoulder to shoulder with no visible soil between them; their canopies mesh so tightly that beneath them the air stays dim and still. Vines as thick as cables climb and droop in dense curtains, their leaves overlapping in hundreds of layers. Mosses and liverworts carpet trunks, branches, and the narrow strips of exposed rock, all in the same muted spectrum of blue-green and slate. Growth here is slow and crowded; every new shoot must push between existing stems, so the forest holds its shape for centuries. The cool temperature prevents the usual riot of tropical decay; instead, fallen leaves and needles compress into dense, almost leathery mats that resist rot and remain slightly damp for years.

The same crowding produces an industrial quiet. Inside the packed biomass, slow chemical exchanges happen at planetary scale: leaves release faint, metallic-scented aerosols that drift upward and catch the light as faint, shifting glints. Certain thick-stemmed plants maintain steady internal currents—nutrient solutions drawn upward by capillary action and returned through root networks that resemble buried conduits. Where two such root masses meet, faint warmth radiates for a hand’s width, just enough to keep the surrounding mist from freezing. These processes run continuously yet produce almost no audible sound; the only regular noises are the soft, spaced ticks of water droplets striking the leaf mats and the occasional dry rustle when a heavier mist bank rolls through and strips moisture from the canopy.

Fauna moves within this density rather than across it. Small, multi-legged browsers the size of a closed fist travel along established vine corridors, their weight barely disturbing the mesh. Larger, slow-moving herbivores—broad-flanked and low to the ground—graze by sliding broad mouths between leaves instead of tearing them, preserving the weave. Predators exist but announce themselves only by sudden absences: a patch of vines suddenly empty where something has withdrawn into the deeper canopy. Because the temperature stays low, metabolisms run deliberately; movement is economical and therefore quiet.

The terrain beneath all this has long since settled into equilibrium. Ancient flows of stone and ice carved broad, gentle swales and low domes; subsequent growth and erosion have rounded every edge further until the hills feel less like obstacles than like the contours of a vast, breathing lung. No new gullies form because the living cover absorbs and redirects water before it can cut. The landscape is therefore both ancient and actively maintained, its scale and density sustaining one another across whatever cycles govern this height. Light arrives as ordinary daylight filtered through mist and leaf, neither dim nor bright, simply present.
