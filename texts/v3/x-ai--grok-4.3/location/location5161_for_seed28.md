In a shallow mineral basin no wider than a single courtyard—its rim lost in glare and distance—a pocket settlement clings to the baked floor like a carved seal set into salt. The ground is pale, veined stone the color of sun-bleached bone, its surface already rounded and softened by long, patient wind that has scraped away every sharp edge until even the streets feel like the inside of old pottery. There is almost no moisture; what little exists lingers as ghostly films inside translucent mineral tubes that honeycomb the bedrock, feeding the city without ever pooling or flowing.

From these tubes rise the vegetation—lush, improbable towers of folded, coral-like tissue that hold their own internal chemistry. Each “tree” is a dense spiral of living membranes stretched over rigid salt spines, pulsing with slow, visible contractions that draw trace vapors from the air and convert them through heat into something like sap. The membranes are thick and rubbery, the green of deep pond algae under bright sun, and they quiver constantly, producing a low, layered drone that fills the basin with deafening, constant vibration. The sound is not wind or voices but the rhythmic flexing of thousands of these membranes sliding against one another, amplified by the stone bowl until conversation must be conducted in touch and gesture.

Buildings rise among them in primitive layers: stacked plates of the same pale stone, dry-fitted without mortar, woven through with fibrous ropes spun from discarded membrane strips. The structures are intimate in scale—some no taller than two people—yet densely urban, with narrow alleys that open without warning into empty plazas the size of threshing floors. Nothing moves through those plazas except fine white dust and the slow drifting of shed membrane fragments, large as papery leaves, that catch the light and spiral downward. The city feels hollow, its population long since folded into the walls or departed, leaving only the vegetation to occupy volume.

Light is relentless, striking straight down from an unmarked sky and reflecting upward from every salt surface until shadows are thin and blue. Temperature holds steady in the warm range; heat radiates from stone and tissue alike, rising in slow currents that coax the membranes into faster vibration and louder drone. Rare fauna appear only as occasional quick shadows—small, armored things that lick salt from the tubes and vanish into crevices—leaving the vibrating plants as the dominant, almost solitary life.

Nothing here is still. The membranes expand and contract in overlapping cycles; dust devils crawl along the plazas and die against the buildings; the woven ropes slacken and tighten with warmth. Erosion is complete and balanced: every surface already belongs to its own weathered state, and the settlement continues only because the same processes that wear it down also supply the mineral tubes and the living tissue that has learned to sing with them. The basin remains sealed, self-contained, and deafening, a single small feature of stone and membrane that has turned its own dryness into ceaseless, noisy abundance.
