A low-ceilinged hollow no larger than a modest chapel, pocketed a few dozen meters beneath a surface that has not yet fully formed. Raw basalt and pale, unweathered dolomite still push outward in blunt, unfinished lobes; water films them constantly, weeping from countless seams so that every exposed rock face stays varnished and dark. The water never pools into open lakes; instead it beads, trickles, and re-condenses inside a living lattice that has claimed the entire volume.

That lattice is the place itself. Thick, rope-like stems and flattened fronds interlock at every scale, from microfibrils to load-bearing pillars three hand-spans wide. The plants grow without gaps or soil; each cell is both wall and root, exchanging nutrients through direct cytoplasmic bridges. Their tissues run cold—gelid to the touch—yet remain metabolically active because nanoscale organelles engineered long ago continue to cycle minimal entropy even at near-freezing temperatures. Moisture clings to every surface in a permanent sheen; droplets form, swell, and fall with deliberate slowness, striking lower leaves with a resonant, hollow percussion that carries farther than it should in the confined space.

Light is scant. A faint, violet-grey bioluminescence leaks from thread-like veins within the stems, enough only to reveal textures and the slow drift of vapor. The glow pulses at intervals measured in hours rather than seconds, synchronized across entire chambers as though the whole mass shares a single sluggish clock.

Animals move through the lattice the way termites move through wood, yet they are not insects. Pale, translucently furred forms—some no larger than thumbs, others the length of forearms—press tunnels through the living substrate, their bodies reshaping the plant cells behind them so that passages seal again after they pass. These creatures vocalize in long, subsonic groans produced by inflating specialized bladders; the sound travels as much through the water-saturated tissues as through air, arriving as a low, continuous pressure one feels in the bones before the ear registers it. The calls rise and decay over minutes, never hurried.

Because nothing here is hurried. Growth proceeds at a rate visible only across many human lifetimes; a stem thickens by a fraction of a millimeter while an observer watches, yet the cumulative pressure is sufficient to fracture the unfinished dolomite still pushing outward from the ceiling. Erosion, such as it is, is therefore constructive: the rock is being shaped by the very biomass that will eventually replace it. There are no artifacts, no tools, no remnants of earlier intention—only the continuous, patient replacement of mineral by organized plant matter whose original programmers are forgotten.

In this slow, resonant dimness, distinctions between floor, wall, and ceiling blur. One simply moves within a single, cold, breathing tissue that is still deciding what shape it will ultimately take.
