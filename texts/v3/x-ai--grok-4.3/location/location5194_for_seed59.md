The place is a highland hamlet pressed into the side of a ridge where the air stays cool and the ground never truly dries. Everything here has grown together for so long that the distinction between what was built and what simply grew has blurred into a single, living mass. Broad-leafed vines as thick as a person’s arm have been trained, bent, and lashed into curving walls and half-domes; their living surfaces still photosynthesize, so the interiors are dim but not dark, the foliage overhead letting through a steady, even wash of daylight. Between the living walls, narrower saplings have been woven into lattices that hold platforms and short ladders; water beads constantly along the undersides of the leaves and drips into stone-lined channels that run through the middle of each narrow lane.

The ground itself is springy with layered root mats and packed leaf mold, always damp. After any rain—common here—the channels overflow into shallow sheets that sheet across the lanes before sinking away again, leaving the air smelling of wet bark and crushed herbs. Small stone terraces, worn smooth by generations of feet, step up the slope; their edges are already being reclaimed by mosses and creeping succulents that soften every corner into a rounded, organic form. Nothing looks newly built; everything looks as though it has belonged in its exact shape for a very long time.

Insects fill every layer of space: iridescent beetles the size of thumbs crawl along the vine walls, clouds of midges hover in the shafts of light between leaves, and larger, louder cicada-like creatures cling to the highest arches, their calls arriving in rolling waves that overlap into a continuous roar. Birds no bigger than sparrows dart through the latticework, their wings brushing past ears. Larger animals—slender, quick-furred climbers—move across the roofs and through the upper canopy, occasionally shaking loose a brief shower of droplets that patters onto the lower levels.

The people who live here move through this green architecture with practiced economy. Their tools are simple: curved stone knives, bone needles, lengths of braided fiber. They cut narrow vents in the living walls when a chamber grows too close and damp, or splice new runners across a sagging span. Because the whole settlement is small—barely a hundred paces across at its widest—the activity feels constant and close; voices, footsteps, and the steady background of insects and dripping water keep the air filled with sound. The cool temperature prevents the moisture from turning oppressive, so the place remains breathable even when the foliage is thickest and the lanes are busiest. Water is never far: it condenses on every surface at night, runs in thin streams after midday rain, and pools in shallow stone basins placed where the drip is heaviest. The hamlet feels less like a clearing carved from wilderness and more like a single organism that has learned to house its own people inside itself.
