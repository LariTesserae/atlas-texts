A low basin lies cupped within a ring of low rises, the kind of depression that once held seasonal flow but now holds nothing at all. The floor is a single, unbroken sheet of mineral crust, pale and faintly crystalline where it has dried into shallow polygons. Over centuries the surface has been planed smooth, edges rounded, any once-sharp fissures softened into subtle troughs that lead nowhere. Scattered across this plain are small, woody tufts no taller than a hand’s span—pale, almost colorless plants whose leaves lie pressed flat against the crust, drawing what little moisture remains from condensing night air before the warmth returns. They stand far apart, each one claiming its own quiet interval of ground, nothing growing between them.

The air is warm and perfectly still, carrying a faint mineral tang that never changes. Light does not arrive; what little reaches the basin is filtered and thinned to a permanent twilight, turning the crust to dull pewter and the sparse plants to darker silhouettes against it. No wind moves across the open space. No insects click or stir. The only sound, if sound exists at all, is the occasional, almost imperceptible settling of a single grain of salt within the crust, a noise smaller than breath.

Boundaries are close: the encircling rises are low enough to walk over in minutes, yet high enough to keep the basin isolated from any farther horizon. Within them the ground remains level, the temperature constant, the thin vegetation neither advancing nor retreating. Time has settled here into a single, unbroken state—warm, dry, dark, and silent—where nothing is added and nothing is taken away.
