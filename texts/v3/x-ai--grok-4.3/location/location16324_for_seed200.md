The basin stretches outward in low, unbroken swells, a region too wide to cross in a single day yet never rising enough to break the horizon. Everything here feels half-assembled. The ground is a soft, dark substrate still knitting itself together—layers of fine sediment and mineral ooze that have not yet hardened into proper stone. Fresh extrusions of pale clay and glassy rock shoulder upward in slow blisters, their surfaces still slick and unweathered, as though the world has only just decided what shape this place should take and is still testing the edges.

Moisture never leaves. It beads constantly on every curve and slope, trickling in thin, persistent threads that gather into shallow, tea-colored pools no deeper than a footprint. The air itself is saturated; warm vapor rises in slow, visible curls even when nothing stirs, and distant rock faces appear to weep in long, vertical streaks that refresh as quickly as they evaporate. The heat is constant and close, a heavy press that never lifts or sharpens. It keeps the moisture suspended rather than letting it rise away, so the entire basin sits inside a perpetual, warm fog that thins only slightly above head height before thickening again among the leaves.

Vegetation crowds every surface that will hold it. Broad, rubbery fronds unroll from the newer clays in dense stands, their undersides lined with fine root-hairs that drink directly from the condensing air. Vines as thick as wrists lace between them, forming layered canopies that filter the already weak daylight into a permanent greenish dusk. Nothing here grows in single specimens; each form repeats in thousands, overlapping until the eye loses count. The result is a single, continuous mass of living matter that begins at the damp ground and continues upward in unbroken tiers, each level pressing against the next.

Life moves through every layer in overwhelming numbers. Small, slick amphibians with translucent skins rest in the lower pools, while larger, many-legged forms crawl along the vines above them. Clouds of insects—some no larger than dust, others the size of a palm—hang in shifting columns that twist through the dim air without ever dispersing. Stranger presences appear at the edges of vision: segmented bodies that glow a faint, steady orange when they move, or clusters of pale, jointed arms that reach from the thicker foliage and retract again. None of these forms change. The populations neither surge nor collapse; the same number of calls, the same density of movement, the same quiet feeding continues at every hour.

Sound accumulates rather than rises or falls. The steady drip of water from leaf tips, the low, continuous murmur of countless small bodies shifting, the dry rustle of fronds against one another, and the overlapping chorus of throat-sacs and wing-stridulation together produce a single, constant volume that fills the basin without peaks or pauses. Because the conditions never vary, the sound itself becomes part of the stillness—an unchanging pressure that defines the place as surely as the heat or the moisture.

In this equilibrium the raw geology continues its patient emergence beneath the living tangle. Each new blister of clay or glassy extrusion is already clothed in rootlets before it has fully cooled, so the distinction between what is still forming and what is already inhabited remains impossible to draw. The basin simply holds itself in place, complete in its incompleteness, while every element—water, plant, animal, stone—remains exactly as it is, without beginning or end to the arrangement.
