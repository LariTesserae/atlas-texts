The world is a single unbroken surface of poured and riveted matter—alloys, sintered stone, and translucent mineral sheets—laid down across an entire planet in one continuous act of manufacture. There are no seams between “ground” and “building”; every ridge and basin was formed by the same casting process, so the horizon is simply the farthest limit of the same material continuing until curvature takes it away. Because the surface was completed and then locked into equilibrium, the edges of every extrusion remain knife-sharp: cooling fins still end in perfect right angles, conduit housings still show the crisp draft angles of their molds, and the vast, shallow basins hold water in geometrically precise pools whose rims have never rounded.

Water is everywhere but never flowing. It condenses in slow, persistent droplets on the undersides of overhanging beams and drips, once per hour, into catchment channels that never overflow. Thin films slide along invisible thermal gradients across vertical faces, gathering into pendant beads that catch the ordinary daylight and shine like brief lenses before they fall. The air itself is saturated; a person moving through it feels the constant brush of micro-droplets on the skin and a faint, uniform warmth that never quite becomes oppressive.

Nothing grows. In place of plants there are only the native crystallites of the construction itself—slowly lengthening needles of doped silica that push outward from older surfaces, recording the passage of centuries in annual layers only a few micrometers thick. Between these needles move the fauna: countless small, jointed mechanisms whose bodies are made of the same materials as the world they inhabit. They do not eat or breed in any organic sense; they simply exchange worn modules at standardized interface ports set flush into the ground. At any moment the surface is thick with them—crawling over one another, stacking, handing components along in long, patient chains—yet the only sounds are the faintest clicks of ceramic on ceramic and the almost subsonic hum of idling motors. The quiet is so complete that the regular, spaced impacts of condensing drops become the loudest event in the acoustic field.

Because the entire construct exists in a single, time-invariant state, nothing ever arrives or departs. The mechanisms perform the same micro-adjustments they performed on the first day; the water films advance and retreat along the same molecular tracks; the crystallites lengthen at their fixed rate. The vastness is therefore experienced less as distance and more as repetition: walk in any direction and the same geometries, the same patient crowds of mechanisms, the same patient condensation repeat without variation, an industrial plain that is also an ocean of warm, motionless weather.
