The expanse stretches in every direction as a single plane of fractured mineral, its surface neither sand nor stone but a lattice of pale, interlocking plates that catch what little light exists. Farther out, the ground tilts upward without transition into sheer escarpments whose summits are lost in the upper dark; some of these walls rise several thousand body-lengths in a single unbroken face before they splinter into narrower needles that continue ascending until they are only suggestions against the black. Between the needles lie shallow basins still in the process of taking shape; their floors are cross-hatched by hairline ridges that lengthen and rejoin while an observer watches, as though an interior pressure is still deciding the final map.

No moisture settles on any surface. The air itself is warm and thin, carrying only the faint metallic scent of heated silicates and trace halogens. Occasional veils of dry vapor rise from narrow vents along the basin rims; the vapor drifts a short distance, cools, and condenses into fresh crystalline dust that sifts down and is immediately incorporated into the growing ridges. Nothing remains long enough to be called sediment. The process repeats at irregular intervals, so that the terrain is never still yet produces almost no sound beyond the soft, dry tick of new facets locking into place.

Scattered across the plane and halfway up the lower slopes are low, angular heaps that resemble collapsed walls or the foundations of vanished buildings. Closer inspection shows them to be the same mineral as the surrounding ground, only grown thicker and more regularly jointed; some retain faint internal layering that once corresponded to older, now-buried surfaces. These structures do not accumulate. When a needle overhead fractures, the falling shards strike an existing heap and the entire mass shifts, re-crystallizes along new planes, and settles again into a slightly altered outline. Over longer spans the heaps migrate a few paces, following the slow expansion of the basin ridges beneath them.

Above the warm plane the dark remains absolute except for a single diffuse glow that hangs at shoulder height wherever one stands. It is not a sky but a suspended stratum of ionized dust, too tenuous to illuminate anything beyond a few paces yet sufficient to give the mineral edges a faint internal sheen. The light never brightens or fades according to any visible rhythm; it simply persists, a constant reference against which the shifting geometry below registers as motion rather than shadow.

No living thing moves through the space. The only changes are mechanical: a spire collapsing somewhere to the north sends a low, prolonged vibration through the lattice that fades after several minutes; a new vent opens in a basin floor, jets a brief plume of vapor, and seals itself again within the hour. Each event alters the local arrangement without leaving a scar. The landscape continues to assemble itself from within, warm, dry, and nearly silent, its extreme relief still deciding what final form it will allow to stand.
