The chamber is low-ceilinged and entirely assembled, its boundaries no farther than the reach of a thrown stone. Every surface—floor, walls, low vaults—consists of thin ceramic slabs fitted edge-to-edge by hand and sealed with a pale resin that still carries the faint smell of its firing. The slabs meet at crisp right angles, their corners knife-sharp because nothing has yet worn them. Between the slabs run narrow channels packed with crushed mineral, still faintly warm from whatever slow exothermic process keeps the air dry and the temperature steady.

From these channels sprout dense, living ropes of matter that are neither plant nor fungus. They are white and ochre, fibrous, branching in flat, overlapping fans that press against one another until hardly a finger-width of space remains between them. Light from unseen apertures high in the vaults falls through them in soft, broken shafts, revealing that each fan is actually a lattice of tiny hollow tubes whose walls are translucent enough to show slow circulation of a honey-colored fluid that never evaporates. The fans grow upward and outward until they meet the opposite surface; the result is a layered, breathing thicket that fills the chamber from floor almost to ceiling in most places, leaving only narrow, winding lanes where a person might push through shoulder-first.

Small, slow creatures inhabit the lattice. Most are no larger than a thumb and resemble segmented velvet worms with crystalline bristles. They move by rippling contractions that take minutes to cross a single fan, pausing for long intervals to press their bodies against the tubes and sip the circulating fluid. Larger forms, perhaps the size of a cat, drift through the thicker groves; their bodies are flattened, translucent membranes stretched over jointed frames that click softly against the ceramic when they settle. They do not hurry. Their passage bends the fibrous fans aside and lets them spring back, the motion visible long after the creature has stopped.

Because the entire place is fabricated, nothing beneath the slabs is soil or rock; the structure rests on a single, continuous foundation layer whose seams are invisible. The foundation itself flexes imperceptibly over the course of hours, producing a faint settling sound like distant parchment being smoothed. In places the flex reveals fine hairline gaps through which the mineral dust below can be seen glowing with its own faint internal light. At longer intervals—perhaps across a full day—the gaps widen enough that one can glimpse inverted copies of the chamber beneath, identical yet oriented differently, before the foundation reseals and the vision is lost. These moments pass without noise and leave the fibrous growths undisturbed, as though the lapse had occurred elsewhere.

The air is still and warm, carrying only the faint mineral tang of the resin. Sound travels poorly through the dense lattice; the only consistent noises are the soft, arrhythmic ticking of bristles against ceramic and the occasional dry exhalation when one of the larger membrane creatures adjusts its position. All change arrives gradually: the advance of a worm across a fan, the widening of a foundation seam, the slow deepening of the honey color inside the tubes as light angle shifts. Nothing arrives suddenly, yet nothing remains quite where it began.
