The Verdant Expanse is not a surface but a suspension: a planetary lattice of living architecture erected so long ago that its builders’ names have dissolved into the cellulose. Everything here is plant, yet the plants do not crowd; they frame colossal voids whose diameters can be walked for days without touching a single stem. At this height the air thins, but the canopy’s own breath keeps the temperature warm enough that mist forms and lingers rather than falling away.  

The lattice itself is woven from braided lianas thicker than city avenues, their fibers still showing the blunt notches of hand-cut stone adzes. Where two strands cross, the living wood has fused into seamless vaults and terraces whose grain records centuries of patient adjustment rather than sudden growth. Water does not pour; it weeps. Every curve collects condensate that travels along hidden conduits inside the stems and emerges hours later as single, deliberate droplets that fall the height of several trees before striking the next level or evaporating mid-descent. The sound of that impact is the loudest event most inhabitants ever hear.  

Light arrives in slow gradients. Above the uppermost mesh the sky is a permanent dusk; below, illumination filters through successive translucent leaves until it becomes a dim, chlorophyll-gold haze that makes every distant form appear to float. In the deepest voids the haze sometimes fractures. A traveler walking a suspended causeway may glance sideways and see, for the space of a breath, an entirely different lattice—perhaps one still under construction, or one already collapsing—superimposed upon the present. These intrusions last only until the next droplet falls.  

Fauna moves inside the emptiness rather than among the plants. Armored moths whose wing membranes are thin enough to transmit the haze drift on the faint currents between strands; colonies of long-limbed gliders pass in perfect silence, their membranes catching the same updrafts that carry seed. Larger creatures—slow, heat-shaped herbivores whose bodies are plated with overlapping leaf-scutes—graze by extending tongues into the vascular bundles of the lattice, then retracting without ever leaving the open air. Their passage registers only as a slight pressure change felt through the soles of the feet.  

Because the structure is both ancient and actively maintained, erosion has reached equilibrium: frayed fibers are re-woven by smaller, specialized growths that appear exactly where tension has begun to slacken. The same equilibrium governs the larger weather of the lattice. Entire districts exhale together on a cycle measured in days, producing continent-scale updrafts that roll through the voids like visible rivers of warmer mist. When two such rivers meet, reality sometimes thins further; droplets halt in mid-air, refract light into brief, localized rainbows whose colors do not belong to the local spectrum, then resume their fall as though nothing has occurred.  

Nothing is wasted. Even the voids are cultivated; certain openings are left deliberately empty so that the next generation of creeper-strands can be trained across them with ropes and stone weights, a process measured in generations. The builders’ descendants still move along the edges of these openings with the same stone tools, marking each new splice with a single silent notch that will be absorbed into the grain within a century.  

The overall impression is of an inhabited absence: a warm, dim, high-altitude greenhouse whose true mass is mostly air, whose inhabitants speak—if they speak at all—in pressures and pauses rather than sound, and whose living walls occasionally remember other versions of themselves before forgetting again.
