High on a wind-scoured shoulder of ancient stone, the land gathers itself into a shallow basin no larger than a cathedral nave, its rim defined by long-weathered granite that has folded into low, rounded bulwarks. The rock itself weeps without haste. Clear droplets gather along hairline fissures, swell, and fall at intervals measured in heartbeats rather than seconds, each fall leaving a faint silver track that the next droplet will follow. Because the elevation is high, the air remains warm enough that the moisture does not freeze; instead it lingers as a fine, breath-warm film across every surface.

Between the stones the vegetation has taken sparse hold—broad, leathery fronds of a deep celadon hue rise singly or in loose pairs, each plant separated by several body-lengths of bare, glistening rock. The leaves themselves are thick and slightly translucent; faint internal veins pulse with an almost imperceptible rhythm, so slow that the movement registers only as a change in how light passes through them when viewed over many minutes. Water condenses along their undersides and drips into shallow, perfectly circular depressions worn into the stone beneath, depressions that never overflow because the ground absorbs the moisture at the same gradual rate it arrives.

The light is ordinary daylight, yet the basin’s scale and the constant film of water turn it into something quieter and more interior. Sunlight arrives already softened, filtered through a low, stationary vapor that refuses to lift or disperse. No wind stirs; the only motion is the slow migration of droplets along curved stone and the even slower opening or closing of the occasional leaf. In the resulting silence the sound of condensation becomes audible: a faint, recurring plink that arrives from different directions at different moments, never overlapping into a continuous trickle.

Nothing moves quickly here. The erosion that shaped the basin has long since reached equilibrium; every edge that could be sharpened has been rounded, every hollow that could hold water has been calibrated so that it releases its burden at the same rate the sky returns it. The absence of animal life leaves the space strangely clean of scent and disturbance; only the faint mineral tang of wet stone and the green sharpness of the leaves remain. Over hours or days the fronds shift their angles by degrees, the droplets trace new paths along the rock, and the basin continues its patient exchange of water and warmth, unaltered by urgency or arrival.
