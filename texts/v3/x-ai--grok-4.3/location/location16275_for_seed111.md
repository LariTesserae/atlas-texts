A narrow cleft splits the flank of a newborn peak, walls rising sheer for hundreds of body-lengths before pinching together overhead. The rock is still raw, its facets knife-edged and pale where it fractured only decades earlier; no weathering has yet rounded a single corner. Water arrives not as rain but as slow condensation that beads on every upward-facing surface, then slides in crooked threads until it gathers in shallow, stone-rimmed pans no wider than a palm. These transient pools mirror whatever faint light leaks from the cleft’s distant mouth, then evaporate again within hours, leaving behind faint mineral rings.

Vegetation is limited to tight, pale crusts that hug the driest seams—lichen-like films whose colors shift from chalk to bruised violet depending on how long they have been without moisture. They never form continuous cover; instead they appear wherever a ledge offers a few hand-widths of stability.

Deeper inside, the cleft widens just enough to allow a single, continuous column of cooler air to descend. Light fails quickly. What remains is a diffuse gloom in which shapes are sensed more by temperature than by sight: warmer exhalations rising from the living mass below, colder drafts sliding down the stone. In this half-darkness the walls themselves seem to move.

The movement belongs to layered congregations of soft-bodied organisms—segmented, many-limbed, translucent—clinging in dense mats that coat every available vertical plane. Each individual is no larger than a finger, yet they press against one another so closely that the entire surface appears to be a single slow-breathing skin. They shift position only once every few hours, a glacial rearrangement that nevertheless produces constant, overlapping sound: a low, wet scraping amplified by the stone into a continuous, rolling murmur that occasionally surges into sharper clicks when one creature’s grip fails and it tumbles a short distance before reattaching. The volume never drops below a steady roar, yet the individual motions remain imperceptibly gradual.

No tools or traces of hands exist here. The cleft has only just opened; whatever arrives does so on its own terms. The temperature stays just above freezing, enough to keep the condensation liquid and the organisms pliable but never warm. Time passes in the slow widening of mineral rings around the evaporating pools, in the occasional loosening of a single grip somewhere high on the wall, and in the patient redistribution of the living carpet that will eventually cover every surface before the mountain itself begins to erode.
