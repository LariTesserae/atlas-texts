The chamber is not large in footprint—perhaps a hundred paces across at its widest—but it feels vaster because every surface has been claimed and re-claimed by growth. The ceiling, once rough basalt, now sags under layered mats of root and mycelium that have fused into a single living membrane; pale, vein-like tubes pulse faintly along its underside, carrying water and sap. From that membrane, long aerial roots descend like slow rain, never quite touching the floor. They stop a few hand-widths short, trembling, then drink from the air itself.

Between the roots the space is strangely open. The floor is not soil but a patchwork of worn industrial grating, half-swallowed by moss that has hardened into something like cork. Wide lanes of this moss-grating remain clear, as if the plants themselves agreed to leave corridors for movement. In those corridors stand the machines: squat, riveted presses and belt-driven pumps that throb with a low, wet rhythm. Their casings are no longer metal alone; bark has grown over the housings in spiraled patterns, and the bark itself has been drilled and bolted so that pistons can still slide. Steam leaks from seams where living vessels meet brass, carrying the smell of warm resin and hot oil.

Water moves everywhere in small, deliberate ways. Condensation gathers on the high membrane and falls in sudden, heavy drops that spatter across the grating. Some drops collect in shallow basins where the old floor has sagged; others run along deliberately cut channels in the bark-metal, feeding the roots. The air is warm and still, yet never stale; the constant slow dripping and the steady exhalation of the pumps keep it moving.

Fauna fills the emptiness. Pale, many-legged creatures the size of forearms scuttle along the undersides of the roots, their bodies faintly luminous. Larger things—broad-winged, leathery moths with six eyes—drift between the hanging roots, wings rasping like dry leaves. Smaller, quicker forms swarm the warm surfaces of the machines, licking oil and sap. Their collective motion produces a constant, soft percussion: thousands of tiny feet, wingbeats, mouthparts clicking against metal and bark. Over it all the machines add a deeper, arrhythmic thumping, and the roots themselves make a slow, stretching creak when they shift to catch a falling drop.

Nothing here feels finished, yet nothing feels new. The plants have grown into the shape of the settlement rather than against it; the settlement has settled into the shape of the plants. The result is a single, slow-moving system that nevertheless never stops moving: roots lengthen by finger-widths each hour, pistons cycle, droplets fall, wings rasp, and the warm, dim air carries every sound a little farther than it should.
