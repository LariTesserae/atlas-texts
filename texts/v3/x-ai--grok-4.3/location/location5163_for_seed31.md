The landscape is a high, open basin whose edges are still raw. Rock faces stand sharp and unsoftened, newly uplifted in some slow crustal heave that left clean fractures and narrow ridges. Water gathers along every fracture line; it beads on pale stone, collects in shallow ledges, and seeps downward until it meets the gentler floor of the basin. There the ground is never fully dry. Darker patches of saturated earth mark former channels that no longer wander; they have settled into permanent, shallow sheets that reflect the sky without ripple.

Between these sheets the vegetation rises in isolated stands: broad-leaved shrubs whose foliage is dense and glossy, low thickets of plumed grasses, and occasional solitary trees whose trunks are pale and straight. Nothing crowds; each cluster is separated by wide stretches of open, damp ground or bare stone. The plants thrive yet remain scattered, as though the same equilibrium that holds the basin still also limits how many can root in any single place.

A settlement occupies the widest portion of the basin floor. Its structures are modest, built by hand from the same pale stone that forms the ridges and from heavy wooden beams cut with simple adzes. Walls rise in careful layers, some terraces stepping upward along the gentler slopes, others forming low, square compounds whose flat roofs collect thin films of water that later drip from the eaves. Paths of fitted stone connect the buildings, yet the distances between them are generous; courtyards open onto stretches of untouched marsh grass or naked rock. No metal glints; thresholds are smoothed timber, doors hang on leather hinges, and rain channels are lined with baked clay pipes laid by hand.

The air stays warm and close. Mist gathers where water meets stone and lingers in faint veils that never quite lift or thicken. Light arrives evenly, without strong shadows or sudden brightness, as though the basin lies beneath a permanent, neutral sky. Sound is minimal: the soft, constant drip of seepage from ledges, the muted splash when a drop strikes standing water, and occasionally the low rustle of leaves when a warmer current moves across the basin. Nothing else stirs. No birds cross the open space, no insects disturb the surface of the pools. The vegetation holds its lush shape; the buildings remain exactly as they were placed. The entire basin exists in a fixed balance in which water, stone, and plant maintain one another without increase or diminishment, and the small city of hand-shaped stone continues its quiet tenancy among them.
