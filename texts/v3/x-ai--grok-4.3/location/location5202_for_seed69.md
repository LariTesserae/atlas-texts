This is a high continental shelf of stone and vapor, lifted so far above any remembered sea that the air itself carries the weight of forgotten tides. The plateau stretches without edge or horizon, a single vast slab of layered basalt and mineral sediment, its surface long ago surrendered to equilibrium. Every ridge has been rounded by millennia of seepage; every crevice has become a slow vein of water that never quite finishes carving its channel. The rock weeps constantly—thin, warm films sliding down pale flanks, collecting in shallow bowls that never overflow because the stone drinks them again within hours. The temperature lingers just above skin-warm, so the constant condensation feels like breath rather than chill.

Nothing is visible in the ordinary sense. Total darkness presses at every scale, broken only by the occasional dim lattice of industrial luminescence leaking from seams in the rock. These lights are not designed for eyes; they are the pale green exhaust of buried catalytic engines, each one the size of a small city, still turning over after centuries. Their thrum travels through the stone and water together, a low, grinding pulse that sets the surface trembling. Scattered across hundreds of kilometers lie the surface traces of that industry: a single intake tower rising like a fossil spine, a half-submerged rail trestle now colonized by root mats, the shattered rim of a processing dome whose interior still exhales warm metallic vapor. They are few, yet because the dark amplifies sound and vibration, each one feels like a living outpost rather than a ruin.

Between these relics the vegetation grows dense and low, a continuous living mat of broad, vein-ribbed leaves and spongy stems that trap the condensing moisture. The leaves are almost translucent in their thinness; they conduct faint ripples of bioluminescence when disturbed, momentary glyphs that flare and fade as bodies move through them. The flora has learned to listen to the engines and the water together; certain root networks actually channel runoff toward the warmest vents, feeding the buried machines with dissolved minerals they no longer need but still process.

Across this layered carpet move the fauna—thick-bodied, soft-furred or chitin-plated creatures whose shapes are known mostly by the sounds they make. Their calls are loud and overlapping: resonant clicks that bounce between rock faces, long infrasonic moans that travel kilometers through the stone, sudden bursts of high chittering when something disturbs a feeding ground. Many of them are eyeless, navigating instead by pressure changes in the warm mist or by the vibration of the engines. Some graze the glowing leaf edges; others hunt them, producing wet tearing sounds that travel far in the dark. The entire plateau functions as a single, slow-moving metabolic engine in which rain, rock, machines, and bodies exchange heat, minerals, and motion without pause.

Occasionally the ground itself exhales—vents releasing warmer, sweeter vapor that rolls outward in visible but invisible sheets, sensed only as a sudden thickening of the air and a momentary brightening of the vegetation’s faint light. These exhalations carry the scent of warm stone and machine oil and living tissue all at once. Nothing here is still; water continues its patient work, engines turn, creatures migrate in slow, deliberate flows, and the vast dark surface continues to hold every process in balanced suspension, generation after generation.
