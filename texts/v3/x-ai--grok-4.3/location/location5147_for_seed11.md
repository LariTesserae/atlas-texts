The plateau stretches for days in every direction, an immense table of stone that once held entire cordilleras and now holds only their ghosts. Time and wind have planed the peaks until only a few twisted remnants remain—isolated horns of rock, fluted and undercut, leaning like broken teeth against a sky that never quite clears. These monoliths are the only vertical punctuation in a landscape otherwise reduced to gentle, interminable swells of pale, pocked stone.

Between the swells lie long, shallow depressions where moisture gathers. Condensation beads each dawn on the undersides of leaves, drips into thread-thin rivulets, and collects in stone basins no larger than a body. Occasional thin rains pass through like veils, soaking the ground for an hour before the wind reclaims the water. In these pockets the vegetation riots: dense, knee-high thickets of broad, waxy leaves veined with luminous blue, their surfaces perpetually beaded. Vines as thick as wrists climb the bases of the stone remnants and then stop abruptly, as if the air above a certain height refuses to nourish them. The foliage trembles continuously, not from any breeze that can be felt at ground level, but from the low, constant vibration that travels through the rock itself.

That vibration is the voice of the plateau. Winds funnel across the open distances at speeds that never drop below a roar. They find every hollow flute and overhanging lip in the ancient stone, turning the entire landscape into a single colossal instrument. The sound is not one note but a layered, bruising chord—deep chest-thuds from the largest arches, shrill metallic whines from knife-edge ridges, a thousand overlapping hisses and groans. Conversations are impossible; even thought feels shouted down. The same winds scour the stone into powder-fine dust that rises in transient ghosts, only to fall again when the gust shifts.

Nothing moves across the visible distances except those dust ghosts and the occasional low ripple through the leaf-mats. Animal life is present but hidden: small, pale amphibians that press themselves into the condensation basins, their skins matching the exact gray-green of the stone lips. They emerge only in the brief intervals when the wind momentarily drops, then vanish again. Larger traces—wide, three-toed prints—sometimes appear overnight in the softer dust, yet the creatures that made them are never seen.

Scattered across this emptiness are the remnants of earlier hands: low rings of unmortared stone, half-buried under leaf mold; a single obsidian blade still lodged upright in a crack, its edge undulled; shallow petroglyphs whose spirals have been sanded nearly flat. No pattern connects them. They simply appear at irregular intervals, as though left by people who crossed the plateau once and never returned.

The light is ordinary daylight, filtered through high, fast-moving vapor that never quite becomes cloud. Shadows are short and hard, yet they slide across the ground at unnatural speeds because the wind keeps the haze in constant motion. Time feels both frozen and violently accelerated: the stone itself is older than language, yet the surface is never still. Dust devils the height of a person spin up and collapse within seconds. Whole mats of vegetation lift at their edges like skirts, then settle again. The plateau is simultaneously the most static and the most unsettled place imaginable—an emptiness that refuses to stay empty for more than a few heartbeats at a time.
