The high plateau stretches without boundary in every direction, a single coherent skin of wind-burnished mineral that has forgotten what sharp relief ever felt like. Its surface is cool to the touch, pale and faintly iridescent, the final record of eons of abrasion that have left only the gentlest undulations—like the memory of waves frozen in salt. No liquid exists here; the chemistry of the world moves instead through drifting aerosols of finely divided silicates and metal vapors that the air itself carries like breath.

Across this immensity the living mass stands sparse. Every few kilometers, one of the great phytarchs rises, a tower of living crystal lattice grown from the plateau itself. Their trunks are translucent columns ridged with hexagonal pores; between these pores run slow internal currents of mineral slurry that never quite solidifies. The phytarchs are the only architecture. Their branches meet neighboring towers in airy catenaries of the same glassy tissue, forming irregular, incomplete urban canopies. The spaces between remain almost empty—just a thin, unbroken film of dormant microbial mat that clings to the mineral like frost.

Light arrives filtered and low, a perpetual dusk that turns the lattices into dim stained-glass lanterns. Inside each translucent column, faint pulses of bioluminescence travel as data or sap, marking metabolic rhythms too slow for a human eye yet perfectly legible to the fauna that navigate them. The temperature stays fixed in a dry, steady coolness that would feel like early autumn if autumn had no memory of moisture.

Wind is constant. It threads through the millions of precisely spaced pores in every phytarch, producing a layered chord that rises and falls across kilometers: low drones from the main trunks, glassy whistles from the high branches, subsonic pulses from chambers deeper underground. When the aerosols thicken, the same currents set up sympathetic vibrations that make whole towers hum in slow interference patterns. Over these engineered resonances layer the calls of the abundant fauna—creatures whose exoskeletons are themselves grown from the same mineral lattice. They glide between towers on translucent vanes, land in swarms that clatter like falling gravel, and burrow through the living walls to feed on the moving slurry inside. Their movements are constant but not frantic; they are simply the visible metabolism of an ecosystem whose every participant is composed of the same dry, resonant substrate.

Growth and erosion have reached an equilibrium older than any geological epoch. The phytarchs extend by microns each cycle, guided by nanoscale templates laid down when the plateau was still young; at the same time they shed microscopic flakes that the winds carry back to the surface film, where new nuclei begin. Nothing here is finished, yet nothing feels urgent. The plateau continues, cool and resonant, its living architecture spaced like the piers of an unfinished bridge whose deck is the wind itself.
