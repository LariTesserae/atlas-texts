The ridgeline stretches for miles, a raw shoulder of stone that looks as though the planet has only recently remembered to push it upward. Its flanks are still shedding their birth-slick of powdered quartz and mica; pale new gullies weep thin threads of water that bead and vanish before they can reach the valley floor. Condensation gathers on every exposed surface, turning the rock dark in patches and feeding small, improbable gardens of moss and fern that cling wherever a ledge offers an extra hand’s breadth of soil. Between these bright green interruptions the stone remains naked, its fractures still sharp, its bedding planes still announcing themselves with the slow patience of something that has not yet been told how to erode.

Across this half-formed geology an industrial city has been grafted rather than built. Iron scaffolds follow the ridge’s spine like stitches, their rivets still bright because the weather has not had time to stain them. Foundry sheds perch on cantilevered platforms that jut from the cliff; their chimneys exhale not smoke but slow, cool vapor that mingles with the perpetual low fog. Mills turn on gears the size of houses, yet the motion is almost imperceptible—teeth meshing at the rate of one rotation per hour, their teeth polished by months of contact rather than speed. Power arrives through thick copper braids strung between pylons that themselves look half-submerged in the rock, as if the mountain were still deciding whether to accept them.

Fauna moves through the gaps. Slender, six-legged grazers with wet-looking pelts browse the scattered fern ledges; their hooves click against fresh stone in patient, spaced rhythms. Larger, slower creatures—something between beetle and turbine—cling to the undersides of the platforms, their carapaces vibrating in sympathy with the mills. At night the air fills with the soft, constant ticking of membranous wings: airborne larvae that hatch in the condensation films and drift between the buildings like luminous dust. Their collective hum stays just below the threshold of words, yet it never quite fades.

Light is scarce. The sun, when it appears at all, is filtered by a permanent veil of high mist and the city’s own exhalations, so evenings never fully relinquish their grip. Lanterns burn low and blue along the walkways, their glow swallowed quickly by the surrounding air. Reality itself behaves as if it is still drying: sometimes a length of railing will cast two shadows at different angles; sometimes the sound of a distant gear will arrive seconds before its vibration is felt through the soles. These fractures are small and localized, like blisters in wet paint. They linger, then mend themselves over the course of hours or days, leaving only a faint metallic aftertaste in the mouth.

Sound travels far in this thin, cool atmosphere. The deep, deliberate groaning of the ridge settling is the bass line; above it rise the slower, rhythmic sighs of the mills and the high, intermittent calls of the flying larvae. Occasional sharp reports echo when a new fracture opens in the stone, yet even these cracks feel measured, as though the mountain is still negotiating its own shape rather than surrendering to weather. Rain arrives in brief, vertical curtains that soak nothing for long, then lift, leaving every surface jeweled and the vegetation momentarily luminous.

The city does not sprawl so much as accumulate, one platform locking into the next across successive ridges. Between them lie wide intervals of raw col, empty save for drifting mist and the occasional browsing herd. From any single vantage the settlement looks incomplete—scaffolding still threaded through half-finished towers, cranes poised mid-lift for days at a time—yet it functions. The industrial rhythm has adapted to the mountain’s own deliberate tempo; nothing here is rushed except the occasional collapse of a still-forming cliff, whose roar is already beginning to feed new pockets of damp soil for the next generation of ferns.
