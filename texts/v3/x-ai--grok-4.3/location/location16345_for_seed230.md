The hamlet clings to a narrow shelf of weathered basalt high on a cirque wall, three hundred paces from end to end, a single curving street of packed cinder and fitted stone. The buildings themselves are low, single-story, their walls raised from the same dark rock that forms the cliff behind them; over centuries the masons have learned to follow the natural fractures rather than cut against them, so the roofs sit flush with outcrops and the façades read as continuations of the mountain rather than additions to it. Rainwater gathers in shallow channels cut along the eaves and trickles into a sequence of stone basins that never overflow and never run dry; condensation beads nightly on the undersides of the slate lintels and drips softly into the same basins at dawn.

Between the houses the ground is bare and firm, darkened only by faint collars of gray-green lichen that follow the joints where wall meets paving. A few hardy rowan saplings have rooted in the deeper crevices, their trunks no thicker than a wrist and their crowns pruned by the steady katabatic wind that moves downslope every afternoon; otherwise the shelf is open, empty space stretching between thresholds. At this elevation the air remains cool even at midday, carrying the metallic scent of wet stone and distant snow. When the clouds part, the light arrives clear and uncolored, falling evenly across the street and into the open doorways so that interiors and exteriors share the same sober illumination.

Nothing stirs that has not always stirred. The same wooden shutters hang at the same angle; the same iron latch on the communal well has polished itself to a mirror on one side from generations of the same hand. Birds pass overhead but rarely land; once in a long while a small brown goat appears on the upper ledges, watches the empty street, and vanishes again. The only persistent sound is the measured drip of water into stone, a metronome so constant it has become the silence itself. Nothing arrives and nothing departs; the hamlet remains exactly as the mountain has allowed it to remain, balanced, weathered into equilibrium, a fixed point on the high rock.
