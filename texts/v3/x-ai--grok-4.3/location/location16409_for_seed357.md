The plateau hangs in the air like a single, unfinished thought. It rose only a few centuries ago—sharp new bone pushed up through older crust—so its edges still cut clean against the sky, ridges and buttresses too fresh to have slumped or rounded. Nothing here is stone or soil. Every surface is living tissue: layered mats of root-callus, translucent vascular walls, and braided vine scaffolds that have grown into one another until distinction between “plant” and “terrain” has dissolved. The whole land is one slow, continuous organism that decided to stand up.

Across distances measured in hours of walking, the tissue opens into wide, nearly empty basins. Between the occasional eruptive towers the ground is only a thin, trembling membrane stretched over airier chambers beneath; step too heavily and the floor answers with a low, wet drumbeat that rolls outward for hundreds of meters. Water is everywhere and nowhere at once. The atmosphere itself is slightly over-saturated; every morning a slow rain of condensation forms on the upper surfaces and runs downward in silver threads that collect in hanging bladders the size of houses. When one bladder bursts, the released fluid cuts new channels through living ridges before being reabsorbed elsewhere, so the plateau is perpetually rewriting its own drainage.

Heat rises from below. Deep fermentation chambers keep the interior temperature high enough that steam sometimes issues from vents in the membrane, carrying the sweet-acrid smell of cooking sugars. The same warmth drives constant internal motion: vascular pulses visible as slow bulges traveling along cable-thick veins, sudden fountains of new growth that stiffen within minutes into temporary arches or walls, and equally sudden withering collapses that fold whole sections into themselves. From a distance the plateau seems to breathe in ragged, arrhythmic waves.

Because no animals exist to prune or pollinate, the plants have become their own architects and their own demolition crews. Towers rise in braided spirals until their own weight triggers a cascade of programmed apoptosis; the fallen mass is digested and redirected into new spines elsewhere, sometimes kilometers away. The result is a shifting, half-made city of green and ochre and translucent rose, empty rooms opening and sealing, stair-like root terraces extending and retracting, ceilings of leaf-laminate that iris shut or dilate according to internal rhythms no outside observer can predict.

Sound carries far in the thin high air. The loudest notes come from the living structures themselves: deep hydraulic groans as pressure equalizes, sharp detonations when over-ripe seed chambers split and fling wet fiber in ropes, the continuous soft roar of sap moving through conduits the diameter of tree trunks. Wind crossing the open basins produces long, hollow whistles that modulate as the shapes below change, turning the plateau into a vast, restless instrument.

Light arrives ordinary and diffuse, softened by constant low mist yet still strong enough to cast pale shadows. Under that light the membrane floors sometimes become briefly translucent, revealing layered galleries of root and vessel far below, a second inverted landscape visible only for minutes before the surface clouds again with new growth. The whole place feels unfinished precisely because it is never finished; every hour remakes yesterday’s architecture into tomorrow’s ruins, and the horizon stays empty only because the next tower has not yet decided where to stand.
