The place is a sunken hollow no wider than a few long strides across, tucked into the flattened crown of an ancient highland ridge that has long since surrendered its peaks to time. The stone underfoot is pale and chalky, a single unbroken skin of mineral that was once mountain and is now only a suggestion of it—warm to the touch even in the dim air. Nothing liquid moves here. Instead, the ground is laced with fine white filaments and crusts, dry as bone yet somehow alive with slow chemistry. From these crusts rise dense thickets of low, stiff growths the color of dried sage and pale ochre: not leaves exactly, but layered mineral skins that have learned to catch what trace of vapor the wind sometimes carries. They crowd together, shoulder to shoulder, forming low canopies and walls until the hollow feels crowded with green that never drinks.

Among the growths stand the remains of habitation—simple stacked stones fitted by hand, low platforms and half-walls that once marked rooms and now lean against one another like old neighbors. The construction is modest, no higher than a person can reach, yet the hollow is saturated with it; every gap between the mineral thickets holds another ledge or corner, another place where someone once sat or stored or slept. The light is thin, filtered through a permanent haze of fine dust that never quite settles, so that everything is seen in half-tone and close detail. Sound does not travel far, yet there is no silence. A constant soft percussion rises from the growths themselves—small dry pods clicking, fibrous stems rasping against one another in the steady warm breeze that funnels down into the pocket. Beneath that runs an undercurrent of life too numerous to count: the high, needling whine of wings, the papery rustle of bodies moving through tight spaces, the sudden soft impacts of many small feet on stone. The air feels thick with motion even when nothing is visible.

Nothing here grows tall or spreads wide; every living thing stays pressed close to the mineral skin of the hollow. The same creatures that pollenate the crust-growths also gnaw at the stacked stones, smoothing their edges and carrying grit from one place to another. In the warm dimness the entire pocket pulses with this exchange—wind moving dust, insects moving pollen, stone being worn and rebuilt at the same slow rate until the boundary between what was made and what simply accumulated has blurred. The place feels both exhausted and inexhaustible, an old equilibrium that still holds its shape only because nothing any longer arrives to disturb it.
