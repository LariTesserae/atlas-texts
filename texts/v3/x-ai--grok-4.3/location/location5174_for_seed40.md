The plateau is not stone. It is a single seamless skin of pre-stressed alloy laid across an entire mountain range, every joint fused so cleanly that the seams have already begun to forget they were ever separate. From the high vantage of the central turbine spire, the fabrication extends in every direction until the flat horizon dissolves into the glare of perpetual noon. Nothing beneath the skin is permitted to show; every rivet, conduit, vent, and catwalk is itself a manufactured object, each layer pressed directly onto the one below until the entire landscape reads as one continuous, slightly resonant machine.

Water never arrives as rain. Instead it condenses in thread-thin films along the underside of every overhanging girder and slips downward until it pools inside the shallow collection pans that ring every vertical shaft. These pans brim and empty on cycles too slow for a single observer to notice, yet the level is never the same from one visit to the next. Where the pans overflow, the water follows grooved channels cut into the alloy itself; the channels are still sharp enough that droplets bead rather than spread, catching the bright light like rows of miniature lenses.

Vegetation is almost absent. Only a single species of pale, hair-fine lichen survives, growing exclusively inside the heat-exchange slots of the exhaust manifolds. It forms fragile mats no wider than a handspan, fed by the intermittent mist that rises when the cooling loops below cycle from cold to colder. The lichen does nothing useful. It simply persists.

Fauna is everywhere and almost silent. Small six-legged crawlers—matte-black, segmented, each the size of a thumb—move along every conduit and rail in slow, deliberate caravans. Their feet never quite lift; they glide on capillary films of condensation. Larger, kite-like forms drift between the upper gantries, their wing membranes stretched across carbon spines that flex only once every few minutes. When they turn, the shift registers as a pressure change rather than a sound. Occasionally one of the smaller crawlers stops, crystals form on its carapace, and it becomes part of the architecture until the next slow thermal pulse releases it again.

Everything feels newly assembled. Welds still carry the faint iridescence of recent fusion; edges remain crisp, untouched by wind or grit. Yet the structures are already showing impossible wear patterns—long, straight grooves that run across plates with no mechanical explanation, as though the material itself has decided to forget its original shape. In places the air thins without warning; a gantry that was fifty meters away is suddenly twice as distant, or the angle of a stairwell folds inward until it meets itself at the top and bottom simultaneously. These folds last between three and thirty heartbeats, then resolve with a soft, inward click that no instrument records.

Light never varies in intensity, only in quality. Direct sun strikes every surface at once, yet the alloy refuses to heat beyond cool-to-the-touch. Shadows therefore appear as hard geometric absences rather than gradients, and they persist long after the object casting them has moved on. Inside these persistent shadows the crawlers gather, their motion becoming even slower until they seem to fuse with the metal.

The only motion with any visible speed is the gradual realignment of the great overhead cranes. Every few hours one of the massive arms inches a few millimeters along its track, accompanied by the softest possible creak of lubricant being redistributed. The sound is so faint that it registers less as hearing and more as a change in the pressure inside the ears. Between these events the plateau holds itself in perfect, bright, layered stillness, waiting for its next infinitesimal adjustment.
