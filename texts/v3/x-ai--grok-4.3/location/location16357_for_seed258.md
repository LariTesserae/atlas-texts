High above the raw, still-forming world, the Great Canopy Plateau extends in every direction for many days’ walk. It is not a mountain so much as an unfinished platform of naked stone that has only just begun to remember how to be solid; its surface remains porous, unfinished, still knitting together beneath everything that covers it. Over this embryonic geology there is no bare rock left, no soil, no exposed substrate at all. The entire landscape is plant.

The plants are not growing on anything; they are the thing. Thick, pale-green trunks the width of towers rise at wide intervals, each one curving and branching in slow, unchanging parabolas that meet overhead to form vast, open vaults. Between these vaults the space is generous, almost empty, giving the plateau its sparse character: long sight-lines of mist and air stretch between the living arches. Every surface of every trunk and branch constantly weeps clear water; droplets gather along vein-like ridges and fall with the steady, deliberate rhythm of a metronome. The sound is loud and constant, a layered drumming that fills the air, punctuated by deeper resonances as sheets of water slide down the inside curves of the larger vaults and strike shallow, plant-rimmed pools far below.

Because the entire system exists in perfect equilibrium, nothing ages, flowers, or decays in any measurable way. The same leaves remain tense and open, the same droplets form and fall, the same thickness of aerial roots hang motionless between structures. The temperature stays gently warm, enough to keep the atmosphere humid and the water near the surface from ever chilling into mist banks that would obscure the plateau. Light arrives as ordinary daylight filtered through translucent upper canopies, diffused and even, neither harsh nor dim.

The plants themselves are the only architecture. Walls are woven screens of living fiber, floors are springy mats of interlocking stems, ceilings are layered domes of leaves that shed water inward to feed the roots beneath. There are no tools or additions; every angle and span was shaped by the plants’ own growth habits long ago and has since locked into place. The only movement is the slow, continuous descent of water and the faint, steady vibration the trunks produce when sheets of falling water strike them, creating the low, pervasive drone that underlies all other sound.

Fauna is almost absent. The rare creatures that exist are small, translucent, and slow; they drift or cling among the aerial roots, drinking from the constant film of water and adding almost nothing to the acoustic or ecological texture. The dominant presence remains the plants: a single, continuous organism whose architecture is its body and whose unchanging state is its way of being permanent upon ground that has not yet finished becoming stone.
