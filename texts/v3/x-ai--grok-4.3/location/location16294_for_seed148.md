The place is a low, half-submerged industrial court no larger than a cathedral nave, its floor tilted just enough that condensation runs in slow threads toward a central depression that is already beginning to fill. Everything is plant. Great translucent leaves, veined like circuit boards, have grown up through the cracked concrete and fused with the steel frames of loading gantries, turning them into pale green buttresses. Between these columns hang immense empty volumes—cathedral-scale voids—where the air is cool, almost cold, and carries the faint sweet-metal scent of wet copper and crushed chlorophyll. The plants are not crowded; they are spaced like sparse architecture, each one a tower or arch or hanging gallery whose next frond has not yet decided where to reach.

The ground itself is still forming. Pale mineral rinds are precipitating along the edges of the concrete slabs, budding into new, soft stone that the plants immediately root into. Nothing is old here. The rust on the abandoned press machines is still bright orange, and the machines themselves are only half-swallowed; their flywheels turn one slow revolution every few minutes, driven by the contractile tension of vines that have grown through the gear housings like living belts. When a wheel completes its turn it clicks once, a sound so small it is absorbed by the surrounding darkness before it can travel three meters.

Light does not arrive from above. Instead, certain leaves have become faintly photoluminescent, their undersides giving off a steady, low green that outlines the edges of the voids without ever illuminating their centers. In those centers hang or perch the fauna: clusters of translucent, almost weightless rodents that roost by clinging to one another in mid-air, their tails braided together into living cables; pale salamanders no thicker than pencils that slide along the inner surfaces of the voids, grazing on films of condensation; and long, slow moths whose wings carry microscopic mineral grains they brush onto the new stone, accelerating its formation. All of them move with deliberate quiet, the hush broken only by the soft patter of droplets falling from leaf to leaf and the occasional distant sigh of a machine completing another cycle.

Space behaves strangely. A gantry twenty meters away may suddenly appear beside you if you look at it too long, its plant-wrapped railings now part of the same structure you are standing inside. The voids themselves expand and contract by a few centimeters between breaths, as though the whole court is inhaling. When the contraction is strongest, the new stone at the edges cracks with a sound like knuckles popping, and fresh water seeps upward, glinting for an instant before the darkness reclaims it. The plants lean toward these new seams, roots already deciding how the next chamber will be shaped.

Nothing here is finished, yet everything is already occupied. The industrial ghosts and the living architecture and the forming geology are still negotiating their final arrangement, and the animals, the condensation, and the slow turning of the living machines are the only witnesses to the terms.
