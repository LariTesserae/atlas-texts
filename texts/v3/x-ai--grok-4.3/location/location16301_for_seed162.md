The plateau rises in broad, unfinished planes, its surface still riven by the fractures of its own recent birth. Black glass and pale stone stand at steep angles that have not yet learned how to slope; knife-edged ridges catch the constant film of warm condensation that rolls down from the low, unmoving cloud deck. Moisture gathers in long, irregular seams where the rock splits, forming thread-thin rivulets that never quite become rivers. Between these seams the ground remains dry enough that fine mineral dust lingers in sheltered pockets, pale as ash.

Vegetation has taken every available surface. Thick, rubbery vines the color of bruised plum mat across the sharper faces, their leaves holding droplets that act as weak lenses. Beneath them, mosses and shelf fungi grow in stacked layers so dense they muffle footfalls into near silence. The air itself feels saturated; every breath carries the faint metallic sweetness of dissolving stone and the heavier green scent of the canopy. Light stays low and even, a perpetual half-light that never quite reaches the ground. What illumination exists comes from slow, internal pulses within certain clusters of fruiting bodies—dim coral flashes that flare once every several minutes and then fade, barely enough to mark a path.

The plateau is not empty. Creatures move through the layered growth in constant, quiet traffic. Many are small, segmented things that travel in synchronized columns along the vines; others are larger, slow-moving masses whose outlines shift as they pass behind or through curtains of translucent leaves. None announce themselves loudly. The only persistent sound is the soft, repeated tick of droplets striking the largest leaves, and beneath it a deeper, irregular vibration—like distant machinery turning over—but the source is never a single place. It seems to rise from the rock itself wherever fractures are widest.

In those fractures the ground behaves strangely. Veins of ore glow with low, furnace heat that never ignites the surrounding vegetation. The glow pulses in slow, rhythmic bursts that coincide with faint shifts in air pressure; when the pulse peaks, small motes of stone flake away and vanish before they reach the surface. Moments later the rock reforms, slightly rearranged. These micro-events repeat across hundreds of fissures at once, never loud enough to dominate the quiet, yet creating an impression of vast, distributed industry without any visible works. Between pulses the air warps. A ridge may appear ten paces farther away than it did a moment earlier, or a stand of vines may briefly display a second, transparent layer of leaves occupying the same space. The shifts are brief, random, and leave no permanent trace except a faint ozone smell that dissipates quickly in the warm damp.

Fauna respond to these moments by pausing or altering direction; they have learned the local grammar of instability. When the terrain settles again, motion resumes at the same unhurried pace. Water continues to bead and fall. The plateau continues its slow, unfinished self-assembly under a sky that never clears and never darkens further than its present dimness.
