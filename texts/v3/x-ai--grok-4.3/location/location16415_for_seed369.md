High on a narrow shoulder of stone, the mountain folds inward to form a single sheltered basin no wider than a modest grove. Long ages of slow erosion have rounded every edge: the enclosing cliffs lean gently outward, their faces polished to a matte sheen where water has traced the same paths for centuries. From fissures in the rock, clear threads seep and gather, weeping steadily down the walls to collect in shallow, stone-rimmed pools that never quite overflow. The air stays warm even at this height, a soft, steady warmth that keeps the moisture from chilling; instead it clings as a fine, perpetual mist that condenses on every surface and drips again.

Between the pools, vegetation grows in deliberate isolation. Enormous, fleshy-leaved plants—broad as doors yet spaced many strides apart—rise from cracks in the stone. Their fronds are thick and glossy, veined with pale luminous threads that catch the ordinary daylight and turn it faintly green. Roots trail across bare rock like slow-moving ropes, seeking the next seep without ever crowding one another. The emptiness between them feels spacious, almost ceremonial; nothing fills the gaps except the slow fall of droplets and the faint haze.

Sound fills the basin despite its emptiness. Each seep produces a distinct tone—some a low, resonant plink, others a higher, ringing tap—as drops strike stone or water. Because the walls curve, the notes multiply and linger, layering into a continuous, deliberate music that never rises to a clamor yet never falls silent. The rhythm is patient: a drop forms, gathers weight, falls, and the interval between falls can stretch long enough for mist to thicken again. Over years the pattern of sound shifts only as a new fissure opens or an old one seals with mineral skin.

Few creatures move here. Occasional small, translucent slugs traverse the undersides of the largest leaves, leaving glistening trails that dry to faint silver lines. Their passage is almost soundless beneath the water-music. The place holds no trace of tool or shelter; any hand that entered would find only rounded stone for anvils and the tough leaf-veins for cord, as though the basin itself already offers the simplest means of working.

Change arrives in long measures. A leaf thickens over decades, a pool edge builds another rim of dissolved stone, a new seep begins where pressure from inside the mountain finally parts a hairline crack. The warmth, the moisture, the spaced growth, and the layered echoes remain in quiet balance, a small, high room of slow water and patient stone.
