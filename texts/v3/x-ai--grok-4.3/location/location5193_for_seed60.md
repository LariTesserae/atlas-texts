A low, enclosed basin no wider than a modest square, its edges marked by a continuous palisade of braided trunks and veined membranes that rise, curve inward, and knit overhead into a living vault. The ground itself is not soil but a compressed quilt of roots, rhizomes, and leathery pads, every surface living and slightly yielding underfoot. Warm vapor hangs in the air, condensing on the undersides of broad, translucent leaves that droop like greenhouse panes; droplets roll down the fibrous walls and rejoin the slow seepage that threads through hidden channels. Water is never pooled in open sheets yet nowhere is it absent—every leaf margin weeps, every stalk sweats, the entire mass functioning as a single, slow sponge.

Scattered through this green lattice are the relics and still-active remnants of an earlier mechanical order: squat cylindrical housings half-swallowed by callus-like growths, their riveted casings now serving as trellises; low, ribbed turbines whose vanes have been replaced by fleshy, rotating petal arrays; and occasional vertical stacks where sap-blackened belts still turn, driven by pressure gradients within the living floor rather than by fuel. These engines do not roar; they produce a continuous, low-frequency thrum that travels through the plant mass like a slow pulse. The sound is amplified by the density of surfaces—every leaf, every stalk, every conduit acting as a resonator—so the basin is filled with an enveloping, bodily drone, occasionally interrupted by the soft, deliberate snap of a maturing seed pod or the long, wet sigh of a pressurized membrane releasing vapor.

No animals move here. The only motion belongs to the plants and the machines they have colonized. Growth occurs across days and seasons rather than hours: a vine thickens visibly only if watched for most of a waking cycle; a new chamber forms when adjacent canopies lean together and fuse over months. The industrial artifacts participate in this patience. Their gears turn a few degrees per hour, powered by osmotic pressure and thermal micro-gradients between the warm, wet interior and the slightly cooler outer shell of the basin. In return, the plants route excess water and dissolved minerals through the old pipework, keeping corrosion in check and maintaining the subtle balance that has allowed both kingdoms to persist together.

Light filters through the living canopy in ordinary daylight quantities, yet the spectrum is altered: chlorophyll-heavy layers absorb the reds and blues, leaving a diffuse, chlorophyll-filtered green that makes every surface appear internally lit. The air tastes of crushed leaves and hot metal. Boundaries are absolute; beyond the woven walls the land drops away into drier, emptier terrain, but inside the pressure of water, plant, and slow machinery remains constant, self-regulating, and without haste.
