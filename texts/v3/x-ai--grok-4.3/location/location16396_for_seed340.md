Beneath the surface of the world lies a single vast hollow that has never known water. Its walls and floor are not stone but a continuous living mass—thick mats of root-plate and fiber that have replaced geology. The space extends for many miles in every direction, a warm, open cavern whose ceiling is lost in layered canopies of translucent, veined fronds. These fronds filter ordinary daylight that seeps through distant hairline fissures and then re-emit it as a steady, slightly amber glow, so the light feels familiar yet slightly thickened.

Nothing here is still. The plant-mass grows, contracts, and reorients itself in slow convulsions that register as constant low thunder. Vines as wide as roads lift and settle; sheets of leaf-fabric peel away and are reabsorbed within minutes. New chambers balloon outward while older ones collapse, the raw geology of the place still being written by the vegetation itself. The air is saturated with drifting spores, pollen, and fragments of living tissue; every surface is either growing or being overgrown. Small, hand-shaped clearings appear where earlier generations of the living mass has been pressed flat, then vanish again as new growth floods back in.

Creatures move through this turbulence in such numbers that their bodies form a second, mobile layer of density. Armored slugs the size of oxen drag themselves along vertical root-walls, their passage carving temporary channels that seal behind them. Clouds of hard-shelled flyers rise and fall like weather systems, their wingbeats merging into a single continuous roar. Smaller, many-legged forms swarm inside the hollow stems and between the overlapping leaves; their combined motion produces a ceaseless scraping and ticking that underlies every other sound. The din is almost physical—creaks, groans, wet slaps, the ripping of fibers being torn by rapid growth, the sudden explosive release of seed pods. Voices, when they occur, are swallowed instantly.

Scattered through the living architecture are faint human traces: a ladder of braided fiber dangling from a root rib, a platform of pressed leaf-laminate holding a few stone and bone tools, a circular clearing where the plants have been trained into low walls. These marks are already being reclaimed. A hand-axe lies half-swallowed by a rising mat of rootlets; a shelter of woven fronds is visibly thickening into a new chamber wall. The builders remain present only as rumor—quick shadows that slip between moving trunks or vanish into the breathing floor.

No liquid water exists, yet the entire volume is damp to the touch from resins and saps that the plants circulate among themselves. The temperature stays steadily warm, encouraging the rapid metabolic churn. Growth and decay are locked in the same instant; a stem may lengthen a body-length in one breath and be gnawed to nothing in the next. The result is an unfinished landscape that is always arriving and already departing, an underground region whose only stable rule is that nothing is permitted to remain unchanged for long.
