The highland shelf runs for miles beyond the eye’s first reach, a broad table of pale stone that once rose in stacked terraces and has since been planed level by centuries of water and wind. The rock is the colour of old bone, its surface grooved into low, patient channels that never quite empty; every groove still holds a skin of water that beads and slides forward at the pace of a slow hour hand. From the channels rise stubby chimneys of the same stone, some only a hand-span tall, others three or four storeys high and braced with iron hoops blackened by long exposure. Between them the ground is open—wide intervals of gravel and slick mineral crust punctuated by the occasional iron walkway or pipe run that seems to have grown rather than been laid.

The air is warm and close, carrying a continual film of moisture that condenses on every upward surface. From the higher stone flanks water seeps in thin curtains that never reach the ground; instead the droplets gather, swell, and fall again from the same ledges in a private, endless rainfall measured in minutes rather than hours. The same moisture beads along the lips of the iron pipes and along the corrugated roofs of the long, low sheds that fill the wider intervals. Inside those sheds the machinery turns at the same tempo as the dripping stone—massive reciprocating engines whose flywheels complete one revolution while a person walks the length of a street. Their exhaust, vented through stone flues, mixes with the natural seepage to produce a perpetual low mist that softens every edge.

Vegetation is present only as pale, salt-tolerant crusts that vein the gravel like faint lace; between the crusts the ground remains bare, its emptiness part of the balance the plateau has achieved. The only movement visible at any distance is the slow advance of a single dark stain where an older channel has undercut a factory wall; over years the wall will settle a hand’s breadth and the roof will tilt by the same fraction, yet the engines inside will continue without adjustment. The sound of those engines fills the shelf so completely that it no longer registers as noise; it has become the medium through which everything else is heard—the drip of water, the scuff of boots on iron grating, the occasional dry cough of a lone night-shift worker whose silhouette appears and vanishes among the pipe runs. No birds answer the sound. The fauna that remain are small, translucent, and live inside the cooler seams of rock where condensation collects; they emerge only at the scale of seasons and are rarely seen.

Light arrives as a steady, even wash that neither brightens nor dims across the long plateau day, filtered through the constant mist until shadow and surface merge into one muted tone. At night the same light continues, now supplied by rows of sodium lamps mounted on the stone chimneys; their glow is steady, never flickering, so that the distinction between day and night is felt only as a slight change in temperature and the lengthening of condensation cycles. The city therefore appears always half-formed, its industrial bones already half-reabsorbed into the weathered stone that continues to weep and settle around them at the same deliberate pace.
