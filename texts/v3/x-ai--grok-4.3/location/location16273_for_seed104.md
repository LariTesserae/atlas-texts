The plateau rises like a memory that refuses to sink. Vast as a lost continent, its surface has been worked for uncountable ages by pressure and current until every peak has become a low, rounded swell and every valley a broad, shallow trough. The rock itself seems half-dissolved, its once-sharp strata now layered in soft, concentric ribbons of mineral that glow faintly when disturbed, like sediment remembering light.  

Water here is not a surrounding medium but a living weight, warm and strangely viscous at this altitude, pressing against the body from every direction. It carries its own slow weather: immense, laminar currents that drift like continents of breath, sometimes layering two or three distinct flows at once so that a single vertical column can contain opposing directions. Where these currents shear against one another, the water brightens to a pale, milky green for hours or days before settling again.  

Vegetation grows in stratified canopies that follow these shear lines. Great translucent fronds, each the size of a hillside, drift anchored to the smoothed stone; their edges fray into filaments fine enough to catch the dim light and hold it. Between them, denser mats of crimson and bronze filaments carpet the stone in velvet thicknesses, releasing slow pulses of ochre spores that linger in the water like suspended dust. Everything is lush yet somehow thinned by depth; the plants do not crowd so much as accumulate, one generation softening into the next until the slope feels upholstered rather than overgrown.  

Light arrives already half-forgotten. It filters down from an unseen surface as a permanent dusk, occasionally brightened by the faint, rhythmic flares of the vegetation itself. In places the illumination warps: a shaft of warmer gold will appear where no opening exists, illuminating a single ridge for a few heartbeats before folding back into the general dimness. These anomalies move. They do not obey the curvature of the plateau but slide across it as though the light were choosing where to rest.  

Rare creatures pass through the layers like afterthoughts. A single translucent ribbon, longer than a ridge line, might undulate once across an entire valley and then vanish for years. Smaller, slower forms—globular clusters of tissue trailing long, hair-fine tendrils—drift among the fronds, their movements almost indistinguishable from the current’s own drift. Their presence registers more as a change in pressure than as sight or sound.  

Sound itself is scarce. The water damps most vibration; only the deepest, slowest pulses travel—the creak of vast organic lattices expanding, the distant, muffled thunder of a current colliding with an older one. Everything else arrives as texture rather than tone: the soft fibrillation of spores brushing skin, the almost tactile hush of water moving over velvet stone.  

Scattered across this immensity are fragments that look worked. Low, circular ridges of darker mineral rise just above the general smoothing, their edges perfectly even, their interiors filled with the same layered sediment as the surrounding rock. Some contain faint radial patterns that repeat at mathematically precise intervals. Others have collapsed inward, leaving only a shallow bowl whose lip has already begun to soften back into the plateau’s ancient curve. No tool marks remain, yet their regularity suggests intention long since absorbed into geology.  

And beneath these certainties the world occasionally refuses to stay fixed. A ridge that should be miles distant will appear, for a moment, to lie directly beneath an outstretched hand; a current that was flowing east will, without transition, have always been flowing west. These fractures are brief and local, yet they leave an afterimage: a sense that the plateau continues not only outward but sideways through versions of itself that never quite match. In those instants the warm water feels momentarily thinner, as though one has drifted for a single breath into a neighboring depth that has not yet agreed to remain separate.
