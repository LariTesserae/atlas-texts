Beneath a planet-wide ceiling of unbroken water, the world is an artificial basin so large that its own slow currents function as weather. Kilometers-deep trenches have been replaced by kilometer-high lattices of alloy and ceramic whose girders still carry the matte sheen of recent extrusion; their edges remain knife-sharp because nothing has had time to blunt them. Between these colossal beams the water is nearly empty—vast, transparent galleries where only the occasional drifting scrap of insulation or lost tool glitters on its long fall. Light comes from endless belts of sodium-white lamps embedded in the upper surfaces; the glow is steady and ordinary, neither twilight nor noon, simply “day” maintained by relays that never sleep.

The machinery that built this basin is still running. Convection pumps the size of mountains circulate cool water from the lower strata, keeping the average temperature a few degrees above freezing even while the pumps themselves radiate waste heat in rippling columns. Chains of automated welders crawl along fresh seams, their arc-light flickering like submarine lightning; the sound travels for leagues, a continuous low thunder of metal against metal, punctuated by the groan of pressure-equalizing valves and the hiss of super-cooled fluid venting into the sea. These noises do not echo so much as accumulate, filling the empty volumes with a dense, directionless roar that masks the smaller sounds of life.

Life is present, but not in the form of waving forests. A thin, film-like algae clings only to the warmest lamp housings; everywhere else the surfaces remain bare, sterile metal and ceramic. Instead the abundance is animal. Shoals of swift, translucent fish pour between the girders like schools of living mercury, feeding on the steady rain of nutrient paste extruded from maintenance ports. Larger predators—sleek, pressure-armored fish with metallic-looking scales—patrol the open galleries, their lateral lines tuned to the constant industrial thrum that drowns the small movements of prey. Colonies of filter-feeding invertebrates have colonized the lee sides of every beam, forming living curtains that billow in the pump-driven currents.

The entire construct is still young enough that sections of it are still being completed. Freshly lowered modules drift downward on cables thicker than redwoods, their interiors already lit and humming, already releasing warm water and faint chemical scents that draw the fauna. Where two modules have just mated, the joint is a bright scar of molten alloy solidifying into new girder-work; cold water streams across it, carrying away the heat and the first thin streamers of metal dust.

Nothing here is natural substrate. Even the sediment drifting across the lowest platforms is manufactured grit from grinding wheels and drilling rigs, not silt from eroded stone. The world is therefore both finished and unfinished at once: fully artificial, already loud with its own industry, already inhabited by creatures that treat its engines and lamps as permanent features of the sea, yet still growing, still sharp, still filling its own vast, cool, and echoing volume with new corridors of light and sound.
