In the throat of an unfinished spire that has not yet decided whether it belongs to mountain or sky, a single crevice no wider than a ribcage holds an entire world under furious construction. The walls are not stone but layered assemblages of hand-split slate, woven reed mats already half-swallowed by fresh clay daub, and bundles of green saplings bent into arches that still drip pitch. Nothing here remembers a natural substrate; every surface has been worked by callused palms and stone flakes. Water is everywhere and never still. It beads on the unfinished inner skin of the crevice like sweat on fevered hide, gathers in shallow clay dishes set into the floor, and trickles from the seams where new masonry meets older, already softening weave. The air is hot enough to press against the skin like a living flank, yet it constantly condenses, so that every breath arrives already half-liquid and tasting of clay and crushed leaf.

Vegetation has been coaxed into every cranny at once. Thick ropes of fibrous vine, their cut ends still weeping milky sap, have been lashed to the scaffolding of green wood and are already knitting themselves across the ceiling. Broad leaves the color of bruised plum thrust between the struts, their veins visibly pulsing as though the heat itself were pumping sap through them. Some of these plants are recognizably tended—neat rows of swollen tubers planted in pockets of black soil carried up in skin bags—while others seem to have been urged into growth only yesterday; stems still bear the shallow gouges of the flint knives that shaped them. There is no room between any two growing things. Where one leaf ends, another root or tendril has already claimed the sliver of air.

Because the entire pocket sits at an altitude where breath should thin and freeze, the heat feels like a deliberate trespass. Steam rises in wavering columns from the clay cisterns and is caught by the half-formed canopy, trapped and re-warmed until the small chamber becomes a single continuous exhalation. Light barely reaches this height. What little filters down arrives fractured through a lattice of living stems and unfinished thatch, arriving as a low greenish dusk in which edges blur and distances refuse to resolve.

Sound fills every remaining space. The drip of condensing water lands on taut reed mats with a soft, rapid drumming; the vines themselves creak as they lengthen; and from deeper fissures comes a low, many-throated hiss like an audience of serpents perpetually drawing breath. Occasionally a louder report cracks through the dimness when a swelling tuber splits its clay pot or a bent sapling finally gives way under its own furious growth. These noises do not echo so much as accumulate, layering until the air itself seems to vibrate at the threshold of becoming solid.

Fauna is almost absent, yet the space feels watched. Once in a long while a pale, translucent lizard no longer than a finger darts across a wet clay patch and vanishes into a deliberately bored hole, leaving only a thread of displaced steam. The absence of larger creatures is not emptiness but compression: there is no interval left for bodies to pass without touching living matter on every side.

The place is still deciding what it is. Fresh mats of reed are being pressed into the floor even as earlier ones rot and sink; new layers of clay are smeared over older ones while the older ones continue to weep and shift. Reality itself frays at the margins. A hand thrust between two leaves may briefly encounter the chill of open sky at impossible altitude before the vines close again; a drop of water falling from the ceiling sometimes reverses direction halfway down and returns to the leaf it left. These instabilities arrive and depart like weather, leaving the constructed surfaces subtly rearranged— a new stem where there was none, a seam of clay that has moved a finger’s width during the blink of an eye. The pocket remains loud, hot, and occupied, continuing its noisy, unfinished labor of becoming.
