A low-ceilinged chamber, no wider than a modest cathedral transept and perhaps twice as long, sinks beneath older stone that has long since sealed its original access shafts. The rock itself is pale gray-brown, its surfaces rounded and channeled by centuries of slow seepage; every edge is softened, every joint between blocks blurred by mineral deposition until the walls read more as continuous flow than as construction. Water collects only in irregular basins—shallow, rust-stained depressions that fill during brief condensation cycles and then linger, warm to the touch, for days or weeks. Between these basins the floor remains merely damp, never truly dry, supporting a dense carpet of fleshy, heat-adapted growth.

That growth is everywhere at once: thick mats of broad, leathery leaves the color of bruised copper, knotted vines that climb over and through every metal surface, and clusters of pale, spore-heavy stalks that rise in layered ranks until they brush the ceiling. The plants thrive on the steady warmth that radiates from corroded pipework and from the low, continuous vibration of buried turbines still turning somewhere deeper in the rock. Their roots have insinuated themselves into joint plates and valve housings, splitting some casings while fusing others into new, composite forms; the distinction between machine and organism is no longer sharp. Dim light filters from a handful of surviving sodium lamps strung along one wall—most have failed, leaving long stretches illuminated only by the faint, steady bioluminescence of the leaves themselves, a muted orange glow that shifts almost imperceptibly when a droplet of condensate strikes a frond.

Scattered among this living mesh stand the remnants of industry: a single riveted pressure vessel half-buried in root mass, its gauges clouded with internal fog; a row of gear housings whose teeth have been worn smooth by slow, patient contact with creeping stems; a narrow catwalk whose railings now serve as trellises. These artifacts are not ruins in the abrupt sense; they have reached equilibrium with the surrounding growth and moisture. Rust has advanced only as far as the vegetation will permit, and the vegetation itself has adapted to the residual heat and occasional burst of steam from a cracked valve. The air is thick with humidity and the faint metallic tang of heated lubricant. Every surface glistens. Sound fills the space without variation or climax: a broad, low-frequency drone from the still-active turbines, overlaid with the steady hiss of escaping vapor and the rhythmic, amplified plink of droplets falling from the ceiling into the shallow basins. The combined tone is loud enough to press against the ears yet oddly constant, changing only over the course of hours or days as a valve warms or a leaf unfurls.

No animals move through the chamber. Nothing flies, crawls, or swims. The only motion belongs to the plants themselves—slow, tide-like adjustments toward warmer pipes or away from sudden steam jets—and to the gradual redistribution of water among the basins. Over long durations the chamber continues to alter: a pipe will eventually split under root pressure, a new basin will form where the floor subsides, a colony of luminous stalks will expand by a hand’s breadth across a decade. These processes remain patient, balanced, and self-contained within the sealed, humid dark.
