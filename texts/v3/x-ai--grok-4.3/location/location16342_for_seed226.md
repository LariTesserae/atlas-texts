The place perches on a narrow saddle of newborn stone high above the cloud line. The rock itself is still congealing; pale, pumice-like sheets push upward in slow, almost imperceptible surges, leaving ragged seams where water gathers and drips in long, deliberate threads. These threads never quite reach the ground. Instead they are caught by the air and turned into a permanent, cool haze that beads on every surface and refuses to settle.

Between the emerging ledges stand a handful of structures—low platforms and single-storey walls of the same pale stone, joined only by hand-set pegs of green wood. They are spaced so widely that each reads as a separate island. From one platform to the next is a walk across open mist so empty it feels like crossing a plaza the size of a hamlet, though only three platforms exist. No two share a common wall; the builders left deliberate, wind-scoured intervals between them, as if the settlement were still deciding where its next building should root.

Everything that grows does so in the intervals. Ferns and hanging mosses trail from every lip and gutter, their fronds heavy with condensed droplets. Vines the color of wet slate drape across the gaps, forming soft, living bridges that shiver when the constant drip finds them. The vegetation is dense enough to drink the available moisture yet thin enough that the platforms remain visible as stark interruptions—pale stone rectangles bordered by living green.

The only movement is slow. A single tendril of vine lengthens across a gap by the width of a finger every season. A fresh film of stone continues to extrude from the saddle’s central seam at a rate no eye can witness. Water migrates from rock to air to leaf and back again in closed loops measured in days rather than minutes.

Sound fills the emptiness without filling the space. Each drop, detaching from an overhang, produces a clear, resonant note that lingers because there is nothing to absorb it. The notes arrive in loose, overlapping cascades—sometimes three or four tones sounding together—creating an unceasing, cool music that seems louder precisely because the intervals between structures are so vast. Beneath this drip-choir runs a lower, slower vibration: the muffled creak of new stone shifting a fraction of a millimeter deeper in the mountain. The two layers of sound never quite synchronize, so the ear keeps trying to align them and keeps failing.

Nothing larger than a mist-moth crosses the platforms. The insects themselves are translucent, barely heavier than the droplets they ride, and they appear only when the light is ordinary and even, which it nearly always is. No shadows gather long enough to matter. The cool air preserves the stone and the green alike, allowing both to remain raw and unfinished for as long as the mountain beneath them continues its deliberate rise.
