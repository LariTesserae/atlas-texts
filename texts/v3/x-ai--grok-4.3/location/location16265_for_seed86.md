Beneath the skin of the world lies a single, unbroken hollow that stretches for miles in every direction, its scale more valley than room. The chamber floor tilts in long, gentle undulations rather than abrupt drops, so the eye travels across pale, newly fractured stone before the ceiling dissolves into dimness hundreds of paces overhead. Rock surfaces still carry the glassy, razor edges of recent separation; angular shards of basalt and calcite lie exactly where fracture left them, untouched by later smoothing. Thin films of moisture bead along these edges and then slip downward in slow, patient threads that never quite become drips. The water arrives from no visible source—condensation that gathers during the stable coolness, lingers, then returns to vapor in an unbroken cycle.

Faint, almost colorless lichens and thread-like roots from some unseen surface world cling only to the highest seams where moisture collects longest. Between these scattered patches the floor remains bare stone, interrupted here and there by shallow, circular depressions that hold standing water no deeper than a hand’s breadth. The water itself is perfectly clear, its surface never broken by wind because the air below moves in no currents at all; temperature and pressure remain fixed, so the tiny pools neither evaporate nor overflow.

Life moves through this fixed coolness in continuous, soft multitudes rather than concentrated swarms. Pale, translucent isopods the length of fingers travel singly across the stone, their legs producing a steady, minute ticking that blends into a single, unbroken texture of sound. Larger, blind salamanders rest in the shallow water pockets, gills pulsing at the same slow rhythm for hours. Above, in the upper dark, uncountable small wings beat without variation—countless bats or swiftlets whose individual cries have merged into one constant, soft roar that neither rises nor falls. The sound fills every crevice yet changes nothing; it simply is, the acoustic signature of an ecology that has reached perfect, time-invariant balance.

No artifact of deliberate making interrupts the surfaces. Tool marks, soot, or stacked stones are absent because no hand has ever entered with purpose. The only light arrives as a faint, sourceless wash that clings to the wettest rock, enough to reveal the nearest sharp facets yet leaving deeper fissures in permanent half-dark. Because the system is young, the air carries the faint mineral bite of freshly exposed stone rather than the must of long habitation. Everything—water films, insect passage, distant wing-roar—remains exactly as it was an hour, a day, or a century earlier, held in motionless equilibrium beneath the weight of the world.
