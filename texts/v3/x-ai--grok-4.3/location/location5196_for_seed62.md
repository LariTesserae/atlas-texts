The plateau rises in fractured tiers, its bedrock still raw from uplift, edges cut clean as if the mountain had only just remembered how to stand. Ridges remain knife-thin, their flanks etched with hairline fractures that have not yet softened into slopes. Between these sharp bones, vegetation has taken hold with improbable density: layered canopies of broad, waxy leaves that overlap like scales, thickets of pale green stalks rising through every crevice, root mats that dangle from overhangs and drink the constant condensation. The plants are cool to the touch, their surfaces pearled with moisture that gathers overnight and lingers through the long, bright day.

Light arrives without warmth. At this height the sun strikes hard and white, turning every wet leaf into a mirror and every exposed pipe into a blade of glare. The pipes run in loose, wandering lines across the rock—some half-buried, some still bolted to cliff faces—connecting low, blocky housings whose metal skins have begun to flake and green beneath living growth. Inside a few of the housings, flywheels still turn, almost too slowly to see; their motion is registered only by the faint, rhythmic drip of condensed water that falls from cooling fins into moss-choked basins below. The sound is smaller than rain, smaller even than a footstep, and it is the only consistent interruption in the prevailing quiet.

Elsewhere the silence is nearly absolute. No insects drone, no leaves rattle in wind that rarely visits these elevations. The vegetation itself seems to absorb what little motion the air carries, holding each droplet until it grows heavy enough to release it downward through successive layers of foliage. In places the plants have fused around the older machinery, bark-like growths encasing turbine housings or sensor arrays so that the metal appears to sprout directly from living tissue. These hybrid forms are motionless, yet they register a continuous, patient exchange: the machines draw stray static from the bright air and route it through the roots, while the roots, in turn, keep the housings from overheating.

Across the open distances of the plateau—valleys wide enough to hold a town, ridges spaced so that one can see the next tier rise, blue-grey and sharp against the sky—the same pattern repeats at a scale that feels both intimate and vast. Scattered among the living layers are the remnants of access roads, now little more than linear clearings where the densest growth has not yet closed over crushed stone. Here and there a single upright stanchion remains, its lattice thick with hanging vines that end in clusters of translucent pods. In the right angle of light the pods glow faintly, storing the day’s excess illumination for gradual release after the sun has dropped behind the next ridge.

Change arrives measured in seasons measured by condensation rather than weather. A new fracture may appear along one ridge, water will find it, a fresh seam of moss will advance a hand’s breadth in a year, and somewhere a flywheel will complete one more fraction of its turning. The plateau holds its cool, bright equilibrium through this slow accrual, every element—stone, leaf, machine—settling into the others without haste or noise.
