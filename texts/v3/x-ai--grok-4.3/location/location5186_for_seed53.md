High in a knife-edge cirque, the air thins to a cold, knife-bright clarity. Sun strikes the enclosing walls at an angle that never softens; every facet of stone throws back light in hard, splintered planes. The basin itself is no larger than a cathedral nave, yet feels smaller still because the rock is crowded: dense clusters of pale, fine-grained quartzite and frost-shattered schist stand shoulder to shoulder, some still holding the last night’s rime in their hollows. Between them lie shallow concavities where condensation gathers, turns briefly to slush, then evaporates again by mid-morning, leaving behind faint mineral rings like tide marks.

Nothing grows and nothing moves of its own accord. The only active processes are physical and slow. Temperature swings crack the rock along ancient joints; each new fracture is already rounded at the edges, the work of millennia of identical cycles. Occasional thin veils of mist drift in from the cirque lip, condense on the upright stones, and slide downward in single droplets that strike with a soft, isolated tap before the sound is swallowed again. Otherwise the place is quiet, the silence itself dense, as if the packed crystals absorb and hold vibration.

Scattered among the natural forms are the faint signatures of earlier hands: a low ring of stones set flush with the surface, a few angular flakes of the same quartzite that show deliberate removal scars, a shallow basin pecked into a single boulder. These remnants lie where they were left, neither decaying nor accumulating; the cold and the dry air preserve them as they are, while the same processes that smooth the cliffs have begun to blur their edges as well. No one returns to add to them. The only continuing activity is the patient rearrangement of moisture and fracture, light and shadow, across a surface already settled into its own slow equilibrium.
