The place is a high, sharp-edged hollow no larger than a cathedral nave, where newly risen stone has been thrust upward into a body of warm, dense liquid that behaves like an ocean sky. Jagged granite spears and fresh fracture planes still carry the matte sheen of rock that has never known air; their edges remain angular, unsoftened, because they emerged only moments or centuries ago into this medium. Between the spears hangs mostly emptiness—clear, warm volumes where no particle lingers more than a heartbeat—yet the emptiness is never still. Currents slam through it in sudden, contradictory sheets, folding back on themselves without warning, so that a current arriving from one direction is already tearing itself apart while another, warmer ribbon replaces it.

Light arrives thinly from somewhere above and behind the highest spires, filtered to a bruised violet that never quite reaches the floor. In that half-light the water itself occasionally fails: a cubic meter will turn mirror-bright and rigid for three beats, then collapse into a flurry of bubbles that rise sideways before dissolving into ordinary turbulence again. These glitches leave no trace except a faint metallic taste that lingers on the tongue of anything still alive.

The only persistent presences are thin, translucent sheets of something between plant and animal—pale, veined membranes that drift at the edges of the currents, never attaching, never multiplying. When one is caught in a shear it shreds instantly and its fragments continue moving independently for a few seconds before they, too, vanish. Rarely, a single, slow-moving shape the size of a large fish will glide between the spires; its skin is the same temperature as the water, so it appears only as a momentary displacement of light before it threads itself into a fracture and is gone.

Sound is continuous and total. The colliding water masses produce a layered roar—low grinding tones from stone against water, mid-range cracks as the liquid briefly solidifies and fractures, and high, glassy screams when a glitch swallows a volume of space. The noise does not echo; it replaces the air, or the space where air would be, so that every movement, every thought, feels conducted through bone rather than ear. Within that roar, fragments of other landscapes sometimes surface for an instant: the smell of hot pine needles, the click of dry stones underfoot—then they are ripped away and replaced by another current.

Scattered across the lower faces of the spires are faint architectural traces: a single smooth lintel set flush into living rock, a half-buried curve of what might once have been a stair, a circular depression whose rim still carries the ghost of carved lettering now blurred to mere texture. These remnants are not ruins in the usual sense; they appear to have risen with the stone itself, as if the mountain and its forgotten builders were extruded together. Nothing metallic or mechanical exists here—only the rock, the inconsistent water, the drifting membranes, and the rare, vanishing swimmer.

The whole chamber is in continuous re-formation. One spire may shorten by a hand’s breadth between one surge and the next; another lengthens, its new facets already sharp. The water accepts these changes without resistance, then immediately contradicts them with an opposing current. Nothing settles. Nothing accumulates. The place simply continues, loud, warm, half-lit, sparsely inhabited, and perpetually unfinished.
