In the warm press of the deep, a single drowned complex occupies the floor like a cathedral whose nave never ended. Its walls—layer upon layer of riveted iron and poured stone—are thick with a velvet of luminous kelp and ribboned bladder-wrack that has learned, over slow centuries, to grow lengthwise rather than upward, filling every seam and strut until the structure seems upholstered in living tissue. The plants are not scattered; they are continuous, a single tissue that has claimed every surface, softening angles and sealing gaps so that what was once a factory floor now reads as a continuous green chamber, dimly veined with its own faint bioluminescence.

Currents arrive in patient pulses rather than waves, each one slow enough to be counted against a held breath. They carry pale threads of silt that drift downward in long, unhurried spirals, settling into the deep nap of the vegetation and into the open throats of rusted exhaust stacks. Over time these threads have built gentle slopes and aprons around every base, rounding the sharp edges of catwalks and loading ramps until the architecture begins to resemble natural reef rather than engineered grid. The metal itself has reached an equilibrium: corrosion has eaten inward until each beam is only a fragile core wrapped in its own oxide skin; nothing new falls, and nothing further is taken.

Light is scarce. A few intact sodium lamps still glow, their bulbs filmed with algae, throwing narrow cones of ochre that barely reach the opposite wall before exhaustion. Between them the water is the color of weak tea, and the only other illumination comes from the kelp’s own slow pulses—single cells brightening and fading on their own long rhythms, never in unison. In the intervals between these pulses the space feels genuinely empty, though it is not; every square meter is occupied by stem, frond, or rusted plate.

Fauna are present only as rumor. Once in a long while a translucent ribbon the length of an arm will undulate past, its movement so gradual it might be mistaken for the current itself. More often the only motion is the vegetation breathing—entire walls inhaling and exhaling on a cycle measured in minutes, the water displaced by their expansion brushing against skin with the softest, coolest pressure before withdrawing again.

The rules here are simple and patient. Nothing arrives suddenly. Time is counted in the slow settling of silt, in the incremental creep of oxide across a rivet head, in the single new leaf that lengthens by the width of a fingernail between one visit and the next. Sound is reduced to the lowest possible register: the faint, almost imagined groan of a beam adjusting to its own weight, the occasional soft pop of a gas bubble escaping from a pocket inside a stalk. Even these are spaced so far apart that silence becomes the dominant medium.

What remains is a strange intimacy. The industrial bones and the living tissue have become one material, neither fully built nor fully grown, held at a depth and temperature where pressure and warmth cancel each other into a constant, enveloping weight. Walk through any doorway and the same continuous carpet of kelp continues on the far side, threading between turbines whose blades have long since fused with the plant mass, as though the machines are merely another species of root that once tried to turn.
