Beneath a perpetual veil of black water lies a single drowned works-yard no larger than a cathedral nave, its floor a shallow basin of compacted silt and fractured basalt. The enclosing walls are not natural rock but the fused remnants of loading cranes, pressure tanks, and conveyor housings, all riveted together into a continuous, load-bearing skin. Over decades the surfaces have acquired a velvet coat of manganese oxide and fine clay; the oxide grows in slow, iridescent whorls that record the passage of every quiet current. Between these layered shells, narrow slots and vents remain open, admitting threads of colder abyssal water that sink, pool, and rise again in minute, almost motionless gyres.

Nothing green grows here. Occasional tufts of white, glass-spined filaments drift from the highest seams—chemosynthetic, barely alive, and too sparse to color the gloom. The only motion comes from the machines themselves. Pumps the size of altars continue to cycle, their diaphragms flexing with the regularity of sleeping lungs; the sound is conducted through the metal rather than the water, arriving to any listener as an almost inaudible bone-deep thrum. From time to time a single bubble, perfectly spherical and silver-sheathed, detaches from a vent and rises like a slow thought; it does not pop at the ceiling but is drawn into a lattice of capillary pipes and returned, chemically altered, to the lower chambers.

Farther in, corridors of bolted plating intersect at odd angles, their intersections marked by clusters of pressure gauges whose needles still quiver though no hand has calibrated them for generations. Sediment has settled in the lower angles, yet never enough to clog them; some imperceptible equilibrium between settling silt and periodic hydraulic flushing keeps the passages marginally clear. Tiny metallic mites—autonomous inspection drones no larger than thumbnails—crawl along the seams, their lights extinguished, their legs tapping out soft, syncopated rhythms against the steel. They are the last fauna; larger swimmers avoid the yard, repelled by the residual electromagnetic fields that still leak from the old generators.

Light, when it exists at all, arrives as a single diffuse glow from a phosphor strip half-buried in oxide along one wall—faint enough that the surrounding water appears to drink it before it can travel a body-length. In this perpetual half-dark the geometry feels larger than it is; every pipe and strut seems to recede into further distances, yet three careful strokes in any direction return a swimmer to the opposite wall. The cold is uniform, just above freezing, and clings inside joints and beneath plating, preserving the metal even while it leaches heat from any warmer body that enters.

Rules here are simple and self-enforcing. Water moves only in closed loops; nothing is added, nothing is lost. Sound travels farther through steel than through liquid, so the dominant language is vibration rather than voice. Erosion has already done its work; what remains is neither ruin nor functioning city but a single, balanced organism of mineral, metal, and residual current, small enough to be circled in a minute yet dense enough that every surface is spoken for by either machine or mineral film.
