In a pocket of basalt no larger than a burial chamber, the city clings to every surface like lichen that never bloomed. Walls, floor, and low ceiling were quarried long ago into terraces and lintels, each edge now rounded by patient seepage rather than wind or time. Water beads constantly from hairline fractures and drips in slow, unwavering rhythm; the droplets strike stone with a sound that never rises or falls, only accumulates into a single unbroken roar that fills the space the way heat fills a forge. Everything within arm’s reach is damp and cool to the touch, the temperature fixed as though the rock itself had decided once, long ago, what “cold” meant and would not alter.

No soil, no roots, only mineral. The builders worked with wedges and fire-spalls; their structures remain—low vaults, narrow causeways, stair-risers no higher than a shin—packed so tightly that one can stand in the central lane and touch four separate roofs. Surfaces are layered with mineral glaze where water has flowed and evaporated for centuries, leaving faint, permanent veils of white and pale rust. Between the buildings, the gaps are narrower than shoulders; some dwellings are entered only by crawling, others by stepping onto a neighbor’s ledge. Nothing grows. Nothing moves that was not placed by hand.

Light arrives from nowhere visible, a dim, sourceless wash that outlines edges but hides depth, as if the air itself were faintly luminous with dissolved stone. In this half-light the only living presences are occasional translucent threads—mineral accretions still forming—that occasionally detach and drift a finger’s length before settling again. Their motion is so slow it registers only as a change in the light after many heartbeats. Otherwise the chamber is empty of animals. The sound of water replaces them: constant, close, without variation or source one can reach.

The place feels finished rather than abandoned, as though every surface has reached the exact degree of polish, every joint the exact degree of fit, that its makers intended and then simply ceased to alter. One could remain inside a single lifetime and never perceive any difference except the incremental thickening of the mineral glaze on the stones under one’s palms.
