The plateau rises like a single unbroken organism, a continent of living tissue still braiding itself together from the raw substrate below. In this high, cool realm the air never quite settles; thin veils of mist lift from every surface and fall again within moments, so that water is always arriving from above and seeping from within. The light that reaches the plateau is the color of late twilight, a permanent half-glow that leaks through a perpetual high canopy hundreds of kilometers across. Nothing here is built; everything is grown, and the growth itself is the architecture.

Enormous, cable-thick vines the width of rivers rise in slow helical towers, their outer bark still translucent, still deciding what mineral it will become. Between these towers the ground is not soil but a living mat of interlocking leaves and root-weaves so densely packed that a human stride would sink only an arm’s length before the tissue simply closed again, sealing the depression as if it had never been made. Water moves everywhere at once: it condenses on the undersides of vast, translucent fronds, runs along vein-channels that have not yet hardened into permanent conduits, and reappears as sudden curtains of droplets that fall, collect, and are reabsorbed by the same mat within minutes. The rocks that do exist are embryonic—half-digested nodules still wrapped in cambium, their surfaces weeping mineral-rich sap that the surrounding vegetation drinks before the droplets can gather.

No animals move through this place. Only the plants themselves are active, and their activity is deafening. A constant, layered thunder rolls outward from every scale of growth: the low, grinding roar of cellulose fibers lengthening at visible speed; the sharp, glassy pops of new vascular chambers inflating with pressurized sap; the sustained, hurricane-note of billions of microscopic stomata cycling open and shut in perfect synchrony to regulate the perpetual mist. The sound has texture—sometimes it drops into a subsonic pulse that can be felt through the living floor, sometimes it rises into an insectile whine of cavitation as water is forced through still-forming xylem at pressures modern physics would consider impossible. All of it together forms a single, continent-wide chord that never resolves.

Because the plateau is still assembling, its topography is provisional. Ridges lift themselves in the distance like green spines testing their own strength; valleys collapse into themselves only to be refilled by surging root-mats that arrive from three cardinal directions at once. Gravity itself seems slightly undecided here, as though the living mass has not yet agreed how much it ought to weigh. Where the growth is newest, the light that filters through is dimmer still, absorbed by tissue that has not yet learned to reflect. Where it is older, the same light strikes surfaces that have begun to mineralize, throwing back faint, prismatic gleams that look almost like circuitry.

The vegetation does not merely occupy space; it is the weather. Lifting fronts of transpiration create localized downdrafts that scour the canopy and return as new condensation within the same hour. Entire slopes of leaf-matter tilt in coordinated waves, steering mist toward regions the growth has decided are still too dry. These adjustments happen visibly, audibly, and without any external signal—an engineering so far beyond contemporary technique that the distinction between organism and machine has become meaningless. The plateau is not finished with becoming. Every sound, every droplet, every slow heave of its living crust is part of that unfinished sentence.
