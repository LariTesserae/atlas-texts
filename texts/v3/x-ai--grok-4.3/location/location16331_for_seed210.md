A compact highland shelf, no wider than a modest hall, clings to a knife-edge ridge where the rock has only lately torn itself upward. The stone is still raw, its fractures clean and glass-bright, edges thin enough to cut shadow. Between these fresh shards the ground dips into a shallow basin that never quite drains; thin films of water cling to every surface, beading and sliding in slow, repeating cycles that leave the air tasting of wet stone and cold iron. Mist forms directly on the rock faces and drips downward in silent threads, keeping the temperature low and the light perpetually dulled, as though the shelf lies just beneath a ceiling of low, unmoving cloud.

Vegetation grows in deliberate intervals—tall, fleshy rosettes of deep-green tissue spaced several body-lengths apart, each leaf veined with fine metallic striations that catch what little light remains. The plants are motionless yet saturated with moisture, their surfaces never drying, never wilting. Between them the ground remains largely bare, exposing more of the young rock and the slow seepage that keeps it dark. No paths cross the shelf; nothing has worn the stone smooth.

Scattered through the same space are forms that resemble engines—vast, ribbed housings half-sunk into the living rock, their casings the same mineral as the ridge itself. They carry no seams of human fabrication. Pipes of translucent crystal branch from them like roots, pulsing with a faint internal luminance that never brightens or fades. These structures emit a continuous, layered roar: the basso thrum of turbines, the higher whine of unseen belts, the occasional thunderclap of pressure released. The noise is unvarying; it does not rise or diminish with any visible motion. The machines have never started and will never stop.

Fauna fills the intervals between the great leaves and the half-buried engines. Small, many-legged creatures the color of wet slate crawl across every damp surface in dense but ordered constellations. Their movements are frozen at the scale of human perception—limbs arrested mid-step, antennae fixed at precise angles—yet the collective scrape and tap of their feet supplies an additional layer to the industrial din. Larger, translucent slugs glide along the weeping rock faces, leaving trails that momentarily glow before fading back into the prevailing dimness. None of these creatures age or breed within observable time; the shelf holds them in permanent abundance.

The rules here are simple and inflexible. Nothing decays. Nothing arrives or departs. The water condenses, the machines roar, the creatures remain suspended in their exact positions, and the sharp stone continues its unmoving claim on the high air. Only when a new fracture opens elsewhere along the ridge does the equilibrium expand by a few hand-widths, incorporating another weeping engine or another motionless cluster of life before the change ceases once more. The entire place exists at the threshold where geology and machinery have become indistinguishable, held in loud, motionless suspension by whatever process first lifted the rock and has since refused to let it settle.
