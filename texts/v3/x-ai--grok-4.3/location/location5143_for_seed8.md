The land lies low and open beneath a warm sky, an immense basin whose edges dissolve into distant haze. It has the look of something that finished its shaping long ago: an ancient plain scoured flat by uncountable seasons of water and wind, its surface now a single, patient curve of earth. The ground is soft underfoot, dark with retained moisture, a skin of clay and silt that never quite dries. Water is everywhere and nowhere at once—seeping up in slow threads between the sparse tussocks of grass, collecting in shallow depressions that mirror the sky, and condensing each night into a fine silver film that beads along every stem and pebble before the sun burns it away again.

Scattered across this openness are clumps of tough, low vegetation: wiry reeds whose roots drink from the near-surface water, occasional mats of succulent leaves pressed close to the mud, and here and there a lone, crooked shrub whose branches are furred with salt-tolerant fuzz. Between them stretches bare, glistening earth, pocked with the prints of animals and the faint, permanent channels where water has flowed and reflowed for centuries. Nothing grows thick enough to hide the horizon; the eye travels far across the plain, meeting only slight rises that are themselves worn almost level.

Life moves through the spaces rather than crowding them. Insects rise in glittering clouds above each temporary pool, their wings producing a constant, high metallic drone that underlies every other sound. Frogs and toads call from the wet hollows in overlapping choruses that swell and fall with the slow pulse of the day. Birds—long-legged waders and smaller, darting species—wheel and settle across the open ground, their calls sharp and numerous. Smaller mammals slip between the grass clumps at dusk, leaving narrow trails that are soon softened again by condensing mist. The sound is constant and full: water trickling through hidden veins of soil, the low rush of warm wind across damp surfaces, the steady percussion of legs and wings and throats. Yet nothing hurries. The water rises and recedes by inches across weeks; new growth pushes upward by fractions of a finger’s width each year; the flat horizon remains unchanged within any single lifetime.

The air itself feels thick with the memory of rain even when the sky is clear, carrying the faint mineral tang of ancient stone reduced to powder and redistributed. In the warm light the surface of the plain gleams where moisture lingers, then dulls to a soft matte as the film evaporates. Everything here moves at the pace of a process that no longer expects an ending—water returning to water, life threading itself through the same sparse, enduring pattern year after patient year.
