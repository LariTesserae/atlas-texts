The chamber is a single, self-contained hollow no larger than a modest warehouse, yet it feels crowded to the point of compression. Freshly hewn walls of pale, glassy basalt rise in clean, unweathered facets that still carry the faint chatter of whatever drills or solvents opened them days or weeks ago. Against those sharp planes, prefabricated struts of blackened iron and riveted alloy have been wedged and bolted at odd angles, forming the skeletons of narrow walkways, catwalks, and low-roofed sheds that nearly brush one another. Pipes of varying diameters crawl across every surface like veins; many still glisten with beads of condensation that form, swell, and release in slow, metronomic drips.

Heat radiates from the iron itself. Somewhere deeper in the lattice of structures an engine idles, its exhaust routed through the rock to keep the air at a steady, oppressive warmth. The light is thin and sourceless, a perpetual dusk that seeps from strips of dimly glowing filament embedded in the ceiling. It is enough to show edges and silhouettes but not enough to banish shadow; the result is a world of half-seen planes and sudden glints where moisture catches the light.

Moisture arrives in irregular pulses. Every so often a valve somewhere opens and releases a thin, steaming sheet that runs down a chosen channel, pools briefly in a shallow basin lined with mineral scale, then drains away through grated floors. Between these events the air simply grows heavier, fogging every horizontal surface and encouraging a sparse, almost reluctant growth: tight rosettes of pale, salt-tolerant lichen that have colonized only the coolest corners where metal and stone meet. Nothing moves on its own; no insects, no rodents, no airborne spores thick enough to see. The space is biologically quiet yet mechanically restless. Automated winches click and reset on their own schedules. A single conveyor belt, empty, advances one meter every few minutes and then pauses, its rubber surface tacky from the heat. Distant pumps thump once, twice, then fall silent again, leaving the overall acoustic profile closer to a held breath than to industry.

The settlement—if it can be called that—has no center and no periphery; every structure is pressed against its neighbor, stacked where vertical space allows, ductwork braided through the gaps like the weave of a net. Fresh mineral dust still coats some of the lower ledges, proof that the rock is still settling around the intrusion of metal and heat. Condensation continues its patient work, blurring the distinction between stone and machine until both seem equally alive and equally inert.
