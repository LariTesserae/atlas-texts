The Hollow is reached by way of a single, low corridor whose walls have been worked and reworked until the chisel marks have merged into one continuous ripple, like cloth that has been smoothed by many hands over generations. At its end the passage opens without ceremony into a single vaulted chamber no larger than a modest grove, perhaps thirty paces across at its widest point. Everything inside has been built: the floor is a tight mosaic of fired clay tiles, many of them cracked and then patched again with smaller fragments set in a darker mortar; the ceiling is a lattice of sapling poles lashed with rawhide and then plastered over with successive layers of clay that have sagged and hardened into gentle, overlapping lobes. From these lobes hang irregular curtains of condensation that collect, swell, and drop at unpredictable intervals onto the mosaic below, leaving dark rings that never quite dry before the next drop arrives.

The air stays warm and close, thick with the mineral scent of wet clay and the sharper, greener smell of crushed leaves. Pockets of water gather wherever the floor tiles have settled into shallow basins; some are no larger than a cupped palm, others stretch the length of a forearm. In the larger ones float mats of pale root-threads that have found their way down from cracks overhead. The vegetation itself remains sparse—just these few threads, plus a gray-green lichen that coats the lower third of every vertical surface and the occasional tuft of tough grass sprouting from a seam where two walls meet. Yet the chamber feels crowded. Primitive timber frames lean against one another at odd angles, forming low platforms and bins that hold stores of dried roots, small clay vessels, and bundles of stripped bark. Between and beneath these structures the space is filled with movement: dark beetles the size of thumbs travel in steady processions along the grout lines; pale, long-legged lizards the color of wet stone flick across the tiles; and clusters of winged insects rise and fall in tight spirals above each water pocket, their wings producing a constant, dry rustling that never quite fades.

Light enters through a single shaft cut into the apex of the vault. At certain hours it falls in a clean column that strikes the central mosaic; the rest of the chamber receives only the softer illumination that bounces from the clay surfaces, enough to read the grain of wood or the pattern of lichen but never harsh. The soundscape is continuous and layered: the irregular percussion of condensation, the scraping of small claws on tile, the low creak of the sapling lattice expanding and contracting with the warmth, and, beneath it all, a slow, wet breathing sound from somewhere deeper in the walls, as though water is being drawn and released through hidden channels. No one surface remains untouched for long; every morning a fresh film of dust or a new scattering of seed husks appears, only to be trodden or scraped away again by the constant traffic of feet and bodies.

The rules of the place are unspoken but visible in its wear. Nothing grows quickly, yet nothing is ever allowed to remain still; every constructed surface is maintained by the same hands that once raised it, so that the Hollow keeps its shape even as the water, the insects, and the small warm bodies that move through it continually test its edges.
