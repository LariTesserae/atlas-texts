A single, unbroken plain of plant-tissue rises from the floor of an abyssal trench whose walls have long since subsided into the surrounding sediment. Everything that exists here was grown rather than built, yet every surface follows the logic of architecture: colossal vaults whose ribs are fused bundles of hollow, water-filled stems; avenues defined by parallel colonnades of petiole-like pillars spaced hundreds of body-lengths apart; and, overhead, a continuous canopy of overlapping fronds whose undersides are tessellated into shallow domes. Because the growth cycle reached completion in some unrecorded epoch and then stopped, no new tissue is added and none decays; the structures remain exactly as they settled, their once-living cells now mineralized to the translucency of sea-glass.

The water itself is the medium of the place. It fills every internal chamber and every external interval, carrying the same steady, pressure-equalizing current that has flowed unchanged for geologic time. Visibility ends at the limit of faint, cold blue that filters from unimaginable distances above; below that limit lies true darkness, relieved only by the slow pulses of light that move through the tissue itself—slow trains of bioluminescent droplets migrating along pre-formed conduits like sap, outlining the architecture in drifting constellations.

Fauna drift or lope between the distant pillars. Most are large, slow-moving vertebrates whose bodies are adapted to the same constant pressure and temperature; their calls are infrasonic and therefore loud, low-frequency wavefronts that travel for kilometers without losing force, striking the plant surfaces and returning as layered echoes that never vary. Occasional smaller, faster forms dart through the lattice of stems, but even these motions are contained within fixed corridors; they do not deviate from paths worn through the tissue over the same immense duration that shaped the structures.

There is no loose sediment. The substrate underfoot—if one can reach it—is another layer of the same plant mass, now compressed into a dense, slightly yielding pavement whose surface is patterned with faint hexagonal scars where rootlets once emerged and then withdrew. Between any two pillars the distance is great enough that a human crossing would feel, for many minutes, as though moving across open water rather than inside a constructed volume; yet the pillars remain visible at either horizon, and the overhead canopy continues without interruption. The entire expanse is therefore both empty and perfectly enclosed.

Nothing changes. The temperature gradient is absent; the current does not pulse; the migrating lights inside the stems follow identical routes at identical intervals. The fauna repeat the same circuits, their calls overlapping into a single, steady chord. The only measurable motion is the imperceptible advance of mineral deposition along the inner walls of the hollow stems, a process so slow it registers as stillness.
