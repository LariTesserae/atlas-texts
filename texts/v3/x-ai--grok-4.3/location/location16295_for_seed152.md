In a low pocket of raw, half-formed ground—sunken just enough that the surrounding terrain rises like unfinished ramparts—a miniature district is taking shape. The earth here has not yet decided what it will be. Walls of pale, unweathered clay stand at irregular heights, some no taller than a person’s waist, others already reaching overhead to cast long shadows. Their surfaces are still soft and slightly tacky, marked only by the slow settling of their own weight and the faint imprints of whatever has brushed against them. Condensation gathers along the upper edges and drips at long intervals, each drop landing with a single muted tap before soaking into the bare intervals of soil between the plants.

Vegetation has answered the constant dampness with sudden, crowded vigor wherever moisture lingers longest. Ferns and broad, waxy leaves push up in dense stands, their surfaces beaded and glinting even in the weak light. Between these stands the ground remains open, showing stretches of smooth, pale sediment and occasional shallow puddles that reflect nothing but the dim canopy above. Vines have already begun to stitch the clay surfaces together; some hang in loose festoons, others lie pressed flat, tracing faint green lines that will later become darker paths. The warmth keeps everything supple. There is no crispness in the air, only a steady, skin-warm humidity that makes the clay sweat and the leaves stay pliant.

Small lives move through the gaps without announcement. Pale beetles follow the seams where root and clay meet. Thin lizards rest on the half-walls, their sides pulsing so faintly the motion is felt rather than seen. In the largest puddle a pair of translucent newts drift, their gills opening and closing in slow rhythm. None of them produce sound louder than the occasional soft click of a carapace settling or the smallest ripple against a leaf edge. The larger quiet is filled instead by the continuous, almost inaudible working of water itself: the slow seepage through pores, the gathering of droplets, the patient loosening of mineral grains that will one day become channels.

Everything here is still under construction. The clay walls are not yet braced or capped; they lean wherever the next condensation runlet has encouraged them to settle. The vegetation is still deciding which pockets it will claim and which it will leave open. The creatures arrive and depart along routes that exist only because the ground happens to be soft enough to take a temporary print. No straight lines have been imposed, yet the emerging forms already suggest alcoves, passages, and low plazas—the first gestures of a settlement that is still mostly memory and moisture. In the dim warmth the whole pocket feels like a single, slow inhalation, holding its shape only as long as the condensation and the roots continue their quiet labor.
