The plateau stretches across hundreds of square kilometers of highland, an ancient surface worn by countless ages into a single, softly undulating plain that still sits far above the distant cloud line. Its bones are not stone anymore. They are interlaced root plates and petrified vascular bundles, flattened and re-flattened until every rise and shallow trough feels like the final settling of some immense, long-dead living thing. The temperature stays cool and even, never quite reaching warmth, so the perpetual condensation that beads along every curved surface never fully evaporates. Rivulets form in the hollows of leaves the size of district blocks, then spill into cup-like depressions where the water lingers for days before vanishing again into the living substrate.

Across this open reach, vegetation is not ground cover; it is the ground, the walls, the ceilings, the scaffolding. Gigantic stalks rise like unfinished towers, their surfaces plated with woody callus that has grown into load-bearing ribs and load-bearing rails. Between them lie broad clearings of thinner mat-plant, springy underfoot yet veined with slow-moving sap channels that glow faintly when the normal daylight strikes them. These clearings function as plazas and thoroughfares; the stalks themselves have been coaxed into multi-story volumes whose interior cavities once held machines and still do. Engines the color of bruised chlorophyll turn on sap pressure and resin gears, their pistons exhaling sweet, humid exhaust that drifts in visible sheets. The city is therefore not built upon the vegetation but extruded from it, layer after patient layer, joints sealed by living cambium that hardens only when the engineers prune it back.

Because the density remains low, these towers stand widely separated, sometimes hundreds of meters apart, so that the plateau feels both crowded and strangely empty. Wind moves through the gaps unimpeded, carrying the constant loud chorus of the place: the wet slap of condensation sheets falling from great heights, the low grinding of expanding xylem, the rhythmic clack of industrial valves opening inside hollow trunks, and an intermittent tearing sound whenever a pocket of reality thins. At such moments a section of air simply folds, revealing a brief, depthless view of the same plateau repeated at a different angle or a different hour; the fold seals again within seconds, leaving only a lingering scent of ozone and chlorophyll. These breaks occur most often along the old sap-rail lines, as though the industrial pulse itself frays the membrane between layers of the world.

Fauna is scarce. A few translucent, slow-moving slugs the length of freight cars migrate across the clearings at dusk, grazing on surface algae; otherwise the ecosystem runs on plant metabolism alone. Rain arrives in brief, intense curtains that drum against the woody plazas and then stop, leaving every surface jeweled and the air sharp with the smell of turned earth and machine oil. Growth is visible if one stands still long enough: a rail joint visibly lengthens over the course of an hour, a new conduit buds from a trunk like a slow green fist. The entire plateau therefore moves, not with haste but with the steady, unstoppable adjustments of an organism that finished eroding long ago and is now only refining its final, living form.
