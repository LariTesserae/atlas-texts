A high, cool plateau lies drowned beneath an unbroken skin of water, its surface so near the light that shafts of ordinary daylight still penetrate, green-tinged and slow-moving. The ground itself is living: vast, rope-thick stems of kelp and sea-cane have grown through centuries of quiet deposition until they form the solid substrate—broad, fibrous platforms, buttressed walls, and hollow colonnades that once served as streets and dwellings. These plant-formed structures rise in low, irregular terraces, their edges softened by long erosion; every surface has been rounded, pitted, and re-sculpted by the patient passage of currents until the original hand-worked edges are only faintly legible, like letters worn from an old coin.

Because the settlement is small—little larger than a single shaded square and its bordering lanes—the sparsest scattering of these plant-architecture remains still stands upright. Gaps between them are wide; a swimmer can drift the length of what was once a boulevard without touching either wall. In those open volumes, smaller gardens of bladder-wrack and lace-coral drift like untended plazas. Water moves through the lanes at the pace of seasons: a gradual exhalation from the deeper shelf below, cool and steady, carrying fragments of leaf and shell that settle, over decades, into new floor layers.

Light arrives from above in pale, shifting panes. It catches on the gas-filled floats of the canopy kelp, turning them into drifting lanterns that slowly rise and fall with the internal tides of the plants themselves. The air—now water—carries sound without hurry: the low, continuous groan of stems flexing against one another, the resonant knocks of larger fauna moving through hollow stalks far overhead, the occasional sharp report of a gas bladder bursting at the surface film. These noises accumulate rather than compete; the entire grove resonates at the frequency of a single, very slow bell.

Fauna is abundant yet never crowded. Pale, long-finned grazers the size of seals drift in loose schools, scraping diatoms from the plant walls with primitive stone-set teeth; their passage leaves pale scars that the kelp will take years to overgrow. Smaller, quick-eyed crustaceans live inside the hollow stems, their scraping audible as a constant soft percussion. Stranger forms appear at intervals: translucent, many-armed shapes that seem only half-animal, half-floating root, drifting on the same slow currents that rearrange the settlement’s loose fibers. Nothing moves quickly; metabolism and current alike have settled into the same measured rhythm.

The only evidence of deliberate making is the way certain stems were once guided—bent, lashed, or pruned—into door-like openings and level platforms. Those interventions have long since been absorbed; the plants continue the work now, thickening or thinning according to light and cool flow. The whole place feels both built and still building, an urban remnant half-dissolved into the living substrate that first supported it and then quietly replaced it.
