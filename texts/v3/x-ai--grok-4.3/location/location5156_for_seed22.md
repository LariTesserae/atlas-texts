The plain stretches for hundreds of kilometers in every direction, a single unbroken floor of pale, almost glassy basalt that only lately cooled into existence. Its surface is still faintly warm to the touch in patches, threaded with hairline fractures where steam occasionally hisses upward. Between those fractures the stone has already begun to collect a thin film of condensate that beads and runs in slow, branching rivulets every few hours, feeding shallow, temporary basins no deeper than a handspan. These basins are the only reliable water; elsewhere the plain is merely damp, the air itself carrying a perpetual fine mist that never quite becomes rain.

Scattered across this immensity are the nodes: dense, island-like clusters of vegetation and construction separated by kilometers of bare stone. Inside each node the growth is riotous. Towering fans of translucent black leaves unfold from jointed stems that curve overhead like the ribs of some enormous creature, their undersides veined with slow-moving sap that pulses with its own faint light. Between the stems rise the buildings, or rather the living scaffolds that have replaced buildings. Their walls are woven from the same dark vegetation interlaced with threads of something smoother and darker than metal, seamless where plant and structure meet. Walkways hang suspended between them on tensioned filaments that resonate at frequencies just below human hearing, turning every footstep into a low, continuous chord that travels the length of the node and beyond.

Farther out, where one node fades into the next empty stretch, the sound does not diminish; it merely changes character. The plain itself is never silent. Subterranean channels carry pressurized vapor that escapes through the fractures in intermittent, thunderous bursts, each lasting several minutes and rolling across the flat stone like distant artillery. Between these eruptions the filaments of the nearest node continue to sing, and the leaves of the black fans beat a slow, synchronized rhythm as they flex to capture the dim, copper-colored light that filters through the perpetual haze overhead. The combined volume is physically oppressive; conversations must be conducted by touch or by stepping into the lee of a stem where the air momentarily quiets.

Creatures move through this architecture without apparent fear of the noise. Many are small and numerous, scaled versions of the black-leaf plants themselves—mobile polyps that crawl along the filaments and sip the condensate. Others are larger, jointed silhouettes that glide between nodes on membrane wings held rigid by the same resonant threads. Their passage adds new overtones to the constant chord. All of them seem to treat the vast intervening emptiness as simply another medium; distances that would take a human hours to cross are traversed in minutes or disregarded entirely.

The entire arrangement appears recent, as though the plain, the nodes, and the life within them had been assembled only a few decades earlier and are still settling into their final configuration. New stems push upward through cracks each day; fresh filaments extend between existing towers like exploratory fingers. Nothing here looks worn or eroded—only unfinished. The light remains low, the air remains damp, and the sound never ceases. Only the locations of the next basin, the next stem, and the next resonant bridge remain to be discovered.
