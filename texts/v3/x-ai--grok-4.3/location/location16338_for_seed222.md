High on a weathered plateau where the rock has settled into long, flowing ridges rather than sharp peaks, the land opens outward in every direction under a sky that never quite dims. The stone itself is pale and salt-streaked, its surfaces rounded by centuries of condensation rather than hard rain; each morning a thin film of moisture beads along fissures and hollows, lingers for an hour, then vanishes into the warm air. These brief pockets collect in shallow basins no larger than a cupped hand, creating chains of tiny, evaporating oases that move across the plateau with the slow turn of light.

Vegetation clings only where the rock has cracked deepest. Thin, silvery filaments—half plant, half mineral—thread along the fissures, drawing moisture before it disappears. Their leaves are no bigger than scales, and they grow in broken, wandering lines that avoid one another by meters, leaving broad expanses of bare stone between them. Yet the emptiness is never truly empty. The same bright, steady light that warms the rock also reveals movement: clusters of translucent, many-legged grazers no larger than a palm drift across the open ground, their bodies catching and releasing faint prismatic flashes as they harvest the filaments. Larger shapes, angular and slow, glide between ridges on vanes of folded membrane that never quite touch the ground; they leave no prints, only momentary ripples in the air where gravity seems momentarily thinner.

Scattered across the same stone are fragments of something older and stranger than ruin. Low, curved shells of material that refuses to match the rock’s color or temperature rise at irregular intervals—sometimes no taller than a person, sometimes half-buried and spanning tens of meters. Their surfaces are smooth, unnaturally so, and they pulse once every several hours with a light too faint to see directly, only felt as a brief lessening of weight in the bones. Between these shells the plateau continues unbroken; nothing connects them into cities or machines, yet each shell locally alters the way moisture condenses, drawing threads of water vapor toward it like invisible roots.

Sound here is mostly absence. The wind, when it moves, does so high overhead, leaving the surface in a hush broken only by the soft, dry click of grazing fauna or the occasional crystalline chime when two membrane-gliders brush in passing. Even the advanced engineering embedded in the shells contributes to the quiet: whatever forces they once commanded now manifest only as subtle, constant corrections—temperature held just above comfortable, erosion held in patient equilibrium, the entire plateau suspended between decay and renewal by processes too fine to hear.

From any single ridge the eye travels outward across repeating sequences of stone, filament, shell, and drifting life, each element spaced by wide intervals of bare ground. The pattern does not repeat exactly, yet it never becomes crowded; the emptiness itself feels engineered, a deliberate medium in which the sparse living and the lingering artifacts can keep their distance and their balance. Water appears, vanishes, reappears elsewhere. Creatures feed, drift, vanish into the light. The stone continues its long, slow breathing.
