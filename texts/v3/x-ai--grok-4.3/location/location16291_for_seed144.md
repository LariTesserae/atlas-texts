High on a wind-scoured plateau that drops away in every direction for hundreds of miles, the land rises in long, broken steps of dark basalt and pale limestone that have spent centuries settling into one another. The heat is constant and close, rising from vents no wider than a hand that exhale slow, mineral-rich steam; even at twilight the rock holds the day’s temperature. Yet the air is never truly dry. Every morning a thin film of condensation beads across every horizontal surface—cool enough to gather, warm enough to evaporate again by noon—so that the same stones bear both crusts of salt and momentary threads of water that run into shallow pockets before vanishing.

Nothing grows tall here. Low mats of gray-green cushion plant hug the fissures, their leaves no larger than coins and spaced just far enough that the eye still reads the ground as mostly bare. Between them, however, the plateau feels crowded: every crack holds a cluster of something—lizards the color of rust, thumb-sized beetles whose shells click against the rock, and the occasional gaunt hare whose ears swivel at vibrations too faint for human hearing. They do not compete for vegetation so much as for the narrow margins of shade and moisture that collect beside half-buried machines.

Those machines are the scattered built presence: single turbines half-tilted into the ground, their blades fused with flowstone where geothermal water once trickled across them; low concrete pads whose edges have weathered into rounded shoulders; lengths of pipe that now serve as burrows. None operate at industrial scale, yet each still ticks or hums once an hour when pressure inside the vents shifts, a soft mechanical heartbeat the animals have learned to ignore. The overall silence is therefore not emptiness but restraint—wind moving across open stone, the faint tick of cooling metal, the brief scurry of feet across gravel.

The landscape remains active through these small, repeating cycles: steam rises, condenses, trickles, evaporates; a beetle drinks, is eaten, its shell returned to the dust; a turbine blade turns a quarter degree and stops again. Over centuries the forms have reached equilibrium—neither wilderness nor ruin, but a single weathered surface in which rock, metal, and the sparse living things occupy the same plane without crowding one another out.
