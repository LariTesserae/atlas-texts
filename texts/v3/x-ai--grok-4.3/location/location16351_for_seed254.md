Beneath the surface, the chamber opens like a single sunken room no larger than a modest hall, its boundaries pressed close by walls of living green. Thick mats of roots and vines have colonized every plane: floor, ceiling, and the ribs of stone that still show between them. The plants grow inward from the rock as much as outward from one another, knitting together into partitions, platforms, and low arches whose surfaces sweat a constant film of water. Drops gather along the undersides of broad leaves and fall at long intervals, each landing with a soft, repeating percussion that layers over the deeper mechanical drone.

The stone itself is old and balanced under its burden. Water has traced dark channels along its grain for centuries, rounding edges and leaving pale mineral stains, yet nothing here feels freshly carved. The rock has settled into its shape; the plants have followed those same settled lines, filling seams and hollows until the original architecture is only a faint memory beneath living mass. Warm air rises from vents set into the floor, carrying the mineral scent of seeping groundwater and the faint sweetness of bruised stems. Moisture condenses on every surface, turning the warm dimness into a steady, breathable haze that catches what little light reaches downward from grimed industrial lamps strung along the upper vine layers.

Metal pipes and brackets run through the vegetation like foreign arteries. Some are rusted and half-swallowed; others still carry slow pulses of pressurized fluid that make the leaves around them quiver. At intervals, low engines housed in cages of woven stalk and steel hum with patient rhythm, driving belts and screws whose motion is measured in minutes rather than seconds. The sound fills the space evenly—water striking leaves, the steady exhalation of valves, the creak of thick stems adjusting to their own weight—without any sudden spikes or silences. Nothing moves quickly. Leaves unfurl over days; droplets form and fall at their own measured pace; the engines turn with the unhurried certainty of machinery built for endurance rather than speed.

There are no gaps. Where one plant ends, another begins, stems braced against stems, roots threaded through roots. The effect is of a single continuous substrate that happens to contain corridors, work platforms, and reservoirs within its thickness. Water tables sit just below the living floor; when a section is disturbed, moisture wells up briefly before the surrounding roots draw it back down. Light remains low and even, filtered through successive canopies until it arrives as a soft, directionless glow that makes every surface appear slightly farther away than it is. The entire place feels occupied to its limit, yet settled, as though it reached this density long ago and now maintains itself through slow, self-correcting growth rather than expansion.
