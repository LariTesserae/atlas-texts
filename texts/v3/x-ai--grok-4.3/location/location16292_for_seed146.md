The place is an immense dry lattice beneath a planet’s skin, warm enough that the mineral air carries a steady faint heat but never stirs. Every surface is crystalline and freshly sharp: needle quartz, blade feldspar, and long fracture planes of dark glass that have not yet been dulled. Because nothing ever changes here, these edges remain exactly as they first cooled, each facet locked in equilibrium with its neighbors. The lattice repeats at every scale—narrow clefts no wider than a body, then sudden halls whose far walls are lost even to the faint internal light of the creatures that live among them—so that distance itself feels layered rather than linear.

The air contains no water and therefore no scent of rot or growth, only the dry metallic breath of the crystals themselves. In the darkness, the only illumination comes from the fauna: translucent, many-limbed mineral organisms whose shells are grown from the same quartz and feldspar that make the walls. They move so slowly that their motion registers more as a redistribution of weight than as travel; when one shifts position, the faint click of shell against crystal travels only a short distance before it is absorbed by the dense press of bodies and surfaces. Most remain still for what would be centuries on any changing world, their interiors faintly glowing with stored geothermal warmth.

Scattered among the living lattice are small, deliberate constructions: low rings of sorted mineral shards, shallow basins pecked into the glass, and occasional straight rods of fused stone balanced across crystal ledges. Each artifact sits exactly where it was placed; dust does not settle, crystals do not overgrow them, and nothing topples or erodes. The same fauna that glow and rest also maintain these objects, nudging a rod back into alignment with the same unhurried care they use to settle their own shells against a neighbor.

Because the system is closed and time-invariant, the creatures neither breed nor die in any observable way. Their density remains constant; when one finally dissolves, its substance is taken up atom by atom into the surrounding lattice and redistributed elsewhere, preserving the overall count and arrangement. Sound is therefore rare and brief: a single soft chime when a shell settles into a new cradle, or the low, almost inaudible pulse of heat exchange between two touching bodies. The warmth never varies, the darkness never lifts, and the sharp mineral edges never blunt, so the entire volume—continent-sized, planet-spanning—remains exactly as it has always been, a single unchanging arrangement of crystal, warmth, and slow, dense life.
