In the cool dimness of this low basin, the ground rises not as soil but as a pale, compacted mineral crust—layered sheets of translucent halite and gypsum that have only lately buckled upward. The crust is young: its edges remain knife-like, their fracture planes still bright where they split from whatever pressure forced them into being days or weeks before. Between these low ridges the flat lies empty, an expanse of fine white grit no thicker than a handspan over an impermeable floor of darker stone. Distance here is measured in a few dozen strides; the entire place feels enclosed, a single chamber cut from a larger world.

Scattered across the flats stand the towers. They are not ruins and not built things exactly, yet they mimic both. Each is a sheaf of interlocking crystal columns, some no taller than a person, others twice that height, their facets catching what little light filters down through the perpetual haze. In places the columns lean together like the walls of abandoned rooms; in others they stand alone, spaced so widely that crossing from one to the next requires long minutes of walking across nothing. The towers continue to adjust themselves—faces re-align by fractions of a degree, new seams open with a sound too faint even for the quiet air to carry.

Nothing grows in the ordinary sense. Yet across the lower third of certain columns a sparse, almost colorless film has taken hold: thin, vein-like mats that pulse once every several minutes, drawing faint ions from the mineral surface. The mats leave behind delicate traceries of etched glass. Between the towers, nothing moves at human scale. Only now and then a single thread-like creature, no longer than a finger, slides across the grit, leaving a momentary darker line that the dry air soon erases. Its passage is the loudest event the basin offers.

The air itself is active in slow cycles. Every so often a cooler current descends from the unseen ceiling of haze, stirring the finest particles into slow, low spirals that coil between the towers before settling again. The spirals rearrange the surface into faint radial patterns that last until the next descent. Temperature drops a few degrees during these moments, then eases back; the crystal columns contract and expand by the width of a hair, producing the only audible phenomenon—a single, glass-clear ping that fades before it can be named an echo.

Light never strengthens. It remains the same even, directionless dimness, as if the basin lies beneath a shallow but permanent overcast. In this half-light the white crust and translucent towers appear almost luminous from within, while the empty intervals between them remain pools of deeper shadow. Nothing here suggests haste or conclusion; only the continuous, barely perceptible work of mineral realignment, of rare threads crossing empty ground, of cool air turning in patient spirals.
