High on a single stone shelf no wider than a cathedral transept, the mountain has worn itself into a shallow, roofless chamber over ages. The rock is pale basalt, veined and honeycombed by long, patient erosion; every surface has been rounded and dimpled until it looks more grown than carved. Water weeps from hairline cracks in the upper walls, tracing slow, silver lines that catch what little light filters through drifting mist. The droplets collect in shallow basins that overflow into narrow runnels, threading across the floor and vanishing over the edge into nothing.

Between these runnels the rock is patched with dense, low mats of moss and small-leafed cushion plants, their colors shifting from deep emerald to a wet, almost luminous blue-green where they drink the constant seepage. The vegetation never rises more than knee-height, yet it covers every usable surface so thickly that the stone seems upholstered. Thin vines hang from the lip of the overhang, their roots drinking air as much as rock. No trees can root here; the shelf is too small, the winds too relentless. Instead the green has learned to lie flat and drink sideways.

The air stays cool and heavy with suspended moisture. Breath condenses on lips and lashes. Every surface sweats; even the scattered remnants of earlier hands—three low rings of fitted stones, a single obsidian blade snapped at the tip—wear a permanent film of droplets. The structures are primitive: no mortar, only gravity and the patient fitting of what the mountain already offered. They sit empty now, half-buried in the living carpet.

Life moves, but always at a distance. Small, quick-footed lizards with translucent skin dart between the moss hummocks, leaving momentary tunnels that close behind them. Larger shapes—shaggy, low-slung herbivores the size of dogs—graze the cushions in short, nervous bursts before vanishing around the curve of the wall. Their calls are sharp and echoing; the constant drip of water and the low, booming passage of wind through the great hollows below turn every sound into a layered chord. The wind itself is the loudest presence, pressing against the chest like a living thing, carrying the distant thunder of rockfall far below and the higher, thinner cries of things that hunt the updrafts.

Nothing here crowds; the scale of even the smallest living thing is dwarfed by the sheer drop on three sides and the immense, mist-filled vault above. Life clings in pockets separated by spans of bare, weeping stone that feel like distances. The shelf exists in its own slow weather—mist rises, water falls inward, plants drink, animals move, wind reshapes the mist—and the whole system continues, balanced between the violence of height and the persistence of water.
