The landscape unfolds across a high continental shelf, a vast and weathered plateau lifted just far enough above the world’s breath to feel its own thin, restless air yet still hot enough that every surface radiates a slow, steady fever. Long ago the rock was carved into broad, sloping terraces whose edges have rounded like old bone; erosion has settled into equilibrium, so the stone no longer yields dramatic cliffs but instead gentle, patient undulations carpeted in living tissue.  

Between these swells stretch enormous clearings—sometimes kilometers across—where the ground is only faintly dusted with pale, salt-tolerant lichens. Elsewhere the vegetation asserts total dominion: colossal, interlocking mats of thick, rubbery stems rise in slow arcs and vaults, their leaves layered so densely they form habitable ceilings and walls. These living architectures are not forests in the usual sense but continuous scaffolds of flesh and fiber, veined with channels that carry both sap and captured rainwater. The plants themselves are the primary substance of the place; soil is thin and rare, appearing only in pockets where older growth has died and slumped into loam.  

Moisture arrives in intermittent pulses—night-long mists that condense along the upper surfaces of the biggest leaves, sudden cloudbursts that drum for an hour and then vanish, leaving the air damp for a day before the heat reclaims it. Water lingers longest in the shaded concavities of the plant-structures: shallow, tea-colored pools cupped by foliage, or slow trickles that follow the natural grooves of stems down into the clearings, where they disappear into the porous rock.  

Fauna moves through this lattice like a second circulatory system. Swarms of heavy-bodied insects the size of fists patrol the leaf highways, their wings producing a constant, layered drone that scales into outright thunder when millions lift at once. Larger, slower creatures—segmented herbivores whose backs sprout symbiotic moss—graze methodically along the stem-roads, their passage marked by resonant creaks and the wet slap of displaced water. Predators are present but rarely seen; they announce themselves through sudden silences or by the low, subsonic thrumming that travels through the living floor. The combined noise is deafening at most hours, rising and falling with the daily cycles of light and condensation.  

Scattered across the clearings and half-swallowed by the encroaching growth lie remnants of earlier inhabitants: single-story frames of hand-shaped stone and cured fiber, their roofs long since replaced by living canopies. Crude tools—flaked obsidian blades bound to wooden hafts, simple clay vessels still holding traces of pigment—lie half-buried where vines have lifted and shifted them over centuries. These artifacts are never numerous, always isolated, as though whoever made them moved through the same sparse intervals that separate today’s living masses.  

Light arrives evenly, filtered to a soft bronze by the high haze yet strong enough to keep the photosynthetic engines working at full tilt. Heat radiates upward from every surface; the air itself shimmers above the larger clearings. In this constant warmth and steady biological churn, the world maintains its own slow weather: localized updrafts born inside the plant-architecture carry scent and spores for kilometers before releasing them in sudden, fragrant downdrafts when the growth cools at dusk.  

Nothing here is still for long. Leaves reorient toward the moving light, water migrates along capillary routes, swarms rise and settle in visible pulses. The rules are simple and unbending: growth continues, moisture returns at its own intervals, and any structure that cannot flex or be absorbed eventually vanishes beneath new layers of living mass.
