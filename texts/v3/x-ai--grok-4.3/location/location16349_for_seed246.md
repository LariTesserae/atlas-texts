The place is a high, wind-scoured basin no larger than a small cathedral close, its floor flattened by eons of slow attrition until the rock lies almost level beneath a thin skin of pale gravel and black lichen. Elevation keeps the air thin and sharp; temperature rarely climbs above freezing, yet moisture arrives in fitful pulses—nighttime condensation beading every horizontal surface, sudden brief rains that turn the gravel into a mirror for an hour, then vanish into cracks. The water collects in shallow, perfectly circular depressions worn into the stone, each depression ringed by a collar of frost-resistant cushion plants whose leaves glint metallic green even under ordinary daylight.

These plants are the only lush element: dense emerald mats no taller than a handbreadth, their roots prying into microscopic fissures and softening the rock into something almost like soil. Between the mats the ground feels empty, hollow, the distances between each clump measured in body-lengths rather than inches. Nothing fills those intervals except drifting veils of mist that form and dissolve on their own slow schedule, giving the basin a breathing rhythm visible to the eye.

Scattered across this open floor stand low stone clusters that read at first as natural outcrops, then as something arranged. Each cluster is a tight grouping of flat slabs tilted together like the pages of a half-closed book, their surfaces etched by ancient water into flowing channels that still carry trickles when the air grows heavy. The clusters are spaced far enough apart that walking from one to the next feels like crossing plazas; yet their alignment follows a loose radial pattern, as though the rock itself once flowed toward a common center now worn away. No tools shaped them. They are simply the final posture into which the mountain settled after uncountable years of settling, cracking, and re-sealing.

Sound does not behave as it should in such emptiness. Every drip from a condensing slab lands with a resonant click that carries across the entire basin; wind threading between the tilted stones produces a low, continuous chord that shifts pitch when the mist thickens. Occasional deeper booms arrive from below, the hollow-stone echo of subterranean water moving through unseen galleries the ancient erosion opened long ago. These sounds arrive without visible source and depart again, leaving the cold air ringing.

Rare fauna moves through the intervals between clusters—slow, segmented creatures the color of wet slate that travel only at the moment condensation begins to form. They pause at each circular pool to drink, then continue, their passage marked by faint scraping rather than footfalls. The vegetation tolerates them; the stone tolerates everything. Nothing here is hurried, yet nothing is still. Condensation, wind, the slow slide of gravel downslope, the incremental widening of a fissure by a single frost crystal—the processes continue at their own measured pace, each one old enough to have become its own law. In this small, high room of the world, the final form of the mountain is still quietly rearranging itself, loud with the sound of its own patience.
