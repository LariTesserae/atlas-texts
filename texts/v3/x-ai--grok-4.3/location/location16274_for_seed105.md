The place is a high drowned tableland, suspended beneath an unbroken membrane of falling water that never reaches a surface. From the black, the plateau rises in broad, worn steps—each level a several-kilometer shelf of pale, pressure-hardened stone veined with darker minerals that have settled into smooth, ancient channels. Water moves through these channels in slow, deliberate rivers that never quite stop; they carry threads of colder, heavier liquid that sink and rise again in looping currents, keeping the whole expanse in slow, constant circulation.

Vegetation grows everywhere the currents allow purchase. Tall, translucent fronds root in the stone and reach upward in loose, drifting canopies, their surfaces covered in fine crystalline growths that absorb the faint pressure-borne vibrations and turn them into steady, internal light. The light is cool and blue-green, enough to reveal form but never to illuminate distance; beyond a few dozen meters the fronds and the stone simply fade into uniform darkness. Between the larger stands lie wide, empty stretches of polished rock scoured by centuries of the same current, so the gardens feel both dense and strangely open, as if the plants themselves have chosen to leave corridors clear.

Scattered across the shelves are the remains of older construction: low rings of hand-fitted blocks, some still joined by simple woven lashings that have petrified into mineral ropes; a few upright slabs whose edges have been worn into soft curves; occasional platforms that once stood above the flow and now sit just submerged, their tops furred with shorter, tougher growth. Nothing metal remains; everything is stone, shell-derived cement, or the same pressure-hardened wood that now serves the living fronds as substrate. The structures are never close together. Each is an isolated artifact, half-buried by slow deposition and half-revealed again by the same currents that feed the plants.

Sound travels far and without interruption. The dominant note is a low, continuous roar produced by the meeting of the descending atmospheric water and the plateau’s own rising currents; beneath it run sharper, metallic clicks as mineral grains tumble along the channels, and a deeper, almost vocal groan that rises whenever a large stand of fronds shifts position in unison. The noise never drops below the threshold of hearing, yet it carries no living voices—only the friction of water against stone and the slow stretching of the vegetation itself.

No mobile creatures move through this volume. The plants have evolved to capture and metabolize the constant rain of suspended particles directly; they exchange nutrients along shared root networks that spread across the stone in pale, branching mats. Where two frond systems meet they fuse rather than compete, creating larger, slower individuals that may span an entire shelf. Over time the networks thicken and redirect the currents, so the plateau continues to reshape itself even while its overall form remains balanced and settled. The water is cold enough that any unprotected organic matter would quickly lose flexibility; the plants protect themselves with glassy sheaths and internal warmth generated from the mechanical energy of the flow.

At the scale of the plateau the darkness is not empty. It is a medium in which pressure, temperature, and steady motion remain in equilibrium, and in which the only motion that accumulates is the patient rearrangement of stone and living tissue under the same long-established forces.
