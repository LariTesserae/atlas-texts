The landscape rises in a single, unbroken ascent that never crests, a vast tilted table of stone and living matter that stretches beyond any single view. At this height the air is fierce and unmovingly hot, yet water refuses to leave; it beads constantly on every surface, drawn from deep rock veins and from the thick, slow breath of the growth itself. The vegetation does not grow upward so much as it accumulates—layer upon layer of broad, waxy leaves, rope-thick stems, and mineral-hardened root plates that press against one another until the distinction between trunk, wall, and ground dissolves.  

These accumulations have taken the form of long, curving terraces and staggered vertical masses that read, from a distance, like the stacked districts of an abandoned city now swallowed by its own gardens. Where one terrace ends, another begins a few body-lengths higher, connected by sloped causeways slick with black algae and white mineral runoff. Water films every edge and drips from the undersides of the broad leaves, striking the levels below in an unvarying, thunderous rhythm that does not rise or fall with weather or hour. The sound fills every cavity; it is the same roar inside a hollow stem as it is on the open terraces, so constant that silence has never occurred here.  

Nothing moves that has not always moved. The erosion that once carved the underlying stone has long since reached equilibrium with the living weight above it; the great buttresses and hanging gardens sit exactly where they settled centuries ago, their edges rounded but not crumbling, their lines preserved by the same mineral secretions that now feed the next layer of leaves. Light arrives in ordinary measure, neither amplified nor swallowed, yet because the structures interleave so densely the illumination is always dappled, always arriving through successive planes of translucent green and wet grey stone.  

The absence of animals is not felt as emptiness; the only inhabitants are the layered canopies themselves. In some places the vegetation has folded space oddly: a single terrace may appear twice if viewed from certain angles, the duplication fixed and permanent, a scar in the world that never widens or heals. In other places a narrow gap between two root walls opens onto a deeper drop than the eye expects, the drop itself lined with the same unchanging gardens all the way down. These anomalies do not disturb the equilibrium; they are simply additional fixed features of the place, as settled as the terraces and the constant water.  

At every scale the wetness persists. Even the highest, most exposed crowns of the biological towers remain beaded, because the heat draws moisture upward from the saturated lower layers as steadily as it draws it from the weeping rock. The entire region therefore remains a single, dense, open, and perfectly balanced system of stone, growth, falling water, and permanent roar, extending without variation across the long slope and into the white haze that marks its farther edges.
