The land is an ancient silence made material: an open, mid-scale basin of dry mineral flats ringed by monoliths that rise from the floor like the last ribs of something long finished. These towers are not mountains in the usual sense; they are the final, settled wreckage of earlier ranges, reduced by unthinkable time until only their cores remain—broad pillars of compacted salt, gypsum, and glassy silicates that still reach extreme height, their surfaces rounded and almost featureless, worn past the point of useful detail. Between them the floor is dense with smaller presences: low, overlapping plates of the same material, layered like fossil armor, each plate no thicker than a hand yet stacked until the footing becomes uneven and close. Nothing shifts underfoot. Nothing continues to fall.

No liquid exists here, not even trace films. Chemistry occurs along grain boundaries and through slow exchange of ions between solids; the air itself feels faintly gritty, carrying sub-visible motes that settle and do not rise again. Sparse vegetation clings in the protected angles: brittle, paper-thin mineral fibers that grow only a few centimeters before hardening into an unchanging pale lattice, their color a desaturated dun that absorbs normal daylight without reflection or shadow play.

Overwhelming numbers of small fauna occupy every unoccupied volume. They are not moving animals so much as dense, translucent shells—ellipsoids the size of thumbs, faceted like early-carved flint yet soft at the edges. They hang at fixed intervals, suspended at every height from ankle to the upper reaches of the monoliths, forming a kind of living sediment that thickens toward the center of the basin. At certain angles the shells catch light and become briefly opaque; at others they disappear entirely, leaving only a faint refractive distortion that marks where each one still is. Their arrangement is absolute; no drift occurs.

Scattered through this volume are faint human traces. A few low cairns of the same mineral plates have been stacked by hand into unstable-looking tripods that nevertheless never collapse. Near some of them lie single tools: a narrow wedge of polished silicate still seated in the position where it was last pressed into a crevice, its edge blunted by use yet free of further wear. These objects feel neither recent nor abandoned; they register as permanent features that simply happen to record an earlier action.

Reality thins in places. Along the bases of the tallest monoliths, small patches of terrain repeat identically a short distance away, the duplication exact down to the placement of individual mineral plates and suspended shells. The duplicated zone does not move; it simply exists twice at once, the line of overlap marked by a faint vertical seam where light bends without scattering. Crossing the seam produces no sensation. The world remains cool, evenly lit by a featureless sky, and without sound beyond the occasional soft click of one shell tapping another when a viewer’s motion shifts the angle of view.

The entire arrangement holds in perfect equilibrium. Nothing grows, nothing falls, nothing erodes further. The landscape is already complete.
