In the high, cool throat of an underwater ridge still knitting itself together, a single vaulted cavity no larger than a cathedral holds an entire dense world. Fresh mineral blades and spires rise from every surface, their edges still raw from recent fracture, too young for the smoothing work of current or time. These formations pack so tightly that their tips nearly brush one another, leaving only narrow, sinuous lanes of water between them. Light arrives at ordinary strength from above, filtered through miles of clear water into a steady, shadowless blue that makes every crystalline plane glint like wet steel.

The water itself is slow-moving, a patient circulation that carries faint threads of dissolved metals in long, unhurried loops. Yet the sound inside the chamber is constant and loud: a layered chorus of low grinding as the spires micro-adjust to one another, punctuated by the sudden, resonant clicks of fauna striking their flanks. Creatures the size of forearms drift or crawl in uncountable numbers, their bodies plated in the same living mineral that forms the walls. They move with deliberate, almost mechanical patience, rasping against one another or against the spires in rhythms that last minutes rather than seconds. Where two plates meet, faint electric flickers travel like welding arcs, brief and cool, leaving behind new glassy sutures that strengthen the structure without ever building anything that could be called an artifact.

No living plant or fungal thread exists here. Instead, the mineral matrix itself performs a kind of slow, distributed chemistry, precipitating and re-dissolving in gradients too gentle to notice except over hours. The abundant fauna feed directly on these micro-reactions, scraping fresh surfaces and in turn depositing waste that the next slow cycle reincorporates. Their presence makes the water feel crowded; every drift of current carries dozens of bodies sliding past at near-touching distances, their faint bioluminescent seams adding pinpricks of steady light to the ordinary illumination.

The whole cavity breathes on a timescale measured in tides rather than waves. When pressure shifts by a fraction, entire constellations of spires answer with a single, deep chord of grinding that lingers, then fades. Nothing is hurried. The sharp edges remain sharp; the dense population remains dense; the cool water stays cool; and the loud, continuous texture of sound continues without urgency, as though the chamber has only just remembered how to be loud and has not yet grown tired of it.
