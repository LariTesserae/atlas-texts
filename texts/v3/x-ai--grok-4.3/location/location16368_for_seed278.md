The high plain stretches without edges, a continental shield lifted so far into the thin air that the horizon itself feels curved. Its surface is not flat but settled into long, patient undulations—broad swells of stone rounded by eons of slow frost and infrequent water, the kind of erosion that no longer fights the land but has become its shape. Between these swells lie shallow basins where night after night condensation gathers into temporary sheets no deeper than a hand. By midday most of the sheets are gone, leaving only dark rings of mineral residue that the next cold will crack and the next slow wind will drift across.

Nothing built interrupts the expanse. Instead the rock itself carries the memory of industry: faint, branching veins of crystalline lattice that still conduct faint chemical gradients, as though the stone once hosted vast, heatless reactions and has never quite stilled. When moisture lingers in the basins these lattices darken and release threads of vapor that drift only a few body-lengths before re-condensing on the next chilled surface. The process is nearly silent; only the rare, resonant click of contracting crystal can be heard if one stands motionless for a long while.

Vegetation clings in isolated colonies—low, black-green cushions no wider than a boot print, spaced so that each colony occupies its own faint thermal shadow. They draw what water they can from the overnight films and then seal themselves beneath a waxy rind until the next damp arrives. Between them the stone remains bare, save for the slow passage of the land’s true inhabitants.

These are the fauna. Numberless, they move at speeds measured in days: fist-sized lithic forms whose interiors contain grinding plates of the same crystal lattice that veins the ground. They ingest the mineral residue left by evaporating water, reduce it, and excrete new, slightly altered grains that settle into the next layer of the ancient pavement. Because each creature travels only a body-length or two in a full rotation of the world, their overwhelming numbers register less as motion than as a continuous, nearly invisible reworking of the surface. At certain angles of the dim, constant half-light their carapaces catch and return a faint metallic sheen, so that from a distance the plain sometimes appears dusted with motionless frost that is not frost.

Above the plain the atmosphere holds its own weather—slow cyclonic cells that drift for seasons before dissipating, never hurried, never violent. When one of these cells passes, the temperature drops another fraction and the basins fill once more; when it departs the surface re-freezes along the same established fracture lines that have opened and closed for millennia. Sound travels poorly through the cold, rarefied air; even the gentle settling of stone or the faint internal rasp of a lithic grinder arrives already attenuated, more felt in the soles than heard.

The place does not hurry toward any future form. Its vastness, its height, its industrial memory, and its innumerable slow inhabitants all sustain one another in a balance older than any single cycle of moisture or frost.
