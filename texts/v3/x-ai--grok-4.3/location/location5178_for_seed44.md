A low basin of living mass presses close on every side, a single bounded district perhaps two hundred paces across where plant and settlement have become indistinguishable. Broad, slow-braided channels of warm water thread between root-walls and collapsed brick, their surfaces always filmed with a faint mist that condenses on every leaf and ledge before sliding back into the dark. The ground is never quite solid; each step sinks a little into a springy felt of moss, peat, and fine root hairs that release a warm, sweet exhalation when compressed.

Everything stands at cathedral height, no taller than the tallest trees that once grew here, yet the space feels densely layered. Vines the thickness of mooring cables form the main structural members, woven through and around the remains of older buildings until lintels, railings, and the casings of long-stilled engines are held in a permanent green lattice. Newer growth has filled the gaps: walls of tightly packed stems and leaves function as both partition and insulation, their surfaces beaded with condensation that drips in patient, audible rhythms onto the water below. Where industrial frames still show, they are scaled to the same measure—low platforms, narrow catwalks, belt housings—now softened and connected by the same living fabric.

The air stays warm and still except where the slow movement of water or the occasional working of an archaic engine stirs it. Those engines—belt-driven, low-revolution things housed in vine-choked sheds—turn at the pace of the surrounding growth, their gears coated in a living patina that must be cleared only once every few seasons. Their sound is constant: a low, many-layered thrum that travels through wood and water alike, punctuated by the heavier drip of condensed droplets falling from high leaves into the channels. The noise fills the small district without urgency, absorbed and returned by the surrounding mass so that no single source ever feels sharp.

Fauna keeps largely to the hidden seams—thin lizards that move only at the cycle of light change, translucent snails along the undersides of root bridges—yet their scarcity leaves the soundscape to the water, the leaves, and the machinery. Light arrives filtered, ordinary in color, arriving from above through a loose canopy that parts just enough for diffuse daylight to reach the walkways and the dark water. Nothing here moves quickly; erosion has long since reached equilibrium, the channels shifting their banks by centimeters across years, the vine architecture settling rather than collapsing. The district holds its own slow shape, a warm, loud, patient pocket where plant mass and old industrial rhythm continue together at the same measured pace.
