Beneath the crust, the world unfolds as a single, unfinished organism. Vast galleries of stone stretch for kilometers in every direction, their ceilings low enough to feel the weight of the planet yet high enough that the far walls dissolve into pale distance. No rivers cut these halls; moisture exists only as a faint mineral film that coats every surface and never gathers into droplets. The air moves in slow, dry loops, carrying the coolness of depths that never feel sunlight yet somehow receive a steady, even illumination—light that arrives already diffused, as if the rock itself had learned to remember day.

The floor and walls are not separate from life. They are life: a continuous carpet of pale, leathery tissue that rises in low, curved ridges and shallow basins. These living planes are thick enough to walk upon without sound, their surfaces etched with fine, branching channels that conduct gases instead of water. Here and there the tissue thickens into architectural elements still half-formed—slender columns that have not yet decided whether they will become walls or pipes, broad platforms whose edges ripple outward in slow, vegetative pulses. The growth is deliberate; entire districts remain embryonic, their outlines visible more as suggestions of future streets than as finished construction.

Sparse among these living foundations stand the worked elements: industrial shells of dark metal and glass that rise directly from the tissue like barnacles. Some are little more than open frames whose girders are still being enveloped by slow, creeping growth; others contain visible machinery—slow-turning vanes, belt drives that move without haste, low coils that emit a constant, almost inaudible vibration. The plants and machines appear to be negotiating their joint future; where they meet, the living matrix has begun to sheath the metal in a translucent, nacreous layer that will one day become both insulation and conduit.

Fauna moves through the spaces between these emerging structures. Pale, many-legged creatures the size of dogs travel along the channels in the living floor, harvesting gas nodules that swell and burst at regular intervals. Larger, flatter forms drift just above the surface on broad, membrane wings, their undersides sifting the mineral film for trace compounds. None of them call; the only sounds are the soft rasp of tissue expanding, the dry click of a vane completing its rotation, and the occasional sigh of pressurized gas escaping a maturing stalk. The quiet is not emptiness but concentration—the soundtrack of a place still deciding what shape it will finally take.

Farther out, the same pattern repeats at regional scale: kilometer-wide terraces of the same living substrate stepping downward into darkness, each level carrying its own scattered constellation of half-built factories and open frameworks. Between them lie wide, empty basins where nothing has yet risen. These basins are not vacant; the living floor continues across them, its surface gently heaving as though breathing in slow motion, waiting for the next round of construction to decide what to become. The entire landscape remains cool, dry, and in motion, an unfinished city still drawing itself up out of raw geology and living matter at once.
