Beneath the skin of the world lies a labyrinth of vaults whose ceilings stretch beyond sight, their heights lost in a perpetual haze of faint, self-generated luminance. The light does not fall from above; it seeps outward from veins of pale crystal running through the stone like marrow, bright enough to cast crisp shadows yet cool enough that breath fogs and lingers. Stone here has been worked and then left alone for ages, its edges rounded by time rather than tools. What remains are broad plazas of fused rock, low walls no higher than a person’s waist, and clusters of shallow basins that once guided water but now hold only occasional condensation rings. The surfaces feel slick one moment, powdery the next, as though the rock itself exhales a thin film of moisture before drawing it back in.

Nothing green grows. Instead, the mineral fabric of the place hosts thin, branching seams of living stone that pulse with slow, internal light when disturbed. These seams are the only structures that move under their own power, extending filaments across open floor space over decades and then retracting them just as slowly. Between these filaments the ground is largely bare, a pale, gritty expanse interrupted at great intervals by hand-shaped monoliths and low, roofless enclosures whose doorways face one another across empty courtyards. The builders who raised them worked with flint wedges and fire-hardened levers; the marks of those tools remain as shallow, repetitive scars along every upright surface.

Despite the emptiness, life is plentiful. Small, translucent creatures the size of spread hands drift just above the floor, buoyed by internal gas bladders that catch the crystal light and turn it faintly iridescent. They graze on airborne dust and on microscopic flakes that flake from the living stone seams. Larger, six-legged forms—each limb tipped with a single hooked claw—patrol the perimeters of the plazas in loose spirals, their carapaces clicking against stone only when two pass too close. Their motion is constant yet nearly silent; the only sounds are the soft, periodic drip of condensation gathering on an overhanging crystal and the faint friction of bodies brushing past one another in the open air.

Periodically the entire vault trembles. The trembling is not earthquake but something nearer to breathing: a slow heave that lifts thin plates of stone a finger’s width before letting them settle again. During these episodes the crystal veins flare brighter for several heartbeats, the drifting fauna rise higher, and loose mineral dust lifts in slow, localized spirals before falling. Then the motion subsides, the light dims to its ordinary steady brightness, and the wide, half-empty plazas return to stillness. Water appears in the aftermath as fleeting films inside the ancient basins, evaporating within hours yet leaving behind faint mineral tracery that records each visitation.

Over everything hangs the sense of an immense, unfinished civic project abandoned not by catastrophe but by simple completion. The primitive builders never erected roofs because none were needed; the light and the cool air are constant. They spaced their structures widely because distance itself was the intended material—rooms of emptiness between walls of stone. The fauna, the living seams, and the slow heaving of the rock continue the work now, rearranging dust, etching new faint lines, and keeping the ancient plazas from ever becoming either fully deserted or fully occupied.
