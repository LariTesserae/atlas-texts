In the high, young latitudes of a continent still buckling upward from its own recent birth, the land rises in knife-edged ridges and freshly sheared plateaus where stone has not yet learned softness. The air is cold enough that breath hangs in slow, crystalline veils, yet the scale of the place is so immense that local weather systems form and dissolve inside single valleys—tiny storms that never reach the horizon. Between the ridges lie gaps large enough to swallow cities, empty volumes where wind travels unhindered for hours before striking the next wall of growth.

That growth is total. Every surface that can hold moisture is claimed by a single, continuous organism whose body is expressed as myriad interlocking forms: vast curtains of black-green fronds that function as both leaf and root, lattices of woody tubules that carry water upward against the cold, and hanging sheets of pale translucence that condense mist into slow, audible drips. The plants do not stand apart from the ground; they are the ground, the architecture, the only substrate. Cavities large as cathedrals open inside the living mass where internal circulation has simply ceased for a time, leaving hollow galleries lined with glistening condensation films. Occasional pockets of free water collect in these galleries—shallow, black, perfectly still until a drip from above sets concentric rings moving.

Light barely arrives. What little filters through the kilometers of overlapping growth arrives already filtered to a permanent twilight, so the interior world is lit only by the faint blue-white phosphorescence of certain root strands and by the occasional flash of an animal’s bioluminescent signaling. The darkness magnifies sound. The entire organism resonates like a single wooden instrument: low, groaning flexures travel through the lattices when wind shifts, sharp reports echo when a tubule contracts to move water against gravity, and a constant, soft rain of condensation falls in overlapping rhythms that never quite synchronize. Louder still are the voices of the fauna that have evolved to inhabit the voids. Colonies of long-limbed, gliding creatures call across the empty distances with infrasonic pulses that the plant walls turn into rolling thunder; smaller, swarming flyers produce a dry, metallic scraping as their wing scales rub against the same surfaces they feed upon.

Because the formation is young, the ridges remain angular, their freshly exposed rock faces still streaked with mineral veins that the plants have only begun to colonize. Here and there, half-absorbed into the living substrate, rest the traces of earlier visitors: low, circular platforms of stacked stone, each no larger than a single chamber, their edges already softened by root pressure. Crude hand-axes and adzes of polished basalt lie wedged inside the fibrous walls where hands once left them, slowly being drawn downward as the plant grows around its own wounds.

The ecosystem moves on multiple overlapping clocks. Sap surges on a cycle measured in days rather than seasons; entire galleries will fill with water and then empty again as the lattice contracts. Predators glide between plant towers on membranes stretched across the voids, while herds of smaller, browsing forms migrate along the interior root roads that connect distant massifs. The emptiness between these towers is not void of life but merely vast; the next living wall may lie fifty kilometers distant, yet the wind that crosses the gap still carries spores, scent, and the low-frequency warnings of distant movement. In this place distance itself behaves like another material—thin, resonant, and saturated with the steady, cold music of water returning to the living mass that holds it.
