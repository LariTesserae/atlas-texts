The landscape unfolds as a chain of colossal, time-smoothed mesas and spires lifting into a perpetual haze, each summit broad enough to contain entire vanished cities yet narrow enough that their edges drop away into vaporous depths. At this height the air thins to a shivering clarity, yet the rock itself radiates a steady, skin-pricking warmth that keeps the entire region in a state of simmering humidity. Water does not fall from above so much as it is born from the stone: every cliff and outcrop weeps slow rivulets that trace centuries-old channels, collecting in shallow, steaming basins where the surface never fully cools. The basins brim and overflow into hanging gardens of root and leaf, then rejoin the weeping cycle lower down, a closed loop that has been running longer than any living observer can measure.

Across the open distances between these formations, the vegetation has knitted itself into layered canopies thick enough to walk upon in places. Broad, waxy fronds the color of verdigris and bruised plum overlap with vines whose translucent stems carry visible pulses of sap. Flowers the size of shields open and close over hours rather than days, releasing clouds of warm, metallic-scented vapor that drift between the spires like slow weather. The growth is dense enough that individual trunks and stalks disappear into a single undulating mass, yet open enough that shafts of ordinary daylight still reach the rock below, stippling the underlayers with shifting coins of light.

Fauna moves through this density without haste. Armored gliders the length of skiffs coast between mesas on rising columns of heat, their membranous wings cracking like distant canvas. Smaller, multilegged browsers cling to the weeping rock faces, their mouths sealed against the constant moisture, while swarms of resonant insects fill the intervals between larger sounds with a continuous, sawing drone that rises and falls with the temperature of the stone. The combined chorus is loud, but it does not surge or recede quickly; it is the sound of an equilibrium that has already lasted millennia.

Scattered through the living mat are the faint signatures of an earlier order: low platforms of fused mineral whose edges have been softened by the same slow erosion that shaped the mesas themselves. Their surfaces are veined with hair-thin conduits that still carry a faint, blue-white luminescence, too steady to be natural. These remnants do not intrude; they have been absorbed into the root matrix and the weeping channels, their original geometries blurred into the larger, patient geometry of the living rock. Occasionally a lone tower of darker, unnaturally smooth material punctures the canopy, its summit crowned by a halo of condensing droplets that never fall.

Nothing here accelerates. The mesas themselves shift only by the width of a hand across centuries, their edges receding and reforming in balance with the vegetation that grips them. Water migrates downward at the pace of a slow heartbeat. The luminous veins beneath the platforms dim and brighten over generations of the largest gliding fauna. Even the swarms of insects maintain their stations, rising and settling like a single distributed organism whose individual members live and die without disturbing the larger rhythm. Heat, moisture, and light remain locked in the same measured exchange they have held since the first spires rose above the surrounding haze, an arrangement kept intact by forces no longer visible yet still quietly present in the stone.
