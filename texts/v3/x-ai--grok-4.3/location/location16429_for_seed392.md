In the low basin of the world, beneath a sky that never dims, stretches a constructed flatland of baked clay tiles and reed-woven lattices, each panel fitted by hand with mortise joints and plant-fiber lashings. Water seeps everywhere through deliberate seams: narrow channels no wider than a forearm run in perfect grids between the tiles, fed by low, porous walls that weep without pause. The moisture beads on every surface in a constant film, yet the film never thickens or evaporates; it simply is, the same glistening layer it has always been.

Vegetation has been coaxed into the same arrested state. Lush mats of broad-leafed sedges and hanging mosses grow from every seam and ledge, their roots threaded through drilled holes in the clay like embroidery. The leaves are vivid, crowded, heavy with droplets, yet they neither lengthen nor yellow. Between these green clusters lie wide expanses of bare tile, so the overall impression is one of scattered abundance rather than solid cover: a field of bright green islands separated by pale, empty floor.

The air is motionless and hot, thick with the smell of wet clay and crushed greenery. Light falls straight and unfiltered, turning every droplet into a fixed point of glare. Shadows are short, sharp, and permanent. No wind disturbs the hanging fibers of the woven walls or the surface of the shallow water. The only motion belongs to the fauna—dense clouds of tiny, long-legged insects and occasional flat-bodied lizards that rest on the hottest clay. They are everywhere and nowhere, scattered across the tiles in loose constellations, yet they produce no audible vibration; even their wings and claws move in perfect silence against the constant hush.

Farther out, the same pattern repeats at a larger grain: low, curved platforms rise just enough to break the horizon line, each one supporting another identical arrangement of channels, tiles, and plantings. The distances between these platforms are empty and bright, an intentional openness that makes the constructed field feel larger than any single eye can hold at once. In places the pattern frays. A section of wall will suddenly reveal itself as layered repetitions of the same leaf motif, stacked until the eye loses count; a water channel will double back on itself in an impossible loop whose surface remains mirror-still. These breakdowns appear without warning and recede without explanation, leaving the rest of the landscape unchanged, as though the world had briefly remembered it was made rather than grown and then chose, once more, to forget.

Nothing shifts. The temperature stays fixed, the water level constant, the plants full, the light unrelenting. Only the slow, soundless passage of scaled bodies across the clay and the perpetual, motionless sheen of condensation remind a visitor that this place, for all its stillness, is not truly empty.
