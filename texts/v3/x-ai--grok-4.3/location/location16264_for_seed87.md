The place is held inside a single, slowly inflating membrane of living tissue, no larger than a few rooms stacked together, yet the membrane is itself embedded deep inside a larger body of water that never ends. From within the pocket, the outer water registers only as a constant, soft pressure against the translucent walls, a cold so pervasive it feels like silk laid against bone. The light that reaches here has already been filtered through many layers of living silt and distance, arriving as a bruised, uneven dusk that barely outlines shapes before they move again.

The structures inside are not built but are still forming. Ribs of porous carbonate rise from the floor in incomplete arcs, their surfaces still soft enough to dent under the weight of passing bodies. These arcs lean toward one another as if remembering the shape of streets or corridors, but none of them have yet met at the top. Between them drift thin sheets of mucus that harden only where currents press them against the growing ribs; in places the mucus has already begun to suggest doorways or low partitions. Everything is half-material, half-intent—raw geology caught mid-gesture.

Fauna fills the remaining volume at near-solid density. Small, transparent creatures the size of thumbs drift in slow spirals, their bodies forming living braids between the unfinished ribs. Larger, lantern-jawed forms glide along the membrane walls, pressing their flanks against the boundary as if testing its give. None of them produce more than the faintest pressure waves; the water transmits only a continuous, low friction of movement—soft collisions, the brush of cilia, the occasional wet click of a closing mouth. The quiet is not absence but saturation: every available layer of the pocket already holds something that must pass or be passed.

A few strands of vegetation hang from the upper membrane, pale and nearly leafless, their holdfasts still searching for purchase on ribs that have not finished growing. They drift like loose threads, rarely touching anything twice. What little they release into the water is quickly taken by the swarming fauna before it can settle.

The membrane itself flexes with a rhythm slower than breathing. Each slow contraction pushes a thin film of new mineral outward along the ribs; each expansion draws a fresh veil of colder water inward through microscopic pores. The pocket is therefore both shelter and nursery, a place still condensing its own boundaries while already crowded with life that treats those boundaries as temporary. Nothing here is finished, yet nothing waits.
