The landscape is a single, unbroken horizon of living tissue that has risen only recently from some deeper substrate, its surface still crisp and unrounded. Low and level as a drained seabed yet planetary in extent, it supports its own slow weather of warm vapor that never disperses. Moisture gathers continuously on every upward-facing plane and trickles downward along channels no wider than a finger, returning to the same reservoirs it left moments or millennia earlier. Nothing evaporates here; the air stays saturated and close, a permanent film of condensation sliding across leaves, stems, and the translucent skins that sheath older growth.

Everything visible is plant. There are no gaps, no exposed ground, no mineral that has not already been threaded and replaced by root and rhizome. The mass lies in stacked strata, each layer pressed against the next so that light cannot penetrate more than a few body-lengths before the tissue itself absorbs or reflects it away. In that permanent dimness the plants continue their work: rigid conduits of lignified fiber carry thin fluids under pressure, their walls pulsing once per day in perfect, inaudible synchrony. Smaller tubules branch and rejoin like a loom that never stops or starts, only maintains. Clear sap weeps from seams where one layer meets another; the droplets collect, merge, and flow into hidden cisterns whose levels remain exactly the same from one observation to the next.

Sharp ridges and angular buttresses still mark the places where the mass first unfolded. These crests have not yet been softened; their edges catch and split the slow sheets of water that slide across them. Between ridges the surface dips into shallow basins whose edges are overhung by fringed mats that meet and fuse overhead, forming low vaults whose interiors stay darker still. Inside each vault the same tissues continue, producing new walls and partitions that never quite close the space or open it.

No voice or footfall disturbs the air. The only movements are internal: a minute shift in turgor pressure traveling along a vein, a droplet detaching and falling the length of a single cell before rejoining its parent film. The whole continuum exists in chemical and mechanical balance so complete that no further alteration registers. Growth has already filled every available volume; decay is countered at the same instant it begins. What looks like an engine is only the visible portion of that equilibrium, its cylinders and sumps made of the same living matter that forms the ceiling and floor. The result is a world that feels both newly made and already finished, its processes locked at the precise moment when nothing more can be added and nothing can be lost.
