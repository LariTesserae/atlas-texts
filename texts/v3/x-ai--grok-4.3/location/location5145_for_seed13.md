High on a plateau that rose not long ago, the land holds a wide, open basin ringed by fresh-cut ridges whose edges are still knife-bright. The rock faces show no softening, no rubble skirts or rounded shoulders; they simply stand, as if the uplift happened and then the world decided to stop. Between those ridges the ground spreads in low swells and shallow depressions, enough room for a regional city to spread without crowding. The settlement itself is primitive in craft yet unmistakably urban: terraces of hand-split slate and unmortared stone step down the gentle grade, roofs of split timber and woven reed overlapping in careful layers, narrow stair-streets linking open plazas where cisterns catch whatever moisture arrives. Nothing is metal; every beam was adzed by hand, every joint fitted and wedged.

The air stays cool and still. Condensation beads on every upward-facing stone at night and lingers into the slow mornings, so the city stones are always faintly darkened along their tops. Occasional light rains fall straight down, never driven by wind, and the water gathers in shallow stone basins or trickles briefly along the terrace edges before sinking into the ground. Between the buildings, vegetation grows lush yet widely spaced: broad-leaved ferns, trailing creepers with thick waxy foliage, and low cushions of moss that have rooted in every joint and crack. The plants do not encroach on one another; each clump occupies its own allotted patch of damp stone or soil, leaving clear ground between them so the city never feels choked.

Fauna is plentiful but almost inaudible. Small, matte-scaled lizards rest on sun-warmed ledges and blink slowly; delicate, long-legged insects drift among the fern fronds without audible wingbeats; clusters of pale, soft-feathered birds perch along the ridge crests yet rarely call. Their movements are infrequent enough that the plateau often seems deserted even when it is densely inhabited. The silence is not absolute—there is the soft drip of condensation, the faint scrape of a lizard shifting position—but it holds the quality of a room in which nothing is meant to disturb the equilibrium.

Because nothing changes, the same films of moisture remain on the same stones day after day; the same ferns cast identical shadows when the light is overhead; the same birds occupy the same perches. The ridges have not crumbled further since they first appeared, and the city has not expanded or decayed. Rain arrives at irregular intervals but leaves the overall dampness precisely as it found it. The light itself is ordinary, passing through ordinary daylight and starlight, yet the temperature, the moisture films, the spacing of leaves and buildings all remain balanced, as though the plateau reached its present configuration and then chose to keep that exact configuration forever.
