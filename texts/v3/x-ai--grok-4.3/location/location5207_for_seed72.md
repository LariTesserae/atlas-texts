The place is a half-formed industrial cavern, small enough that its boundaries feel like the walls of a single vast chamber rather than an entire city. Raw limestone and basalt still show the scars of recent boring—jagged ledges and drill spirals not yet sheathed in concrete. Water seeps constantly from hairline fractures in the ceiling, gathering into slow trickles that run along half-laid drainage channels before vanishing into sumps below. The air is thick with steam that never quite clears; it condenses on every metal surface, beading and falling again in patient, rhythmic drops.

What passes for streets here are wide, unfinished aisles left between clusters of machinery still under assembly. Massive turbines sit on temporary cradles, their housings open, cabling draped like unfinished tendons. Pipes of every diameter run along the walls, some already carrying pressurized water or superheated vapor, others capped and waiting. The scale keeps everything intimate: no district stretches farther than a few hundred paces before the rock closes in again, so the entire settlement feels like one continuous workshop rather than a city.

Light comes from sodium lamps strung on temporary gantries; they burn steadily but never brightly, their glow swallowed by the steam and the dark stone. Heat radiates upward from geothermal vents that the engineers have only partially capped, keeping the temperature near the edge of what human workers can endure for long shifts. Vegetation is almost absent—only pale, translucent mats of thermophilic algae cling to the wettest ledges near the vents, and a few wiry ferns struggle in the dimmest corners where condensation keeps the rock constantly damp.

Fauna, however, has arrived in force. The combination of moisture, warmth, and industrial waste has drawn cave crickets, pale millipedes, and dense clouds of tiny midges that hatch in the standing water of unfinished sumps. Rats move along the new pipe runs with the calm of residents who have already claimed the space. Larger, slower creatures—blind salamanders the size of a forearm—lurk in the deeper drainage trenches, drawn by the constant warmth and the scraps that fall from worktables. Their presence is constant but never frantic; they move at the same deliberate pace as the water itself.

Sound fills the chamber without hurry. The dominant tone is a low, continuous thrum from pumps that run day and night to keep the lowest levels from flooding. Over it lie intermittent metallic clinks as workers fit new sections of pipe, the hiss of pressure valves releasing steam, and the amplified dripping that seems to come from everywhere at once. Echoes travel slowly here, lingering long enough that one sound blends into the next rather than overlapping in chaos. The whole space feels like it is still deciding what it will become—machinery half-buried in raw geology, water and heat negotiating their own slow equilibrium with the emerging city. Nothing rushes. The rock cools and reheats by degrees; the water finds new cracks only after years of pressure; the workers install one beam, one conduit, one sump at a time, while the insects and the steam continue their own patient occupation of the gaps.
