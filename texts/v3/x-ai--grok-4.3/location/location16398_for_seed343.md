The highlands stretch in low, patient swells, their outlines softened by uncountable ages of rain and root until the ground feels less like stone and more like a single, breathing hide. At this altitude the air is thin and warm, holding a steady daytime glow that filters through a perpetual haze of water vapor lifted each morning from countless shallow basins. These basins never quite become lakes; they are instead brief, glassy pools cupped in the broad, vein-like troughs where the plant-mass has sagged under its own weight. Condensation beads on every surface after dusk and runs in slow, deliberate threads toward the nearest depression, feeding roots that have already claimed every usable molecule of mineral.

Between the pools the living mass rises in spaced, cathedral-like formations: walls of interlaced trunks and woody lattices, some as tall as hills, others low and sprawling like collapsed roofs. They are not forest in the usual sense; they are the only substrate. Where one formation ends, hundreds of meters of open, faintly undulating plain begin again—empty enough that a walker can see the next structure as a dark bar on the horizon. The intervals are crossed by nothing except wind and the occasional wandering column of small, many-legged herbivores that migrate between the living walls. Their passage raises a constant, layered roar: the ticking of jointed limbs on tough cellulose, the dry rasp of wings, the low, throbbing calls that travel through the ground itself. At times the sound becomes so dense it seems to displace the air; the plateau vibrates with it, and loose fibers on the plant structures tremble in sympathy.

Every so often the eye catches a different geometry half-swallowed by the growth: a ring of low stone slabs now bridged by thick aerial roots, or the curved remnant of a timber frame whose surface has been colonized into a living lattice. These are the last legible marks of earlier hands, spaced so far apart they function almost as landmarks rather than ruins. No one repairs them; the plants simply incorporate the angles into their own slower geometry.

Above the plain, swarms of iridescent fliers rise and fall in vertical columns that catch the light like drifting smoke. They feed on the constant exudate that seeps from the plant walls and, in turn, disturb clouds of still smaller pollinators. The result is a perpetual acoustic storm that rolls across the open ground in visible waves of vibration. When rain finally arrives—light, warm, and infrequent—it strikes the upper canopies and releases a sudden, deeper register: thousands of water channels suddenly speaking at once. Within minutes the sound settles back into its ordinary, suffocating drone.

Nothing here is young. The plateau has already been reduced to its final gentle contours; the living architecture has reached the limit of its vertical ambitions and now expands mainly sideways, knitting new bridges between existing towers. The few remaining clear intervals are not empty so much as patiently reserved—spaces the plants have not yet needed, though every decade they creep a little farther into them.
