High on a weathered summit plateau no larger than a cathedral nave, the rock rises in slow, settled folds of pale mineral, its surfaces etched by centuries of wind into soft, repeating ridges that never quite repeat. No liquid lingers here; the air itself is dry enough to draw moisture from bone, yet the plateau still supports a dense frieze of growth—translucent lattices of silicon-salt that branch like coral yet flex and shimmer with internal currents. These structures do not drink water; they drink electrostatic tension, pulling charge from the thin, cool atmosphere and releasing it as faint, drifting motes of powder that glitter and vanish.

Between the lattices the space is vast and hollow. An observer can stand for long minutes without seeing motion, until the turbulence arrives. Then the air fractures into sudden vortices that carry the lattices into wild, synchronized trembling; whole groves of them lift slightly on invisible threads before settling again. The sound is not wind but the grinding of displaced charge—layer upon layer of it, a continuous low thunder that swells to a physical pressure against the skin and bones. It never stops, only changes register, so that silence itself feels impossible.

Fauna move through the same emptiness like punctuation marks. Small, many-legged mineral clusters drift just above the surface, their legs never quite touching the stone. Larger, kite-shaped organisms ride the charge currents, folding and unfolding translucent membranes that catch stray electrons and release them in silent blue sparks. When the turbulence peaks they cluster together, forming temporary, living conductors that briefly ground the entire plateau; the lattices around them glow brighter, then dim. The creatures disperse again into the gaps, leaving the space feeling even emptier than before.

Scattered across the plateau are a few remnants of older engineering: a hexagonal slab half-buried at one edge, its surface still emitting a steady, subsonic hum that contributes to the roar; a pair of slender columns whose tops have dissolved into drifting filaments that never quite detach. These artifacts have been here long enough to share the established weathering of the rock itself, their edges softened, their functions reduced to slow, patient contributions to the ambient charge.

Light arrives as ordinary daylight filtered through high, thin air—clear enough to show every facet of the lattices yet never warm. The cool temperature keeps the lattices rigid; only the turbulence supplies the energy they need to flex and grow. In quieter intervals the plateau feels like a single balanced organism holding its own equilibrium; when the next wave of charge arrives, the whole small height of it convulses together—stone, lattice, drifting fauna, and buried machines—before subsiding once more into its patient, echoing stillness.
