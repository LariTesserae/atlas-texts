High on a lone, wind-scoured mesa no wider than a cathedral nave, the rock has spent millennia settling into its own shape. Broad, shallow bowls and low ribs of stone rise where softer minerals have already been carried away, leaving behind a pale, salt-veined pavement that rings underfoot like dry bone. Nothing flows here; the air itself is the only solvent, thin and warm, tasting faintly of iron and ozone. What grows does so without water, drawing instead from the slow rain of metallic dust that the winds never cease to deliver.

In scattered pockets the vegetation stands thick and tall—ribbons of translucent, paper-dry fronds that stand upright like kelp in an invisible current. Their edges are serrated with minute crystal facets that catch the dim, bronze light and fracture it into moving glints. Whole thickets of them tremble and realign in seconds when a gust shifts, producing a continuous glassy rustle that rises and falls like breath. Between these stands the stone lies bare, the emptiness deliberate; the living mats leave deliberate corridors where the wind can pass without tearing them loose.

Small, hand-stacked rings of the same pale rock interrupt the pavement at irregular intervals—waist-high windbreaks or roofless shrines whose original purpose is lost. Their surfaces are polished on the windward side and still rough on the lee, showing long equilibrium between construction and weather. Occasionally a larger, toppled slab reveals a single tool mark, a shallow groove made by something harder than the local stone, then abandoned.

Fauna moves constantly through this arrangement. Swarms of paper-winged gliders the size of hands dart between the frond stands, their flight paths bending sharply when they hit an updraft. Larger, slower creatures—segmented, low-slung, and covered in the same dry crystalline growths—graze the mats, their chewing producing a wet-paper tearing sound that carries farther than it should in the thin air. The overall chorus is loud: the high, sustained whistle of wind through the frond edges, the percussive rattle of loosened crystal plates skittering across stone, the overlapping shrieks and clicks of the gliders, and beneath it all a low, pulsing vibration in the rock itself, as if the mesa breathes through hidden veins.

Light never grows brighter than late twilight. Thick, slow-moving layers of dust hang at the height of the mesa rim, diffusing the sun into a steady copper haze. When a stronger gust punches through, the haze momentarily clears and the temperature spikes; within moments the dust curtains knit again and warmth returns to a steady, skin-warmth that never quite becomes heat. Nothing here is still for long, yet the motion has its own tired rhythm: every upheaval of dust and frond and creature is answered by an equal settling, so that the whole small height remains in continuous, balanced agitation.
