The basin spreads wide under a sky that never quite commits to daylight. Low swells of ground, scarcely higher than a shallow tide could reach, roll outward until distance itself turns the features into a single unbroken wash of shadow. The rock here is new—knife-edged ridges and needle spines still raw from their first heaving upward, their surfaces faceted rather than worn, catching what little light filters down and flinging it sideways in faint, prismatic glints. Between these spines the ground is darker, slick where water has already begun to seep outward along hairline fractures.

Condensation gathers on every upward face. Droplets form, swell, and descend in threads so fine they look like silver wire until they snap and fall. The sound is continuous and overlapping: a dense, metallic pattering that echoes between the spines and returns altered, lower, as though the stones themselves answer. Where threads meet pooled water, the surface trembles with each new impact, sending concentric rings outward until they touch the next thread and the next. The air stays cool and close, heavy with moisture that never quite becomes mist yet never lifts.

Nothing green or soft breaks the mineral expanse. Instead, the ground hosts layered sheets of glassy precipitate, pale blue-white and faintly translucent, stacked like exhaled breath frozen mid-fall. Some sheets have already cracked under their own slight weight; others still flex, very slowly, with the patient rhythm of deposition. Between them occasional seams of darker, granular material catch moisture and darken further, but remain inert. Scattered through the field are low, circular depressions whose rims stand slightly proud of the surrounding rock. Their inner walls are smooth, almost polished, and their floors hold shallow lenses of water that reflect nothing except the dim overhead glow. These depressions sit at irregular intervals, too similar in size and angle to be random yet showing no mark of tools or intention beyond the rock’s own slow habits.

Life registers only as absences and faint disturbances. Now and then a thread of water halts mid-fall, held for several seconds before resuming, as though something small and translucent has passed through it and displaced a fraction of its weight. The disturbance leaves no track. At longer intervals a low, resonant tone rises from deep inside a spine and fades again, the sound traveling outward through the dense field of needles until it is swallowed by the constant dripping. The tone repeats once every few hours, always from a different direction, always at the same measured pace.

Movement, when it occurs, is glacial. A droplet tracing the edge of a facet takes minutes to complete its journey. A new sheet of precipitate thickens across hours until its color shifts from clear to milky. The spines themselves lean, imperceptibly, toward one another, their bases softening just enough for gravity to exert its quiet claim. Over generations this leaning will close some corridors and open others, but the change registers only as a gradual crowding of echoes and a slow redistribution of drip patterns. The basin remains open, the horizon an unbroken line of dim light and darker needles, yet within that openness every cubic span of air and stone is occupied by falling water, answering stone, and the patient accumulation of new surfaces.
