The high plateau stretches without boundary, a single unbroken surface lifted so far above the world’s lower basins that it carries its own climate. Here the air is warm and heavy, yet the light never rises above a permanent charcoal dusk; thick, slow-moving layers of vapor drift just above the ground, filtering whatever sun might reach this altitude into a constant half-dark. Condensation forms steadily on every exposed face of rock and then evaporates again when the vapor shifts, leaving behind faint mineral traceries that mark where the moisture briefly stayed.

The rock itself has been shaped for so long that its forms no longer look carved but grown. Broad, shallow basins alternate with low, rounded ridges whose edges have softened into continuous curves. Water collects only in these basins, shallow lenses that appear after a passing veil of mist and linger for days before sinking into fine fractures. Between the basins the surface is dry and pale, a fine-grained stone whose color has been leached by endless cycles of damp and warmth. Nothing interrupts the expanse—no towers, no walls, no lines of any human making—yet the plateau is never still. Slow geothermal pulses travel through the stone like the rhythm of distant engines: at intervals too long for a single life to measure, mineral-rich fluids rise along ancient fissures, deposit new crystalline layers, and subside again, leaving behind hexagonal plates and stepped terraces that accumulate without any hand directing them.

Vegetation is present but never dominant. Tough, dark-green crusts hug the edges of the moisture lenses and the undersides of the vapor-draped ridges, spreading only a few centimeters before giving way to bare stone again. Their spores travel on the slow drafts that move between the vapor layers, seeding new patches wherever a basin holds water long enough. Scattered among the crusts are wiry, leafless stems that draw moisture directly from condensing fog rather than from the ground; they stand in isolation, meters apart, their surfaces faintly phosphorescent so the dusk is never entirely without points of reference.

Fauna fills the spaces the plants leave empty. Small, soft-bodied creatures move through the shallow fractures, their passage marked by the faintest vibrations rather than by sight. Larger forms—low, many-legged, and nearly translucent—emerge only when the vapor thickens, feeding on the mineral films left by retreating water. Their numbers are high, yet their movements are measured; sound travels poorly through the warm, dense air, so the plateau remains mostly quiet even while life and geology continue their exchange. The only audible elements are intermittent: the soft tick of cooling stone after a geothermal pulse, the whisper of vapor sliding across rock, and the occasional dry click of a creature settling into a fissure.

Because the plateau is large enough to generate its own weather, the vapor layers sometimes thicken into brief, warm showers that arrive and depart within hours. Each shower re-wets the crusts, recharges the basins, and sets the mineral terraces growing another imperceptible fraction. Over centuries the terraces widen and the basins deepen by fractions of a millimeter; the entire surface is therefore both ancient and still in motion, its established erosion balanced against ongoing deposition. No structure has ever been raised here, yet the land itself performs slow, distributed work: dissolving, transporting, and recrystallizing its own substance on a scale that mimics industry without any separate machinery.

In the constant half-dark the phosphorescent stems provide the only steady illumination, spaced so far apart that each one seems an isolated outpost. Between them the emptiness is vast and deliberate, a geography whose rule is simple: moisture appears, life answers, stone remembers, and the plateau continues.
