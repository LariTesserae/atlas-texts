In the upper reaches of a forming world-ocean—itself suspended at the cool, high-altitude fringe of a gas-giant’s liquid envelope—water behaves like a slow, planetary weather. Currents the size of continents shear past one another, each carrying its own temperature micro-climate and mineral load. Light arrives as a steady, directionless glow, neither day nor night, filtered through countless kilometers of dissolved silicates until it arrives as a soft, even silver that makes every object appear slightly translucent.

The medium itself is sparsely populated. Only a few drifting veils of thread-like, glass-fingered growths exist, each veil kilometers across yet only a few meters thick; they photosynthesize by harvesting the steady light and the faint chemical gradients between colliding currents. Between these isolated mats the water is almost empty for hundreds of kilometers in every direction—true near-void, broken only by the occasional drifting shard of raw planetary crust still cooling from its own accretion.

Those shards are embryonic. Freshly condensed silicates and native metals crystallize directly from solution, forming angular lattices that have not yet been rounded by time or friction. Where two such proto-mountains drift within a few body-lengths of each other, the water between them begins to sing: a low, continuous roar that rises into sharp infrasonic booms whenever pressure equalizes through newly opened fractures. The sound fills the entire volume; there is no foreground or background, only a single, turbulent chord that modulates as the lattices grow or dissolve.

Far below the visible layer, nanoscale catalytic surfaces—remnants of an earlier, still-unfinished engineering epoch—continue their patient work. They seed the lattices with precise atomic arrangements the ambient physics has not yet learned to forbid. The lattices therefore grow faster in some directions than others, budding spiral vanes or internal capillary networks that will, in another age, become rivers or data conduits. For now they remain raw geology, loud with cavitation and the slow thunder of phase changes.

Nothing moves of its own volition. The only locomotion belongs to the water itself: vast, chaotic overturnings that can lift a forming mountain several kilometers in a single cycle and just as casually grind it back into suspended dust. In the rare intervals when two currents briefly cancel, the sudden hush is itself a kind of violence—an expectant vacuum into which the next roar will shortly arrive.
