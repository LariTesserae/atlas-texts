The place is a drowned industrial enclave no larger than a few city blocks, yet sunk inside a sealed oceanic cleft so deep that pressure and warmth fold together like a single clenched fist. From the outside it is invisible: a fracture in the seafloor that admits only a thin tongue of water, then seals again behind currents that rearrange sediment faster than any map can follow. Inside the cleft the water is warm, thick with dissolved minerals that catch what little light filters down and turn it smoky bronze. Visibility holds for perhaps twenty body-lengths before the gloom swallows everything except the occasional red flash of a pressure-equalizing valve still cycling on residual charge.

The buildings themselves are skeletal, never finished or long abandoned: girder lattices, turbine housings, and pressurized conduit spines that once carried super-heated fluid between refineries. Now they stand as open scaffolds, their interiors emptied by the sea. Between them the space is mostly void—cathedral-scale plazas and canyons where a single abandoned gantry crosses overhead like a fallen bridge. Only the occasional intact pressure dome or control blister breaks the emptiness, its curved glass crazed into lace. Over decades the warm current has softened edges and laid down thin skins of precipitated salts; the metal has not rusted so much as petrified into something between stone and ceramic, its original color replaced by chalky ochre and sulfur yellow.

Currents inside the cleft refuse to settle. Every few minutes a surge funnels through one of the intake tunnels, reverses, then explodes outward again, shredding loose panels and flinging sediment in sudden brown ropes. The noise is constant and layered: the low, bone-deep growl of water forced through narrowing throats, the higher metallic shriek of turbine blades still spinning in their cages, and the staccato hammer of collapsing mineral accretions. Even when the surge pauses, the water itself seems to hiss as dissolved gases come out of solution along every joint and seam.

Nothing organic grows here. What looks at first like coral is only successive layers of precipitated carbonate and metal oxides, record of temperature spikes and chemical pulses rather than living tissue. The only moving things are rare and small: transparent, shrimp-like scavengers that shelter inside the old conduits and emerge only when a fresh surge cracks open a new mineral seam. They dart, feed, and vanish again, leaving the larger emptiness untouched.

The whole pocket operates on its own unstable clock. A surge that begins as a low rumble can, within seconds, invert the local pressure gradient, sucking water and loose debris toward a single intake vent before slamming it outward again in a boiling wall. The structures creak and realign fractionally with each reversal; a gantry that stood straight an hour ago now leans a hand-span farther, its bolts singing. The light is never bright enough to cast hard shadows, only enough to make every mineral crust shimmer as though it were breathing. In that dim, continuous roar, the city remembers its original purpose only as pressure, heat, and motion—its own architecture now merely the fixed points around which the sea keeps reinventing turbulence.
