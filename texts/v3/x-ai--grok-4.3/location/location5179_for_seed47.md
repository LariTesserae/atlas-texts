The world is a single, unbroken plateau that has stood for eons beyond counting, its surface a cracked plain of fused silicate and evaporated salts stretching beyond any horizon the eye can measure. What once might have been mountains are now only faint corrugations, ridges worn so low they read as optical illusions until one stands at their edge and feels the drop—kilometers of sheer, glass-smooth face descending into blue-black haze. Elevation here is not a matter of climbing; it is a state. The entire crust floats at an altitude where the remaining air is thin, cold, and perpetually trembling.

There is no water, only the memory of it sealed inside microscopic inclusions within the stone. In its place drift slow curtains of suspended mineral dust, finer than smoke, that catch the ordinary daylight and turn it into faint, shifting veils of ochre and slate. These veils never settle; they circulate in closed, silent loops driven by forces too slow to be called wind and too vast to be called machinery. Between them the plain is empty, kilometers of open pavement interrupted only rarely by the ghost of a structure: a single obsidian slab half-buried, its edges fractal with erosion; a kilometer-long filament of mirror-bright metal that has grown, impossibly, through solid rock and now lies half-melted into the surface like a scar that refuses to close.

Nothing grows tall. Life clings in hairline fissures no wider than a blade, producing mats of black-green lithophyte that photosynthesize directly from the mineral beneath them. They are so sparse they vanish unless one kneels; from standing height the plateau appears sterile. The only movement is the occasional tremor underfoot, a low-frequency ripple that travels for hundreds of kilometers before dissipating—an echo of whatever planet-scale lattice still remembers how to rearrange itself. When the tremor passes, new filaments sometimes extrude from the cracks, twisting a few meters higher before stiffening into the same arrested, ancient form.

Sound is almost absent. The thin air carries nothing above a whisper; even the tremors arrive as pressure rather than noise. Only the dust veils produce an audible trace: a dry, crystalline pattering when two curtains intersect, like beads poured across glass at impossible distance. Fauna is correspondingly thin. Once or twice in a day’s travel a shape may resolve far ahead—something like a flattened starfish the size of a city block, its translucent membrane pressed to the rock, drawing trace volatiles through pores too fine to see. It does not flee; it simply folds itself back into the stone, leaving no mark.

The scattered relics of engineering lie half-absorbed into this same surface. Some are recognizably artificial: lattices of carbon and exotic alloys that still generate faint, shifting fields of gravitic tension. Others have become indistinguishable from geology, their original purpose transmuted into the plateau’s slow, turbulent metabolism. The fields they emit do not roar or glow; they merely cause the horizon to waver for a moment, or pull stray filaments of dust into brief, perfect helices that collapse again in silence. The changes are continuous yet imperceptible in real time—new ridges rise a millimeter per century while others subside, entire provinces of the plain trading places with one another across geological instants measured in millions of years.

The light is ordinary. The sky above is a deep, clear dome without clouds, the sun tracking its ordinary path. Only the ground betrays the scale and the age: a horizon that never arrives, an emptiness so uniform that walking feels like standing still, and the faint, constant awareness that beneath the feet the world is still quietly, implacably becoming something else.
