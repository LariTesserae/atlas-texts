The place sits at the raw edge of an unfinished ridge system, still heaving upward in geological time. From a vantage on one of its higher shoulders, the land drops away in long, irregular planes rather than sharp cliffs. Rock surfaces are matte and newly fractured, their edges still angular because rain and frost have not yet rounded them into the familiar shapes of mature mountains. Water is everywhere and nowhere at once: it beads constantly on the undersides of ledges, runs in thread-thin films down slab faces, and rises again as mist when the cold air meets warmer seepage from below. The mist never clears; it simply thins or thickens, turning the distances into soft, gray layers that recede for kilometers without ever offering a horizon line.

Nothing here has accumulated much soil. In the wider expanses between rock ribs, the ground is only a thin scatter of frost-shattered gravel over harder substrate. Hardy cushion plants and dark, almost black lichens find purchase in the narrowest seams, spaced so far apart that the eye registers mainly stone. Yet the emptiness is not sterile. Narrow meltwater trickles support dense, short-lived blooms of algae along their banks, and these in turn draw clouds of midges that hatch in shivering pulses after each brief warming. Larger animals move through the same intervals of space: small, thick-coated grazers that travel in loose, widely spaced groups, and the low-slung predators that follow them at even greater remove. Their passages leave scuff marks that vanish within hours under new frost or condensing dew.

The light stays low and even, filtered through the perpetual veil of vapor. Colors are restricted to dull grays, muted greens, and the occasional rust-orange streak where iron begins to oxidize in freshly exposed seams. Sound travels poorly. The main audible events are the soft, repeated ticks of water dropping from overhangs and the faint, almost subsonic groan of rock adjusting to the cold. Occasionally a larger slab settles with a single muffled report that rolls across the open ground and dies quickly. There are no echoes yet; the landscape has not formed enough enclosed space to create them.

Beneath the surface the rock is still working. Mineral-rich water rises along fresh fault lines, cools abruptly, and deposits microscopic crystals in layered seams that look, if one watches long enough, like the slow accumulation of machined parts on an invisible belt. Steam hisses from hairline vents in short, regular bursts, then stops, then resumes elsewhere, as if some unseen pressure system were cycling through test runs. Nothing has been built here by hand, yet the processes repeat with a regularity that suggests mechanism rather than chance. The cold keeps every motion deliberate; the water keeps every surface slightly mobile; the emptiness keeps every event distinct and countable across the long distances.
