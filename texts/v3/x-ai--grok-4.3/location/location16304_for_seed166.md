In the cool depths, a small submerged chamber sits in perfect suspension, its scale bounded like a single vaulted nave carved not by design but by the patient work of water across unmeasured time. The surrounding rock has long since surrendered to equilibrium: surfaces curve and fold in settled, weathered planes where every ridge has worn to its final contour and every depression holds a film of fine sediment that will neither deepen nor disperse. Light arrives evenly from above, filtered through layers of clear liquid until it falls without glare or shadow, revealing the stone in its true muted tones of gray and pale ochre.

Vegetation appears only as isolated tufts—filamentous algae clinging to a few high ledges, their threads waving once and then still again, spaced far apart across the open volume. Between them the water itself is the dominant presence: dense, motionless, and uniformly cool, pressing against every surface with the same gentle insistence. No currents shift; the chamber exists in a frozen balance where temperature, chemistry, and pressure have reached an accord that admits no further alteration.

From narrow seams in the lower walls, mineral-laden fluid weeps in steady, thread-thin streams. These seepages accrete in slow, concentric laminations, building rigid lattices that resemble pipework or the housings of drowned engines—yet every joint and flange is the product of precipitation alone. The lattices grow outward in measured increments, never accelerating, never ceasing, their surfaces already polished by the same water that feeds them. The sound of their formation is negligible: a faint, almost tactile settling rather than any distinct report.

Nothing moves except the rare, detached particle drifting downward in a straight, unhurried line. In this absence of life or change, the chamber maintains itself as a self-contained system—industrial in its repetitive mineral output, untouched in every other respect, and held forever at the threshold of further becoming.
