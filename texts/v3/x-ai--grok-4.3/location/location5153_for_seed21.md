The plateau measures perhaps two hundred paces across, a shelf of fresh basalt and pale quartz that juts from a cliff face still warm from its own making. The rock is sharp; fractures run in clean, glassy planes where the strata have not yet learned how to soften. Water seeps continuously from these planes in thin, silvery sheets that never quite become streams, only slow veils sliding downward to vanish into hairline fissures. The air is heavy with the same water, held close by the heat that rises through the stone and by something else—an invisible lattice of forces that keeps the vapor from escaping upward into the thinner sky. Condensation forms on every surface, then re-evaporates within minutes, a private weather cycling at the scale of a single outcrop.

Between the fissures grow low cushions of vegetation, vivid and improbable against the dark rock: fronds the color of new blood, stems that glisten as though lacquered, leaves no larger than a thumbnail yet veined with threads of metallic silver. Each plant stands alone, anchored in a pocket of dust no bigger than a cupped hand. They do not crowd; the empty stone between them is as important as the growth itself. Where two cushions approach each other, their outermost leaves lean away, creating a deliberate margin of bare basalt etched with faint, straight lines—scars left by whatever decision, long ago, set the spacing.

Now and then the lines flare with a brief, interior light, no brighter than a firefly and gone before the eye can name the color. These are the only artifacts of the builders: not ruins in the ordinary sense, but residual fields still nudging matter into preferred patterns. A few of the larger fractures have been coaxed into shallow bowls; rain that strikes them does not drain away but hovers for a moment in perfect spheres before the spheres drift sideways and rejoin the weeping rock. The effect is silent. Only when the light catches the spheres at certain angles does a single, glassy note drift upward, so faint it might be imagined.

Heat pools along the surface yet never quite becomes oppressive; the same unseen lattice that holds the water also draws excess warmth into the rock and downward, where geothermal channels carry it away. The plateau therefore feels both tropical and austere, a greenhouse the size of a cathedral nave whose ceiling is open sky. Normal sunlight passes through, but its spectrum is slightly altered at the edges, producing soft prismatic fringes around every shadow.

Nothing larger than a thumb moves here. Once an hour or so a thread-thin creature—translucent, segmented, no color of its own—emerges from a crack, drinks from a hanging veil of water, and retreats. Its passage leaves the veil trembling for several seconds afterward, a disturbance shaped like a slow ripple rather than ordinary motion. The ripple continues even after the creature has vanished, propagating along the seepage until it fades. This is the loudest event the place offers.

At dusk the fields change register. Condensation pauses; the metallic veins in the leaves begin to carry a deeper current, outlining each cushion in a brief, internal dusk-glow that lasts exactly as long as the sun remains above the horizon line. When full darkness arrives the glow extinguishes at once, leaving the plateau again defined only by the pale seams of quartz and the faint, persistent sound of water refusing to fall.
