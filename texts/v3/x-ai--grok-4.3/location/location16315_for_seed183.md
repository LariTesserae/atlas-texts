Above the world’s ordinary limits, a high plateau tilts and folds like a sheet of hammered metal left too long in the weather. Its surface is pale feldspar veined with darker gabbro that has already begun to shed, grain by grain, into shallow runnels of water. The water never collects into standing pools; instead it clings, trembling, to every slight depression, condensing again the moment a gust strips it away. From a distance the plateau looks dry and bright. Up close the rock is always filmed, and small beads slide beneath the soles of any foot that steps there.

No lichen, no moss, no strand of green interrupts the mineral skin, yet the plateau is not silent or empty. Thin white filaments—half crystal, half condensation—grow in the lee of every outcrop and dissolve again when sunlight strikes them directly. Between these filaments, the air itself carries weight: motes of suspended quartz and basalt hang at shoulder height, never settling, stirred by the constant low thunder of the wind that funnels up the escarpments. The wind is loud, but beneath it another sound pulses—dull, arrhythmic cracking, as though the rock were adjusting its own joints.

Scattered across this surface are the remains of earlier hands: low rings of basalt blocks no higher than a knee, their edges worn to a uniform dullness. Inside a few rings lie split cobbles whose fractures still show the deliberate angle of a single blow. These structures do not accumulate; every few hours one simply ceases to be where it was and appears again, rotated or halved, a dozen paces away. The shift is quiet, but it adds its own dry click to the louder chorus of wind and dripping water.

The light is merciless and high. It casts no soft shadows, only hard black lines that snap from one angle to another when the unseen fractures beneath the plateau realign. Gravity feels briefly lighter across those lines; then the body drops back with a small, visceral jolt, as if the plateau had exhaled beneath it. Distant ridges duplicate for a heartbeat—identical silhouettes standing side by side—before one of them folds away into the glare. The eye accommodates the duplication without remark; the mind does not. 

At this elevation the cool air moves fast enough to strip heat from any exposed surface, yet the constant film of water keeps the rock itself from ever growing truly cold. Breath fogs, but the fog travels only a short distance before it is shredded into the same drifting mineral dust that already populates the air. Nothing arrives or departs. The plateau simply continues its slow, internal negotiation between solidity and the bright, wet possibility of becoming something else.
