The plain stretches without horizon or interruption, a single low slab of compacted mineral that has eroded itself into finality over epochs no one tracks. Everything stands packed edge-to-edge: towers of prismatic glass no higher than a person’s chest, ridged plates of black olivine, and slow-turning crystalline rotors half-sunk in the wet grit. No structure was placed here; the rotors and plates grew upward from the substrate the same way frost grows on stone, their angles dictated by the same ancient settling that flattened the entire region. Water moves constantly across every surface—condensing out of the dim air in bead-chains, weeping from the seams between crystals, pooling in the narrow galleries between plates until it spills again. The temperature keeps the liquid thin and metallic-tasting, never quite freezing, never warming enough to rise as vapor.

Light arrives already half-swallowed, a permanent bruised twilight that makes every facet throw double shadows, one of which sometimes lags and detaches. When that happens the detached shadow keeps the shape of the rotor or plate but drifts a body-width away before rejoining with a sound like distant sheet metal buckling. The sound never stops. A constant low grinding issues from the rotors as they turn on their own mineral bearings; each revolution forces pale condensate through internal capillaries until it jets from the far side in fine, hissing fans. Between these jets come louder, arrhythmic claps when two opposing crystal faces shear past one another, then the ringing aftermath travels across the plain like struck bells rolling under water.

Fauna fills every interval the minerals leave. Shard-beetles the color of dirty ice crawl in dense mats along the wet undersides of plates, their legs tipped with living abrasives that score new grooves as they move; the grooves immediately begin to weep again. Larger, slug-shaped entities made of suspended metallic droplets slide between the rotors; when two meet they exchange droplets through brief, bright arcs of current that leave the air smelling of ozone and wet iron. None of these forms photosynthesize or graze on anything that could be called life. They metabolize the constant mechanical friction of the plain itself—the rotors’ torque, the shearing of facets, the micro-quakes that still ripple through the ancient crust. Their waste is fresh mineral slurry that the next cycle of condensation immediately incorporates.

Because the plain is already fully occupied, motion never expands outward; it only re-arranges what is already present. A beetle mat will suddenly lift as one shifting sheet of animals and glide fifty paces before settling into another gap, the space it vacated filling at once with sliding mineral droplets. Occasionally the weirdness asserts itself more sharply: an entire rotor-field will stutter, the rotors reversing direction for several turns as though the plain briefly remembered an earlier configuration, then snap back; any creature caught mid-motion is sheared in half and the halves re-seal moments later, now facing opposite ways. The air carries overlapping echoes—grinding that occurred minutes earlier, jet-hiss that has not yet begun—until the auditory present grows thick enough to feel against the skin. No boundary marks the edge of this behavior; it simply continues at the same density as far as the dim light allows sight, a single unbroken mechanism that has forgotten how to end.
