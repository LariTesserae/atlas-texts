The high plateau rises in unfinished layers, its stone still warm from recent extrusion. Veins of dark metal run through pale basalt like slow blood; some veins pulse faintly with residual heat, others have cooled into rigid ribs that prop up half-formed arches and cantilevered platforms. The ground is not yet level. Freshly cooled slag lies in overlapping sheets, each sheet thinner than the last, as if immense rollers passed overhead decades apart and are due to return only after another generation. Between these sheets, narrow seams hold shallow films of condensation that bead and evaporate in the steady heat. The water never gathers into streams; it lingers just long enough to darken the mineral surface before vanishing again.

No chlorophyll exists here. Instead, faint crystalline lattices—half mineral, half ordered energy—grow outward from factory vents in millimeter increments each year. These lattices catch the normal daylight and fracture it into pale refractions that shift only when the lattice itself lengthens, an event measured in human lifetimes rather than seasons. Where the lattices meet older metal ribs, they sheath them in translucent armor that cracks and reseals with the slow rhythm of thermal expansion.

Dense stacks of industrial architecture rise directly from this raw geology. Blast towers lean against natural escarpments; their brick and iron skins have fused with the parent rock so that it is impossible to say where construction ends and geology resumes. Pipes as wide as avenues descend into the plateau in helical coils, carrying superheated slurry that cools into new paving stones before it reaches the lower terraces. Gantries and catwalks thread between structures at multiple heights, their railings already half-buried by the next layer of extruded stone. The city does not expand outward; it thickens upward and downward at once, each new floor both foundation and roof for the one above.

Sound is scarce. The great engines that drive the plateau’s growth turn once per decade, their revolutions marked only by a single low harmonic that travels through the stone for weeks afterward. Drips of condensing moisture strike metal at irregular intervals, each impact absorbed almost at once by the surrounding mass. No wind moves across the open distances; the plateau sits above most weather, and the few clouds that form condense, release their brief moisture, and dissipate without stirring the air.

Occasionally a crevice will open along a stress line in the cooling rock. From these fissures emerge sparse, heat-adapted creatures: flat, mineral-shelled organisms that drink the condensation films and then retreat again, their bodies leaving behind microscopic polishing marks on the stone. These are the only mobile life visible across the entire regional expanse. Everything else—tower, vein, lattice, platform—continues its patient addition of mass, governed by cycles too long for urgency and too regular for accident.
