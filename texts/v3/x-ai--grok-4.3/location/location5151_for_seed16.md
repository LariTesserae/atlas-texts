In the heart of a sealed basalt vault—carved so long ago its walls have slumped into gentle, waterless curves—lies a city that was never meant to be seen from outside. The chamber itself is pocket-scale: a single, self-contained basin no wider than a large courtyard, yet the low ceiling hangs so high and featureless that distances warp. Everything inside feels both intimate and hollow.

Buildings rise in irregular clusters of hand-dressed stone and packed mineral dust. Their edges are not sharp; centuries of slow settling have rounded every cornice and lintel until the structures resemble wind-sculpted buttes more than deliberate architecture. Streets are wide, absurdly so—vast empty plazas of compacted salt and powdered feldspar separate one cluster from the next, so that crossing from one block to another feels like traveling between separate settlements. The city reads as empty because most of its volume is simply unoccupied air, dim and faintly shimmering with heat.

Lushness here is not green. From cracks in walls and plazas sprout dense thickets of translucent mineral filaments—thread-fine at the base, flaring into palm-sized, petal-shaped plates at the tips. These formations drink only the trace vapors that cycle through hidden geothermal vents; they grow, flex, and reorient toward the warmest currents with a motion too slow to watch but fast enough to change the skyline overnight. Standing fields of them create the impression of overgrown gardens, yet the ground beneath remains perfectly dry. Their surfaces catch the single, distant light source—an orange glow leaking from a narrow fissure high overhead—like living stained glass. In the dimness they seem almost to hum, a low optical vibration that registers more as pressure than sight.

Temperature holds steady at a pressing, skin-prickling heat that rises from the floor itself. The air is motionless at ankle level yet threaded, higher up, with slow convective rivers that carry fine grit in visible sheets. These currents strike the mineral growths and set them shivering; the resulting clatter—glass bells struck by invisible hammers—fills the vault with continuous, echoing sound that never resolves into melody or rhythm. It is loud without being violent, an ambient roar that makes conversation possible only when bodies are pressed close.

Nothing moves that is not mineral or thermal. No insects crawl the stones, no birds cross the empty plazas. The only living motion belongs to the slow expansion and contraction of the crystal plates as they drink and release heat. Over generations the city’s builders—long vanished—learned to align their doorways and windows with the prevailing currents, so that the constant mineral chorus now serves as both clock and warning: when the pitch rises, fresh vents are opening; when it drops, the city cools enough for work. The stones themselves carry the memory of that craft. Every surface has been abraded into a satin finish by the passage of countless grit-laden winds, yet none of the original angles have disappeared entirely. The forms are balanced between what hands once made and what the slow, dry weather has since permitted.
