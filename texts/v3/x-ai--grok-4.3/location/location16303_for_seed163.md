High in the raw shoulders of a forming cordillera, the land still wears its first skin of rock. New tectonic slabs lie exposed in great tilted plates, their fractures still sharp and unsoftened by time. Between and over them spreads a continuous but open weave of vegetation: thick mats of pale-green spongy growth that rise in low domes and long, pillow-like ridges, their surfaces beaded with condensation that never quite dries. The plants have no need of soil; they root into one another, forming an incomplete but living architecture whose gaps reveal the dark, weeping stone beneath.

Water is everywhere and nowhere at once. It gathers in shallow, clear lenses on the plant mattress, seeps from rock joints in slow curtains, and hangs in the cool air as a constant fine mist that brightens under the fierce, high-elevation sun. When the light strikes the droplets at certain angles, the entire slope flashes white for minutes at a time before the mist shifts again. The temperature holds a steady chill; breath clouds form and linger among the plant cushions.

Scattered across this tissue are the traces of industry: low concrete housings half-swallowed by the growth, their metal doors ajar, cables trailing into the vegetation like roots. Inside some of them, massive flywheels turn with glacial patience, driven by the steady drip and pressure of collected water. Their motion is almost soundless in itself, yet the water they force through narrow stone channels produces a continuous, rolling roar that fills the valleys and ridges for kilometers. The sound does not rise and fall; it simply is, a low thunder that vibrates through the plant mass and makes the droplets quiver on every leaf.

Nothing moves quickly here. The wheels complete a single rotation in the time it takes most forests to add a new ring. The plant domes expand outward by a hand’s width each decade. Where the mats have not yet closed over the raw geology, rain and condensation carve the first faint channels, but these too advance with geological slowness. Between the living ridges lie stretches of open stone and shallow vapor, spaces large enough for a traveler to walk for an hour without stepping on green. In those intervals the only presences are the occasional concrete slab or rusted pulley left from earlier, slower phases of construction.

Animal life is almost absent. The rare insects that do appear are tiny, translucent, and silent, their wings barely disturbing the mist. The deafening sound belongs entirely to water, stone, and the patient machinery; nothing biological answers it. The light remains relentless and clear at this altitude, turning the perpetual damp into a landscape of sparkling surfaces and deep, cool shadows within the plant architecture itself. Over centuries the mats will thicken, the wheels will continue their imperceptible turning, and the young rock will remain just slightly less raw, but the essential arrangement—bright, wet, open, and overwhelmingly slow—will persist.
