The Pocket Forge is not a room. It is a single riveted node lodged inside the throat of a tower that has not yet finished climbing. Walls, floor, and ceiling are the same alloy—dull gunmetal laced with frost-spun condensation—that has been stamped and bolted in place and then left alone. No substrate remains visible; every surface was poured or pressed into being at the same frozen instant of assembly.

Water never stops arriving. It beads from seams in the ceiling plates, traces slow silver veins down the rivet heads, and collects in shallow trays cut into the floor grating. The trays are overfull yet never spill; the overflow simply recondenses on the nearest upward surface and begins the circuit again. The air is cold enough that each droplet forms a momentary skin of ice before the next arrives to melt it.

Nothing grows here except a sparse, almost reluctant film: hair-thin filaments of gray-green polymer lichen that cling only to the warmest patches around exhaust vents. They do not spread. They occupy their assigned squares of mesh and no more.

The space is small—arm’s reach in any direction—but saturated. Pipes the thickness of a wrist cross and recross at every angle, some carrying vapor, some carrying current, some carrying threads of pale blue fluid that never quite flows. Between the pipes move the vermin: thousands upon thousands of nickel-shelled mites, each the size of a grain of sand, locked in identical orbits around every joint and valve. Their legs tick against the metal in a single, unchanging rhythm that is also the room’s only sound, amplified by the close walls into a ceaseless, tooth-rattling roar. The roar has no beginning and no modulation; it has always been exactly this loud, exactly this pitch.

Light is a thin, failing phosphor wash that leaks from seams in the pipe casings. It renders everything in the same dim bluish half-tone, enough to reveal form but never detail, never shadow edge. The mites themselves are the brightest things present; each carries a single pinpoint diode on its back that glows the same steady, unblinking hue as the chamber’s permanent light. Because nothing here ages or advances, the diodes never dim, never fail, never change their spacing.

High above the mites, where the ceiling plate has been left slightly ajar during installation, a continuous thread of colder air descends. It carries the metallic scent of new solder and the faint mineral bite of water that has never touched soil. The draft does not stir the mites; they are held to their tracks by fields too weak to feel but strong enough to enforce perfect repetition. The entire node therefore exists in a state of perfect, deafening, insectile equilibrium—one that has not yet become a landscape, only the promise of one, sealed inside its own unfinished metal skin.
