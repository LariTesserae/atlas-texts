High in the throat of a freshly sheared mineral spire—its facets still ringing with the memory of their own birth—a single pocket opens like a held breath. The pocket is no wider than a modest hall, yet the elevation is absolute; the surrounding rock drops away on every side into knife-edge declivities that have not yet softened into slopes. Light arrives only as a thin, russet seepage through a fissure two hundred body-lengths overhead, so the air itself seems to dilute the glow, turning every surface into a study in half-tones and subsurface glints.

The floor is a tessellation of matte, heat-retaining slabs laid edge-to-edge without mortar. Between the slabs run hairline channels no thicker than a fingernail; every few hours a film of condensate gathers along these seams, beads, and finally releases in slow, deliberate droplets that vanish into sub-floor lattices before they can pool. The droplets are the only recurring moisture. When one strikes, a faint mineral film is deposited and, seconds later, drawn back upward through capillary threads invisible except as a momentary darkening of the stone. Nothing grows from these films. The rock itself refuses colonization.

Scattered across the tessellated floor stand the remnants of an urban grammar reduced to a single courtyard block. Low, open frameworks—more lattice than wall—rise at precise intervals. Their uprights are not metal or glass but a single extrusion of whatever the parent spire is made from, merely persuaded into right angles and then left alone. Inside each framework, volumes of empty air are subdivided by finer grids that shift one increment every few minutes, realigning according to unseen thermal or pressure gradients. When two grids intersect, they produce a soft, sub-audible click that fades before it reaches the ear; the pocket therefore remains mostly silent, yet never entirely still.

The heat is steady and close, radiating upward from the deeper strata and trapped by the pocket’s geometry. It makes the rock surfaces feel almost fevered to the touch, yet the air itself moves in micro-currents so slight they register only as a change in the direction of a hanging filament of condensing vapor. These filaments, when they form, drift like slow cilia before the lattices reabsorb them.

Because the entire assemblage is young, nothing has rounded. Corners remain acute; fracture lines still show the pale, almost luminous inner matrix of the mineral. In places the rock has already begun to delaminate along its original bedding planes, producing floating platelets no thicker than a page that hover a millimeter above their parent surface, held by electrostatic or gravitic gradients the lattices maintain. When two such platelets drift close, they exchange a single faint spark and then repel again—an interaction without sound, without damage, and without repetition exactly as before.

Nothing moves of its own accord except the grids, the vapor, and the quiet rearrangement of the floating mineral leaves. The pocket therefore feels both occupied and vacant at once: a place engineered for a city that never arrived, or that arrived and then chose to withdraw into its own hidden interval between rains.
