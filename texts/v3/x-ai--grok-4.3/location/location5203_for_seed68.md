The place is a shallow industrial basin, no more than a few hundred paces across, cupped inside freshly upthrust ground. The surrounding ridges still show ragged, unweathered edges where stone split only decades ago; water has already begun to etch thin runnels down their faces, but the cuts remain knife-sharp. Inside the basin the ground is level and damp, a dark alluvial loam that never quite dries. Low brick-and-iron structures stand scattered rather than clustered, each one a single-story hall or shed roofed with corrugated tin now softened by moss. Pipes and catwalks connect them at irregular intervals, yet wide stretches of open ground lie between—tangles of reed and low fern, pocked with shallow pools that reflect the sky.

Condensation forms constantly on every metal surface. Even when the light is ordinary and even, droplets gather along the undersides of pipes and valves, fall in slow, spaced intervals, and bead on the broad leaves of the vines that have climbed the walls. The vines are thick-stemmed, their foliage a deep, slightly bluish green; they push between rivets and grow around the flywheels of idling engines without being cut back. The air stays cool, carrying the faint mineral chill of recent groundwater. Rain does not fall in steady curtains; instead, a fine mist gathers toward evening and lingers until mid-morning, leaving every surface filmed and the soil soft underfoot.

Machinery runs at a patient, unvarying tempo. Large reciprocating engines turn inside the halls, their flywheels visible through louvered panels, producing a low, continuous throb that carries across the open ground and mixes with the sound of water dripping from eaves and the occasional splash of wading birds. Smaller electric motors hum in counterpoint from pump houses. The combined noise is loud enough to press against the ears, yet it does not quicken or spike; it simply continues.

Animals have colonized every niche. Herons stalk the larger puddles between buildings, their reflections fractured by the slow rise of bubbles from submerged vents. Smaller, rust-colored lizards dart along warm pipe runs, and clouds of midges rise and settle above the condensation pools. In the evenings, pale moths the size of a hand gather at the single row of sodium lamps along the main walkway. Nothing seems hurried; the engines turn at the same measured rate day after day, the vines lengthen by fractions of an inch each season, and the sharp ridges around the basin erode by only a hair’s breadth in a human lifetime.

The settlement feels complete yet provisional, as though the factories and the vegetation reached an accommodation only recently and are still testing its limits. No boundary wall exists, but the abrupt rise of the young ridges marks a clear edge; beyond them the land continues into older, gentler country. Inside, the rules are simple and slow: water collects, machines turn, green things grow over metal, and the sound never ceases.
