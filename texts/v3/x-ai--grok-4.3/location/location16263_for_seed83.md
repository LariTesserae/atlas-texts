The place is a sealed industrial crypt, no larger than a few connected chambers the size of a modest house, buried so far beneath the surface that surface and sky have become irrelevant abstractions. Everything here was built: floors, walls, ceilings, and the very air conduits are composites of riveted alloy and poured synthetics, now settled into a single continuous skin. Long exposure to heat and moisture has given every surface the same soft, matte patina, as though the entire room had been dipped in warm wax and left to harden over centuries.

Moisture arrives in quiet pulses. Condensation forms along the upper pipes during the long thermal cycles, gathers into droplets the size of coins, and falls at intervals measured in hours. Some droplets strike hot casings and flash into brief steam; others slip through hairline seams and travel downward along hidden channels, feeding small pockets of standing water gathered in the depressions of machine bases. The air stays thick and close, just damp enough to feel on the skin yet never enough to pool or flood.

Dense ranks of conduits, valve trees, and stacked heat exchangers fill nearly every cubic span, layered so tightly that narrow crawl spaces remain between them like the gaps between ribs. The machinery itself is archaic by any standard—slow-turning flywheels, belt-driven pumps, and piston arrays that still move, though their motion is almost imperceptible from one minute to the next. A single engine somewhere deeper in the complex produces a low, constant warmth that radiates through the metal, keeping the temperature high and steady. No natural stone or soil remains visible; every surface has been replaced or covered.

In the few intervals where a pipe has sagged enough to leave a pocket of shadow, pale, thread-like growths have taken hold—something between lichen and mineral deposit, extending delicate filaments across warm metal. They are sparse, each cluster no larger than a hand, yet they give the only living color in the gloom. The only fauna are occasional pale, eyeless mites that travel along the film of condensation; they move so slowly that their progress registers only across long observation. Their faint, dry rustling is the nearest thing to deliberate sound.

Light is almost absent. A few dull phosphor wafers, long past their prime, emit a faint greenish wash that barely reaches the center of each chamber. Most illumination is incidental: the brief glint of a droplet catching a wafer’s glow before it falls, or the dull red of a cooling element. In the near-dark the eye learns to read shape by temperature gradients and faint reflections rather than by sight.

Time here moves in patient increments. Corrosion advances grain by grain. Belts stretch and reseat themselves over decades. Condensation cycles repeat without urgency. The space feels both ancient and still in use, as though the machines continue a task whose purpose has long since outlasted its makers, sustained only by the slow, reliable logic of heat, metal, and dripping water.
