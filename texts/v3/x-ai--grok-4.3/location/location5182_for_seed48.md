The basin lies low and wide under a heavy sky, its flatness broken only by low ridges of eroded shale and the angular silhouettes of old industrial blocks. Everything here sits close to water level: channels of sluggish runoff thread between cracked concrete pads, and the ground itself sweats—thin films of mineral-rich moisture bead on every surface and evaporate almost at once in the heat. The air feels thick, faintly metallic, condensed droplets catching what little light filters through the perpetual overcast. No green breaks the palette; the only living textures are dark mineral crusts and the faint iridescence of iron oxides left by decades of slow seepage.

Factories and warehouses stand in long ranks, their brick and corrugated steel weathered to the same muted grays and ochres as the surrounding rock. Roofs sag where water has pooled and frozen and thawed again over many seasons; support beams are streaked with runoff that has carved shallow channels down their faces. Between the buildings the streets remain, but they are mostly empty—broad paved avenues where wind and occasional sheet-flow move freely. Loading bays gape open; inside, cranes hang motionless and conveyor housings are caked with dried silt. Only the occasional low thrum of a still-running ventilator or the drip of condensation from a roof joint breaks the quiet.

Yet the place is not still. Small, heat-tolerant animals—lizards, insects, and burrowing rodents—move constantly through the spaces, using the hollows beneath floor plates and the gaps in rusted machinery as shelter and hunting ground. Their numbers are high; at certain hours the surfaces seem to shift with their passage. Water keeps them alive: every weeping seam in the concrete, every shallow basin where rain collects, supports clusters of activity. The animals drink, lay eggs in damp crevices, and prey on one another in short, silent cycles that repeat without pause.

Because the elevation is slight and the rock is soft, erosion has had time to smooth the edges of everything made and unmade. Pavement has cracked into plates that tilt gently toward the low points; walls lean where their footings have been undercut. The industrial bones remain recognizable—rail spurs, chimney stacks, the rectangular ghosts of switchyards—but they no longer dominate the surface. They have become part of the same slow drainage pattern that shapes the surrounding mineral flats.

Light stays low and even throughout the long days. Sound stays low as well: the soft percussion of dripping water, the dry rustle of scaled bodies over metal, the distant mechanical wheeze of a fan that has not yet failed. No voices carry across the wide avenues. The basin simply continues—hot, damp, and busy with small, ordinary lives moving through the settled remains of larger ones.
