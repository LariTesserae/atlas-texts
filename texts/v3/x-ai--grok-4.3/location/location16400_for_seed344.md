In a narrow declivity no wider than a freight car and perhaps twice as long, the land has only lately decided to exist. Its walls are raw, knife-edged stone still gritty with the dust of their own birth—unsoftened ledges that have not yet learned how to crumble. Water beads constantly along these fresh faces, drawn upward by some unseen warmth, then sliding down in slow, deliberate threads that gather at the bottom into a shallow, rust-colored film. The film never quite becomes a pool; instead it trembles with subsurface tension, as though the ground itself is exhaling moisture in measured breaths.

Scattered across the damp floor are the remnants of small, half-buried machines. Their housings are low, riveted boxes of iron and ceramic, spaced ten or fifteen paces apart. Some still turn: a single broad paddle rotates once every several minutes inside a corroded housing, lifting droplets that catch the ordinary daylight filtering from the slit of sky above and throw them back as brief, coppery gleams. The motion is patient, almost ceremonial. Where the paddles pause, condensation accumulates on their upper rims and drips into open funnels; the sound is loud, a measured metallic plink that echoes upward between the sharp walls and refuses to fade quickly.

Nothing grows in dense stands. Here and there a single stalk of pale, jointed reed emerges from a crack, its leaves reduced to translucent spines that vibrate when a drop strikes them. Between the stalks the ground is mostly bare—damp mineral crust interrupted by occasional streaks of dark oxide where water has lingered longest. The air itself is thick with vapor that never quite becomes fog; it hangs in faint, shifting layers, bending the light only slightly so that distances inside the declivity seem both intimate and strangely elongated.

No living creature moves. The only motion belongs to the water and to the machines whose gearboxes, somewhere beneath the crust, continue their long, underpowered cycles. The heat rises steadily from these buried workings, keeping the rocks and the water in a state of slow exchange: the stone weeps, the vapor condenses, the paddles turn, and the film at the bottom rises and falls by imperceptible degrees across hours that feel like days.
