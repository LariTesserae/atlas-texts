The world is a single, unbroken ocean whose surface lies kilometers above any possible sky. From any vantage the water extends in every direction as a uniform medium—clear enough that shafts of ordinary daylight still reach the mountaintops, yet thick enough that those shafts never quite seem to arrive from a single source. The peaks themselves are high, their summits crowded together like a submerged cordillera that has only recently finished rising. Because they are young, their ridges remain knife-edged; hydrothermal vents and mineral exhalations have left them sheathed in fresh crusts of glassy black and sulfur-white, so angular that a drifting body would be sliced open before it could settle.

Nothing grows. The rock and the precipitated salts that coat it support no algae, no mats, no recognizable chemistry of life. Instead the same minerals accrete into slow, branching lattices that record nothing except pressure and time. Between these lattices the water is almost empty—kilometers of transparent volume containing only stray flecks of suspended carbonate and the occasional slow ribbon of warmer current. Yet the emptiness is inhabited. Schools of translucent, pressure-tolerant organisms the size of freight cars drift through the intervals like living lenses, their internal organs visible as shifting constellations of metallic granules. Smaller, quicker forms—segmented, faceted, armored in precipitated silica—wheel in coordinated vortices that last for hours before dispersing again into the general void. Their numbers are immense, but the distances between them are larger still, so the impression is never of crowding, only of constant, measured presence.

Scattered across the same peaks stand the remnants of an industrial order that has not altered in geological time. A single colossal turbine housing, its vanes locked at a fixed angle, hums through an intake grate the diameter of a stadium. Kilometers farther on, a latticework of pipes climbs a razor ridge and terminates in an open manifold that releases steady, silent bubbles the size of houses; the bubbles rise, burst against nothing, and are instantly replaced. Platforms, gantries, and pressure domes appear at irregular intervals, some half-buried in fresh mineral growth, others still connected by taut cables that have never parted. Their surfaces are neither corroded nor overgrown; they simply persist, radiating a low, continuous vibration that travels farther through water than light ever could.

That vibration is the only sound. It does not rise or fall. Every turbine, every manifold, every flexing plate of the living machinery contributes to the same infrasonic chord, so constant and so total that it occupies the entire spectrum of hearing. Inside the chord are smaller, repeating pulses—the opening and closing of valves larger than cathedrals, the slow passage of armored fauna through resonant chambers—but these too remain locked in phase. Nothing begins, nothing ends. The light remains ordinary; the water remains cold; the mineral ridges continue to sharpen under their own hydrostatic weight. The world holds its single configuration without gradient or decay, an equilibrium measured in the lifetimes of mountains rather than of any creature that moves through it.
