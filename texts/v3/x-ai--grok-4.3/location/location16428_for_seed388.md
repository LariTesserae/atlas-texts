In the low basin where the city’s oldest drainage once gathered, a single square block of land has been claimed and sealed by its own weight. High brick-and-iron walls, stained the color of wet iron, rise on three sides; the fourth opens onto a channel that was never meant to be a street. Rain finds no outlet here. It condenses on every surface, runs down in slow, persistent threads, and re-forms as beads that never quite fall. The air itself feels saturated, as if the boundary between water and vapor has simply dissolved.

Machinery still operates, though its purpose has long since outlived the people who built it. Condenser towers the height of three men stand at irregular intervals, their copper coils weeping condensate into open troughs. The troughs feed a lattice of smaller ceramic pipes that cross and re-cross between the buildings like veins. Steam engines the size of small houses turn at a pace slow enough to watch: pistons rise, pause, descend, pause again, their motion measured in the time it takes a thick drip to travel the length of a valve. Each cycle sends a low, resonant boom through the brickwork, loud enough to fill the enclosed space yet never quickening. The sound does not echo so much as accumulate, layering upon itself until the entire square feels upholstered in sustained tone.

Vegetation has filled every joint and seam. Mosses the color of tarnished brass coat the lower courses of brick; thicker, leathery leaves—almost plates—protrude from every vent and seam, their surfaces beaded with water that never evaporates. Vines of a deep, wet green climb the condenser towers and then drape downward in curtains that nearly brush the ground. The leaves are so dense they create their own low canopy, dimming the light further. What sun does reach the square arrives already filtered through layers of vapor and leaf, arriving as a dull bronze sheen that moves only when the great pistons shift and momentarily alter the drift of steam.

Nothing moves quickly here. Water advances by capillary action and gravity; machines advance by fractions of a degree per hour; the vines advance by the width of a single new cell per day. Even the constant dripping is metronomic rather than urgent. Over centuries the surfaces have worn into one another: iron flanges rounded smooth, brick edges softened to curves, pipe lips thickened where mineral scale has built up. The erosion is not destruction but equilibrium; every feature has found the shape it will hold for another few centuries.

Within this bounded volume, absence is as present as anything else. No insects crawl the leaves, no birds alight on the towers, no small scuttlings occur in the pipework. Only the water, the slow machines, and the dense, patient growth remain. The square feels both inhabited and empty, a single room scaled to the size of a hamlet, inside which the rules are simple and unchanging: nothing leaves quickly, nothing arrives suddenly, and the water never stops finding new paths downward through the layered city that has become its own slow tide.
