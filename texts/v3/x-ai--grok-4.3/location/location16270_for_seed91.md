The place unfolds as a vast, cold hollow beneath the skin of the world, a regional cavern system whose ceiling lies just far enough overhead that its edges dissolve into dimness. Sharp ridges of recently fractured stone rise from the floor in long, blade-like fins; their surfaces are unsoftened by time, still showing the pale, clean planes where some immense pressure once split them free. Between these ridges the ground is not bare but given over to a living substrate: thick, fibrous mats of pale, cold-tolerant vegetation that creep outward in slow, patient lobes. The plants do not grow upward so much as outward and downward, forming continuous spongy platforms that bridge gaps and line every available surface. Their tissues are dense enough to serve as floor, wall, and occasional low canopy, yet the overall impression remains sparse; wide lanes of exposed stone still run between the living masses, and the eye travels easily across these empty corridors before meeting the next green-gray swell.

Moisture arrives in intermittent veils. Condensation beads constantly on the colder rock faces and on the long, translucent filaments the plants extend upward like slow antennae. When the cycle peaks, fine rain falls from the unseen ceiling in brief, localized showers that last only minutes before the droplets are drawn back into the plant network or gather in shallow stone cups that never quite empty. The water never forms rivers here, only scattered lenses and threads that reflect what little light reaches them.

Scattered through this living architecture stand the remnants of industrial presence: low, windowless blockhouses half-swallowed by the mat, their concrete surfaces cracked in straight, deliberate lines from which thick root-like strands emerge. Some still house slow-turning turbines whose blades move at a speed perceptible only after long minutes of watching; their housings emit a continuous, low-frequency pressure that fills every open space and returns from the distant walls in overlapping echoes. The sound is not localized; it is the constant atmospheric note of the place, loud enough that even the drip of water or the faint shift of a plant lobe registers only as variations within it.

Light is meager and steady, arriving from high, narrow fissures where surface frost somehow filters down as a pale, bluish-gray wash. The plants themselves contribute nothing luminous; they simply absorb what little arrives and hold it in matte, almost velvet textures. Temperature hovers near freezing, enough to slow every process yet not enough to halt the gradual expansion of the living mass or the incremental turning of the turbines.

Fauna is almost absent. An occasional translucent isopod traverses a stone lane in deliberate, millimeter-per-minute progress, its path leaving a faint track across the condensation. No birds, no insects in flight, only the rare soft click of chitin against stone that is immediately absorbed into the larger mechanical drone.

The rules of the place emerge from the tension between its youth and its patience. The rock is still adjusting to its new exposure; the plants are still claiming it, ridge by ridge, decade by decade. The machines continue their work at the same tempo as the plants that now cradle them, neither fully obstructing the other. Water arrives, lingers, and is redistributed without haste. Over spans of seasons and centuries the cavern widens by the width of a single plant lobe or the depth of one additional fracture, while the deafening background tone never rises or falls, only persists.
