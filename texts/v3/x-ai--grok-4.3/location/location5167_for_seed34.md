The highland basin stretches without visible shore or horizon, a single continent of stone lifted above the clouds that drift below its rim. At this elevation the air is thin and steady, cool enough that breath hangs and moisture gathers on every surface. Water does not fall from above so much as rise from within: pale limestone and dark basalt both weep in slow, continuous threads, tracing the same grooves they have followed for ages. The rock therefore stays dark and faintly glistening even when no rain passes.

Across this immense floor the stone has been worked by time into an array of vertical slabs, shallow terraces, and intersecting walls. The forms stand close enough that a person walking between them must turn sideways in places; their tops meet overhead in broken vaults or leave long skylit lanes open to the ordinary daylight. Nothing here was quarried or placed, yet the result resembles an old quarter of a city whose streets were carved downward rather than built upward. Joints and bedding planes have widened into passages; harder layers remain as lintels and sills. Water follows every vertical seam, so that from any single vantage the eye meets dozens of thin silver lines falling and rejoining before they sink into cracks at ankle level.

Vegetation is present but meager. Small cushions of moss and pale lichen occupy the shadiest ledges where condensation lingers longest. Occasional tufts of grass root in pockets of wind-blown dust, their blades no taller than a finger. Between these patches the rock surfaces stay bare, scoured or coated only by the same mineral film that darkens the weeping walls.

Movement is almost entirely fluid. Mist forms and dissolves inside the deeper lanes, rising from the saturated stone and sliding along temperature gradients until it meets a shaft of clearer light and thins again. The process repeats at every scale: droplets gather, descend, pool briefly on a horizontal tread, then overflow into the next fissure. Wind moves through the same corridors, but softly; it rarely lifts above a low, steady murmur against the stone. No other sounds carry far. Occasional faint clicks mark grains of sediment settling deeper in hidden sumps, yet these vanish into the prevailing quiet.

Animals are scarce and largely invisible. A few pale salamanders press against the wet undersides of ledges, their presence betrayed only by the momentary shift of a shadow when one slips farther into a crack. High above, once in several hours, the silhouette of a gliding raptor may cross an open lane and disappear behind the next wall, its cry already swallowed by distance before it can echo.

Because the entire formation exists at continental scale, local weather systems develop inside it. One lane may hold drifting fog while a neighboring court, warmed by a brief interval of direct sun, releases its own rising vapor. These micro-cycles feed the larger pattern of condensation and seepage, so the stone remains in slow but constant exchange with the air above and the aquifers below. The balance between erosion and deposition has long since settled; the city-like shapes are neither growing nor collapsing, only continuing to guide water along the paths they have already established through uncounted seasons.
