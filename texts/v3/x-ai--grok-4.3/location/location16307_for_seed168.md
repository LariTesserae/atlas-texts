High on a windless plateau, the land rises in broad, unfinished swells of raw mineral. Vast distances separate each upwelling from the next; the eye travels kilometers across pale, unfractured flats before meeting the next cluster of nascent forms. Nothing has yet been worn smooth. Every surface still carries the matte texture of first crystallization, edges abrupt and slightly luminous where light catches the lattice planes.

Between these distant clusters the ground is nearly vacant, a pale salt-and-silica pavement that reflects heat without storing it. The air is warm and perfectly still, dry enough that breath feels thin. Scattered across the flats are low, dense thickets of mineral filaments—silicate threads and branching selenite spines that grow without moisture, drawing their substance from the slow upward migration of dissolved ions. They form lush, overlapping canopies only a few paces high, yet they are so finely divided that each thicket reads as a single living field rather than distinct plants. Their surfaces are faintly warm to the touch, radiating the same steady temperature as the surrounding air.

Farther apart stand taller formations still locked in their original posture of emergence. Some rise like unfinished engine blocks, flat terraces and cylindrical cores aligned with mathematical regularity; others resemble ribbed conduit or the skeletal frames of turbines, all cut from the same continuous stone. No joints or seams betray manufacture; the geometry is simply the geometry the rock found stable. Between and beneath these shapes the ground remains open, the intervening space deliberately empty, as though the plateau has decided exactly how much presence is sufficient and refuses any more.

Nothing moves. No grains shift, no crystals lengthen or contract. The entire arrangement exists in an arrested state of becoming, its processes balanced so precisely that change would require an external push the environment no longer supplies. Light arrives at ordinary intensity, diffused slightly by a high, invisible haze that never thins or thickens. Sound is reduced to the subtlest internal resonance: an almost inaudible crystalline sigh that seems to come from inside the ear rather than the rock. No animals traverse the flats, and the mineral filaments do not sway.

At the horizon the same pattern repeats at regional scale—identical embryonic industrial silhouettes spaced at impossible intervals, each group lush with its own mineral canopy, each separated by the same deliberate emptiness. The plateau holds them all in perfect equilibrium, a finished geography that has not yet finished arriving.
