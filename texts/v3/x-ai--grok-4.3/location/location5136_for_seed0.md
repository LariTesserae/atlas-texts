The basin unfolds as a low, wide declivity that has only recently torn itself out of older stone, its floor still carrying the knife-edge ridges and splintered basalt spines of fresh uplift. Sharp black spines rise like half-formed teeth through the mat of growth, their surfaces still gritty with unsmoothed fracture planes; rain has not yet rounded them, and the occasional mist that drifts through leaves them gleaming instead of dulled. Between these spines the ground is sodden but not deeply flooded—pockets of standing water gather in every depression, each one a shallow mirror that steams gently in the warm air before sinking back into the root lattice.

Vegetation packs the space so tightly that movement itself seems to rearrange the layout. Broad, slick leaves overlap in three or four vertical layers, their surfaces continuously filmed with condensation that falls in slow, audible drops even when no rain is falling. The leaves themselves pulse with a low, internal phosphor, enough to mark the veins of stems and the trembling edges of fronds but not to lift the overall gloom; the light stays at the level of deep dusk, a permanent half-silver that makes distance difficult to judge and turns every thicket into a layered silhouette. Because the canopy and undergrowth are so continuous, the basin feels both open and claustrophobic at once: sightlines extend for hundreds of paces across the undulating floor, yet every line of sight is broken by the sheer number of stems, vines, and hanging sheets of translucent growth.

The air is warm and heavy, carrying the constant sound of water negotiating new routes through the living mass. That sound is only the quiet substrate beneath a louder, ceaseless commotion. The basin teems with fauna whose numbers and variety produce an unbroken roar—wings the size of hands clatter against ribs of leaf, choruses of stridulating bodies rise and fall like surf, and heavier, slower creatures push through the undergrowth with wet, tearing noises that are answered from every direction. The density of life is such that individual movements blur into a single turbulent surface; a herd of soft-bodied climbers may flood a ridge in minutes, stripping its vegetation and leaving behind a slick of mucus and half-digested fiber before the next wave arrives from another quarter. Plants respond by closing wounds with rapid sap exudation or by shifting entire stem clusters a hand-span sideways, their roots dragging small stones in the process. These slow rearrangements, multiplied across thousands of organisms, keep the ground in constant minor flux.

No artifact of deliberate making interrupts the scene. What looks at first like a low wall of woven fiber is only the accumulated castings of colonial burrowers; a glint of metal is a shed mandible or a mineral concretion precipitated where two currents of warm, mineral-laden water meet. The creatures themselves practice only the most rudimentary modifications—stacking damp pellets to steer condensation into their nests, gnawing a tunnel wider for a season or two—yet even these small acts add to the overall din and churn.

The basin therefore operates under a simple, self-reinforcing rule: everything that is not actively moving or secreting is quickly covered, displaced, or consumed. Sharp new stone is already beginning to wear under the pressure of roots and the friction of numberless small bodies; water that is not immediately absorbed or evaporated is trampled into temporary channels that last only until the next surge of migration reroutes it. The darkness, the warmth, the crowding, and the noise together maintain a single continuous agitation in which growth and collapse occur at nearly the same pace, leaving the landscape always freshly itself and never quite the same from one moment to the next.
