In the depths of a recently fractured stratum, where the rock has split and heaved upward from some patient geothermal pulse, lies a bounded hollow no larger than a cathedral nave. The ceiling hangs low and uneven, its fresh fractures still bright with the pale gleam of unweathered mineral. Sharp ridges and narrow ledges jut outward, their edges knife-like; water, warmed from below, condenses on these surfaces in slow beads that collect, merge, and finally release as single, deliberate drips into shallow basins cut only centuries ago.

Between the ridges, pockets of damp soil—thin, black, and faintly steaming—support scattered stands of vegetation that thrive without light. The plants are broad-leaved and heavy, their surfaces slick with condensation; they grow in loose clusters, leaving generous intervals of bare stone between them. Some leaves have a metallic sheen, as though they draw trace elements from the surrounding rock and the half-buried machinery that shares their soil. Their growth is almost imperceptible, a thickening of stems and an unfurling of new fronds measured in decades rather than seasons.

Scattered through the same intervals are the remnants of an earlier industrial effort: lengths of pipe that once carried pressurized vapor now leak thin, continuous plumes of warm mist; the housings of pumps and small turbines lie half-embedded in the floor, their casings split along crystalline seams by the same young expansion that opened the cavern. A few copper coils still hold a residual charge, releasing, once every few hours, a thread of dim blue light that travels along mineral veins before fading. The machines do not hum; they merely radiate a steady, low heat that mingles with the geothermal warmth already present.

No animals move here. The only sounds are the measured intervals of condensation falling into pooled water and, occasionally, the soft sigh of a pressure valve settling after long stillness. The air remains hot and saturated, pressing against the skin without wind or circulation, so that a standing figure feels suspended inside a single, unchanging breath.

Over longer durations the rock continues to part along its newest fissures, allowing additional moisture to seep downward; the plants extend their roots toward the warmer seams; the corroding metal slowly donates its elements to the soil. Each process advances by microns and degrees, yet all remain visible if watched across the span of a human life. Within this small, enclosed volume the landscape is still forming itself, its lush, mechanical, and mineral elements balanced in a slow, mutual accommodation.
