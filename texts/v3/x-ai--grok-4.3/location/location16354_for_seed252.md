In a cramped cavity no larger than a modest room, the rock itself seems newly opened, its walls still carrying the raw geometry of fracture. Sharp ridges and angular facets catch the eye everywhere; there is no smoothing here, only the clean, glinting edges of material that has not yet known long abrasion. Water moves constantly through the setting, though never as a free-flowing stream. Instead it gathers in invisible films along pore networks, then condenses into slow, heavy drops that cling to the undersides of ledges before falling. The drops strike the floor with a soft, measured rhythm, each impact absorbed rather than echoed, so that the space remains mostly quiet even while never truly still.

The heat is steady and penetrating, enough to keep the air thick and the rock surfaces warm to the touch. Because the cavity lies well below the surface, no daylight reaches it; the brightness that fills the space comes from within the minerals themselves. Microscopic domains inside translucent crystal veins re-emit accumulated energy as a clear, even illumination that makes every facet and droplet plainly visible without glare or shadow.

No plants or conventional life occupy the surfaces. What grows instead are delicate mineral accretions: needle-like sulfate formations and wafer-thin plates that extend outward a few centimeters before terminating in open space. These structures appear at irregular intervals, leaving wide stretches of bare wall between them; the emptiness feels deliberate, as though the chemistry that produces them proceeds slowly and selectively. Occasionally a droplet lands on one of the needles and spreads into a brief, trembling film before evaporating again, restarting the same cycle elsewhere.

In the intervals between drops, faint vibrations travel through the stone—minute expansions and contractions as temperature gradients shift and new layers of dissolved salts precipitate. These movements are too subtle to register as sound, yet they keep the chamber in continuous, low-level activity. Once in a long while, a cluster of the needle crystals will shear away under its own slight weight and tumble to the floor, where it dissolves back into the damp film already present. The loss is quickly compensated; within hours, fresh needles begin to extend from the same site.

Nothing constructed interrupts the surfaces. No fauna larger than a fleck of sediment moves through the open volume; only the water, the heat, and the slow, precise deposition of new mineral record the passage of time. The cavity feels at once enclosed and unfinished, as if the rock around it is still deciding what shape to settle into next.
