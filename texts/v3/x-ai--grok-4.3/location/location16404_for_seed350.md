The world is a single, slow-flooded basin whose water has not finished arriving. It arrived warm, the way bathwater holds the memory of a body, and it has not quite decided where the surface is supposed to be. From any given altitude the basin looks like an open, low plain that has been pressed beneath a moving lens of brownish water two to three hundred meters deep in places. The lens itself keeps thickening; new sheets of rain fall through the upper layers and dissolve before they reach bottom, so that the entire volume feels like a continuous, slow-motion downpour that never strikes ground.

What passes for ground is a young, still-settling plain of industrial sediment: half-submerged rail beds, collapsed gantry frames, and the ribbed shells of turbines whose blades have begun to leaf. Fresh fractures show sharp edges of concrete and alloy; erosion has not yet softened them. Over these geometries a dense, warm-water vegetation has grown in layered canopies. Broad, vein-thick fronds rise in slow spirals from every flat surface, weaving between rusted I-beams and threading through the open stator housings of drowned generators. The plants are not quite kelp and not quite vines; they extrude thin metallic threads that conduct faint, directionless current, so that the foliage itself occasionally shivers with synchronized pulses of galvanic light.

Nothing swims. The water contains only the continuous negotiation between vegetable mass and mechanical carcass. Where a pressure vessel has split, bundles of cable now serve as root systems; where a crane boom lies toppled, its lattice has become a trellis for sheets of translucent membrane that billow like slow lungs. The membranes trap rising bubbles of methane and superheated steam from deeper fissures, so that the canopy is always rearranging itself around sudden, silent pockets of lift.

Light is the color of weak tea. It arrives already filtered through the upper rain-layers and is further diminished by the overlapping fronds, yet it is still enough to drive the vegetation. In the dimness the plants have become slightly phosphorescent along their fracture lines; when a stalk tears, the wound glows the same dull orange as an idling furnace.

Sound does not travel here so much as occupy every available volume at once. The continuous low-frequency thrum of submerged turbines that have never been switched off, the higher, glassy screech of metal expanding against living tissue, the arrhythmic slap of membrane sheets against themselves, and an underlying, directionless roaring that seems to come from the water deciding, at every scale, whether to remain liquid or to become something else. That roaring occasionally fractures into momentary silence or into a doubled register, as if two different recordings of the same event are playing microseconds out of phase; these fractures are the visible seams of the world coming apart. In the instants when reality slips, a gantry leg may appear to stand on its own reflection ten meters higher than it ought to be, or a curtain of fronds will briefly reveal the negative of itself, black against the brownish light, before the image reseals.

Scattered through this volume are the last legible artifacts of the surface that existed before the water: a single intact control room whose armored glass has bowed outward but not yet broken, a signal tower whose lamps still cycle through three rusted colors, a row of lockers whose doors hang open, their contents replaced by compacted mats of root. These remainders are few enough that they register more as punctuation than as settlement, yet they continue to leak heat, current, and the faint ozone smell that the water has not yet learned how to wash away.

Currents move through the basin in abrupt, reversing surges rather than steady flow. When one passes, whole canopies lean, membranes inflate or deflate, and the metallic threads inside the plants emit brief, coordinated sparks that travel upward like fuse runs before fading. In the wake of each surge the water itself may momentarily clarify, revealing, far below, the angular silhouette of an entire factory complex whose roof has collapsed inward and whose interior now functions as a single vast lumen for the vegetation. The complex is still drawing power from somewhere; its conveyors turn without cargo, and its ventilators push slow columns of warmer water toward the surface. Between the conveyors, new growth has already begun to sprout in the same repeating industrial pattern, as though the plants have learned the original blueprints and are now executing them more faithfully than the machines ever did.

The basin continues outward in every horizontal direction until the dim water and the overlapping layers of frond reduce it to a single, greenish-brown haze. There is no shoreline visible, only the suggestion, at the extreme limit of sight, of further towers and further canopies rising and falling in the same turbulent, sound-saturated rhythm. The place is still forming. Every surge rewrites a small portion of its own floor plan, and the vegetation follows the revisions without hesitation.
