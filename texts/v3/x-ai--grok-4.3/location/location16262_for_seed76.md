The landscape is a single, unbroken highland that rises until the horizon itself begins to curve away. Its surface is a pale, wind-polished mineral plain veined with darker strata that have been planed flat by epochs of steady abrasion; the rock feels like cooled lava underfoot, yet it is veined with seams of translucent quartz that catch the constant light and throw it back in soft, milky refractions. Between these exposed ribs the ground is covered by a thin skin of fine, chalk-colored grit that never quite settles; every slow gust lifts and redeposits it in low, comet-tailed drifts that trace the prevailing wind for kilometers before fading.

Scattered across this plain stand the only living things that can be called plants. They are not green. Their trunks and boughs are built of layered silicates and hydrated oxides that gleam like polished bone; each “leaf” is a broad, wafer-thin plate of the same material, arranged in overlapping fans that catch and slowly condense the scant atmospheric moisture into droplets that roll inward along microscopic channels cut into the mineral itself. Because the plateaus are vast enough to generate their own weather, these fans flex in rhythmic response to the diurnal pressure gradients, opening at dawn and drawing slightly inward by midday. From a distance the effect is of a motionless white forest breathing; up close one hears nothing louder than the faintest crystalline click when a flexure joint reaches its limit and resets.

The air is thin, cold, and almost silent. The only persistent sound is the slow, granular whisper of dust sliding across dust, punctuated at irregular intervals by the soft report of a single quartz plate snapping back into alignment. No creatures move during the bright hours; the sparse fauna—minute, armor-plated grazers no larger than a thumbnail—remain torpid inside narrow fissures until the light angle lowers and the temperature drops another few degrees. Then they emerge in ones and twos, leaving hair-fine trails across the mineral crust before retreating again.

Because nothing ever accumulates here—no standing water, no thick soil, no layered forest canopy—the entire plateau exists in a state of perpetual, self-similar equilibrium. Wind, light, and the slow flex of the mineral flora move the same grains of grit along the same paths year after year; erosion has already removed every protuberance that could create local turbulence, so the only weather is the great, continent-spanning circulation of dust and pressure that the plateau itself sustains. The light remains constant and fierce, yet the surface never heats; the same mineral plates that condense moisture also radiate heat upward so efficiently that the ground temperature stays fixed a few degrees above the chill of the thin air. Nothing is built, nothing is tended, nothing accumulates beyond the steady redistribution of the same pale dust across the same pale stone.
