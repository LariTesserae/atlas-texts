The place is one sealed chamber, roughly the volume of a small cathedral, that has been built and rebuilt until nothing inside it can be called natural substrate. Its floor is a single plate of composite mesh, ribbed and riveted, held far above the clouds by a lattice of struts whose lowest joints are already lost to mist. Everything is metal, ceramic, or cured resin: walls formed from successive skins of riveted plating, ceilings stepped like the inside of an enormous gearbox, structural pillars that once carried load now half-dissolved into baroque flanges and dripping stalactites of slag.

The air is warm, thick with the smell of warm oil and wet stone. Condensation forms on the upper vaults and falls in long, deliberate intervals, striking the mesh floor with soft metallic notes that are immediately swallowed by a deeper, slower sound: the breathing of the engines. They do not cycle quickly. Their flywheels turn through minutes, their pistons descend with the patience of geological time, yet each movement is massive enough to send low-frequency pressure rolling through the chamber like distant thunder. The sound never stops; it only changes register as heat expands a pipe or a slow valve reseats itself.

Between these colossal mechanisms lie empty volumes. A person could walk for minutes across open mesh before the next machine-island appears, its shape half-obscured by hanging gardens. The gardens are lush—ferns, mosses, and flowering vines whose roots have found the constant warmth radiating from the pipes. They drape across catwalks and spill through perforated housings, turning the abandoned machinery into living architecture. Water collects in shallow trays where pipes have wept for decades; the trays overflow in measured drips that keep the vegetation damp without flooding the lower levels.

Fauna moves through the same emptiness. Pale moths the size of plates patrol the condensation zone, their wings brushing the undersides of the vaults. Smaller things—lizards with heat-sensitive scales, colonies of metallic beetles—live inside the slowly turning housings, feeding on the micro-flora that grows where lubricant meets moisture. Their numbers are high, yet the scale of the chamber keeps them from crowding; each creature occupies its own territory of pipe or leaf.

Light arrives through a grid of quartz lenses set high in the southern wall. During the day the beams are steady and white, falling in clean shafts that pick out individual leaves and the slow rotation of a distant camshaft. At night the same lenses admit faint city-glow from whatever lies far below, turning the chamber into a dim lantern of reflections.

Nothing here is new. Every surface shows the same settled erosion: rust that has reached equilibrium with protective coatings, edges rounded by centuries of slow vibration, moss that has grown into the exact pattern the windless air allows. The machines themselves continue their gradual revolutions, their rhythms measured in hours or days rather than seconds. Change arrives only as heat-warped metal finally cracks, or as a vine thickens enough to jam a once-moving linkage, or as a new pocket of water forms where a joint finally weeps. Each alteration is absorbed into the larger, slower order of the chamber, so that the whole place feels both utterly artificial and profoundly ancient, a single constructed organism still turning inside its own skin.
