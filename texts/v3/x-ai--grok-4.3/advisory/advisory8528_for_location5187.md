**Preparation for the Saturated Basin**

This is not a place you visit so much as one you temporarily inhabit while the water and the geometry decide whether to tolerate you. Treat it as an active, semi-sentient wetland whose rules are still being written.

**Clothing and Skin**
You will be wet for the entire duration of your stay. Condensation forms directly on exposed surfaces, including your face and hands, and the air itself is a slow-moving aerosol. Bring a layered system built around vapor-barrier garments rather than typical waterproof shells: a thin merino or synthetic base layer next to skin, a vapor-barrier suit (the kind speleologists or extreme kayakers use), and a loose, highly breathable outer shell that can be vented. Anything that traps sweat will turn into a personal swamp within hours.

Footwear should be high, soft, and grippy rather than stiff hiking boots. The peat surface is like saturated sponge; rigid soles punch through and mire you. Consider neoprene socks inside lightweight rubber overshoes with aggressive lugs, or frog-tongue style amphibious boots. Change socks at least twice a day if you value your feet.

Skin will macerate. Bring antifungal powder, zinc oxide, and a silicone barrier cream. The constant moisture plus the faint ozone tang suggests the local biology is not entirely friendly to terrestrial epidermis.

**Movement and Navigation**
There are no durable paths. Any line you try to maintain dissolves. Carry a lightweight staff or probe both for balance and to test depth; the surface can go from ankle-deep to thigh-deep without visual warning. Mark your route with floating, biodegradable indicators (small cork or foam tabs on monofilament) rather than cairns or blazes; anything heavier sinks or is overgrown within hours.

GPS and compasses behave unreliably near the fractures. Bring a mechanical compass as backup and learn to navigate by the subtle grain of the vegetation and the consistent downhill pull of hidden drainage. The basin’s micro-topography creates its own slow current; if you pay attention, water always finds the same subtle channels.

**Reality Fractures**
These are brief but disorienting. Gravity can tilt, space can flatten or fold, and local temperature can drop enough to flash-freeze condensation. Carry a short tether (3–4 meters of 3 mm spectra line) clipped to your belt and a nearby stable object or companion when fractures feel imminent; the chime-like sounds and sudden silence are often precursors. Do not fight the tilt; let your body adjust and wait for the geometry to zip shut again. The event usually lasts only a few seconds.

The cold spots that form on your skin can be startling but are transient. They do not appear to cause lasting thermal damage.

**Fauna and Sound**
Most animals remain out of sight. The audible layer is dense enough that you may not notice their presence until something large displaces water under the peat. Keep voices low and movements deliberate. The constant water patter masks small sounds; if the background hiss suddenly hushes, stop and wait. Many of the creatures seem to respond to attention itself.

**Mindset and Duration**
The basin exerts a slow, osmotic pressure on attention. People report losing track of time and the urge to simply stand still and listen. Set strict time limits and physical markers for return. The landscape is not hostile, but it is profoundly disinterested in your continued distinction from itself. Prolonged exposure blurs proprioception; many travelers begin to feel their own outline softening.

Bring nothing you cannot afford to lose or have altered. Objects left behind do not remain objects for long.

In short: dress like you are entering a cold cloud forest that occasionally forgets which direction down is. Move slowly, mark everything, assume the moisture is permanent, and treat the acoustic and spatial glitches as weather rather than events. The basin will continue its patient cycle whether you adapt to it or not.
