**Preparation for the fractured geothermal hollow**

This is a young, still-settling pocket of rock and industrial ruin maintained at roughly 38–42 °C with near-100 % humidity and zero airflow. An ordinary traveler must treat the visit like an extended stay inside a sealed, slowly cooking engine room that is also still being born.

### Core environmental protection
- **Heat and moisture management**: Wear a single layer of loose, high-wicking technical fabric (merino or synthetic) under a lightweight, fully sealed rainshell. The shell is not for rain but to create a micro-climate you can vent; otherwise condensation will soak you within minutes. Bring at least two liters of water per hour of planned stay—more if you will be active—plus electrolyte salts. A small misting bottle or damp cloth around the neck helps, but do not expect evaporative cooling; the air is already saturated.
- **Footwear**: Stiff-soled, high-ankle boots with aggressive tread and a rigid shank. The floor is a mixture of knife-edged new fracture surfaces, slick mineral deposits, and half-buried machine housings. One misstep onto a copper coil or pipe edge will end the visit.

### Lighting and navigation
- The only native illumination is an intermittent, very dim blue discharge along mineral veins every few hours. Carry a headlamp with both white and red LEDs, plus a backup chemical light stick per person. Map the space mentally or with a simple compass; the low ceiling and irregular ridges create blind pockets that feel larger than they are. Mark your entry point with a physical object rather than relying on memory—the rock is still shifting along its newest fissures.

### Respiratory and dermal concerns
- The air is hot, wet, and chemically active from geothermal gases and corroding metal. A simple particulate respirator (N95 or better) is advisable for stays longer than twenty minutes; the metallic aerosols and possible trace sulfur compounds accumulate quickly. Long sleeves and light gloves protect against both sharp stone and the mildly caustic film that condenses on every surface.
- Bring a small desiccant pack for electronics and optics. Any exposed metal or glass will fog instantly and stay fogged.

### Interaction with the machinery and biology
- The copper coils retain residual charge. Do not touch exposed conductors or allow conductive tools to bridge mineral veins when the blue glow appears. The corroded housings are structurally compromised; treat every piece of embedded equipment as unstable.
- The broad-leaved plants are not aggressive, but their metallic sheen suggests they concentrate elements from the rock and machinery. Do not ingest leaves, sap, or soil. Their growth is measured in decades; even careful footsteps compact the thin soil enough to be noticeable over a human lifetime, so move slowly and minimize contact.

### Temporal and psychological preparation
- The space reveals itself only across hours. Plan to remain still for long stretches rather than touring. The acoustic environment is almost silent except for regular drips and occasional valve sighs; many visitors find the combination of heat, darkness, and near-perfect acoustic absorption disorienting after thirty or forty minutes. A small timer or watch with an alarm is useful to prevent losing track of elapsed time.
- Because the rock is still parting along fresh fractures, re-entry on subsequent visits may reveal measurable changes in ceiling height, drip patterns, or new soil pockets. Document the configuration on the first visit if you intend to return.

### Exit protocol
- Before departure, rinse boots and outer clothing at the nearest pool to avoid tracking warm, mineral-rich mud into other strata. Check for condensation inside sealed packs; anything left damp will grow visible corrosion within hours once removed from the humid environment.

In short, the traveler must become a self-contained, slow-moving heat sink who brings their own dry air, light, and structural awareness, then remains quiet long enough for the cavern’s own slow processes to become perceptible.
