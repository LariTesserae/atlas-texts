An ordinary traveler stepping onto this plain should expect an environment that behaves like an enormous, half-finished geological machine still running on residual momentum. Preparation begins with treating the body as the only reliable life-support system, because nothing here will supply moisture, food, or shelter.

Clothing should be loose, multi-layered, and mineral-resistant. The air is uniformly cool and desiccating; a base layer that wicks perspiration yet allows slow evaporation prevents both overheating during long walks and the rapid chilling that follows once motion stops. Over that, a windproof shell with a high collar and sealable cuffs blocks the fine crystalline dust that rises with each footfall and settles into every seam. Footwear needs rigid soles thick enough to mute the crackling of the salt crust yet flexible enough to register the faint vibrations that precede each geometric tremor. Bring multiple pairs of thin inner socks; the outer soles will grind against glass and salt until they polish smooth.

Water is the single limiting factor. The metallic dryness at the back of the throat is cumulative and will grow into a persistent cough if humidity is not supplied artificially. Carry sealed, insulated flasks rather than open canteens; the low ambient moisture will evaporate exposed liquid within hours. Electrolyte supplements help counteract the leaching effect of the air. Caloric intake should be compact and dry—pressed bars or sealed rations—because chewing anything with significant water content becomes unpleasant once the throat is already desiccated. No local source of refreshment exists.

Vision and balance require deliberate countermeasures. The shifting, sourceless illumination and the occasional inversion of shadows can trigger mild disorientation after several hours. Polarized lenses with a slight neutral-density filter reduce the glare from the black-glass bands and the white salt without creating false contrast. A lightweight walking staff or pole serves as both probe for unexpected fissures and a physical reference when visual cues drift. During tremors, stop and plant the staff; the realignments are small but can shift footing by centimeters without warning.

Navigation is best treated as temporary. The avenues and mesas repeat at every scale, and the horizon never resolves into anything closer. Mark your route with small, high-visibility objects—bright polymer tags or reflective beads—that contrast with both the white and black strata. The slender filaments that rise and retract do so slowly enough to be avoided, but they can snag loose fabric or equipment if you pass too close; treat them as brittle tripwires.

Psychological preparation matters more than most travelers expect. The sense that the city-like formations were occupied moments earlier is persistent and can produce a low-grade alertness that becomes exhausting. Accept that no other human-scale movement will occur except the occasional dark, faceted shape, which shows no interest in observers and should be given wide berth. The silence is not absolute; the high harmonic that accompanies each tremor is real and can be heard clearly if ambient motion stops. Use it as a clock. When it fades, resume travel; when it begins, pause.

Because the plain is active at a geological pace, treat every surface as potentially mobile over long periods. Camp, if rest is required, on the open pavement rather than inside any rectangular void; the slabs and cubes continue their incremental repositioning even while you sleep. A compact insulated pad and a reflective blanket suffice, since temperature remains steady and wind is rare.

In short, arrive self-contained, move deliberately, mark your path, and leave before the cumulative dryness and spatial repetition erode judgment. The place rewards patience and punishes haste or attachment to any single formation.
