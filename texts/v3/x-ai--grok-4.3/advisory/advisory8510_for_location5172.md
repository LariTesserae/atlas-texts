To visit this place, think less like a hiker and more like someone preparing to spend years inside a single living thing that does not know it has guests.

**Moisture and material survival.**  
You will never be dry. Pack nothing that can rot, mildew, or delaminate. Synthetics and sealed ceramics only. Leather, cotton, paper, and untreated metals will begin to break down within weeks. Bring desiccants for anything you must keep functional (electronics, blades, maps). The air itself is a slow solvent; any joint or seam will eventually host micro-growth. Assume your pack will gain weight from absorbed water and the faint films that settle on every surface.

**Light and perception.**  
The bronze gloom is permanent. Even at noon the interior feels like late twilight. Bring a reliable, non-open-flame light source shielded against constant condensation. Your eyes will adapt to the low light, but color perception will degrade; train yourself to notice shape and texture rather than hue. Navigation by landmarks is nearly useless at human scale—everything looks the same after a few hours. Mark your path with something inert (glass beads, metal tags) because the mass will slowly close over cuts or disturbances.

**Movement and time discipline.**  
Nothing here respects human urgency. If you need to cross a plateau-scale distance, plan in seasons, not days. The living structure grows around obstacles; a passage that exists today may be blocked in a decade. Carry tools for cutting, but use them sparingly—the mass will respond by sealing and redirecting moisture toward the wound. Learn to move through existing galleries rather than across surfaces. When you rest, do so in hollows already formed; trying to create new shelter risks being slowly incorporated.

**Breathing and biology.**  
The atmosphere is warm, thick, and loaded with spores and volatile compounds. A simple filter mask is advisable for the first weeks until your system adjusts. Some travelers report a gradual dampening of lung tissue if they stay too long; others develop symbiotic films in their sinuses that seem to help with the humidity. Do not attempt to suppress these unless they impair breathing—many long-term visitors reach a workable equilibrium with the local microflora.

**Psychology and scale.**  
The greatest hazard is temporal disorientation. You will watch nothing happen for months, then suddenly notice an entire terrace has shifted orientation. Keep a strict log with external time references if you have them. The silence between water sounds can become psychologically loud. Many travelers begin to move and breathe more slowly without realizing it; this is not necessarily dangerous, but it can make return to faster worlds jarring.

**Exit strategy.**  
The living mass does not like rapid departures. Any tunnel or shaft you use to leave may begin sealing behind you. If you intend to return to a quicker timeline, depart from a high plateau where the tissue thins, rather than from deep interior galleries. Allow extra time for the final ascent; the air cools and thins there, and the transition from perpetual interior to open sky can feel like surfacing from deep water.

Essentially, treat this place as a single enormous organism whose immune system is measured in centuries. Move slowly, leave minimal trace, and accept that any schedule you bring with you is already obsolete.
