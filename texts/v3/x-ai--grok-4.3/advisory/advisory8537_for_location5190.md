**Preparation for the Basalt Hollow**

This place is a pressure cooker of rock, iron, and idling machinery. An ordinary traveler needs to treat it like entering a half-woken industrial forge buried in cooling lava. The environment is not actively hostile, but it is relentlessly compressive, hot, wet, and disorienting.

**Physical protection and clothing**
- Wear lightweight, breathable layers that can handle both high heat and sudden moisture: moisture-wicking base layer, a loose long-sleeve shirt that dries fast, and durable work trousers. Bring a thin, heat-reflective vest or jacket you can put on when standing near radiating iron struts.
- Footwear must be waterproof, non-slip, and heat-resistant (rubber or composite soles). The grated floors and mineral-scale basins will be slick; condensation turns every surface treacherous.
- Gloves are essential—thin leather or heat-resistant fabric for gripping warm pipes and avoiding burns from the blackened iron.
- A hard hat or bump cap with a built-in headlamp is mandatory. Ceilings are low in places, catwalks sit at awkward angles, and the light is too poor to judge clearances reliably.
- Optional but wise: a small personal fan or evaporative cooling towel. The air is already heavy; any extra airflow helps.

**Respiratory and climate concerns**
The heat is steady and oppressive rather than extreme, but combined with periodic steam releases it creates a sauna-like effect. Carry electrolyte packets or a hydration bladder with a drinking tube so you can sip continuously without removing gear. A simple dust mask or lightweight respirator is worth keeping on hand; while the air is not toxic, mineral dust still settles and the engine exhaust is routed through the rock.

**Navigation and orientation**
There is no visual center and no natural sight-lines. Before entering, mark your entry point with a small, bright tag or reflective strip—something you can see from multiple angles in the gloom. A compact headlamp with a red filter preserves what night vision you have. Consider a length of paracord or a roll of survey tape if you plan to move deeper into the lattice; the walkways twist and the vertical stacking makes it easy to lose track of your route.

**Tools and contingencies**
- Bring a multi-tool, a small pry bar, and zip ties. The structure is still settling; a loose grating or shifted strut is always possible.
- Pack a basic first-aid kit emphasizing burn cream and rehydration salts.
- Carry your own sealed food and water. The space supports only sparse salt-tolerant lichen; nothing edible grows here and nothing moves on its own.
- A compact thermometer/hygrometer helps track conditions so you know when to retreat if the heat spikes.

**Psychological and timing considerations**
The acoustic profile—occasional mechanical clicks and distant pumps followed by long silences—can feel like the room itself is breathing. Claustrophobia is the real hazard; the crowding of metal against stone creates constant low-level visual noise. Limit your first visit to 60–90 minutes and have a firm exit plan. If the periodic valve releases of steaming water begin to feel rhythmic rather than random, leave; the place has its own slow mechanical pulse and you do not want to be inside when it decides to “inhale” more actively.

In short: dress for a hot, wet, poorly lit machine shop that is still growing into its rock shell. Move deliberately, mark your path, stay hydrated, and treat every surface as either hot, slick, or both.
