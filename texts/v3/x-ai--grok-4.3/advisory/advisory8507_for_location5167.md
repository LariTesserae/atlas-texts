To prepare for the highland basin, treat it as an extreme high-altitude desert of wet stone rather than a conventional landscape. The primary threats are not wildlife or storms but thin air, constant dampness, disorientation, and the sheer difficulty of moving through a vertical labyrinth that has no surface landmarks.

**Physical conditioning and breathing**
Spend time at altitude beforehand if possible; otherwise bring lightweight supplemental oxygen (small tanks or a concentrator) and plan for slower movement. The air is thin enough that exertion quickly produces visible breath and fatigue. Carry electrolyte supplements, as the combination of cool air and constant low-level exertion leads to unnoticed dehydration. Your core temperature will drop faster than expected because of the persistent moisture on every surface.

**Clothing and footgear**
Use a layering system built around waterproof breathable shells and quick-dry base layers. The stone sweats continuously, so you will be in contact with water at ankle and hand level almost constantly. Choose boots with aggressive, stiff soles and good drainage; smooth or flexible footwear becomes treacherous on the glistening limestone and basalt. Add gaiters to keep grit and water out of your boots. Fingerless gloves are useful for the frequent moments when you must brace against wet rock while turning sideways between slabs.

**Navigation and orientation**
The basin has no horizon and no sky reference once you descend into the lanes, so conventional maps and compasses are nearly useless. Carry a durable, brightly colored cord or biodegradable trail tape to mark your path; the stone corridors swallow sound and line-of-sight quickly. A simple altimeter or barometer can help track relative depth in the maze, since some passages rise and fall. Consider a small personal locator beacon or at least a loud whistle—though the quiet is profound, echoes travel poorly through the broken vaults.

**Movement and safety tools**
Bring a short trekking pole or staff; it helps test footing and provides a third point of contact when you must edge through narrow gaps. A headlamp with a strong red filter is valuable for the deeper shaded lanes where daylight shafts are infrequent. A compact tarp or bivy can create a dry micro-shelter if you must stop, because the stone itself offers no flat, dry surfaces.

**Supplies and sustainment**
Food must be carried in; there is almost nothing edible. Water is abundant but mineral-heavy—bring a filter or purification tablets if you prefer not to drink directly from the weeping stone. Because nights (if the term applies) are only slightly cooler than days, caloric needs are driven more by the damp chill than by temperature swings.

**Psychological considerations**
The environment is relentlessly enclosed yet open to the sky in narrow slices, which can produce a mild claustrophobia-agoraphobia tension after several hours. Expect the quiet to feel heavy rather than peaceful. Plan shorter daily distances than intuition suggests; route-finding and careful footing consume more time and energy than distance alone would indicate.

In short, the traveler who succeeds here moves slowly, stays dry where possible, marks every turn, and carries the means to survive a forced overnight without resupply. The basin rewards patience and punishes haste.
