**Preparation for the Plateau of Equilibrium**

Pack for perpetual cool dampness and unbroken silence. The air never stirs, the temperature hovers just above the point where breath shows, and condensation forms on every upward surface each night. Bring layered garments of wool or tightly woven natural fiber—nothing synthetic that will trap moisture against the skin. A lightweight oiled canvas cloak or waxed linen poncho is essential; it will bead the nightly dew and the infrequent vertical rains without adding weight. Footwear should be soft-soled leather or rubber with pronounced tread; the hand-split slate terraces and stair-streets become slick under the permanent film of moisture. Carry two pairs of socks and a small absorbent cloth for wiping soles before entering any interior space.

Because nothing changes, small disturbances linger. Choose clothing and gear in muted earth and slate tones so you do not stand out against the fern-shadowed stone. Avoid bright colors, reflective surfaces, or anything that clinks or creaks. The city’s equilibrium is not a rule enforced by law but a physical habit the place has settled into; loud footfalls, sudden movements, or metallic sounds will feel like cracks in glass.

Water is abundant yet strangely inert. Every morning you can lick dew from clean slate or collect it in the shallow stone basins, but the taste is flat. Bring a small copper or ceramic cup and a few packets of salt or dried herbs if you want flavor; the local water will not spoil, so a single filled skin lasts indefinitely. Food is a different matter. The plateau produces no fruit or grain, only the spaced ferns and mosses whose leaves are thick and waxy. Unless you intend to hunt the slow, silent lizards (an act that feels violently loud here), carry compact, non-aromatic rations—hard cheese, dense bread, dried meat, nuts—enough for your planned stay plus a margin. Waste must be carried out; nothing decays at a normal rate, so refuse left behind remains exactly where it falls.

The silence is not restful; it has mass. After a day or two most visitors notice their own heartbeat and the soft slide of fabric become obtrusive. Practice moving with deliberate slowness. Learn to set objects down rather than drop them. If you carry a journal, write with a graphite pencil; ink dries slowly and can smear across days.

Socially the inhabitants are courteous but distant. They speak rarely and in low voices, and they will watch you for longer than feels comfortable before answering questions. Do not offer tools, news, or stories of change; such gifts are received as disruptions. If you must trade, offer objects already worn or used—stones already shaped by other hands, cloth already softened by wear. Fresh metal or bright new things are politely declined.

Psychologically, the plateau exerts a slow pressure toward stasis. Visitors sometimes find themselves reluctant to leave, or conversely become frantic to introduce any movement at all—scratching names into stone, humming tunes, rearranging a fern. Decide before arrival how long you intend to stay and mark the days by some external token (a knot in cord, a notch in wood). The light never shifts its quality, so ordinary circadian cues are absent.

Finally, expect the ridges themselves to feel like unfinished sentences. Their knife edges will make you want to trace them with a fingertip; resist. The stone is still sharp enough to cut, and the plateau does not heal wounds it did not make. Move through the city as a courteous guest who has already agreed to leave everything exactly as it was found.
