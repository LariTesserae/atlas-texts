**Preparation for the High Plateau**

Pack as if entering a single, continuous abrasive medium rather than a landscape. The plateau’s surface and the phytarchs are chemically and mechanically the same substance: a cool, wind-polished mineral lattice whose dust is carried in every movement of air. An ordinary human cannot survive contact with that air for more than a few minutes without filtration rated for sub-micron silicates and metal aerosols. The minimum rig is a sealed positive-pressure suit with rebreather loops that scrub both particulates and metal vapors; bring at least three times the expected mission duration in scrubber media, because replacement cartridges will themselves become contaminated. Helmet seals must tolerate continuous low-frequency vibration; otherwise the constant chord produced by wind through phytarch pores will loosen fittings within hours.

Water is irrelevant on arrival and must be carried in fully closed systems. The aerosols will strip moisture from any exposed liquid surface within seconds. Hydration bladders should be internal to the suit, with bite valves and no external ports. Cooling is likewise internal: the ambient temperature is a dry steady chill, but the suit’s heat exchanger must also manage the additional load generated by the continuous micro-impacts of wind-driven grains. Thermal underwear rated for dry cold is sufficient beneath the suit; sweat will not evaporate normally and will instead pool against seals.

Mobility on the plateau itself requires low-friction foot surfaces. Magnetic or adhesive soles are unnecessary; the surface is smooth enough that ordinary walking cleats will skate. Use wide, slightly cupped soles that distribute weight across the microbial film without tearing it. Every few kilometers the film gives way to the bases of phytarchs; these can be climbed if you treat the hexagonal pores as hand- and footholds, but any pressure that cracks the living lattice will trigger defensive closure of nearby pores and the release of additional slurry aerosols.

Fauna avoidance is straightforward but absolute. The gliding, burrowing mineral creatures respond to vibration and to the presence of moving internal fluids. Keep suit pumps as quiet as possible and avoid entering any phytarch whose internal bioluminescent pulses are rapid; those towers are metabolically active and attract swarms. If a swarm lands on the suit, remain motionless. The creatures will eventually interpret the rigid exoskeleton as non-living and depart. Attempting to brush them off produces exactly the scratching motions that stimulate further burrowing.

Sound is both navigation aid and hazard. The layered chords that rise from the towers can be used to maintain orientation across the featureless plateau: lower drones indicate proximity to major trunks, high glassy whistles indicate the branch canopy above. Yet the same frequencies will transmit through any rigid material and can induce low-frequency fatigue in bone and inner ear after a full day. Ear protection should be frequency-selective rather than total; you will need to hear the subsonic pulses that warn of distant aerosol thickening.

Light is perpetual dusk. Bring headlamps whose spectrum peaks in the red-orange range; the phytarch lattices will transmit and slightly amplify these wavelengths, turning the interior of a tower into a dim, stained-glass chamber where your own light becomes part of the local bioluminescent data stream. White or blue light is scattered and produces no useful contrast.

Finally, accept the temporal scale. Nothing here moves on human schedules. A phytarch branch that appears to be growing toward you will advance at most a few millimeters in a week. Plan for stillness and repetition. The plateau will continue its resonant equilibrium whether you stand for an hour or a month; your task is only to remain an intact, non-abrasive guest within that equilibrium.
