This place will demand constant, low-level vigilance more than dramatic heroics. The ground is still deciding what shape it wants to be, so treat every step as provisional.

**Footwear and movement**  
Wear boots with stiff soles and ankle support rated for sharp, loose scree—something you can tighten quickly when a slab shifts underfoot. The rock edges are fresh and can slice leather or fabric on contact. Carry a pair of lightweight trekking poles; they will save your knees and give you a split-second warning when the surface is about to settle or tilt. Move deliberately and test each new section of slope with a pole before committing weight. Larger animals here keep wide spacing for the same reason: one wrong footfall on settling rock can carry farther than expected.

**Clothing and moisture management**  
Assume you will be damp at all times. Layer a waterproof/breathable shell over insulating mid-layers that still function when wet. The mist isn’t rain, but it condenses on every surface and finds its way inside cuffs, collars, and packs. Bring spare socks sealed in dry bags and change them at regular stops; cold feet here degrade judgment faster than hunger. Gloves should be thin enough to manipulate small fasteners yet thick enough to protect against rock edges and the sudden hiss of steam from a vent you didn’t see until you were beside it.

**Navigation and sensory allowances**  
Forget relying on distant landmarks. The mist erases distance and horizon, so use a compass or GPS with downloaded topographic maps rather than visual references. Mark your return path physically—bright survey tape or small cairns—because sound does not carry well enough for voice or whistle to serve as reliable recall. Keep one hand or pole occasionally touching rock faces as you walk; the low-frequency vibrations of settling slabs travel through stone better than through air.

**Tools and contingencies**  
- Headlamp with a strong red-light mode for close work at dusk or in thicker mist.  
- Small roll of duct tape and a few feet of paracord for quick repairs to boots or clothing after encounters with sharp stone.  
- Basic first-aid supplies focused on lacerations and puncture wounds rather than fractures.  
- A compact multi-gas detector if you plan to linger near active vents; even brief exposure to concentrated mineral steam can irritate lungs and eyes.  
- Water purification tablets or a filter. The visible trickles may carry fine mineral particles that settle slowly; the safest water is still moving but visibly clear.

**Pacing and expectations**  
Plan shorter travel days than the terrain distances suggest. The combination of poor traction, limited visibility, and the need to stop and listen for the next muffled rock-settlement sound makes progress slow by ordinary hiking standards. Accept that you will often be alone with only the sound of dripping water for company; the emptiness is not hostile but it is indifferent. If you feel a low, broad vibration rising through your boots, pause and wait for any accompanying slab movement to finish before continuing.

The place rewards patience and punishes hurry. Move as if the ground is still negotiating its final shape, because it is.
