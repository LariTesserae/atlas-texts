**Preparation for the Mineral Basin**

This is a place of absolute equilibrium. Nothing moves, nothing grows, and nothing decays at a perceptible rate. Your preparations should focus on preserving that stillness rather than overcoming it.

**Physical protection**  
The crust is a single, thin mineral skin over what may be soft or hollow ground in places. Wear footwear with completely smooth soles—no lugs or aggressive tread—so you do not scar the polygons or dislodge grains. Light-colored, loose clothing that covers the skin entirely is necessary; the air carries a fine, dry salt aerosol that will eventually irritate any exposed moisture (eyes, lips, nasal passages). Bring sealed goggles and a soft cloth mask you can dampen sparingly with your own water. The temperature is constant and warm, so heavy insulation is unnecessary, but the lack of air movement means sweat will not evaporate efficiently. Light, breathable layers that wick without releasing moisture into the air are preferable.

**Water and sustenance**  
There is no accessible moisture except the microscopic nightly condensation that sustains the tufts. Carry all water you will need plus a 50 % surplus. Any spilled liquid will immediately begin to dissolve the crust beneath it, creating visible dark patches that disrupt the basin’s uniformity. Food should be dry and crumb-free; even small organic particles will stand out against the pale surface for centuries.

**Light and navigation**  
Permanent twilight means you will not have a useful day-night cycle. Bring a reliable, low-lumen light source with a red filter if you must see details at close range; white light will cast harsh, unnatural shadows and destroy the basin’s visual character. The encircling rises look identical from inside. Mark your entry point with something that cannot be mistaken for one of the woody tufts—perhaps a small cairn of stones carried in from outside the basin. Do not rely on compasses; the mineral crust may contain enough magnetic material to cause mild deviation.

**Sensory and psychological considerations**  
The silence is not metaphorical. The only audible event is the occasional shift of a single salt grain. After roughly two hours most visitors begin to hear their own blood and breath with uncomfortable clarity. Some experience a growing conviction that time has stopped. Bring a small, mechanical timer or watch with a soft tick if you need an external reference for duration. Avoid staying longer than you can comfortably endure total sensory reduction; the basin does not become more interesting with prolonged exposure—it simply remains exactly as it is.

**Interaction rules**  
- Do not touch the tufts. Their leaves are pressed flat to harvest condensation; disturbing them leaves visible impressions that will not recover.  
- Do not dig, scrape, or test the crust. The polygons are thin; a single puncture may release a brief, localized exhalation of trapped air that smells sharply of minerals and will take years to reseal.  
- Speak softly or not at all. Sound travels with unnatural clarity across the flat surface and lingers against the surrounding rises.

**Exit discipline**  
When you leave, walk back over your own incoming footprints to minimize new traces. Once you crest the low rises, the basin will vanish from view within minutes and the ordinary world will resume. Most travelers feel a brief, sharp sense of loss upon departure, as though they have stepped out of a photograph that will remain unchanged long after they are gone.
