The plain will try to treat you as a temporary misalignment in its existing geometry. Prepare accordingly.

Clothing and physical protection must prioritize flexibility over armor. Wear a single seamless layer of thin, high-density woven ceramic fabric (think competition-level fencing whites but chemically treated for abrasion resistance) that covers every square centimeter of skin. The shard-beetles will attempt to score any exposed surface the moment you stop moving; their legs are literally evolved to cut new channels into wet mineral. The fabric should be slick enough that condensate beads off immediately—otherwise the constant weeping water will turn every seam into a potential conductor for the droplet-slugs’ current arcs. Boots should be thin-soled with hard ceramic nubs rather than traditional tread; the plates and rotors are already perfectly smooth, and deep tread will only trap the metallic slurry that the plain keeps extruding.

Hearing protection is non-negotiable and must be active rather than passive. The overlapping echoes and arrhythmic crystal claps will rapidly exceed safe decibel levels; ordinary earplugs will not suffice because the sound is partly conducted through the substrate itself. Use electronic muffs that can gate sudden spikes (the shearing claps) while still allowing you to detect the low grinding that signals an approaching rotor reversal. Without this, the auditory density will begin to feel tactile within minutes—literally pressing on your skin.

Movement protocol should mimic the plain’s own logic: never expand into unoccupied space, only slide between existing intervals. Walk in short, deliberate bursts that end before any structure has time to register your presence as a new friction source. If a rotor-field begins to stutter, freeze immediately; the reversal shear travels faster than a human reflex. When the beetles migrate as a single sheet, treat the movement as a wave you must ride rather than an obstacle you must cross—step onto the mat only when it is already in motion and step off the instant it begins to settle.

Carry no more than two liters of your own water. The condensate on the plain tastes sharply of dissolved metal and will produce rapid electrolyte imbalance; it is not safe to drink even after filtration. Food is irrelevant. The local life metabolizes mechanical work, not chemistry. Any organic residue you leave will be slowly abraded into slurry within hours.

Psychological preparation is the hardest part. The lagging shadows and temporal echoes create a persistent low-grade disassociation. After roughly forty minutes most visitors begin to feel their own limbs arriving slightly late, as if the plain is processing them on a slight delay. This is not hallucination; it is the same acoustic and optical lag that detaches shadows from rotors. The effect is harmless in itself but becomes dangerous once you start second-guessing your own proprioception near moving crystal faces. A simple physical tether (a short length of cord clipped to your belt and allowed to drag) provides a constant low-friction reference that remains anchored to your actual body rather than its echo.

Finally, accept that departure may require waiting for the plain to rearrange itself around you rather than actively extracting yourself. The mechanism has no memory of its edges. If you become incorporated into a rotor’s turning arc, the safest course is to remain passive until the next stutter cycle releases you. Attempts to force motion outward are what produce the shearing events. The plain already contains everything it needs; your task is only to occupy an interval it has not yet filled.
