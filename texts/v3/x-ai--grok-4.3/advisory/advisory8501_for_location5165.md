This fissure is not a cave in any conventional sense but an active seam in a forming mountain, where rock is still being manufactured under pressure and heat. An ordinary traveler should treat it as an industrial accident site rather than a destination. Survival requires accepting extreme restrictions on movement, air, and time.

Begin with body selection and conditioning. The entry throat is narrower than most adult shoulders for any length of time, so only slim, flexible builds have any chance of progressing without becoming wedged. Prior training should emphasize prolonged crawling, chimneying, and breath-held stillness rather than strength. Hip and shoulder dislocations must be manageable if needed; joint hypermobility helps. No bulky clothing or gear can be worn through the initial descent—everything must compress or be dragged behind on a thin line.

Heat management is the primary constraint. The chamber walls are still shedding stored thermal energy, and fresh extrusions radiate continuously. A traveler needs a full-body thermal barrier suit rated for sustained contact with surfaces above 200 °C, yet the suit must remain thin enough to pass the constriction. Reflective outer layers and internal phase-change cooling packs are mandatory; even then, dwell time is limited to minutes before core temperature rises dangerously. Gloves and boots must be cut-resistant because the rock edges are razor-sharp from recent fracturing. Any moisture (sweat, breath condensation) will flash to vapor on contact, so the suit must vent internally without introducing water into the environment.

Atmosphere is the next hard barrier. The air is chemically reactive and stripped of normal humidity. An ordinary human cannot breathe it for long; the metallic dryness will damage lung tissue within minutes while the low partial pressure of oxygen (if oxygen is even present) induces rapid hypoxia. A closed-circuit rebreather with independent oxygen supply is essential, with enough capacity for the round trip plus margin. The suit must also seal completely at neck and wrists; any leak turns the wearer into a dehydration event. Because the creatures produce no CO₂ that registers to human senses, the rebreather’s scrubbers will not be triggered by biological exhaust, so monitoring must be manual.

Sensory and psychological preparation is equally vital. The ember-light is insufficient for color vision and creates constant motion illusions along the walls. Ear protection or sealed audio dampening is advisable against the grinding and popping, which will transmit through bone as much as air. Claustrophobia training is non-negotiable: once inside the chamber there is no room to turn around, and retreat requires backing out blindly through the same throat. The creatures themselves are not overtly aggressive, but their activity continuously narrows gaps and shifts loose mineral; a traveler must be prepared to remain motionless for long periods while they pass or to gently relocate without crushing them, as sudden movements could trigger defensive rasping or localized pressure releases.

All equipment must be carried in a single slim drag-bag or left at the surface. No radio, computer, or light source that emits significant heat or electromagnetic noise should be taken inside; any introduced energy will be absorbed by the rock or interpreted as an intrusion by the mineral-forming process. A simple mechanical timer and a short length of high-temperature line are the only reliable tools.

The visit must be measured in minutes, not hours. The goal is observation of the active extrusion and the creatures’ maintenance loops, then immediate withdrawal before cumulative heat load or a shifting pillow seals the only exit. Upon return to the surface ridge, rapid cooling and rehydration protocols are required; dehydration will already be severe despite the rebreather.

In short, the traveler needs the mindset of an industrial diver entering a live furnace that is still being built. The mountain is indifferent; preparation is therefore entirely one-sided.
