To visit this place, an ordinary human would need to treat it as a prolonged, fully submerged immersion in a closed, pressurized aquatic biome rather than a conventional structure. The entire lattice exists inside warm water held at roughly human-body temperature and elevated pressure (enough to keep the volume stable against whatever lies beyond the sealed viewports). Entry therefore requires full scuba or rebreather capability rated for at least several hours, plus redundant buoyancy control so you neither sink into the lower transepts nor drift upward against the ceiling ribs. Because the water is almost motionless, conventional finning produces unwanted turbulence that stirs spore clouds and knocks loose the slow-drifting seeds; a traveler should therefore carry small, low-thrust maneuvering units or simply accept a deliberate, hand-over-hand pace along the I-beams.

Thermal protection is minimal: a thin 3–4 mm wetsuit or skinsuit suffices for comfort and also guards against the knife-sharp factory edges still present on every rivet and flange. Gloves are essential; even light contact with an unweathered alloy lip can slice skin. Vision is already optimized by the even phosphor lighting, so no powerful lights are needed, but a low-powered headlamp helps when pushing through the densest leaf mats where the fronds themselves block the overhead strips. The vegetation is rubbery and slick; it yields but does not tear easily, so a traveler should expect to move at roughly the speed of a very slow crawl, parting layers with both hands while keeping the regulator clear of floating particulates. A spare mask and a small bottle of lens-defogging solution are wise, because the metallic-veined leaves leave an oily film on any transparent surface they brush.

Biologically, the water carries no current and almost no dissolved gases in motion, so exhaled bubbles linger as small, persistent clusters before dissolving or rising at a glacial rate. This makes communication by voice nearly useless; hand signals or a very low-power underwater comms unit become necessary. The spore clouds and glittering seeds are light enough to remain suspended; a traveler with any respiratory sensitivity should use a full-face mask with a fine particle filter on the inhalation side. No animals are present, but the vegetation’s slow exhalations and the occasional muted valve sigh can still startle; psychological preparation for total silence broken only by one’s own breathing and the rare soft mechanical flex is helpful.

Because the entire volume feels like a single, patient organism, the subjective experience of time stretches. A traveler should bring no schedule and should plan to rest periodically by simply hanging from a pipe or beam, watching a leaf unfold over the course of an hour. Exit is not obvious; the boundaries are pressure-sealed, so an exit protocol (a specific valve sequence or an external recall beacon) must be arranged in advance. Without it, one risks remaining inside the lattice until the slow processes themselves become the only clock. In short, prepare as for technical cave or wreck diving in warm, still water, but replace urgency with extreme patience and treat the integrated plant-machine matrix as the dominant terrain rather than an obstacle to be cleared.
