To enter this place at all, an ordinary traveler must first accept that conventional notions of scale and access must be surrendered. The seam is physically no wider than a hand-span, so preparation begins with compression or translation rather than force. A traveler might carry a folding charm or a small osmotic capsule that temporarily reduces their effective volume, allowing them to slip between the pressing root-masses like a thought between two pages. Once inside, the capsule can be discarded; the pocket will accommodate whatever arrives, though it will not announce the moment of full arrival.

The water itself requires an artificial gill or osmotic lung that can filter the drifting filaments without clogging. These filaments are not merely decorative; they behave like extremely fine cilia and will work their way into any unsealed joint or pore given enough time. A traveler should wear a second skin of slick, non-porous membrane rather than fabric—something that sheds water and plant matter instead of absorbing them. The membrane must also be tough enough to resist the knife edges of newly unfurled leaves, which will cut ordinary skin or wetsuit material without resistance.

Because the temperature sits just below comfortable equilibrium, passive thermal regulation is insufficient. A small, steady heat source—perhaps a locket-sized chemical warmer or a sympathetic ember from a more temperate universe—should be carried against the sternum. The cold does not merely chill; it slows metabolic processes, so the body must be given an external reminder to keep moving, however slowly.

Navigation here is the most delicate requirement. The pocket rearranges itself without malice or warning. A traveler should carry several small, inert markers (polished stones, glass beads, or anything that will not dissolve or grow) and release them at intervals. These will not prevent shifts, but they will reveal when a path has changed. A compass is useless; instead, the traveler needs an instrument that records the metallic taste that accompanies each rearrangement, something that can log these micro-events as a kind of spatial seismograph. Without such a record, it is easy to circle for subjective hours believing progress is being made.

Finally, the traveler must prepare for the psychological texture of the place. Time here is not hostile, but it is adhesive. The mind will attempt to accelerate events that refuse to accelerate. Bring a small, repetitive task—counting the ribs on a single leaf, tracing one filament to its origin—that can occupy attention without demanding resolution. The pocket will not finish with you. The traveler must be willing to leave before any sense of completion arrives, understanding that completion is not among the experiences this place offers.
