To visit this plateau, an ordinary traveler should treat it less like a destination and more like an environment that slowly erodes anything brought into it. The place does not permit haste or isolation; every preparation must account for perpetual moisture, crushing acoustic presence, vertical compression, and the total absence of organic sustenance.

Begin with clothing. Standard rain gear will fail within hours because the moisture arrives from every direction at once—condensation forming on skin-temperature surfaces, mist rising from below, rain falling from above. The traveler needs a layered system that can be vented yet remains sealed at cuffs and collar: a lightweight waterproof shell over a moisture-wicking base layer, with a second, loosely woven insulating layer that can be removed when steam drifts upward from the lower tiers. Footwear must have aggressive tread that can shed water; the fused silica and iron surfaces develop a permanent glassy film that turns ledges into skating rinks after even a light shower. Gloves should be thin enough to allow grip on riveted handrails yet thick enough to prevent fingertips from pruning after a full day of contact with wet metal.

Hearing protection is not optional. The combined sound of fliers’ silica wings, crawler drumming, and distant machinery forms a single broadband roar that never drops below conversation level and often rises to the point of physical discomfort. Unprotected exposure for more than a few hours produces fatigue and disorientation; simple earplugs or low-profile muffs become essential, though they must still allow the traveler to hear the change in pitch that warns of a sudden release of steam from a vent.

Breathing requires modest filtration. The metallic exhalations rising from the lower galleries are not toxic in any acute sense, yet they carry fine particulates of scale and oxidized iron that accumulate in the lungs after repeated exposure. A simple cloth or activated-carbon mask, changed daily, is sufficient. The air is always near saturation, so any traveler prone to respiratory irritation should bring a mild bronchodilator and plan shorter daily exposure times than they would in drier climates.

Because no plants or animals here can be eaten, all nutrition and water must be carried. The condensate films leave behind mineral residues that are mildly abrasive and, over days, can cause gastrointestinal distress if ingested. Pack calorie-dense, non-hygroscopic food—vacuum-sealed bars, nuts, or dried meat—rather than anything that softens when continually damp. Water can be collected from clean rivulets using a fine cloth filter, but it should be treated or boiled; the same residues that coat the stone also coat every pipe and ledge.

Vertical movement demands simple climbing tools. The walkways and rail spurs stack so closely that a body must sometimes turn sideways to pass between them. A short length of lightweight rope, a few carabiners, and a collapsible walking staff or ice axe (for probing rounded edges and checking stability) allow the traveler to descend or ascend tiers without relying on the original iron ladders, many of which have rusted through. A headlamp with a red filter preserves night vision when entering the deeper galleries where the bead-sized gatherers move.

The psychological load is constant. The plateau’s scale makes every horizon identical; there are no landmarks that change quickly enough to provide a sense of progress. The traveler should expect daily travel distances to feel shorter than the map suggests because the uniform grey palette and ceaseless sound blunt spatial awareness. Carrying a small, bright object—colored cord, reflective tape—helps mark a personal trail when the ironwork geometry begins to repeat.

Finally, adopt the plateau’s own pace. Presses and forges operate on cycles measured in hours; showers cross the table slowly enough to be outwalked. Any attempt to hurry increases the chance of slipping on a filmed ledge or missing the narrow gap between two stacked walkways. The successful traveler moves as the crawlers move: steadily, without urgency, accepting that the city will still be there after the next condensation cycle, and the one after that.
