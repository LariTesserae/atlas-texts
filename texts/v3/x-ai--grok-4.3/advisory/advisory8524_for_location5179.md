**Preparation for the Plateau**

This is not a place for casual exploration. The environment is one of total, ancient stability punctuated by imperceptible change, extreme thinness of air, and sensory deprivation. An ordinary traveler should treat it like an extreme high-altitude desert combined with a slow-motion seismic and gravitational anomaly zone.

### Core Survival Requirements
- **Respiration and pressure**: The air is lethally thin. You will need a sealed positive-pressure suit or at minimum a high-flow oxygen system with CO₂ scrubber, rated for sustained use at pressures equivalent to 8,000–10,000 m terrestrial altitude or worse. Even brief exposure without it will cause rapid hypoxia. Carry 72–96 hours of backup gas minimum; there is no local water or breathable atmosphere to fall back on.
- **Thermal protection**: Temperatures are consistently cold. Insulated, multi-layer suit with active heating elements. Gloves and boots must remain flexible at low temperatures.
- **Dust management**: The suspended mineral veils are finer than smoke and never settle. Your suit seals must be rated against sub-micron particulates. Bring spare filters and a method to clean visor seals. Prolonged exposure without filtration risks lung irritation even inside a suit if seals are imperfect.

### Mobility and Terrain
- Footwear should have high-friction soles for glass-smooth rock and micro-spikes or crampon points for the rare steeper faces. Carry a lightweight harness and 100–200 m of high-tensile line; the kilometer-scale drops at ridge edges are invisible until you are at the lip.
- Expect sudden low-frequency tremors. These arrive as pressure waves through the crust. A simple seismometer or even a contact microphone on your pack can give seconds of warning. When one passes, new stone filaments may extrude nearby; do not stand directly over fissures.
- Navigation is deceptive. The horizon never arrives and visual references are almost nonexistent. Use inertial navigation, a high-precision compass, and physical waymarkers (reflective stakes or beacons). GPS-style systems may be disrupted by the gravitic fields of the buried relics.

### Equipment and Detection
- Gravitic anomaly detector or simple magnetometer. The half-absorbed lattices still produce localized fields that can tug dust into helices or briefly warp visual horizons. These are not instantly lethal but can disorient or pull unsecured objects.
- Redundant comms. Sound barely propagates; use radio or line-of-sight laser links. The silence is so complete that even suit fans and your own breathing become the dominant noises after a few hours.
- Sample tools and containment: If you intend to examine the lithophyte mats or the obsidian slabs, use remote manipulators or sealed collection devices. The stone has sealed its own volatiles for eons; disturbing inclusions may release trace gases or cause micro-fracturing.

### Psychological and Logistical Preparation
The emptiness is the real hazard. Visual monotony, near-total silence, and the knowledge that the surface is still rearranging itself on geological timescales create a powerful sense of isolation and scale. Plan for shortened daily travel distances and mandatory rest periods with external visual stimuli (photos, music, or recorded voices). Carry extra water and calories; the body works harder in thin air and low temperature even at rest.

Travel in pairs or with a remote monitor if possible. Mark your entry point clearly; the uniformity makes return bearings easy to lose. Do not touch the mirror-bright filaments or attempt to interact with any lattice that still shows shifting fields.

In short: treat the journey as a high-altitude, low-sound, low-light, slow-seismic expedition across an active but ancient planetary crust. Everything you need must be carried; nothing here will help you, and very little will even notice you are there.
