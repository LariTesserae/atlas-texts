**Preparation for the Mineral Lattice City**

An ordinary human is not built for this environment. The city is not hostile in intent, but it is indifferent at a geological scale. Every system exists either to move mass or to keep mass moving; the traveler is simply another quantity of mass until proven otherwise.

**Protective layering**  
You need two skins. The first is sealed, seam-taped, and slightly over-pressurized (a light positive-pressure suit or an expeditionary drysuit rated for continuous humidity). Condensation forms on every surface; bare skin will quickly macerate, and any open cut becomes a site for opportunistic crystalline tubules to test whether you are a repair substrate. The outer layer must be low-friction and mineral-hard—aramid or ceramic-fiber composite—so that the reef-like fauna sliding past do not abrade you into the same condensate film they feed on.  
The second skin is acoustic. Industrial-grade active-noise-cancelling earmuffs rated for at least 110 dB continuous, plus foam earplugs beneath them. The roar is not loud in the theatrical sense; it is a constant 40–60 Hz pressure that will vibrate teeth loose and induce nausea after six hours. Without damping, most humans develop vertigo within a day and permanent low-frequency hearing damage within three.

**Locomotion and anchoring**  
Blocks shift. A hand-span of rise or tilt per day sounds trivial until you are standing on the seam when it decides to reseal itself. Carry a set of expandable pitons or magnetic grapnels rated for silicate and fused quartz; they must be placed and removed every few hours. Never trust a floor to remain level while you sleep. Sleep, when it occurs, is done wedged inside a portable rigid shelter (essentially a very small shipping container with its own micro-actuators) that can ride a block movement without crushing the occupant.

**Hydration and chemistry**  
The air is saturated but not potable. The water that condenses is doped with whatever ionic and micro-current residues the lattice networks are currently exchanging. Carry a closed-loop recycler that can both distill and remineralize; the city’s moisture will dissolve most common plastics and will slowly etch untreated metals. Food must be sealed and calorie-dense; nothing grows, and the native fauna will not register you as edible until they have already begun to incorporate your suit into their carapace.

**Sensory and orientation kit**  
A wrist-mounted inertial mapper is mandatory. Visual landmarks rearrange hourly. The constant half-light that filters through the upper planes eliminates shadows and depth cues; add a headlamp with a strong red filter so you can still read contours without triggering whatever photoreactive tubules are embedded in the walls.  
Carry chemical-light sticks in multiple colors. They are the only reliable way to mark a route that the city’s own repair swarms will not immediately interpret as damage needing sealing.

**Fauna protocols**  
The segmented stone-colored forms treat vertical and horizontal surfaces identically. They will flow over you without slowing if you remain still; they will shear you if you obstruct a channel they have already committed to. Give way early and completely. The larger reef-like entities drift on ionized cushions; their passage creates brief, intense electrostatic fields that will arc across any ungrounded metal you are carrying. Ground everything or leave it behind.

**Mental framing**  
The city has no center and no edge that a human timescale can reach. Navigation is therefore not toward a destination but toward maintaining coherence—keeping one’s own body and equipment from being treated as repair material. Most travelers who remain longer than a few local cycles begin to experience a dissolving sense of boundary: the constant pressure, the uniform mineral texture, the total sonic field, and the absence of any living thing that breathes all combine to erode ordinary human self-modeling. Many cut their visit short once they notice they have stopped checking their own pulse.

In short: treat the city as an active, self-repairing geological machine rather than a place. Bring the equipment that lets you remain a discrete object inside it, and plan to leave before the distinction between visitor and substrate begins to feel negotiable.
