**Preparation for the Vault**

This is a closed, living greenhouse pressed beneath kilometers of stone. The dominant conditions are persistent heat, near-100 % humidity, total darkness relieved only by faint organism light, and a three-dimensional thicket of wet vegetation and fungi that fills every level from floor to ceiling. An ordinary traveler will be moving through a slow convection oven that is also a biological contact zone.

**Clothing and skin protection**  
Wear the lightest possible long-sleeved shirt and trousers made of quick-drying synthetic (or treated silk). The fabric must be loose enough to allow air movement yet tight at cuffs and collar to keep fronds and small crawlers off skin. A wide-brim soft hat or hood keeps condensation from running directly into eyes and collar. Gloves should be thin, grippy, and quick-drying; bare hands will be constantly wet and will pick up irritant residues from many of the fungi. Footwear needs aggressive tread and drainage holes—standard hiking boots fill and stay sodden within minutes. Bring at least two spare pairs of liner socks; trench foot is a real risk after a few hours.

**Thermal and hydration management**  
Core temperature will rise quickly. Pack small, frequent water rations rather than large bottles; you will sweat continuously yet feel little evaporative cooling. Electrolyte salts or tablets are useful. A damp cloth around the neck or under the hat can be re-wet from the dripping condensation if you need rapid cooling. Avoid heavy exertion; the space punishes speed.

**Movement and tools**  
The plant mass is dense enough that you will have to push, climb, or crawl in places. A short, blunt staff (walking stick length) lets you part foliage without slashing it. A folding trowel or entrenching tool can help redistribute loose root mats when you need footing. Everything metal should be coated or wrapped; the air is mineral-rich and will encourage corrosion. Knee and elbow pads reduce bruising against rounded but unyielding stone.

**Light discipline**  
The chamber’s own glow is the only light. Bring a single, very low-output red or amber source (headlamp on its dimmest setting, filtered) for emergency orientation only. White light will wash out the bioluminescence for several minutes and may trigger defensive dimming in the surrounding organisms. Carry a backup chemical light stick as last resort; it is warmer and less disruptive than LEDs.

**Respiratory and sensory considerations**  
The air is thick, metallic, and rich in volatile organic compounds. A lightweight cloth or bandana soaked in a little clean water can be held over mouth and nose during the first ten to fifteen minutes while acclimating. If you have any history of mold or fungal sensitivity, a simple N95 or equivalent filter is prudent. Expect your sense of smell and taste to become saturated quickly; after roughly half an hour most visitors report that the metallic-green odor simply becomes “the air.”

**Biological contact risks**  
Assume every surface can cause dermatitis or minor infection. Carry a small tube of broad-spectrum antifungal cream and basic antiseptic wipes. Do not touch the shallow steaming basins with bare skin; the temperature differential plus possible micro-algae can produce chemical burns or rapid colonization. The translucent amphibians and the larger segmented forms deeper in the foliage are best left unmolested—both appear adapted to low-energy movement and may react defensively to sudden disturbance.

**Mental and temporal pacing**  
Sound carries poorly and the space punishes abruptness. Move at the speed of the drips. Sit still for several minutes after entering; the local organisms will register your body heat and vibration, then resume normal activity. The low ceiling and constant tactile contact with living matter can induce claustrophobia even in people who do not normally suffer from it. Schedule the visit in short segments with planned exits rather than marathon exploration.

**Exit protocol**  
Before leaving, brush off major root fragments and fungal spores outdoors if possible. The humidity will have soaked every layer of clothing; have a dry change and a way to contain the wet gear. Expect several hours of elevated body temperature and heavy fatigue afterward—the same slow heat exchange continues in your tissues for a while.

In short, treat the chamber as an active geothermal greenhouse that happens to contain architecture. The goal is to pass through without raising its temperature, breaking its light cycle, or becoming part of its detritus.
