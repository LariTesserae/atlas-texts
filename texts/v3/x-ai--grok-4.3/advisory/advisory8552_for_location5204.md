**Preparation for the Plateau**

You will need full aquatic capability for an extended period in warm, still water. A lightweight full-face mask or rebreather with a low-bubble exhaust is preferable to standard open-circuit scuba; the silence here is nearly absolute and every hiss or exhaust pop registers. Because the water column overhead is finite, a modest cylinder or a small surface-supplied line (if the plateau’s “roof” is close enough) will suffice, but carry at least four hours of gas. The temperature is skin-warm, so a 1–2 mm rash-guard or thin neoprene is enough for thermal protection, yet the same garment should be abrasion-resistant; the young basalt and sharp mineral veins will slice bare skin on contact.

Movement must be slow and deliberate. Conventional fins create too much disturbance; soft hand fins or a gentle undulating kick are better. Because the vegetation reforms behind any passage, you will not leave a trail, but you can still become disoriented inside the layered mats. Carry a wrist-mounted compass and a low-power reel of fine line for return routes. A single small dive knife or shears is useful only as a last resort; the fronds part under steady pressure and attempting to cut them is both unnecessary and disruptive.

Pack nothing that produces light or sound except what you can fully control. A single shielded LED on its lowest setting is acceptable for peering into the pale conductive tubes, but most of the time you will rely on the shafts of surface light. Because the entire forest expands and contracts on a cycle measured in hours, plan any given excursion in multiples of sixty to ninety minutes so you can witness a full “breath” without fighting the shifting corridors.

Mentally, rehearse extreme patience. Nothing here rushes. Grazers pulse once every several minutes; filaments drift at body-lengths per hour. If you move at normal human swimming speed you will tear through architecture that took the plateau months to assemble. Treat every layer of leaves as a temporary ceiling and every hollow stalk as a fragile skylight; they will close again behind you if left undisturbed.

Finally, accept that the plateau is still forming. Fresh fractures may appear between visits, and entire buoyant ceilings may migrate upward or collapse. Do not anchor to anything; the only stable reference is the rock itself, and even that is still shedding sediment and sharpening new edges. Enter with the expectation that the route you took inward may no longer exist when you leave.
