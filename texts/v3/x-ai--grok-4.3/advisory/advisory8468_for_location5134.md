**Preparation for an Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler**

This is not a “visit” in any terrestrial sense. It is an industrial deep-ocean construction site the size of a planet, still being assembled under crushing pressure and perpetual artificial daylight. An ordinary human cannot simply step through a portal and begin exploring; the environment will kill an unprotected person in seconds. Preparation must therefore be treated as hostile-environment expedition planning with industrial and navigational overlays.

**Core Survival Envelope**
- Depth/pressure: Kilometers-scale water column. Any vehicle or suit must be certified for at least 150–200 atm (exact figure depends on how deep the traveler intends to go). A rigid-hulled submersible or atmospheric diving suit with active pressure compensation is mandatory; soft diving suits are useless.
- Temperature: A few degrees above freezing. The suit or habitat must carry redundant heating; cold shock is a real risk even inside a vehicle if systems fail.
- Atmosphere: No free oxygen. The traveler needs either a closed-loop rebreather with CO₂ scrubbers rated for multi-day use or a hard-link umbilical to a larger habitat vehicle. Surface-supplied air is logistically impossible.
- Noise: The environment produces a continuous, directionless industrial roar (pumps, welders, valves). Helmet or vehicle audio systems must include automatic gain limiting and frequency filtering; unprotected ears will suffer damage quickly. Communication will be difficult—radio or sonar protocols with heavy noise cancellation are required.

**Mobility and Navigation**
Currents generated by mountain-sized convection pumps are strong enough to sweep an unpowered human or small vehicle into latticework. Bring:
- A vehicle with independent propulsion (thrusters or impeller) rather than relying on fins or swimming.
- Redundant navigation (inertial + acoustic beacons). The geometry is artificial and repetitive; visual landmarks are mostly identical girders and lamp belts.
- Anchoring or tethering capability for when work platforms or recently mated modules release sudden local flows.

**Hazard Mitigation**
- Sharp edges and falling debris: New alloy and ceramic surfaces are still knife-edged. A reinforced outer shell on the suit or vehicle is advisable; expect occasional drifting tools or insulation fragments on long free-fall trajectories.
- Active machinery: Automated welders produce arc flashes and molten alloy. Maintain standoff distances; the traveler’s vehicle should have external camera systems rather than relying on direct viewport observation. Pressure-equalizing vents can release super-cooled fluid jets.
- Biological interaction: The local fauna is already treating the structure as habitat. Translucent schooling fish are harmless but dense; larger metallic-scaled predators are pressure-adapted and may investigate unusual motion or electromagnetic signatures. Carry low-power deterrent devices (strobe or acoustic) or simply move slowly and predictably. Filter-feeding invertebrate curtains can foul intakes or propellers if the traveler ventures too close to beam lee sides.
- Thermal and chemical plumes: Pumps and weld joints create localized warm or chemically distinct water. Sensors that detect temperature and conductivity anomalies are useful for avoiding sudden scalds or corrosive micro-environments.

**Logistics and Duration**
- Power and consumables: Assume no friendly infrastructure. The traveler must carry all power, oxygen, and scrubber material for the planned stay plus 50 % margin. Docking or mating with newly lowered modules is possible but unreliable because construction is ongoing.
- Lighting: The sodium-white lamp belts provide steady illumination, but shadows between girders are absolute. Supplemental vehicle lights should be narrow-beam and non-disruptive to local ecology if the traveler wishes to observe without startling fish schools.
- Psychological considerations: The space is vast, empty, and filled with directionless industrial noise. Even a hardened traveler may experience sensory monotony or mild vertigo from the lack of natural reference points. Short excursions (a few hours at a time) with clear return-to-vehicle protocols are safer than extended solo roaming.

**Minimal Viable Kit Summary**
Pressure-rated vehicle or suit with independent life support, noise management, redundant navigation, external sensors, modest propulsion, and basic environmental monitoring. No biological sampling gear is needed unless the traveler specifically wants tissue from the artificial-ecosystem fauna. The world is still physically “new,” so every surface the traveler touches will feel recently manufactured.

In practice, the traveler should treat the entire basin like a colossal, still-under-construction shipyard on the seafloor of an alien ocean, operating under continuous industrial tempo. The correct mindset is that of a cautious inspector on a half-finished megaproject rather than an explorer of a natural wonder.
