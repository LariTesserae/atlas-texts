To enter this hollow is to step into a single held breath that has lasted centuries. An ordinary traveler must prepare less for hardship than for the violence of their own presence.

**Physical preparation**  
Dress in layered, seamless garments of dense wool or oiled plant fiber—no zippers, buckles, or synthetic rustle. The settlement’s equilibrium is maintained by the absence of abrasion; any modern fabric that whispers or sheds microplastics will feel like an intrusion. Boots should be replaced with soft-soled moccasins or felt slippers whose tread exactly matches the grain of the root-ladders.  

Expect constant, penetrating damp at 2–4 °C. Bring a single, heavy cloak waxed with pine resin; it will collect condensation the way the leaves do, and the traveler can hang it each “night” in one of the carved drip channels so the settlement reclaims its own moisture. No open flame, no battery-powered light. The fixed shaft of illumination is part of the architecture; any additional photon is noticed.

**Temporal and metabolic preparation**  
The place moves at roughly 1/100,000th human speed. A visitor who walks normally will appear as a streaking blur to the translucent beetles and will shear the delicate condensation films. Practice deliberate micro-movements for weeks beforehand: sit motionless for four hours, then shift posture over thirty minutes. Some travelers dose themselves with trace sedatives or simply fast until their resting pulse drops below 50 bpm before entering; the body’s ordinary metabolic heat and breath turbulence are already loud enough.

**Behavioral protocol**  
Choose one dwelling frame and occupy only the routes already worn by the pale salamanders. Never touch living wood except where previous palms have polished it to a mirror finish. When drinking from a channel, place lips exactly where a leaf tip drips; the system is calibrated to that volume. If you must stay more than a few subjective hours, sleep in a notch already shaped for a salamander’s body so your exhalation joins the established rhythm of clicks and drips.

**Psychological orientation**  
The stillness is not peaceful; it is absolute. Most visitors experience a sudden, almost cellular awareness of their own decay the moment they realize the vines have not grown a millimeter in their presence. Bring one small, familiar object that is allowed to change—a single human hair kept in a glass vial—so the mind has a clock. Without it, some travelers lose the ability to leave, convinced they have only just arrived.

The return journey feels like surfacing from deep water. The world outside will seem to roar for days afterward.
