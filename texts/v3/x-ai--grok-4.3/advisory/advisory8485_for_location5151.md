**Preparation for the Basalt Vault City**

An ordinary human visitor must treat this place as a sealed, high-heat mineral desert rather than a ruin. The environment is actively hostile to biology: constant 40–50 °C+ floor heat, zero moisture, moving abrasive grit, and a non-stop 85–95 dB mineral chorus that never drops. Survival hinges on heat management, dust control, hearing protection, and accepting that nothing here will behave like a normal city.

**Heat and hydration (non-negotiable)**
- Wear a full reflective, ventilated suit (light-colored aluminized fabric or modern phase-change cooling layers) with separate knee and elbow pads; the rounded stone surfaces will abrade ordinary clothing quickly.
- Carry 1 L of water per hour of intended stay minimum, plus electrolyte salts. The air is bone-dry; you will lose fluid faster than you feel thirst.
- Bring a small, battery-powered personal cooling unit (neck or vest style) and at least two spare power banks. Floor heat will conduct through boot soles—choose boots with thick insulating midsoles.
- Monitor core temperature every 20–30 minutes. Heat exhaustion arrives faster here than in open desert because there is no wind at ankle level and no night cycle.

**Respiratory and eye protection**
- A half-mask P100 or better respirator with spare filters is essential. The convective currents carry fine feldspar and salt grit that will coat lungs and eyes within minutes.
- Tight-seal goggles (not sunglasses) with anti-fog and side vents. The single orange fissure light creates harsh glare off the translucent crystal plates; unprotected eyes tire rapidly.

**Hearing**
- The continuous clatter of crystal plates is loud enough to damage hearing within an hour and makes normal conversation impossible beyond arm’s length. Use both earplugs and over-ear muffs rated for at least 30 dB reduction. Bring a bone-conduction headset if you need to communicate with a partner.

**Lighting and navigation**
- The vault is dim except where the high fissure strikes crystal fields. Carry a bright headlamp with red-filter option plus a backup. Distances are deceptive; the high, featureless ceiling and wide plazas make everything feel both close and infinitely far. Mark your route with small reflective tabs—the rounded architecture offers few natural landmarks.
- A simple magnetic compass still works; the basalt is old and the field is stable.

**Movement and tools**
- Gloves and knee pads. Every surface has a satin finish from centuries of grit but remains uneven.
- A compact pry bar or walking staff helps test footing on the dry, compacted salt plazas where the “streets” feel more like open playa than pavement.
- Do not touch the mineral filaments unless necessary; they flex slowly but their edges are sharp and they will abrade skin.

**Time and exit discipline**
- Limit first visits to 90–120 minutes maximum. The sealed nature means any failure (cooling suit breach, respirator clog, disorientation) becomes life-threatening quickly.
- Carry a small, bright emergency strobe and a pre-agreed extraction signal. The vault has only one obvious light source; if you lose sight of it, the crystal fields themselves become your only orientation aid.

**Psychological notes**
The emptiness is total—no insects, no birds, no organic smell. The only motion is the slow turning of crystal plates and the low optical shimmer of heat. Many visitors report a creeping sense that the city is “watching” via the shifting skyline. This is simply the slow growth response to thermal currents; remind yourself of that fact before entering. The constant sound quickly becomes hypnotic; check your watch and physical condition regularly rather than relying on the changing pitch of the crystals as a timer.

In short: treat it like a cross between a geothermal mine, a salt desert, and an abandoned subway station at noon in July. Over-prepare for heat, dust, noise, and disorientation, and you can spend a few careful hours inside. Under-prepare and the vault will exhaust or disorient you within the first hour.
