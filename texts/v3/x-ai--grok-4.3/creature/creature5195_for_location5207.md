In this half-carved chamber the living things do not so much arrive as seep inward, finding the unfinished edges and settling into them the way condensation finds every joint. The first to claim territory are the thinnest films of life, translucent sheets of thermophilic algae that spread across the wettest basalt ledges where geothermal breath keeps the rock above thirty degrees. They pulse faintly under the sodium lamps, their color a sickly chlorophyll green diluted almost to white, as though the light itself has been strained through steam before reaching them.

From those same damp ledges the midges rise in loose, drifting clouds. Their larvae teem in the shallow sumps where drainage has not yet been completed; each hatch sends a soft, almost inaudible rain of wings against the undersides of pipes. Cave crickets follow the same moisture trails, their long antennae tapping along the temporary cradles that still cradle the open turbine housings. They move with a deliberate, almost mechanical gait, pausing to drink from the beads that form and fall from every metal surface.

Larger, slower tenants occupy the lower trenches. Blind salamanders the color of wet concrete lie half-submerged in the trickles that never quite reach the main sumps. Their skin is thick and mottled with the same pale algae that coats the rock; they press themselves against the geothermal fissures for warmth, emerging only when the vibration of a distant pump changes pitch, signaling that a worker has switched off a valve for the night. Pale millipedes share these trenches, their segments clicking softly against the unfinished concrete as they follow the constant supply of organic film left by condensation and spilled coolant.

Rats have taken the pipe runs as their thoroughfares. They travel in small, unhurried processions along the upper tiers where the sodium lamps cast the deepest shadows, their fur already beginning to carry the faint mineral scent of the stone and the metallic tang of the steam. They pause at every junction to test the air with quick, deliberate whiskers, then continue along routes that already feel older than the pipes themselves.

Human workers move through the same space on different schedules. Their boots leave temporary prints in the thin mineral mud that collects along the aisles; by the next shift the mud has begun to re-smooth itself around the marks. They speak little, and when they do their voices are absorbed quickly by the rock and the constant low thrum of the pumps. After they leave, the salamanders return to the trenches they have been displaced from, and the midges rise again from the disturbed water. The entire population—algal film, insect cloud, scaled amphibian, furred mammal, and the larger, slower-moving humans—continues its slow negotiation with the unfinished surfaces. Each finds a seam the rock has not yet decided to close, and each settles more deeply into it as the water and the steam continue their own unhurried work.
