In the layered dark below the highest lattices, life persists as a single, distributed metabolism rather than as separate species. The smallest forms are already described in the constant low roar—millipedes that follow grooves worn into the membranes over centuries, beetles whose ceaseless polishing maintains the feeding channels, salamanders whose throat-pumps move moisture along seams they have occupied since they were larvae. These are not individuals so much as the visible surface of the motion; when one slows or dies, the groove it followed is simply widened by its replacements within days, the body itself reabsorbed into the same bracket fungi it once grazed.

Larger shapes move through this background without disturbing its frequency. They are elongated, almost boneless masses whose skin is the same translucent membrane that hangs between the frameworks, thickened only where it must bear their weight. They travel by pressing against multiple lattices at once, inching along the same fixed routes the millipedes use, their passage registered only as a temporary deepening of the roar when their softer tissues drag across chitin and wood. Their interiors are largely empty; they carry pockets of the condensate basins inside them, redistributing the mineral-rich water to zones where the frameworks are still extending. When they reach a place that needs reinforcement, they halt for years, extruding new rootlets from their own flanks that fuse with the surrounding pillars and are eventually absorbed, leaving only a slight thickening of the structure behind.

At still greater scales, certain basins themselves have become animate. Over generations the mineral films that line them have organized into slow contractile sheets. These basin-entities never leave their depressions; instead they alter their own depth and lip shape by degrees, guiding the drip pattern that feeds the lower frameworks. Creatures too small to be named settle on their surfaces in seasonal drifts, and the sheets accept or reject them according to an ancient rhythm, allowing some to tunnel and others to be sealed beneath new layers of hardened deposit.

Nothing here reproduces in the sudden, visible way of animals that scatter eggs or give birth. Replacement occurs through the same patient accretion that builds the lattices: a millipede’s route is continued by its own substance after death; a membrane thickens where it has been most often pressed; a large translucent mass gradually incorporates the path it once traveled until the distinction between traveler and road blurs. The only true distinction left is between growth that still widens space and growth that has already filled it. Both continue without urgency, occupying the grey dusk at the single low frequency the entire structure has maintained for longer than any living mass inside it can remember.
