The motile spores are not individuals in any ordinary sense. They arrive as faint, refractive motes that condense out of the metallic vapor when a peristaltic thickening contracts a few meters away. Each spore is a droplet of concentrated cytoplasm wrapped in a silica cuticle so thin it bends light twice before releasing it; seen head-on they appear to be holes in the constant illumination rather than objects. They drift with the local exhalation current, but when they touch a piezoelectric strand they pause, flatten, and then skate along it by flipping small crystalline plates that click once against the fiber. The clicks are too small to hear except as an added dryness in the air, like the aftermath of a spark.

Where many spores accumulate they begin to link. Cytoplasmic threads extend between them, first as liquid bridges no thicker than a hair, then as taut, contractile filaments that twitch in time with the slow power surges moving through the lattice. These thread-like consortia are the next scale of life here. A single consortium can span three or four galleries at once by riding the folding metric; what looks like separate filaments from one angle is revealed, after a few steps, to be the same filament seen from a slightly earlier moment in its own growth. They migrate toward zones of recent compression, where older xylem is being crushed into the inert pearl-gray foundation. There they settle along stress lines and begin to secrete fresh silica-laced cellulose, accelerating the formation of a new tension member exactly where the structure has already begun to anticipate it.

Larger forms are rarer and less distinct. Occasionally a consortium thickens until it becomes a translucent sheet stretched across a vertical slot. Light passing through the sheet is delayed by a few milliseconds; the delay registers as a slight lag in the constant noon spectrum, a momentary violet fringe at the edges of whatever is seen through it. These sheets do not move so much as they are moved—carried by the continent-wide breath when the luminous weather fronts arrive. During those intervals the sheet may absorb charge from the amber seams that pulse once per cycle; the stored energy later leaks out as a slow, deliberate ripple of membrane growth that narrows or widens a gallery by centimeters over decades.

Nothing here reproduces by leaving. Spores and consortia can detach and travel, but they remain chemically continuous with the vapor they condense from. When a consortium reaches a region already occupied by an earlier version of itself (visible through a fold that has migrated into alignment), the two simply merge at the molecular level, exchanging crystalline plates and cytoplasmic memory without any evident conflict or novelty. The result is not a population but a distributed thickening of presence, a gradual increase in the density of living surfaces that already matches the density of the architecture they inhabit.

Over centuries the distinction between inhabitant and substrate blurs further. A gallery that was once traversed by a consortium may itself begin to exhibit the same refractive lag, as though the walls have learned the habit of the sheet that once crossed them. The slow exhalation grows fractionally deeper there, and the next generation of spores condenses a little sooner, already oriented toward the same stress lines their predecessors once followed. In this way the living occupants do not occupy the place so much as they continue its unfinished description, adding clauses of movement and charge to a sentence that has been writing itself for longer than any single consortium can measure.
