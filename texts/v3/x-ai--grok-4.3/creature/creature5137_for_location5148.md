Beneath that crushing ceiling the inhabitants are not visitors but extensions of the medium itself. They move as densities within densities, their outlines never quite separating from the slow rain of particles that continually coats every surface. The largest of them are the cloak-wings: broad, nearly flat membranes stiffened only by radial veins of cartilage that flex so slowly the motion is felt rather than seen. They do not swim so much as shear the water, letting one density layer slide across another and riding the resulting gradient for hours before a minute tilt of a single vein sends them gliding in a new direction. Their undersides are a felt of fine, mucus-coated filaments that comb the passing silt for whatever microscopic flakes of organic matter have survived the descent; every few hundred body-lengths the cloak-wing pauses, curls inward, and draws the gathered film into a central mouth that works without haste.

Smaller but far more numerous are the comb-threads. These live anchored or half-anchored, dozens of hair-thin arms emerging from a single buried node no larger than a fingertip. The arms themselves are almost entirely sensory: each bears a staggered row of microscopic cups that taste both particle and pressure. When a bolus of slightly denser water passes overhead the arms splay wider, intercepting the extra suspended matter and folding it back toward the node in a motion too delicate to register as movement unless one is already touching the thread. Once or twice in a long span a thread will withdraw entirely into its crevice, leaving only a faint calcified ring; weeks later the ring sprouts again, as though the organism had simply waited for the next suitable drift of food.

Between these two extremes drift the pin-lights: clusters of fluid-filled spheres, each no bigger than a seed, held together by transparent filaments that tense and relax in slow peristaltic waves. The light they produce is not constant; it is a brief, directionless bloom that occurs only when two clusters brush one another in the dark. The flash lasts just long enough for each cluster to register the chemical signature of the other, then fades, leaving them to continue their separate, imperceptible progress across the plain. Nothing preys upon them; they are too small and too few to be worth the energy of pursuit.

All of these forms share the same quiet metabolism. They neither chase nor flee. They simply wait, positioned at the precise height above the stone where the most useful layer of moving water will pass, and let the world bring them what they require. When the water grows especially still, the cloak-wings settle onto the gentle swells and the comb-threads retract; only the rasping of silt over their own surfaces tells them that time is still passing. In such intervals the plain contains nothing that can be pointed to as an animal; it contains only the continuous negotiation between stone, water, and the minute living films that occupy both.
