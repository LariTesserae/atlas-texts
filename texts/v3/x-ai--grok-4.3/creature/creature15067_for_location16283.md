In the low stone throat, life clings like condensation itself—thin, persistent, and shaped by the same slow pressures that fret the blocks. The smallest tenants move first and most constantly: thumbnail crabs whose shells are the color of wet bone, each one dragging a single shed limb or flake of its own carapace as if the fragment were a map. They travel the seams in loose files, clicking their way along hairline gaps where water weeps, tasting the mineral film with thread-thin legs. When a drop falls they freeze, then resume, their numbers turning the floor into a faint, shifting Braille that only the water can read.

Higher up, the pale slugs travel the ceiling in deliberate arcs. Each is nearly the length of a forearm, translucent enough that the dim vent-light shows a dark thread of gut and a slow pulse beneath the skin. They leave continuous glistening trails that catch and refract the gray threads of light into momentary prisms; when enough slugs cross the same stretch of stone the ceiling appears to glow from within, a low aurora of living mucus. Their movement is soundless except for the soft, regular drip of excess water falling from their tails into the pools below.

The larger forms—those between salamander and eel—emerge only when the cycle of seepage quickens. They slide through the shallow puddles with a motion more liquid than muscular, gills or lung-like sacs pulsing visibly along their sides. Their skin is the same pale porosity as the stone, mottled with darker rings that match the carved spirals hidden on the block faces; when they rest motionless against a wall they vanish into the architecture. Their breathing is the loudest single sound in the chamber, a wet, deliberate gulp that arrives in staggered waves and seems to answer the faster ticking of the condensing water.

Deeper in the gloom, where the vents no longer reach, other shapes register only as sound and displacement. Something with papery wings opens and closes in slow, measured beats, the rustle lasting several seconds each time, as if the creature must first decide whether flight is worth the cost of leaving its perch. Larger, rarer impacts send shallow waves across entire puddles; whatever makes them moves with the unhurried weight of something that has never needed to flee. None of these forms appear together. They occupy staggered rhythms within the same slow breath of the chamber—crabs at the seams, slugs along the stone’s underside, eels in the water, larger presences farther back—each layer rising into brief prominence when the seepage cycle turns and then settling again into the mineral quiet.

The carvings on the hidden faces of the blocks are not empty ornament. When water sheets across them the spirals catch and redirect the flow, guiding droplets into the exact seams the crabs patrol. The alignment is too precise to be accident; the creatures treat the patterns as given paths, following them without hesitation. In this way the old construction and its present inhabitants continue one another’s gestures, the stone still shaping movement, the movement still keeping the stone damp enough to remain slightly alive.
