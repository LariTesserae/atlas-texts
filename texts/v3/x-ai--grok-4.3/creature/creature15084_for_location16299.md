In the galleries where the crust still pulses like cooling dough, the life moves in tandem with the rock’s own hesitation. The armored slugs—each one a living barge of overlapping plates the color of graphite and old bone—never quite stop secreting their glassy trails. The paste they lay down is not simple mucus; it carries suspended grains of the same quartz that veins the ceiling, so that yesterday’s path can still be read as a faint luminous filament even after the ceiling has shifted two body-lengths sideways. When a tremor rolls through, the slugs do not flee. They simply press their broad undersides harder against the stone and keep rasping, turning the new fracture into another permanent groove before the air has time to cool.

The six-legged borers travel in loose, flickering constellations. Their carapaces are thin enough to let the glow from the mineral veins pass through them, so a mass of them moving along a seam looks, from a distance, like a slow constellation of dull orange stars sliding behind frosted glass. They do not eat the stone in the way an animal eats flesh; instead they mouth it, softening it with a solvent that smells of wet chalk, then carry the dissolved slurry in cheek pouches until they reach a place where the temperature is right for re-deposition. The ridges they build are seldom straight for more than a few dozen paces; they curve with the heat flow itself, so that after many cycles a single passage can begin to resemble the whorled interior of a shell.

The herd beasts are the most numerous and the least hurried. Their agate hides are not solid; inside each translucent flank runs a lattice of water-filled channels that pulse in time with the gallery’s own slow breathing. When they crowd together at a lens-pocket, the overlapping bodies create temporary chambers of trapped warm air. Inside these accidental shelters, smaller unarmored things—transparent, thread-like, almost too fine to see unless moisture condenses on them—drift upward from the herd’s skin and drift downward again when the next tremor shakes the mass apart. The beasts themselves pay the threads no notice. Their only sustained activity is the low, grinding rotation of their foot plates, which grinds fresh mineral dust into the air and keeps the light beams in constant soft motion.

Nothing here is truly fixed, yet nothing is ever truly new. The same seam that collapsed last cycle will split again a little farther along, and the borers will already be waiting at the hairline fracture, legs ticking, ready to widen what the rock has only begun to offer. The slugs will follow, laying their luminous roads across the fresh ceiling. And somewhere deeper, where the galleries have not yet opened, another herd is already learning the shape of the next collapse by the vibration that travels up through their water-filled hides.
