This place is not occupied so much as constituted by its inhabitants. The dominant presence is a single, continent-scale organism whose body has learned to behave like terrain. What first registers as “moss” or “lichen” resolves, on closer inspection, into a continuous sheath of interwoven thalli whose individual cells belong to dozens of species yet function as one metabolic sheet. At the scale of meters the sheath thickens into load-bearing platforms and sagging vaults; at the scale of millimeters it remains a moist, three-dimensional felt in which every filament is constantly testing its neighbors for mechanical purchase. Where two growth fronts meet they do not halt; they interpenetrate, forming braided pillars whose living cambium still conducts water even while it supports the weight of later arrivals above it.

Scattered through this matrix are pale, almost transparent fungal collars that rim every permanent pool. These are not parasites but boundary tissues, extruded by the main mass to regulate evaporation; they close like living sphincters when the air dries and reopen, petal-like, when cloud passes. Inside the pools themselves float chains of nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria whose sheaths have fused directly with the surrounding rootlets, so that the water is never merely water but a slow-moving extension of the organism’s circulatory system.

Animal life is reduced to a few specialist detritivores. The translucent mites travel along condensation films the way minute ungulates might follow riverbanks, grazing on shed cell walls and leaving behind microscopic trails of nitrogen that the mat immediately reabsorbs. The rare wingless insects are essentially ambulatory seeds: soft-bodied, short-lived, and tolerated only because they carry packets of novel symbionts that the mat occasionally tests for usefulness before digesting the carrier. Their disappearances are not predation events but incorporations.

Sound and motion are likewise internal. When a gallery slumps, the tearing sigh is the sound of several square meters of tissue deciding, in concert, that vertical support is no longer economical and electing to flow horizontally instead. The faint clicks that follow are not fractures but the rapid deposition of new cross-bracing microfibrils—cellulose laid down at a rate just perceptible to an attentive listener. Over hours the plateau rearranges its own weather, shifting depressions so that collected water migrates downslope, always toward regions whose metabolic rate has recently increased and therefore requires more hydration.

From a distance the entire formation presents the calm profile of long-weathered stone. Up close it is an engine whose every moving part is still embryogenic, still deciding what shape would best suit the next pulse of moisture or the next shift in temperature. Nothing here is finished; everything is in the process of becoming the next version of the same slow, cold, self-repairing landscape.
