In the slow pulse of that fused chamber, life has learned to wear its predecessors like a second skin. The membrane overhead is the eldest resident, neither wholly root nor fungus but a patient collaboration that once crept across the basalt in quiet increments and now holds the entire ceiling in a single shared breath. Veins within it swell and contract with the regularity of a sleeping lung, feeding the aerial roots that hang motionless for hours before twitching toward a falling drop. Those roots are not searching so much as tasting—an ongoing experiment in catching water without ever quite committing to the floor.

Below them the fauna moves through the same experiment at faster scales. The many-legged creatures that cling to the undersides of the roots spend most of their lives upside-down, their pale segments glowing just enough to outline one another when they pass. They drink from the same condensation the roots crave, and when a drop falls they pause mid-step, bodies flexing so that their legs form a living net that guides the water sideways rather than letting it spatter away. The six-eyed moths drift among them like slow kites, wings brushing the roots without ever gripping; their leathery membranes carry spores from the high membrane down to the moss-grating, where they lodge and quietly thicken the cork-like surface. Smaller swarms, quick as sparks, orbit the warm joints of the machines. They are less a single species than a recurring strategy: whatever can lap oil and sap in the same motion and leave a thin film of polished excrement that protects metal from further rust.

The machines themselves have become participants rather than ruins. Bark does not merely cover them; it has learned their rhythms, splitting and resealing around pistons so that the moving parts can continue without tearing the wood. Each thump of a pump sends a small tremor through the surrounding grating, and the moss hardens or softens in response, keeping the corridors open while discouraging anything larger from settling directly on the moving surfaces. Steam that escapes carries both the scent of heated sap and the faint metallic tang of whatever lubricant the small swarms have not yet licked away; the roots above extend fine white threads into that rising vapor, tasting it before withdrawing.

Nothing here reproduces in the old, separate ways. The luminous arthropods leave eggs in the grooves of the bark-metal, and the eggs sometimes hatch into more bark than flesh. Spores from the moths drift into the condensation basins and germinate into new rootlets that follow the old water channels. The membrane overhead grows outward only where the machines continue to exhale warmth; if a pump were to still entirely, that section of ceiling would begin to thin within days. The entire chamber is therefore a single metabolism stretched across former categories—industrial, botanical, animal—each still recognizably itself yet altered by the long proximity of the others. Movement never ceases, only changes tempo: a root shifts one finger-width, a moth’s wings rasp once, a piston cycles, and the warm air carries the combined sound a little farther than acoustics alone would allow.
