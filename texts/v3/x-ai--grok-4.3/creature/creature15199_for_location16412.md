The people who live here number fewer than a hundred, scattered across the twenty dwellings in shifting patterns of occupancy. They move with the same measured quiet as their smaller neighbors, barefoot on the polished stone, their weight distributed so lightly that only the faintest warmth lingers on the rock after they pass. Their skin has taken on the cool, matte tone of the mist itself, and their eyes have widened slightly over generations, pupils adjusting to extract what little light filters through the overhang. They wear loose wrappings of pounded vine fiber dyed in the faint mineral colors that leach from the basalt—dull greens, iron-blacks, and the pale mineral white of dried condensation. When they speak, which is rarely and always indoors, their voices stay low enough that the words seem to sink back into their throats rather than travel.

These inhabitants do not farm or herd. They gather the slow-growing succulents whose leaves hold mist in concentrated droplets, sip from them, and return the emptied husks to the vine bridges for the lizards. They collect the occasional shed wing from a moth or the translucent carapace of a molted crab-thing, using these fragments to reinforce the edges of their stone walls or to sharpen the antler needles with which they mend mats. Their tools remain extensions of the living rock; a basalt flake is never carried far from the seam where it was struck, and an antler tine is set back against the cliff when its usefulness ends so that the next rain can carry it into the moss seams.

Among the small creatures, new forms continue to reveal themselves the longer one watches. There are minute, opalescent slugs that traverse the undersides of the vine bridges, leaving behind traceries of silicate that catch light like frost. Four-winged flies no larger than a seed drift in vertical columns, their bodies so light they rise and fall with each exhalation from the central court. A species of thread-fine millipede lives entirely within the micro-ravines of the newest basalt, its segments the same needle-sharp black as the stone; only the collective shimmer of thousands moving in unison betrays their presence. The translucent crabs occasionally carry smaller passengers—minute, tufted mites that hitch rides to new fern islands and then disembark, their tufts functioning as temporary sails in the shifting air.

Nothing here preys in the conventional sense. The lizards pause to drink from the same droplets the crabs later harvest; the moths alight on the succulents whose stored water the people sip. When a moth expires, its body is absorbed by the moss within hours. When a person dies, the others carry the wrapped form to the lip of the shelf and let the condensation carry it downward in the same patient manner the droplets fall. The settlement therefore expands only by the slow accretion of new fractures in the basalt, each new seam claimed by moss, then vine, then a single additional threshold fitted into the living rock. The entire shelf continues its imperceptible rise, and every quiet resident—human, crab, lizard, moth—threads another fraction of its life through the same cycle of condensation and return.
