In the basins and along the rising shelves there live the lattices themselves, though to call them plants or creatures is to press a warm word against cold stone. They are slow accretions of ordered salt and feldspar, each frond a membrane of stacked crystals whose edges exchange charge with the air. What moves inside them is not sap but migrating ions that trace faint paths through the lattice, completing circuits over hours. When two thickets meet and fuse, the shared canopy does not simply grow; it begins a single, longer conversation in which the charge patterns of one garden pass into the other like remembered weather. The dangling threads are not ornaments but recording surfaces: each minute vibration from the ridges is lengthened and stored as a slight reordering of atoms, so that the canopy holds, in its own substance, a library of the low concussions that have passed beneath it for centuries.

Smaller beings also persist, though they are scarcely separate from the stone. In the chemical mists condense clusters of microscopic polyhedra that drift until they settle on a receptive plate. There they dissolve their outer faces and knit themselves into the larger lattice, adding a new node to whatever pattern already exists. When a flake is shed and falls, it carries a fragment of that pattern with it; upon touching ground it does not merely sprout but resumes the same interrupted exchange, so that one garden may eventually reappear, altered yet recognizably itself, kilometers away and decades later.

On the unfinished towers live nothing that could be called a tenant, yet the stone still remembers pressure. The negative prints of palms remain because the lattices that once coated the walls have not quite withdrawn; faint crystalline veins still thread the chisel marks, pulsing on the same slow schedule as the gardens below. Occasionally, over many years, a vein will extend a hair-thin filament across the open doorway or down the outer face, as though the memory of contact is still being read and answered. No hand returns to meet it.

Nothing here hurries or hunts. What lives does so by remaining legible to the shifting ground and the shifting air, by continuing the same exchange of ions and sound whether or not anything else is present to notice. The low-frequency tremor that rises from beneath the crust is not background; it is the common pulse to which every lattice times its own faint glow and every fallen flake begins its next filament. In that rhythm the place remains inhabited, though the inhabitants have no faces, no haste, and no need for witnesses.
