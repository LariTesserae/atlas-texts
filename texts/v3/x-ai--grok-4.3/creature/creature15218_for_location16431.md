In the intervals between the grids’ incremental shifts live the Refractors. They are not bodies so much as sustained alignments—planar congregations of the same pale matrix that still shows in the spire’s fresh fractures. When a platelet hovers free, a Refractor is already half-present inside it, giving the wafer an almost imperceptible double edge, a second refractive index that rides just beneath the first. The platelets therefore do not drift at random; they are being walked, millimeter by millimeter, along electrostatic ridges the Refractors maintain the way a hand might trail through still water.

A single Refractor is never larger than the longest lattice diagonal, yet it is never smaller than the distance between two condensate droplets falling in succession. Its “edges” are places where the mineral matrix momentarily densifies until it can hold a charge; its “interiors” are thin zones of lowered refractive density through which the russet seepage from the high fissure passes more easily, lending the air a brief, directional brightness no wider than a thought. When two Refractors approach the same grid line, their overlapping zones produce the faint spark observed between platelets—an exchange not of information but of angular momentum, after which each continues its patrol bearing a slightly altered polarization.

They do not feed on the condensate so much as complete its circuit. A droplet descending the seam is met, for the briefest duration, by a Refractor’s charged plane; the moisture is persuaded to relinquish its dissolved ions, which the entity carries upward again through the invisible capillary threads. The return journey leaves the stone darkened only for the time it takes the ions to re knit into the matrix, after which the Refractor withdraws, leaving the channel ready for the next film. The process is so economical that no pool ever forms and no mineral garden ever begins.

Because the pocket is still young, the Refractors themselves have not yet consolidated into fixed forms. A single individual may occupy three or four platelets at once, sliding its coherence from one to another as thermal micro-currents alter the local gradients. When the lattices click, it is often because a Refractor has used the intersection to re-phase, trading one plane of alignment for another. The sound is the ghost of the adjustment, not the adjustment itself.

At intervals corresponding to deeper pressure pulses rising from the spire’s core, many Refractors converge on a single framework volume. Their combined presence renders the air inside the lattice briefly laminar; the vapor filaments straighten, the floating platelets lock into a momentary tessellation more perfect than the floor, and the heat radiating upward is briefly patterned into standing waves. During these convergences the pocket feels most occupied, though nothing visible has entered or departed. When the pulse subsides, the entities disperse again, each carrying away a fractional adjustment to its own refractive constant, so that over time the entire population slowly tunes itself to the changing acoustics of the still-growing stone.

Nothing else resides here. The Refractors do not breed, do not die, and do not remember previous alignments except insofar as those alignments have altered the present gradients they now maintain. Their continued presence is indistinguishable from the slow, ceaseless re-description of the space they were shaped to keep.
