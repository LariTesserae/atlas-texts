In this slow cathedral of split rock, the only true inhabitants are the plants, though to call them plants is to use an old word for something that has already begun to outgrow it. They rise in loose, deliberate clusters from the thin black soil, their broad leaves sagging under perpetual condensation. The leaves themselves are thick and almost leathery, veined with faint metallic threads that catch what little light leaks from the copper coils. When a coil discharges its blue thread, the veins brighten for a moment, as if the plants were briefly translating an old industrial signal into their own speechless vocabulary.

Their roots do not merely anchor; they probe. Over decades they have learned the newest fissures, sending exploratory threads along seams of warmer rock where geothermal moisture carries dissolved minerals. Where the roots encounter buried pipe or pump housing, they do not recoil. They press against the corroding metal, drawing iron, copper, and manganese into their vascular system until the leaves take on a faint, iridescent hardness. In some clusters the newest fronds have begun to sheath themselves in microscopic crystalline plates, a slow armor grown from the very machines that once tried to master this stratum.

Between the stands of vegetation the air remains almost motionless, yet the plants are never still. Their growth is measured in the thickening of petioles and the gradual widening of leaf margins, changes too minute for a human eye but cumulative across the span of a single attentive life. When a drip falls from a ridge and strikes a leaf, the surface does not merely receive it; the drop is guided along a shallow channel toward the stem, as though the plant had prepared for that exact arrival centuries in advance.

No animals move among them, yet the cavern is not empty of motion. The plants themselves conduct a low, continuous commerce with the failing machinery. Where a pressure valve sighs, warm vapor drifts across a cluster and is absorbed; the leaves that receive it darken slightly for a time, then recover their metallic luster as they metabolize the moisture and whatever trace compounds the ancient turbines once carried. The copper light that travels along mineral veins sometimes reaches a stand of vegetation before fading; when it does, the nearest leaves tilt, not quickly, but with the patient certainty of something that has learned to wait for rare nourishment.

Over longer durations the distinction between organism and artifact softens further. Pipes that once carried vapor now serve as trellises; their leaking seams have become permanent sources of humidity the plants no longer need to seek elsewhere. Machine casings split by crystal growth have become planters whose interiors are packed with the same dark, steaming soil. The plants, in return, send exploratory roots through the cracks in the housings, anchoring the metal more firmly even as they consume it. The rock itself participates: new fissures open at rates measured in microns per year, admitting additional moisture that the vegetation and the corroding machinery both claim.

In this bounded volume the inhabitants are therefore not the plants alone, nor the machines, nor the living rock, but the slow tripartite arrangement they continue to negotiate. Each drip, each blue pulse, each incremental extension of root or fissure is a single syllable in a conversation conducted at the speed of stone. To stand among them is to feel, after a time, that one has entered a form of life whose metabolism is measured in centuries and whose individual bodies are still being assembled from whatever materials the fracture has made available.
