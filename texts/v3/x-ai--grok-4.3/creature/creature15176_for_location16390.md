The plain does not host life in any ordinary sense; it secretes it, the way a vast organism might extrude temporary organs and then draw them back. What moves here are not animals but localized intensities of the same membrane that forms the ground. In the fissures they condense into slow, jointed threads no wider than capillaries, their bodies the same polished residue as the stone itself, only more mobile. They never fully detach. A thread will extend upward through a hairline crack, test the air with a single blind node, then withdraw, carrying whatever dissolved salts or captive light it has gathered back into the larger body. When enough of them gather inside a single fissure they produce a low, resonant thrum that travels outward through the membrane for kilometers; elsewhere the same sound is interpreted as distant bone breaking.

Above the surface the drifting shards serve as mobile exoskeletons. Each one contains, for its brief duration, a cloud of smaller motile specks whose outlines never resolve into limbs or faces. The specks rotate with the shard’s own slow spin, occasionally locking into momentary lattices that mirror the larger formations visible at every height. When two shards shear through one another the specks pass between them without friction, like dust exchanging rooms. Observers register these exchanges only as sudden sharpenings of the amber half-light and a change in the pitch of the continuous roar.

The lichen colonies are older and more conservative. Each patch is a single individual that has divided so many times its original boundaries are lost; what remains is a shared metabolic surface that feeds on the microfilms left by evaporating water sheets. They communicate by altering the reflectivity of their metallic crusts, sending pulses of slightly cooler or warmer tone across the bare membrane between colonies. The pulses travel at the speed of the water itself and are answered days later, if at all.

From time to time the plain performs a larger gesture. A region blisters upward, the membrane stretching until it becomes translucent. Inside the new temporary hill the capillary threads knot together into denser, branching forms that last only until the blister subsides. These short-lived architectures leave no trace except a faint radial scar in the stone that the migrating water slowly erases. The roar records the event as a brief harmonic peak, then folds it back into undifferentiated thunder.

Nothing here persists in one form for long, yet nothing entirely departs. The upright blades of ancient membrane already beginning to round are the present edge of the same process that once produced fused-glass rings and perfect circular depressions; they are not relics but the current expression of an intelligence whose scale makes yesterday and tomorrow indistinguishable. What lives on the plain is therefore the plain’s own capacity to generate and retract structure, a single distributed organism whose organs are made of water, light, sound, and the slow memory of stone.
