In this unfinished terrain, the first life does not move across the membrane so much as it moves within it, shaped by the same pressures that are still knitting the surface together.  

Soft, sack-bodied organisms drift just beneath the translucent skin, suspended in the slow currents that thread between the shallow vaults. They are little more than fluid-filled envelopes whose walls are continuous with the membrane itself; when a thread of warm liquid passes overhead, their outlines swell and contract in unison, as if the entire plain were briefly breathing through them. Their interiors are faintly clouded with the same polymerizing sugars that scent the air above the blisters, and they feed by drawing minute volumes of that condensate through pores too small to see.  

Between these drifting sacs, networks of finer filaments have begun to knot into clusters. Each cluster pulses at irregular intervals, sending a visible ripple outward through the membrane like the rings left by a stone in still water. These ripples carry no message yet, only a record of the knot’s own tightening; over time they will become the first pathways along which later, more specialized forms can travel. Where two clusters meet at the embryonic ridges, their filaments fuse into a single thicker cord that now lies half-buried in the new tissue, its surface still sticky and incompletely sealed.  

Occasionally a valve opens in the floor of one of the temporary pools and releases a larger pulse of fluid. In that instant a different kind of life appears: a brief bloom of microscopic spheres that rise on the upwelling current, tumble once or twice in the heavier air, then settle again and are drawn back down when the valve closes. Most vanish into the lattice; a few remain stranded on the surface, where they dry into pale, paper-thin flakes that the next thread of moisture will rehydrate and carry onward.  

Nothing here has yet developed edges sharp enough to cut or limbs long enough to stride. The creatures that exist are still mostly boundary—extensions of the membrane that have only lately begun to close around their own interiors. They inhabit the plane by continuing to thicken it, by lengthening the thread-thin filaments a single cell at a time, and by leaving, in the slow tightening of each new ridge, the faint suggestion of a body plan that no foot has walked upon and no eye has yet seen.
