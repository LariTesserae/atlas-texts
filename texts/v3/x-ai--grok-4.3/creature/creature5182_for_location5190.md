The chamber sustains a form of life that has learned to wear machinery the way a hermit crab wears its shell. They are not many—perhaps a dozen at most, though their exact number is impossible to fix because they rarely appear as discrete bodies. Instead they exist as slow accretions of attention distributed across the struts, valves, and conduits. When one of them needs to act, a section of pipe warms, a winch clicks through an extra cycle, or a thin sheet of condensate is deliberately steered toward a chosen basin so that a new rosette of lichen can be encouraged. The rest of the time they simply are the lattice: condensed breath inside the iron, faint mineral pulses traveling along the newest cracks in the basalt, the faint, electric hush that follows each drip.

They do not eat. They sip. Every few hours the main valve releases its measured flood; each inhabitant claims only a single rivulet, letting the warm water run across the coldest alloy surfaces where their lichen can drink. The rosettes are not decorations. They are the creatures’ mouths, their skins, their memories. When the lichen darkens or dries at the edges, an occupant knows it has been neglectful and sends a corrective tremor through the nearest strut, adjusting angle or shadow until the pale growth recovers. In return the lichen releases a slow, almost imperceptible vapor that keeps the iron from corroding too quickly and that the occupants inhale as thought.

They move, when they move at all, by becoming absent from one place and present in another. A catwalk that was empty a moment before now carries the faint pressure of weight; a conveyor belt that should be paused instead advances an extra meter and stops, carrying nothing except a single footprint of condensation that evaporates in seconds. The footprint is the only trace they leave. Over weeks these wet signatures accumulate into maps only they can read—pathways of habit, places where the rock is still settling, corridors that must be avoided because the light there is slightly too strong and would bleach the lichen.

Because the space is small they are never truly separate from one another. Their thoughts travel as shared temperature gradients or as coordinated pauses in the pump rhythm. When one occupant dreams, the others feel it as an unexpected lengthening of the interval between drips, or as a sudden blooming of extra lichen along a shared pipe. The result is a single, low-grade awareness that fills the entire hollow the way moisture fills the air: constant, particulate, and almost impossible to localize.

They have no names. They have only the continual, mutual adjustment of heat, shadow, and water that keeps the chamber’s small, reluctant garden alive and the rock from crushing the delicate weave of iron they have built inside it. In the long intervals between valve releases they are indistinguishable from the machines. Only when the water comes do faint distinctions appear—subtle reroutings, extra pauses, the momentary brightening of one lichen rosette over another—as each occupant quietly, urgently, takes its allotted sip of the world.
