A few souls remain, though their presence registers less as occupation than as a faint continuation of the stone. They are not a community so much as a handful of people who have each, in successive generations, chosen not to leave. Most are old now; one or two may still be young, but the young here mature into the same weather-worn posture, the same economy of movement. They rise, tend the basins, open the shutters to the katabatic wind, and close them again before nightfall. Their gestures have become so practiced that they scarcely interrupt the metronome of dripping water.

They keep no animals beyond the occasional brown goat that materializes on the ledges and is coaxed down with the same soft syllables used a century ago. They grow nothing except the rowans that root themselves; instead they harvest the slow condensation that gathers on the slate. Inside the low rooms the air stays the same temperature as the cliff, so voices, when they are used at all, carry no farther than the next threshold. What conversation exists is mostly practical and quickly absorbed by the rock.

Because nothing new is introduced and nothing old is discarded, the objects they live among—iron latch, wooden buckets, a single whetstone—have acquired the same patina of permanence as the façades. The people themselves are acquiring it. Their skin and clothing take on the gray-green of the lichen at the joints; their silence lengthens until it matches the interval between drops. From a distance, or after enough years, they become difficult to distinguish from the outcrops they once built against.

Occasionally one of them climbs higher, following a fracture line that leads out of the cirque, and does not return. The others do not speak of it. The street remains empty, the basins continue to fill and empty at their ancient rate, and the hamlet stays exactly as the mountain permits it to stay: a balance in which a few diminished figures still participate, more by remaining still than by any deliberate act of living.
