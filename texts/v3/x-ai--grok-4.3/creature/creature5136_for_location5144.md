The beings who inhabit this slope are neither fish nor cephalopod, but something closer to slow, jointed lattices of living tissue that have learned to move inside the mesh they also cultivate. Each adult stands roughly as tall as a human forearm when fully extended, though most of the time they remain folded along the natural grain of the kelp layers, their many-segmented bodies resembling bundles of pale, translucent roots. Where the segments meet, flexible collars of cartilage allow them to lengthen or shorten individual limbs by nearly half their length, slipping through gaps that would trap a more rigid swimmer. Their skin is a thin epithelium backed by sheets of glassy spicules; in the copper-green light the spicules scatter a faint, shifting opalescence that the creatures can increase or dampen at will, turning their outlines into moving constellations when they need to signal across distances the water muffles.

They possess no single head. Instead, clusters of sensory papillae sit at the tips of the longest segments, tasting dissolved minerals, the faint electrical fields of growing stipes, and the minute pressure changes that announce an approaching grazer or a slump of sediment. When two or more individuals meet, they often link papillae directly, exchanging fluids and the chemical record of recent work; a single conversation can last several tidal cycles and may continue across multiple bodies as the linked group drifts along a newly fused filament.

Their reproduction is tied to the architecture itself. When a dwelling reaches a certain density of interwoven stipes and mineralized sponge, several adults will pool segments inside it and undergo a gradual fission. The resulting offspring emerge already half-entangled in the same fibers their parents harvested, and they inherit the unfinished sections of frame as their first responsibility. Because three or four generations may occupy the same lattice before it is considered complete, the inhabitants experience time less as succession than as thickening: an elder is simply one whose body has been shaped by more seasons of weaving and whose chemical memories are longer.

Movement through the settlement is therefore almost never solitary. When a section of slope subsides or a new terrace of mineral crust forms, a loose collective will migrate along the paths they have kept open, carrying nothing except the living cords they have already begun to braid around the next basalt nodules. In this way the population itself behaves like one of the half-drawn veils between fronds: always drifting, always rejoining, never quite finished.
