In the seams where frost heaves have pried the rock apart, the cushion plants have learned to grow not upward but inward, their tight rosettes pressed against the stone like living mortar. Each leaf is a thick, waxy scale that traps the constant condensation; at the center of every clump a faint green heart persists even when the outer rims are rimed with ice. They do not flower so much as release small clouds of spores when a sudden warming lifts the mist for an hour or two. Between them the black lichens spread in star-shaped colonies, their surfaces so absorbent that a single droplet can darken an entire patch within seconds. These organisms keep the only true calendars here: a slow, measurable expansion measured in millimeters per century, their margins advancing only when new fractures expose fresh mineral surfaces.

Along the thread-thin trickles the algae form living films that shift color with temperature. In the coldest intervals they lie almost translucent, a faint olive glaze; after any rise in seepage they thicken into dense emerald mats that trap silt and midges alike. The midges themselves do not swarm in the usual sense. Instead they emerge in synchronized pulses, thousands hatching at once from the saturated gravel, their wings beating a collective shimmer that the mist turns into brief, localized rainbows. When the air chills again they settle in dense, twitching carpets on the algae, feeding and dying within the same narrow window. Their bodies, scarcely heavier than a grain of frost-shattered rock, become the first layer of new soil wherever they accumulate.

The grazers move through this same brief window. Thick-furred and low to the ground, they possess broad, splayed feet that distribute weight across the loose gravel so they leave almost no trail. Their coats are not merely insulation but living surfaces: pale, almost translucent guard hairs that bead moisture and channel it downward to the skin, where specialized glands convert the constant damp into metabolic heat. They travel in loose arcs rather than herds, each animal spaced so that a predator cannot easily single one out against the gray backdrop. Their grazing removes only the newest algal growth, never exhausting a patch entirely; where they feed, the algae regrow faster on the nitrogen left by their droppings.

Following at greater remove are the low-slung predators, creatures whose bodies seem almost flattened against the rock. Their limbs are jointed in such a way that they can slide beneath slight overhangs or into shallow solution hollows, remaining nearly invisible until they strike. Their fur is shorter and darker, absorbing what little warmth penetrates the mist; across their flanks run faint rust-orange markings, echoes of the iron seams they sometimes cross. They do not hunt by sight in the conventional sense. Instead they read the faint pressure changes in the moving air, the minute vibrations that travel through the gravel when a grazer steps, and the sudden warming of breath that condenses differently when an animal pauses. Their kills are quick and leave little trace; the mist and the next cycle of frost cover the remains within hours, returning nutrients to the algae and the waiting lichens.

Deeper still, within the hairline vents where steam escapes in measured bursts, microscopic consortia of bacteria and archaea thrive on the mineral gradients. These colonies form slow, glistening rinds inside the rock, depositing the same crystalline layers that the cooling water lays down. When a vent opens, the sudden release of pressure carries a few of these organisms outward; most perish in the cold air, yet a fraction survive long enough to seed new seams when the next fracture forms. Their presence turns the rock itself into a living medium, one that grows and rearranges on the same timescale as the cushion plants above.

Across the entire ridge, every living thing moves or grows within the intervals the cold allows. A midge hatches and dies between two rock groans. A grazer crosses a slope in the time it takes for a mineral seam to thicken by a single crystal layer. The mist receives all of it without judgment, holding the small events distinct and countable until the next adjustment of the ridge carries them forward or buries them again.
