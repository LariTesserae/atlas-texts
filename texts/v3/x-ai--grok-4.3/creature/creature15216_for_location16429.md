In this fixed basin the insects are not visitors but extensions of the seams themselves. Each one is a filament of black glass given legs no thicker than a hair, bodies so slight they catch the light only as shifting glints within the constant film of water. They move in loose, drifting aggregations that never disperse and never tighten, their long legs skating across the beads without breaking the surface tension, tracing paths that repeat exactly after unmeasurable intervals. When one pauses, its wings lie flat and translucent against the tile, trembling at a frequency too small to hear, yet the air around it stays perfectly still.

The lizards are fewer and more deliberate. Their bodies are pressed so close to the clay that they seem poured rather than born, broad and pale as dried salt with eyes the color of wet reed. They do not hunt so much as arrive where the insects already are, lowering themselves until their bellies rest in the shallow channels. There they remain for lengths of time that feel like hours or days, tongues flicking once in a while to gather a droplet that already contains one of the glass-legged creatures. The lizards leave no tracks; their weight displaces nothing.

Between these two kinds move smaller, almost invisible presences: thread-thin worms that inhabit the drilled holes in the clay, their bodies the same pale green as the moss roots they share the tunnels with. They push through the plant-fiber lashings in single files that circle back upon themselves, maintaining the embroidered pattern without altering it. Occasionally a larger shape crosses the open tiles—a low, jointed form the size of a hand, carapace the exact shade of the baked clay and marked with the same mortise lines. It travels in straight lines between platforms, stopping only when it encounters a doubled channel; there it folds itself smaller and steps across the impossible loop as though the surface were continuous.

Nothing feeds on anything else in any visible way. The insects replenish their numbers without eggs or mating flights. The lizards thin or thicken by fractions too small to notice until an entire platform’s population has shifted by one individual. Even the worms reappear in previously empty holes without having traveled. All of them share the same arrested quality as the sedges and the water film: present, complete, and unchanged from one moment to the next, as though the basin’s stillness had been divided among many small bodies rather than residing in one empty space.
