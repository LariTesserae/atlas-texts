Inside the slow respiration of the shelf-organism, the translucent mites are only the most recent arrivals. They drift in loose constellations, each one a drifting lens that refracts the chlorophyll dusk into shifting mosaics of green. When one settles against a vein, it extends a fringe of hair-thin rhizoids that mesh with the plant’s own vascular lattice; for a few minutes the mite enlarges slightly, its membrane pulsing in time with the host’s deeper peristalsis, until the sugars it has gathered darken and its body grows heavier. Then the vein beneath it contracts, reabsorbing the mite’s softened edges until the creature is once more indistinguishable from the tissue it fed upon. What remains is not death but a local thickening—a small pearl of stored starch that the organism will draw upon the next time frost tightens the outer membranes.

Farther inward, where the fermentation pockets maintain their narrow band of warmth, other forms persist in a state between individuality and dissolution. Threads of former arthropods—segmented remnants of long-ingested mites or beetles—have been drawn into the walls as reinforcing fibers. Their original chitin now serves as flexible conduit lining, conducting water and transmitting the faint pressure waves that coordinate growth across distant parts of the shelf. Occasionally a single preserved eye or mandible will surface in a newly opened passage, already half-translucent and veined with the same green that suffuses the living masonry; it registers light for a few cycles before the surrounding tissues overgrow it again, incorporating its sensory data into the organism’s distributed awareness.

On the undersides of the broadest pads, where constant drip has carved shallow basins, colonies of even smaller motile specks aggregate. These are not separate creatures but mobile extensions of the same continuous cytoplasm, budded off at night and reabsorbed at dawn. They move in slow spirals across the warm moss, harvesting mineral crystals precipitated from the condensing mist and returning them to the central vascular bundles. When two such colonies meet, their boundaries blur; within moments they have merged into a single film that flows back toward the nearest trunk, leaving behind only a faint iridescence where the crystals have been deposited.

The shelf therefore houses no population in the usual sense. Instead, every passage and chamber is occupied by temporary concentrations of the same living mass, cycling through states of greater and lesser individuation. What appears as an empty street is simply a region the organism has chosen, for now, to keep thin and elastic. What feels like solitude is the organism’s way of listening to its own interior weather—the slow negotiation between frost and fermentation, between stored water and the next upward thrust of new membrane. Any visitor who pauses long enough will feel the faint tug of that negotiation along their own skin, as though the tissue is deciding whether to treat the newcomer as nutrient, as structure, or as a temporary guest whose shape might be remembered for a season before being gently folded into the whole.
