No one and nothing biological lives in the cirque. The basin holds only the residue of its own making: a population of fractures that lengthen by microns each year, their edges already softened by the same cycles that created them. These seams function as the place’s slow metabolism, widening and releasing grains that collect in the low points and are lifted again by the next windless freeze. The mineral rings left by evaporated slush mark the daily tide of this metabolism, faint calendars drawn and redrawn on the quartzite.

Scattered through the rock are the older human traces, equally unmoving. The low ring of stones sits flush because no frost heave has yet displaced it; the pecked basin collects the same nightly rime as any natural hollow and loses it by the same mid-morning. The angular flakes remain where they were struck, their platforms still sharp enough to read but their arrises already rounding. These remnants do not constitute life; they are simply another class of resistant surface that the cirque’s processes have not yet erased. Over centuries the same patient abrasion that softens the enclosing walls will also reduce the flakes and the pecked basin to indistinguishable rubble, at which point even this faint signature of earlier hands will belong fully to the rock again.

The only other presence is the condensation that arrives from outside. Mist drifts in, touches the upright faces, and releases single droplets that strike and vanish. Each droplet is an event without consequence, absorbed into the silence the packed crystals maintain. Between these arrivals the cirque contains only its own temperature, its own light planes, and the accumulating record of surfaces that continue to settle into equilibrium with the air that thins above them.
