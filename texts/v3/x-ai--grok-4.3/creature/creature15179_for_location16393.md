In the shallow film of each platter-pool live the thread-swimmers: finger-long, translucent ribbons the color of watered milk. Their bodies are less flesh than tensioned surface, a single continuous membrane pleated into a ribbon that never quite breaks the water’s skin. When they move they leave no wake, only a momentary folding of light that travels ahead of them like a rumor. They feed by pressing their length against the pool’s underside, drawing dissolved minerals directly through the membrane; the slow percussion of new droplets arrives already half-absorbed into them, so their passage is also a kind of quiet breathing of the basin itself.

Above the vegetation drift the veil-moths, though they are not moths and do not beat wings. Each is a loose assembly of jointed glass rods held inside a slack envelope of vapor-thin tissue. The tissue is so nearly the same refractive index as the constant white light that the creature is visible mainly by the faint distortion its weight makes in the air. When one descends to drink it does so by lowering a single rod until its tip touches a leaf; the leaf’s gathered beads race upward along the rod and vanish inside the envelope without surface tension ever breaking. Once sated, the veil-moth simply lightens, rising on an imperceptible updraft it seems to generate by the very act of thinking itself thinner.

Larger presences announce themselves only through the ground. Where a tread registers, the silt dimples for an instant and then remembers itself; nothing is left behind except a slightly warmer thread of moisture that the lacework roots hurry to claim. Those who have waited long enough speak of a single, continuous body whose segments pass beneath the entire basin in sequence, a living aquifer that visits each crystal ring in turn. The rings respond by shedding a finer condensation, and the thread-swimmers beneath the nearest pools quicken their folding of light, as though acknowledging a shared rhythm too slow for any single lifespan to witness.

At the crystal lattices themselves move the slowest inhabitants of all. They are not separate from the lattices; rather, the nested geometries inside each ring occasionally resolve into a single coherent gesture—a slow reconfiguration of planes that is also an exhalation. When this occurs, the air immediately around the ring cools by a fraction and the next forming droplet pauses an extra heartbeat before falling, already misted. Observers who remain perfectly still sometimes feel, rather than see, a vast and patient attention pass over them, an attention whose scale matches the ancient flatness of the stone and whose interest lies not in individuals but in the maintenance of overflow.

Thus the basin is never empty. Its life is distributed across speeds: the thread-swimmers in their liquid instant, the veil-moths in their drifting intervals, the unseen aquifer in its century-long circuit, and the lattices in their near-geological patience. All of them are held inside the same rule: nothing is permitted to arrive faster than the silt can absorb it, and nothing is allowed to leave except as moisture returning to the constant, white suspension of light.
