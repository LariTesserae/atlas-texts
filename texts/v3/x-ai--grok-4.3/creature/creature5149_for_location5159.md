In that perpetual dark the swarms are not separate beings but a single living skin stretched over the stone, a skin that thickens and thins according to the slow pulse of moisture drawn up from the deeper strata. Each wafer is no larger than a thumbnail, yet its edges are scalloped with minute crystalline flanges that interlock, for a moment, with its neighbors to form temporary, shifting lattices. When the lattice holds, the whole mass can glide as one sheet; when it releases, individuals peel away in spirals that catch against the damp air and drift back down like settling frost.

Their bodies are mostly empty. A translucent matrix of mica and silica encloses nothing more than a faint film of metallic salts and a thread of denser tissue that runs along one margin like a living hinge. Touch one and the hinge contracts; the creature folds into a rigid sliver that can slip between mineral grains and travel a short distance underground, leaving a hair-thin trail of dissolved feldspar behind it. When thousands do this together the basin floor sighs, the vibration rising through the rock as a single low note that travels farther than any sound carried by air. The note excircles, meets its own returning echo, and the wafers that have remained above ground orient to it, altering their drift so the swarm breathes in slow, kilometer-wide contractions.

They have no eyes, only clusters of piezoelectric crystals that register the faint charge given off each time a droplet of condensation strikes stone or another wafer. In the constant abrasion of their passage these charges flicker into brief, shared patterns—ripples of polarity that spread through overlapping bodies and dissolve again. The patterns carry nothing that could be called memory, yet they bias the next migration toward patches of rock whose surface potential is slightly higher, where the condensation film is thinner and the mineral salts more easily harvested. Over centuries these preferences have worn the basin into an invisible topography of favored feeding lanes; the quartz ridges stand higher precisely because the swarms have been gentler with them, while the troughs are lower because generations have worried the same seams.

Occasionally a denser knot of wafers remains below the surface longer than the rest, chewing a temporary chamber whose walls collapse behind them. In that moment the swarm above experiences a measurable drop in collective vibration; the sound thins for a few seconds, the high abrasion giving way to a brief, glassy silence that feels almost like an inhalation. When the knot re-emerges the sound resumes at once, yet the interval has altered something minute in the alignment of bodies overhead, as though the swarm, in its entirety, has taken one almost imperceptible step sideways across the plain. The process continues without climax or rest, the creatures neither multiplying nor dying in any single observable span, but simply wearing and being worn by the same damp stone that holds them.
