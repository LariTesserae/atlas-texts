High on the mesa the fronds are never quite still, yet their motion is not random. Each ribbon of translucent tissue listens. When the wind shifts they tilt in slow unison, edges rasping crystal against crystal as if conferring through the sound itself. In the densest stands the fronds grow shoulder to shoulder, their serrated margins interlocked like the pages of an open book; when a glider swarm passes overhead the entire thicket leans away at once, creating a momentary corridor of silence that closes again behind the thin wings. The plants draw nourishment from the metallic dust that coats every surface at dusk, but they also harvest a finer sustenance: the faint tremor that travels up through the stone whenever the larger grazers feed. That vibration travels the length of a frond in a single heartbeat; the plant stiffens, its crystal facets aligning so that the next gust will fling the loosened dust directly into the waiting veins.

The paper-winged gliders are smaller than thought at first glance, translucent bodies no thicker than a fingernail. Their wings are not true membranes but overlapping plates of the same mineral the fronds extrude; at certain angles they vanish against the copper haze, becoming only a jitter of refracted light. They do not land on the stone. Instead they alight on the upper edges of the tallest fronds, gripping with minute hooked spines while they scrape away fresh crystal growth with their mouthparts. The sound is a soft, continuous ticking, like sand falling on glass. When a stronger wind arrives they fold their plates and let themselves be lifted, spiraling in tight, deliberate helices that carry them to the next stand before they are blown clear off the mesa. Occasionally one drops low enough to be seized by a grazer; the flock answers with a sudden, synchronized flare of wings that briefly turns the entire thicket into a single reflective plane, startling the predator into immobility for the length of a breath.

The segmented grazers move with the patience of tide. Their bodies are low, segmented armor plated with the same pale salt that veins the rock, so that when they rest they seem to disappear into the pavement itself. They travel in loose processions of three or four, never more, chewing the base of the frond mats with mouthparts that work sideways, producing the wet-paper tearing that carries so far. As they feed, fine silvery threads extrude from pores along their flanks and drift upward on the wind; these threads are not silk but living extensions of the same crystalline growth that covers them. When enough threads accumulate they catch the next updraft and are carried to distant stands, seeding new mats or reinforcing old ones. The grazers appear indifferent to the stacked stone rings, but if a gust threatens to topple one they pause, bodies flattening until the wind passes, as though the rings are part of some larger, slower order they recognize but do not question.

Beneath all of this moves a quieter presence. In the shallow bowls where dust settles thickest, the pavement itself occasionally stirs. Minute, many-legged forms no larger than a seed rise through the loosened grains, carrying fragments of frond crystal to the nearest ring. There they press the pieces into the stone with precise, repetitive motions, repairing the windward polish that weather would otherwise erase. They work only at the hour when the haze thins and temperature spikes; once the dust curtains close again they retreat into hairline fissures, leaving no trace except a faint brightening along the ring’s lip. Nothing on the mesa acknowledges them directly, yet the fronds lean slightly toward the rings whenever these small masons are active, as if the entire height were tuned to the same faint rhythm of construction and surrender.
