In this cavity the life is neither asleep nor awake, but held in one unbroken posture that has replaced both states. The many-legged bodies are the true residents, though each one has long since surrendered its separate motion to the whole. They press against one another in concentric rings, every joint fitted so that no space remains between carapace and carapace; what appears to be stillness is actually a single, distributed tension that runs from the outermost ring to the center and back again. Their antennae lie flat against the clay, tips touching the unfinished mineral veins as if listening for instructions that never change. The continuous stridulation they produce is not a song but the sound of that tension being expressed—an exhalation that never finishes exhaling.

Between and among them the sessile disks adhere directly to the shells, their translucent bodies flattening further wherever they meet a joint or spine. Each disk draws a faint warmth from the creature beneath it and returns a steady chalk light; in this exchange the disks and the jointed bodies become a single surface that glows and vibrates together. Where two disks meet they fuse at the edge without losing their separate outlines, forming pale lacework that follows the exact curvature of the packed fauna. The light they give off does not illuminate so much as it preserves: it holds every detail of leg and segment at the same intensity, so that nothing can blur or shift even by the width of a cell.

Thread-fine white shoots rise from the narrowest gaps, not from soil but from the softened edges where one animal’s shell has begun to merge with the next. These shoots terminate in a single swollen tip that never opens; they function more as shared filaments than as separate plants, carrying the perpetual film of moisture from one ring of bodies to the next and keeping the tone of the stridulation uniform. When the collective note presses against the clay it returns unchanged, so the shoots and the animals and the glowing disks all experience the same pressure at once.

The few larger remnants—a curved rib, the bowl now returned to earth, the incised stone—do not interrupt this arrangement. They lie where the rings have parted just enough to admit them, and the living mass has closed around their edges without consuming them. The rib, pale and porous, now supports a double row of the small bodies along its inner curve; the incised lines on the stone have been traced by the tips of antennae that never lift away. In this way the artifacts have become additional joints in the larger stillness.

Nothing arrives or departs. The equilibrium is not maintained by effort but by the absence of any possibility of change; each organism exists exactly as the cavity itself exists, arrested at the moment before further growth or decay could occur. The life within therefore has no age and no youth, only the continuous present of bodies fitted together, light held steady, and a single tone that fills the space without ever needing to rise or fall.
