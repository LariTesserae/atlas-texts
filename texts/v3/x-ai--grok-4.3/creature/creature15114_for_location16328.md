In the slow intervals between pulses, flattened shapes emerge from the membrane clearings. They are not so much animals as accretions of the same tissue that makes the floor—translucent sacs of fluid connected by thread-thin filaments that knot into the rhizomes below them. When the peristaltic stems contract, these sacs contract with them, drawing a measured sip of sap and releasing it again through invisible pores. Their outlines shift only slightly from moment to moment, enough to keep the surrounding rhizomes from overgrowing them, like a living counterweight to the plant-room’s perpetual maturity.

Above them, in the narrow lanes between leaves, move the insects and amphibians already present, but altered by long residence. The insects carry faint, branching networks of the same circulatory fluid inside their abdomens; their iridescence is not from scales but from thin films of sap stretched across their wing veins. The amphibians are even more reduced—almost liquid themselves, their limbs no longer used for leaping but for pressing into the membrane floor so that groundwater can pass directly through their permeable skins. They serve as distributed sensors: when the thrum of the stems falters for even a fraction of a beat, they adjust their posture and the rhythm resumes.

Deeper still, where the rhizomes knot most tightly, something larger and slower occupies the warmest hollows. It has no fixed shape, only a continuous sheet of pale tissue that lines the underside of the canopy like an inner skin. Periodically it thickens in one place, thins in another, and the movement nudges the innermost leaves into slightly better alignment, maintaining the unbroken seal. When a drop of condensation falls, the sheet gathers beneath it, holds it for several heartbeats as though tasting it, then lets it continue downward into the root mass. This single extended organism appears to be what keeps the entire pocket at its fixed stage of ripeness; everything else—the pulsing stems, the membrane floor, the small translucent movers—exists in relation to its slow, distributed attention.

Nothing enters from outside and nothing leaves. The creatures and structures are not separate tenants but successive expressions of the same closed metabolism that has learned, over an immeasurable time, to treat enclosure as the only possible form of growth.
