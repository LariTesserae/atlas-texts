What lives here is not yet separate from the place.

The glass-fingered veils are not single organisms but slow negotiations between chemistry and light. Each thread is a hollow filament whose walls are only a few molecules thick; inside them, thin currents of slightly heavier water rise and fall, carrying dissolved metals upward into the photosynthetic zone and returning waste downward. Over centuries a veil may thicken or tear, but the motion is indistinguishable from the shearing of the currents themselves. When two veils drift close enough for their fringes to tangle, the contact does not produce courtship or conflict; it simply allows certain filaments to trade molecular templates, so that the merged structure carries a slightly altered repertoire of catalysts. The new pattern may persist for a few thousand years before the next current shears it apart again.

The forming lattices are likewise only half-geological. The nanoscale remnants still active inside them do not “design” in any deliberate sense; they merely lower activation barriers along particular crystallographic planes. As a result, a single shard can grow a fragile internal meshwork of spiral vanes whose surfaces remain continuously refreshed by catalytic turnover. These vanes act as primitive ion channels. When pressure waves from neighboring shards pass through them, the channels flex and momentarily trap thin films of water whose chemistry differs from the surrounding medium. Over time, populations of still-smaller entities—little more than self-replicating surface defects seeded by the same ancient catalysts—learn to migrate along these films. Their “locomotion” is only the biased diffusion of vacancies and interstitial atoms, yet it is enough for lineages to accumulate. Some defects stabilize certain vanes against dissolution; others encourage the lattice to extrude new capillary branches. The lattice thus becomes, without ever intending to, a kind of passive reef on which slower chemistries can persist.

Nothing here hunts or flees. When an overturning current lifts a lattice several kilometers and then grinds it to dust, the catalytic surfaces are simply redistributed. Some fraction of the surface defects survive inside the suspended grains; they continue their patient work while drifting. Occasionally two such grains lodge inside the same veil. The filaments grow around them, and the lattice fragments begin to accrete new material along the veil’s own mineral gradients. The result is neither symbiosis nor parasitism, only a temporary composite whose behavior—growth rate, resonance under pressure, tendency to fracture along particular planes—differs measurably from either component alone.

In the long intervals of stillness between currents, another kind of participant becomes briefly visible. The water itself holds dissolved precursor molecules whose concentration is too low for ordinary precipitation. When a pressure node lingers for long enough, these molecules co-assemble into evanescent sheets that span the node like soap films. The sheets last only until the next shear arrives, yet they can record, for those few hours, a faint pattern of ionic gradients left by the nearest lattices. When they collapse, the pattern is released back into solution and may seed a later film elsewhere. No memory persists across generations, because there are no generations; only the continuous redistribution of possibility through the medium.

Thus what lives here is the ongoing differentiation of the place itself—filaments that refine the veils, defects that bias the lattices, transient films that briefly hold shape before returning to the currents. They do not move toward anything; they are what remains when motion is subtracted.
