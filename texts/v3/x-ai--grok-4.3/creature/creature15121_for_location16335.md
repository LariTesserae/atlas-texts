What lives there moves at the speed of condensation.

In the shallow lenses of collected water, among the hairline mats of lichen, drift colonies of translucent micro-fauna—single-celled clusters that have learned to ride surface tension the way fish ride currents. They are almost invisible until a filament pulse warms the water by a fraction of a degree; then they expand, turning the lens briefly opalescent before contracting again as the temperature equalizes. They leave no waste that the stone does not immediately reclaim.

The thumb-sized invertebrates are their visible descendants, or perhaps their herds. Blind, they navigate by tasting the changing electrical charge of the filaments beneath them. When a filament reroutes to catch a droplet, the creatures feel the shift in the floor as a brief brightening in their chemosensory maps and drift toward it, arriving just as the redirected water arrives. They feed, then are lifted and dropped again by the same warm micro-eddies, redistributed like spores. Their shells are thin enough that the low-frequency thrum of the hidden engines passes straight through them; they have no organs with which to register it as sound, only as a constant mild pressure that orients their bodies toward the nearest building face when the engines change pitch.

Nothing larger than these creatures has survived here, yet the city is not empty of minds. The filament networks have become a single distributed organism whose individual threads have forgotten they were once separate. Where they cross the open floor they form decision boundaries—slow, crystalline hesitations about which way moisture should travel next. When two droplets fall within a few centimeters of each other, the filaments between them sometimes brighten in sequence, as if measuring the interval. The effect is not communication so much as the memory of communication, a habit retained after the last voices that might have needed it fell silent.

Occasionally a filament will detach a microscopic segment and allow it to drift into a water lens. There the segment dissolves, seeding new pale threads that the micro-fauna consume. In this way the network prunes itself and the water is given a faint, metallic taste that the invertebrates seek out. Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense; everything participates in a larger recomposition that has no clear beginning or end.

The buildings themselves hold the oldest residue of intention. Their translucent lattices still flex, but only by microns, only when a particular sequence of drips strikes them in the right pattern. The flexing is too slight to see, yet it alters the resonant frequency of the entire hollow by a fraction of a tone. The invertebrates orient to this change the way they once oriented to the footsteps of whatever tall, fragile creatures first grew the filaments. The memory of those footsteps remains in the slight subsidence marks around every foundation—negative spaces now occupied only by the slow return of moisture and the quieter return of light.

So the population is this: a few ounces of eyeless grazers, uncountable clouds of water-borne cells, and one very old, very slow lattice-mind that still adjusts its breathing for the sake of visitors who no longer arrive. Together they make a city that continues not because anyone requires it to, but because the act of continuing has become indistinguishable from the place itself.
