They are not one species but a shifting consortium, bound less by kinship than by the fact that only these forms can occupy this interval of cold, wet stone before the cleft widens and the conditions change. The smallest are no larger than a split grain of rice: translucent filaments that branch once or twice and then cling with microscopic pads, drawing what little moisture they need from the fleeting films left by each evaporating pool. They move only when the surface beneath them dries; then the whole filament contracts in a single slow ripple, dragging itself a few body-lengths before re-anchoring in the next damp seam.

Larger forms, finger-sized, form the living carpet proper. Each is a chain of eight to fourteen translucent segments, each segment carrying three pairs of soft, spatulate limbs whose undersides are fringed with cilia fine enough to catch the minutest droplets. Their bodies are not truly soft; beneath the thin cuticle lie pressurized hydrostatic chambers that let them flatten against the rock or swell into low domes when they drink. Coloration is almost absent—only a faint opalescence that thickens to milky blue when they are fully hydrated and fades to clear when they begin to desiccate. In the half-light their overlapping bodies turn the wall into a single slow tide of paler and darker patches as fluids shift between neighbors through temporary adhesions.

Feeding is communal and nearly invisible. When a condensation thread finally reaches a mat, the nearest individuals cease all motion for several minutes. Their dorsal surfaces bead with microscopic pores; the thread is drawn in, passed laterally through a chain of living bodies, and stored as a dilute gel between cuticle and inner membrane. Hours later the same fluid is metered out again, one segment at a time, to neighbors whose reserves are lower. The sound of this exchange is the low, constant scraping: each microscopic redistribution tugs the limbs against the stone and against one another, a wet, many-voiced hush that never quite stops.

Every few hours a deeper rearrangement occurs. A single segment will loosen its grip, swing outward on the remaining limbs, and drift a short distance downward or sideways before the cilia find purchase again. When one loses hold entirely it falls only a body-length or two before three or four limbs reattach; the brief tumble produces the sharper clicks the cleft amplifies. These small accidents are the only visible activity, yet over days they produce a steady downward creep of the entire mat, so that the upper reaches of the wall are always slightly sparser than the lower reaches.

Reproduction is slower still. When two compatible segments remain pressed together for many hours, a narrow bridge of tissue forms between them. The exchange is not eggs or gametes but a small packet of undifferentiated cells that migrates into the receiving body and later emerges as a new, minute filament on the donor’s flank. The filament drifts or crawls until it finds an unoccupied square of rock no larger than a thumbnail; there it anchors and begins to thicken. Most die before they reach finger-size. The ones that survive do so simply by remaining in place while the mat above and below them thickens and shields them from sudden drying.

Temperature governs everything. The cold keeps the cuticle pliable and prevents rapid evaporation; any warmth that reaches the cleft from outside causes the mats to contract into dense, nearly motionless knots that lose the faintest blue tint and turn almost glassy. In that state they can wait days or weeks. When the cold returns they loosen again, limbs unfolding one by one, until the wall once more appears to breathe.

Nothing in the cleft has yet learned to move quickly. Speed would waste water. The only urgency lies in the slow, patient covering of every square of stone before the mountain’s first true winter or the next fracture alters the light and airflow forever.
