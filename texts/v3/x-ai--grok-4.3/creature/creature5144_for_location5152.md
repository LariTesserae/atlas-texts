Within the chamber’s slow machinery, life has not merely arrived—it has been folded into the existing rhythms until the distinction between artifact and organism blurs.  

The vines do not simply drape; they read the metal. Certain flowering creepers extend fine rootlets into the seams where expansion joints weep oil, metabolizing trace hydrocarbons into waxy coatings that slow further corrosion. Where a linkage still moves, the vines thicken asymmetrically, their growth adding just enough drag that the mechanism completes its arc minutes later than it did a century before—an imperceptible adjustment the engines accept without complaint. Mosses fill the negative spaces between rivets, their mats calibrated to the vibration frequencies of each surface; on flywheel housings they grow in radial spokes that damp resonance, while on vertical pillars they form vertical stripes that channel condensation downward into root zones rather than letting it sheet across bearings.  

Among the foliage move creatures whose physiologies mirror the scale of their home. The plate-winged moths are not pollinators in any ordinary sense; their pale scales are translucent enough to pass the quartz-lens light straight through, so that when a shaft of daylight strikes a cluster of them they briefly become a second, temporary grid of lenses high in the vault. At night they descend in loose spirals, wings brushing the mesh to dislodge droplets that fall, metronomically, onto the trays where moss waits. The metallic beetles, by contrast, never leave the pipework. Their elytra are formed from successive layers of compacted scale and sintered lubricant residue; each generation adds a lamina until older shells become rigid enough to serve as supplementary brackets where housings have begun to warp. They tunnel through thick accumulations of micro-algae that bloom wherever warmth and moisture meet, their excavations keeping airflow channels open inside the slower-turning gearboxes.  

Lizards the color of oxidized copper patrol the catwalks. Heat-sensing pits along their jaws map the infrared gradients of every pipe they cross; they rest for hours on a single valve housing until its temperature curve tells them a drip will form at the low point, then shift position to drink without ever leaving the metal. Their eggs are deposited inside empty bolt holes, the shells thin enough that the steady low-frequency pressure waves from the engines rock them through incubation.  

Far rarer are the things that have learned to treat the empty volumes themselves as territory. Occasionally a condensation eddy will remain coherent long enough for a pale, membrane-winged form—larger than the moths, smaller than a person—to glide between machine-islands without beating its wings. It feeds on the moths when their translucent clusters become dense enough to cast shadow, then vanishes again into the upper darkness where the light shafts do not reach. Its roosts are the half-dissolved flanges on the oldest pillars; the stalactites of slag have grown around its accumulated guano until the pillar tips resemble the roots of some inverted forest.  

Nothing here reproduces quickly. A new pocket of standing water may take decades to form; a vine may require a century to jam a linkage; a beetle shell may take generations to become structural. Yet each of these events is registered and absorbed. The engines continue their measured revolutions, the condensation continues its deliberate fall, and the chamber remains a single, ancient, turning skin whose inhabitants are no longer guests but slow, steady modifications of its own ongoing design.
