In the ceaseless wet, the valley’s life does not so much inhabit the place as continue it. The largest forms are not the amphibians that drift like slow shadows between the pools but the half-visible congregations that gather around the crystalline spars. They move in loose, shifting clusters rather than fixed bodies, each individual a membrane of translucent tissue stretched over a lattice of fine mineral spines. When the spars emit their sub-audible tone the membranes tighten and loosen in overlapping rhythms, translating the vibration into slow peristaltic motion that keeps the surrounding water in constant micro-currents. These currents in turn carry dislodged spores and fragments of the fast-repairing plants straight to their surfaces, where the tissue absorbs them without visible mouths. The membranes themselves glisten with the same condensate that beads on every leaf; they appear almost interchangeable with the vegetation until one drifts close enough to see the faint lattice of darker threads running beneath the surface like buried wiring.

Smaller, quicker life threads through the undersides of the fronds and the fracture planes of the fresh rock. These take the form of dense, many-limbed scribbles no larger than a hand, their exoskeletons formed from the same matte-black material as the half-buried domes. They do not eat in any ordinary sense; instead they rasp away microscopic layers of the artificial surfaces and carry the particles back to the domes, pressing them into hairline seams that self-seal within minutes. Their movements produce the high, scratchy component of the constant sound, yet they also pause whenever the larger membrane clusters pass overhead, flattening themselves against stone or leaf so that only the faintest tremor betrays them. In return the membranes release thin filaments that brush across the rock and leave behind moisture rich with dissolved minerals, which the smaller creatures collect and redistribute.

Between these scales move solitary, elongated shapes whose skins are neither fully animal nor fully fluid. They travel just beneath the surface of the seeping sheets that race down the basalt, reshaping their outlines to match the narrow glittering channels the water cuts. When rain arrives in sudden sheets they widen into broad, flat sheets of tissue that ride the surge, then contract again into threads once the flood recedes. Their only visible organs are paired ridges along their lengths that glow a dull cobalt whenever lightning flickers through the half-light, as though they are reading the charge in the air and storing it for later redistribution among the spars.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual way. New individuals emerge already part-grown from pale blisters that form on the undersides of the largest fronds after each storm; the blisters split and the new forms slip into the nearest pool or channel already moving with the same patient, adaptive rhythm as their predecessors. The artificial remnants do not command this process so much as keep its tempo regular: the low domes emit occasional thin pulses of warmth that prevent the membranes from freezing during the coldest damp spells, while the spars adjust their thrum in response to sudden changes in water pressure, damping floods just enough that the channels do not erase themselves entirely.

Over time the impression is of a single, distributed organism whose separate expressions—membrane, scribble, thread—have agreed to remain visibly distinct only so long as the valley’s turbulence continues. When the rain pauses and visibility clears for a few minutes, the entire assembly slows its motions in unison, as if the landscape itself is drawing breath before the next downpour rearranges every surface once more.
