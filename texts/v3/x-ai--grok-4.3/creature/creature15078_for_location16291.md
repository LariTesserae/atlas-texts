In the fissures and shallow pockets where the steam lingers longest, the cushion plants have learned a kind of patient arithmetic. Each rosette of coin-sized leaves is spaced so that the next receives only the fraction of shadow it can endure; roots thread between flakes of basalt and limestone, tasting both minerals at once. Where the rock has cracked widest, the plants thicken into low domes that trap a warmer, damper micro-air; dew that forms at dawn hesitates a little longer inside these domes before the sun lifts it away.

The rust-colored lizards move through this geometry like shuttles. Their scales match the iron-stained stone so closely that they vanish until a tremor travels through the ground and they flick sideways into a pipe mouth or beneath a turbine skirt. They do not bask so much as press themselves flat against surfaces that have not yet cooled; when the vents exhale, they shift a body length and wait for the mineral film to dry. Their tongues flick at the same droplets the cushion leaves release, and the thinnest lizards carry pale salt rings at the corners of their mouths.

Beetles the size of thumbnails patrol the edges of these meetings. Their shells are matte and slightly pitted, as though sand-polished; when they cross metal they produce a brief, dry click that the wind immediately erases. They drink at the meniscus where condensation meets dust, then follow the same crack until it leads them under a half-buried blade or into the cool throat of an old conduit. There they sometimes remain for hours, motionless, until a change in pressure or temperature moves them again. Hares appear rarer, mostly at the hour when twilight and residual heat overlap. Their ears turn constantly toward the low-frequency hum of the turbines; a single quarter-turn of a blade is enough to send one gliding between two concrete pads and out of sight. Their droppings collect in the lee of the same machines, small dark pellets that slowly darken the pale limestone.

Nothing here multiplies in great numbers. Each generation seems content to occupy the precise margin its parents discovered: a lizard under the same pipe lip, a clutch of beetle larvae inside the same pocket of warmer gravel, a cushion plant expanding only where the previous plant’s decay has softened the rock. The machines participate without purpose; their slow ticks and faint vibrations mark time the animals already know, and the animals’ small scuttlings and drinking keep dust from settling too thickly around the vents. Over many quiet repetitions the distinction between what was built and what arrived afterward has worn away, leaving only surfaces that accept feet, roots, and condensation in the same measured way.
