The basin sustains a sparse but persistent ecology of lithic organisms that have learned to move with the grain of its ongoing formation. Most numerous are the hand-sized translucents already noted: flattened, many-limbed creatures whose bodies are little more than flexible sheets of precipitated mineral laced with fine fluid channels. They glide along the seams between plates, pressing their ventral surfaces into the grooves so that capillary action draws condensation directly into their tissues. When two meet they pause, edges overlapping, and exchange thin threads of dissolved salts; this brief fusion allows them to trade microscopic crystal seeds that will later nucleate new growth along whichever joint they next occupy. Their passage is almost silent except for the faint tick of a plate edge reseating after their weight has passed.

Larger and far rarer are elongated forms, sometimes several paces in length, that inhabit the hollow spaces beneath the ramps and half-built towers. These creatures are not so much animals as mobile scaffolds: ridged carapaces of the same jointed mineral that composes the floor, segmented so they can telescope or fold to fit the narrow interstices. Their method of travel is to lock successive segments into the unfinished wall courses above them, then contract, levering the whole body forward by the width of a chisel mark. In doing so they inadvertently true the stonework, their own weight and grip finishing bevels that tools began. When they settle to rest, their dorsal surfaces flush perfectly with the surrounding plates, becoming indistinguishable from the basin itself until the next slow contraction begins.

Still deeper and only occasionally betrayed by vibration are diffuse colonial masses that occupy the reservoirs themselves. These are dark, granular suspensions rather than discrete bodies—dense clouds of microscopic mineral particles suspended in the collected water. The particles respond collectively to changes in pressure or chemistry; when a new drip strikes the surface they contract or expand in coordinated pulses that send faint ripples outward, guiding the small translucents toward fresher seams or drawing dissolved material toward sites where new plate growth is required. Their presence is felt mainly as a change in the acoustic decay of falling water: the usual ringing strike lengthens or shortens depending on whether the reservoir mass has risen or thinned.

At the farthest edges of the basin, where new excavation continues, even more obscure activity occurs. Occasional low-frequency tremors travel through the plates—too regular to be mere settling—suggesting massive, soft-bodied entities working still deeper, extruding fresh mineral slurry that hardens into the next course of floor. These entities never surface; they leave only the matte residue and the slow, deliberate lowering of stone courses described earlier. Their relationship to the smaller creatures is one of succession rather than predation: when a translucent dies its body dissolves into the grooves and is ultimately taken up by the reservoir colonies, which in turn contribute material to the deeper builders. In this way the basin maintains its own slow, unfinished growth through layered generations of tenants, none of which ever fully depart from the mineral they inhabit.
