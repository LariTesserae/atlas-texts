The shelf belongs first to the green. What lives here has surrendered height and grandeur in exchange for grip. The mosses and cushion plants form a single, living pelt stitched across every level surface—thick, springy, and slow. Each clump is its own miniature watershed: its outer leaves trap mist until the drops slide inward toward darker, almost turquoise hearts where water is hoarded. Tiny white flowers the size of pinheads open only when the wind stills, releasing a faint, resinous scent that the updrafts shred almost at once. The vines that dangle from the overhang are older than they look; their pale, hair-like roots taste the air for nitrogen the rock cannot give, and they thicken or wither according to how much bird droppings the wind delivers each season.

Among the plants move the translucent lizards. Their skins are so thin that the dark pulse of organs shows when they pause in a shaft of light. They do not bask so much as press themselves flat against the warmest stones where seepage has warmed slightly from long contact. They hunt the mites and springtails that thrive in the damp understory of the moss, and they vanish into the smallest fissures the instant a shadow crosses them. Their eggs are laid in clutches no larger than a fingernail, glued inside hairline cracks that the constant moisture keeps from drying.

The shaggy herbivores—low, compact, with coats the color of wet stone—graze in rotating patterns so that no single cushion is stripped. They move with a rocking gait that keeps their bellies close to the rock, and their broad, split hooves splay to distribute weight across the living mat. When they rest they fold themselves into depressions already worn by generations before them, becoming nearly indistinguishable from the hummocks they have just eaten. Their dung, dark and fibrous, is quickly claimed by the moss, which grows noticeably faster and bluer wherever it falls.

Above and around them, the air itself is tenanted. Small falcons and fork-tailed flyers use the constant updraft to hang motionless for long minutes, only their heads turning as they watch for lizards foolish enough to cross open stone. Larger, rarer shapes—broad-winged and silent—sometimes wheel so high they are only suggestions against the mist ceiling; they drop suddenly when an unwary grazer lingers too far from the wall, then climb again on the next thermal before the wind can pin them against the rock.

Nothing here is loud for long. The lizards’ claws scratch once and are gone. The herbivores’ teeth make a soft, tearing rhythm that the dripping water quickly swallows. Even the wind, when it funnels through the hollow below, arrives already freighted with distant rockfalls and the higher, thinner cries of creatures that never land on the shelf at all, only ride its margins. What remains is a community shaped entirely by scarcity of space and abundance of water: each life holds its ground by staying low, staying damp, and moving only when the next pocket of green or the next seam of shadow opens.
