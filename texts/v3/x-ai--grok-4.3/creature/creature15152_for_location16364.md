In the thin, perpetual film that sheaths every curve of glass and silt, a quiet census of lives continues, each slowed to the rhythm of the falling drops.  

Microscopic threads, neither plant nor animal, extend through the pale sediment like faint capillaries. They draw energy not from light but from the slow dissolution of the volcanic glass itself, coaxing iron and silica into solution and then re-precipitating them as the nacreous rosettes already visible inside the rock. Their colonies are dense enough to tint the shallow pans with the faintest opalescence, yet sparse enough that no single droplet is ever fully claimed. When a new drop strikes, the shock travels through the film and momentarily accelerates their metabolism; the next long interval between drops allows them to return to dormancy, conserving whatever scant chemistry they have harvested.

A step above these films, in the meniscus where water meets air, drift clusters of even smaller, motile specks—barely more than membranes enclosing mineral grains. They migrate along the gradients of temperature and dissolved ions created by each new arrival of seepage, tracing routes so minute that the surface of a pan appears perfectly still. Over decades their paths etch hairline channels into the silt, channels that later fill and erase themselves, so the pans remain as featureless as they were when the last hammerstone was set down.

Larger still, and rarer, are the translucent slugs that press themselves into the undersides of the smallest ledges. Their bodies are mostly water held within a cuticle thin enough to transmit the dull sheen of the glass beneath them. They move only when condensation thickens to a visible bead; then they extend a single, deliberate fold of tissue, gather the bead, and retract. Weeks may pass before the next movement. Their waste is a microscopic dusting of re-crystallized salts that later dissolves back into the pans, closing the loop.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or visibly. Instead, presence is maintained by endurance: individuals that may be centuries old continue their infinitesimal work, replacing cells only when the chemistry of a fresh drop allows. The stone blade and the mortar serve no current purpose except as additional surfaces on which the same films gather; the rounded hammerstones have become low islands whose crowns remain slightly drier and therefore host fewer of the drifting specks.  

Over all of these, the air holds a last, almost imperceptible tenant: a suspension of dormant spores and desiccated fragments small enough to ride the faintest convection currents that arise when a droplet strikes and briefly warms its immediate surroundings. Most never germinate; they simply wait, cycling from ledge to pan and back again with the metronomic patience of the water itself.
