They are a spare, deliberate people, shaped as much by the rock as they shape it. Their skin has taken on the cool pallor of limestone newly freed from the dark—neither sickly nor ghostly, but simply untouched by sun, as though light itself has never been invited into the bargain of their skins. Eyes are wide and patient, lids thinned from generations of squinting at reed lamps and the faint glow of moisture reflecting what light there is. Their hands are broad and callused in unusual places: the inner pads of the thumbs and the outer knuckles, from the steady pressure of bone wedges and the small, rotating chisels they favor.

Children move through the unfinished galleries with the unthinking grace of those who have never known ceilings high enough to run beneath. They are small for their age, limbs long and sinewy, and they play a game that involves pressing their ears to the living stone to guess where the next team of diggers will break through. Their laughter is brief and quickly swallowed by the damp, unfinished walls. When a new threshold opens, they are the first to slip inside, feet silent on the wet film, claiming the space with chalk marks only they can read.

Adults work in small crews that share the same surnames and the same exhaustion. They speak little while cutting, letting the rhythm of tool against stone stand in for conversation. Between shifts they gather in the wider chambers, cooking over shallow depressions where dried lichen and root fibers smolder with almost no smoke. Meals are simple—thin stews of the translucent roots, dried fish carried down from the surface long ago, and a bitter tea made from the condensation itself, which they claim tastes of the mountain’s memory. Men and women wear the same loose garments woven from pale, almost colorless fibers that wick moisture away from the body; the cloth never quite dries here, so they accept a permanent faint chill as the cost of living inside the living rock.

There is no clear leader, only those who have cut the farthest or stayed longest with a particular crew. When new chambers open, these experienced diggers walk the fresh walls slowly, palms flat against the stone, deciding where soil pockets might be left for roots and where pillars must remain. Decisions are made by the number of hands that rest on a place at once. Debate is conducted in silence, resolved when enough shoulders turn away from a proposal.

The oldest among them are nearly blind from years of close work in bad light, yet they remain valued for their knowledge of which fissures carry water and which will eventually close under pressure. They sit near the condensation basins, fingers tracing old scars in the floor where earlier channels were tried and abandoned. Sometimes they reach up to touch the pale roots that hang like forgotten hair, murmuring to them as if the threads might answer.

At night—if night can be said to exist when the only light is reed lamps—the settlement contracts. Families roll out their reed mats against the still-warm rock where the day’s chiseling has heated the stone. Bodies press close, not from affection alone but from the need to share warmth against the constant migration of moisture. Sleep comes easily to those who have spent the day removing absence, turning it into walls and thresholds. The sound that finally lulls them is the irregular tap of the night crew, still working farther along the ridge, carving one more careful room into the undecided stone.
