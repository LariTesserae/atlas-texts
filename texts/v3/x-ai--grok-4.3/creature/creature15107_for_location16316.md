In the slow intervals between the kelp’s own faint illuminations, what truly lives here moves on the same timescale as the oxide and the silt.  

There are no schools, no sudden shoals. Instead, single translucent organisms—neither quite worm nor fish—drift lengthwise through the continuous carpet, their bodies flattened and elongated until they can slip between the fused turbine blades without brushing a single frond. Each is a living filament, pale as the threads of silt, marked only by a faint internal thread of deeper green where symbiotic algae travel with them. They do not swim so much as let the minute pressure changes of the kelp’s breathing carry them from one doorway to the next. When they pause, they hook themselves with hair-thin cirri into the velvet nap of the vegetation and remain still for hours, respiring so shallowly that the surrounding fronds barely stir.

Deeper in the structure, where the sodium lamps have failed entirely, larger forms rest against the riveted walls. These are broad, disc-shaped creatures whose margins have grown into the kelp itself; their edges are indistinguishable from the living tissue until they choose to flex. Over decades they have absorbed flakes of corroded iron into their outer layers, so that each slow pulse of their bodies draws a faint metallic sheen across the green. They feed only on the constant rain of silt and the occasional gas bubble rising from the stalks; the motion of their feeding is visible only as a deeper dimple that travels across the “wall” and disappears again. Between feedings they are indistinguishable from the upholstery of the room.

The smallest inhabitants are almost not animals at all. Within the hollows of the larger stalks live clusters of microscopic, armored things whose shells are built from the same layered oxide that once armored the beams. They move by extending single crystalline spines that catch the next slow current, then contract, pulling their bodies a few cell-widths forward. In the rare moments when a sodium cone still reaches them, their spines catch the ochre light like minute sewing needles. To a human observer they would register only as a fine, shifting glitter in the water; in truth they have already begun to colonize the next rivet head, extending the continuous tissue one grain further into the metal.

Occasionally, after many cycles of breathing and settling, one of the large disc-shaped residents detaches a fragment of itself. The fragment drifts, weightless, until it finds an empty seam between two kelp sheets. There it settles, flattens, and begins the same long work of absorption and patience. Over years the fragment becomes another breathing patch of wall. Nothing here arrives or departs. Everything simply lengthens, layer by layer, until the distinction between what was once built and what has always grown is lost inside the same slow inhalation.
