In this place, life does not arrive so much as it is drawn out of the stone and the machines, bead by bead. The pale reeds are the first and most patient inhabitants. Each stalk is not a single plant but a slow colony of jointed segments, each joint housing a thread of translucent fluid that moves downward only when a drop from above strikes the spine-leaves and sends a minute tremor through the whole length. Over hours the fluid thickens into a pale gum that seals the cracks where the reed has forced its way between rock crystals; when the gum dries it flakes into a fine, glittering dust that the rising vapor immediately re-moistens. The reeds therefore drink twice—once from the air and once from their own cast-off skin.

Below the crust, the gearboxes of the buried machines shelter flatter, darker organisms that never surface. These are thin sheets of mineral-hardened tissue, no thicker than a fingernail, pressed between iron plates and ceramic insulators. They feed on the microscopic flecks of oxide that flake from the turning paddle shafts, absorbing them through pores that open only during the long pauses between rotations. Their motion is entirely inward: a slow peristaltic ripple that carries the metal dust toward a central knot where it is converted, over days, into a slightly warmer, slightly softer ceramic node. The nodes enlarge by fractions of a millimeter each year and eventually crack the housings from within, exposing new iron to the weeping walls so that the cycle of rust and condensation can continue.

The rust-colored film itself is not inert. At intervals too long to notice in a single visit, the surface tension breaks in one small place and a cluster of copper-bright motes rises through the film, each mote a momentary aggregation of vapor, heat, and dissolved iron. The motes drift upward a few centimeters, catch the light from the slit of sky, and fall again, leaving behind a hair-thin ridge of darker oxide. In time these ridges intersect and form a faint network whose geometry is dictated by the spacing of the machines beneath; along those intersections the reeds are slightly more likely to germinate.

Nothing here moves quickly enough to be called an animal, yet nothing is still. The entire declivity breathes through the staggered rhythms of weeping stone, turning paddle, and condensing vapor, and the organisms that inhabit it are expressions of those rhythms rather than interruptions of them. They thicken or thin according to the same imperceptible rise and fall of the bottom film; when the film is at its highest they grow, when it sinks they contract into smaller, harder forms that can wait through drier intervals measured in seasons or decades. Over centuries the walls will soften and the machines will be buried deeper, but the same exchange—stone to water, water to machine, machine to reed—will simply narrow its channel and continue.
