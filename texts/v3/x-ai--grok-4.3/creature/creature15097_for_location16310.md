The inhabitants are not settlers in any ordinary sense; they have become the chamber’s own slow metabolism. The fish that drift in loose, gray shoals are thick-bodied and short-finned, their flanks armored by overlapping plates of translucent scale that once flashed silver but now carry a dull nacreous bloom from constant contact with iron. They move less like schools and more like a single distributed lung, inhaling at the clogged western filters, exhaling toward the eastern risers, their passage stirring silt that the currents immediately reclaim. When one pauses to scrape its lips against a rivet head, the others adjust without breaking the pattern, so that the shoal itself seems to breathe with the pumps.

Lower, against the floor grates, the rays and eels have grown interchangeable. The rays are nearly rectangular, their pectoral edges squared by long abrasion against steel mesh; they lie motionless for hours, only the faint ripple of gill slits betraying life, until a valve opens and they rise in a single, heavy flap that sends a pressure wave through the water. The eels, by contrast, have shortened and thickened, their bodies bunched into muscular knots that fit exactly into the mouths of abandoned pipes; they no longer hunt so much as wait, mouths open, for whatever the intake current delivers. Both leave faint trails of mucus that the crustaceans harvest within minutes.

The crustaceans themselves are the chamber’s true steady state. They form a living felt across every joint and flange—pale, almost translucent bodies no larger than thumbnails, each anchored by a single thread of hardened secretion that matches the color of the bitumen sealant beneath it. Where the lamps are warm they darken to a bruised purple; elsewhere they remain the same dull iron-gray as the walls. Their filtering limbs beat in synchrony with the pumps, a faint, continuous whisking that is audible only as an increase in the water’s texture. Over centuries they have learned the rhythm of the valves: when a gate seats, they swarm briefly into the momentary slack water to feed, then retreat before the next pulse.

The algae film is thinnest where the water moves fastest and thickest in the narrow convective cells above each lamp. There it rises in delicate, glass-like stalks that bend and straighten with every temperature shift, releasing microscopic spores that spiral upward like slow snow. These spores catch in the gills of the fish and are later deposited, still viable, on the upper surfaces of the riser pipes, where they found new colonies. In this way the algae travels the same circuit the pumps enforce, completing a loop that requires neither root nor seed.

Nothing here reproduces in the urgent, visible way of surface life. The eggs of the fish are glued in tight orange clusters inside emptied turbine housings where the current is weakest; the eggs of the rays are leathery purses wedged between catwalk struts. Both hatch over years rather than weeks, the young emerging already the size and color of the architecture around them. When one finally dies, its body settles onto a grate and is dismantled joint by joint; within days the bones have been replaced by a new layer of crustaceans, so that the skeleton itself becomes another strut in the permanent, half-mechanical reef.

The only sound that belongs solely to the living is the soft, continuous rasp of mouthparts against metal and, very rarely, the long, low vibration of a ray grinding its dental plates together—an old, patient signal that travels through the water like the memory of a door closing somewhere far above.
