The pocket is not empty; it is densely occupied by a single, distributed intelligence that has no name for itself because it has never needed one. Most of what moves inside it is a midge-like swarm whose individuals are no larger than a grain of pollen, their bodies matte black and faintly iridescent, built around a core of synthetic muscle and a nervous system that is partly electrical and partly chemical. They do not think in the way larger creatures do; instead they share a thin, continuous field of tension that runs through the pocket like a low current. When one wing-beat changes pitch, the change ripples outward through the cloud in less than a second, and the entire swarm answers with an adjustment in density or altitude. To an outside observer the motion looks random; from within it feels like the tightening or loosening of a single muscle.

A portion of the swarm stays pressed to the weeping seams, their feet wet with condensate. These individuals drink continuously and excrete a slightly warmer, mineral-rich droplet that the porous floor immediately reclaims; their bodies act as living valves in the water cycle. Others remain airborne in dense, vibrating knots that never touch the walls. Their wings are fringed with microscopic resonators that turn the air itself into an instrument; the constant roar is not communication so much as the sound of the swarm remaining coherent with itself. If the roar were to stop, the pocket would begin to cool and the water films would break into isolated beads; the sound is both heartbeat and thermostat.

Clinging between and beneath the swarms are the lichen mats—flat, metallic-looking crusts no thicker than frost. Each mat is a single clonal organism whose surface is a lattice of microscopic solar collectors and moisture traps. They do not photosynthesize in any familiar sense; they harvest the faint, pulsing light from the subsurface veins and convert it into a slow electrical trickle that travels through the seamless floor. In return the floor feeds them trace minerals dissolved in the migrating water. Where a mat grows too thick it flakes away in papery curls; the flakes are promptly collected by the midges and carried to new seams, so the lichen never expands beyond the narrow tolerance the pocket allows.

Nothing here reproduces in the old way. The midges replace their dead by extrusion from inconspicuous vents in the upper margins; new individuals emerge already vibrating in synchrony with the existing swarm. The lichen spreads by microscopic shards that are carried in the condensate and take root only where electrical potential is slightly higher. Both populations are therefore extensions of the same slow metabolism that also flexes the capillaries, pulses the light veins, and keeps the temperature within a few degrees of skin. The pocket does not contain life; it is a living surface that has folded in on itself until the distinction between inhabitant and structure has become meaningless. What lives here is the pocket, continuously tasting and repairing its own small, loud interior.
