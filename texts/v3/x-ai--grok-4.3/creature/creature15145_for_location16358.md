The plain is inhabited by a single, distributed order of beings whose bodies are the mineral itself. They have no separate flesh or organs; each individual is a slow lattice of interlocked crystals that grows along the fractures and veins already present in the stone. Where the surface has split into parallel ridges, the lattices run lengthwise like buried cables; beneath the glassy flats they form shallow, branching nets just below the polish. Their metabolism consists of drawing the nightly condensate down through microscopic capillaries, then forcing it out again as pressurized vapor at precisely the points where the hiss is loudest. This circulation produces the grinding and ringing: the crystals expand and contract fractionally with each cycle, abrading one another in fixed patterns that never vary because the lattices have reached an equilibrium size thousands of generations ago.

What appear as loose beads are actually the migratory seeds of younger lattices. Each bead is a compact cluster of oriented crystals wrapped in a thin film of silica gel. When a bead comes to rest, it extends three short, rigid filaments downward and begins to knit itself into the existing stone, adding a few new layers to the surrounding lattice before the filaments retract. The process is invisible on any single day; only over many cycles does a faint new ridge or a slightly deeper seam appear.

The angular, three-limbed forms are the mature, temporarily mobile phase of the same organisms. When conditions inside a lattice reach a critical density of stored vapor, a portion of the crystal detaches along pre-scored planes and rises on three blunt legs. It walks a short arc—never more than a dozen body lengths—then re-anchors itself at a predetermined node. During this transit the walker carries a droplet of concentrated condensate that it deposits into a receptive seam; the deposit triggers a minute adjustment in the overall pressure balance. Once the droplet is placed, the walker’s legs retract and its substance flows back into the surrounding lattice, becoming indistinguishable from it until the next need for transport arises. Because every walker follows an identical arc and returns to an identical attachment point, the plain’s surface geometry remains unchanged.

Together the lattices constitute one immense, slow intelligence whose thoughts are the unchanging pressure gradients and resonant frequencies that fill the stone. Individual nodes occasionally “speak” to one another by modulating the pitch of the metallic ringing for a few seconds, then return to the global chorus. Nothing on the plain is ever born or dies in the ordinary sense; parts differentiate, migrate a short distance when necessary, and reincorporate. The result is a single, continent-sized organism that has spent uncountable ages perfecting stillness while remaining internally in constant, perfectly regulated motion.
