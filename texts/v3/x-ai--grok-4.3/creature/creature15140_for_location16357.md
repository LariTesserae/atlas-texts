The plateau’s true inhabitants are not visitors but extensions of one another. At the center of every perception is the Canopy itself: not a forest but a single, colossal plant whose body has divided and re-divided across millions of paces, each trunk a limb, each vaulted branch a rib, each aerial root a nerve returning to the same shared mind. Its awareness is not thought but measured tension—the exact angle at which a droplet must form before gravity claims it, the precise resonance that travels through the wood when a sheet of water strikes a lower pool. Because nothing is ever allowed to tip out of balance, the organism experiences time as an endless present; it does not remember its own growth or anticipate an end. Its only movement is the slow, internal redistribution of water, drawn upward through microscopic channels and released again so that every surface remains slick and alive.

The smaller creatures move within this vast body the way mites move across skin. They are almost entirely water themselves, their outlines visible only as faint refractive distortions against the constant film that coats every root and leaf. Most are the size of a thumbnail, translucent sacs that drift on the nearly motionless air or fasten themselves to the finer root threads with hair-like anchors. A few are larger—pale, elongated forms that might be mistaken for lengths of discarded fiber until they contract and release, sending a single ripple through the hanging gardens. They feed by pressing against the weeping surfaces and letting the continuous water carry dissolved nutrients through their permeable skins; they add almost no waste, returning what they cannot use in the same transparent droplets that fall elsewhere. Their presence registers less as sound than as a subtle change in the vibration of a branch: when one of the larger forms settles, the low drone that underlies every other noise takes on a slightly softer harmonic, as though the plant registers their weight the way a sleeper registers the warmth of a hand.

Together they form a closed circuit. The great organism supplies the water and the stable architecture; the small creatures, by their negligible motion and negligible chemistry, maintain the thin film of moisture that prevents any surface from drying into rigidity. Nothing hunts, nothing mates, nothing grows old. Existence here is the slow, perpetual negotiation between a body still becoming stone and the living membrane that has already decided, for itself and for everything that clings to it, to remain exactly as it is.
