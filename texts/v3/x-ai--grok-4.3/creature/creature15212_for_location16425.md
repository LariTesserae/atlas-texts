In the dense layering of the plateau, life moves as a slow, interlocking exchange rather than as separate creatures passing through. Small lizards with skin the color of wet bark press themselves flat against the undersides of fern fronds, their throats pulsing in time with the drip from above; they emerge only when a shaft of daylight warms a patch of stone long enough to draw out the pale moths that rest there. These moths beat their wings in short, silent bursts, scattering fine scales that drift downward and settle on the shallow pools, where they dissolve into the mineral film already forming at the water’s edge.

Larger forms keep to the deeper lanes between the broad-leafed trees. Heavy-bodied mammals, their pelts slick and almost hairless, wade slowly through the ferns, each step parting the stalks and releasing a fresh breath of crushed chlorophyll and warm earth. They pause at the steaming vents, lowering their muzzles to drink from the simmering overflow channels; the heat does not deter them but seems instead to steady their breathing, as if the continuous warmth matches some internal rhythm. When they move on they leave shallow prints that fill at once with condensate, turning briefly into tiny mirrors before the next fern frond folds over them.

Birds do not call across open distances here. Instead, low, throaty croaks pass between hidden perches in the vine canopy, answered by the soft click of armored insects shifting along bark. The insects themselves carry small gardens of moss and algae on their carapaces, miniature ecosystems that darken and lighten as the creatures move from sun shafts into shadow. In the pools, translucent salamanders with external gills trail through the warmer layers, occasionally breaking the surface with a single blunt head before sinking again; their movements stir fine threads of bacterial film that drift outward and attach to the cracked stone plates.

Even the vegetation behaves as though it is continuously adjusting its own occupation of space. Vines thicken where steam rises, their leaves turning slightly toward the constant moisture. Ferns at the margins of the vents grow more upright, their tips yellowed by the heat yet still extending new croziers each day. The tallest trees, their trunks perpetually glazed, support colonies of epiphytes whose roots hang in tangled curtains, catching droplets before they reach the ground. These hanging gardens shift and drip in their own slow cycles, releasing minute clouds of spores that catch in the drifting mist and travel only a few body-lengths before settling again.

Nothing here announces arrival or departure. The animals, plants, and the thin veils of microbes that coat every damp surface simply thicken or thin according to the same measured pace that governs the rock itself—dissolving, re-forming, and exchanging heat and water in an unbroken continuum.
