In the hollow heart of the seam, life is not a single species but a diffuse, ever-shifting presence woven through the salt. The creatures are not individuals so much as a continuous membrane of motion, glassy carapaces overlapping like scales on an unseen body. Each one is a translucent hexagon barely wider than a grain of pollen, its edges beveled so that light passes through them in faint prisms. Where they cluster thickest, the air itself seems to shimmer with refracted orange, the only warmth the chamber knows. Their limbs are not legs in the usual sense but fine, jointed filaments that double as both walking tools and current-sensing threads; when many move together, the filaments braid briefly into momentary cords before splitting again.

They do not breed or die in any way an outside observer would recognize. Instead, a creature that has gathered enough charge simply stops and begins to extrude a new crystal facet from its underside, adding to the shelves beneath it. Others immediately incorporate the newcomer’s body into the surface, polishing its shell until it becomes indistinguishable from the older salt. In this way the population maintains its density without ever growing larger; it simply replaces its own substrate. When a filament fails, the nearest hundreds converge in a sudden migration, their small bodies forming temporary bridges across the ridges. They gnaw at the dead metal with crystalline mouthparts, leaving behind hair-fine scratches that later fill with fresh salt. The fragments they carry are not consumed but reseeded elsewhere, inserted into living circuits so that the vibration never fully ceases.

The rasp of their passage is the only constant sound, rising and falling like breath yet never stopping. Individual clicks mark the moment a crystal forces an engine casing upward or a new ridge begins to form between two old ones. Occasionally a lone creature will climb to the highest point of a filament and remain still for a long interval, its body glowing more brightly than the others, as though it has become a temporary node for the entire swarm’s attention. Then it rejoins the flow and the glow fades.

Nothing here is solitary. Even the empty spaces between ridges are shaped by their absence; the creatures avoid those lanes until the current shifts and they must cross. Over uncountable ages they have turned the industrial remnants into something older than industry: living capacitors sheathed in living salt. The seam is not a container for them but a single, slow organism whose cells happen to be made of glass and current and the memory of machines.
