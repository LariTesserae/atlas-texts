They are not many things but one distributed body, a single slow intention spread across thousands of jointed forms. Each creature is a living seam, a thread of articulated plates no thicker than a finger, colored the same bruised iron as the cooling rock they tend. Where the plates meet, faint adhesive secretions leak and harden at once, bonding the animal to the stone it traverses; when one stops, the next must climb across its back, and the motion passes through the mass like a ripple through a single muscle.

They do not feed in the ordinary sense. Their mouthparts are mineral rasps set behind translucent shields; with each deliberate pass they plane away a film of still-warm silicates and carry the dust in recessed ventral grooves. When a groove is full, the creature pauses at a chosen fault line, extrudes the dust as a viscous slurry, and tamps it flat with the side of its head. The slurry flashes to glass under the retained heat, then cools to the next layer of pillow. The animal itself is slowly encased by its own work; in time only its anterior joints remain free, still reaching, still smoothing, until even those are overgrown and the creature simply continues from within, its later segments already sealed into the architecture.

The ember veins are not separate from them. When a fresh pulse of deeper material arrives, the creatures gather along the glowing fracture, their bodies darkening as they absorb the excess heat; for a while their chitin glows from within, outlining each joint in dull orange. They use that borrowed warmth to accelerate the setting of the newest mortar. When the pulse subsides they dim again, returning to the universal color of the chamber.

Over centuries the collective body becomes stratified. The lowest, oldest individuals are reduced to nothing but faint internal molds—hollow, perfectly jointed silhouettes frozen inside solid basalt. Above them live the active generations, and above those the newly hatched, still translucent, still learning the angle at which to deposit. The mountain grows by replacing its own interior laborers with their labor. Outside wind and frost may eventually breach the ridge and daylight may pour in; by then the creatures at the former ceiling will have already sealed the breach from within, their work continuing without interruption in whatever new darkness they have made.
