In the highlands, life has conceded its old boundaries and learned instead to nest inside the joints. What moves and persists is not a collection of separate creatures but a distributed tenancy: mosses and liverworts occupy every seam where water lingers, their tiny rhizoids prying at rivets and micro-fractures until the newest ferrocrete already carries the grain of older growth. Vines the color of worked copper do not climb so much as replace the function of the rails they cover; their stems thicken where a catwalk once needed reinforcement, and in places the original iron shows only as a darker thread running beneath living tissue.

The machines themselves function less as tools than as slow, repeating gestures. A gantry that rolls along its ridge for decades acquires a faint reciprocating rhythm—like breathing—that the crews no longer reset. When a section is lifted for replacement, the neighboring struts have already begun to lean toward the gap, taking new loads before welders arrive. The turbines below the decks turn without interruption, yet their bearings have been colonized by a thin film of algae that dampens vibration and changes the pitch of every bearing note; over generations the engineers learned to tune schedules around these living gaskets rather than scrape them away.

The few animals remain marginal and precise. Pale moths appear only where steam vents meet the underside of plating; their larvae feed on the oily residues that collect in the drip lines. Water-striders skate the permanent gutters between deck plates, their movements synchronized to the slow pulses of condensate that arrive from higher ridges. Nothing larger stays long. Any mammal that wanders in finds footing uncertain and forage already allotted—every edible surface already claimed by root or by the thin biofilm that keeps the drainage open.

The human presence is likewise absorbed. Maintenance crews move like seasonal sap, following pre-worn paths of replacement plate and salvaged strut. Their weld lines and bolt patterns repeat older templates until the distinction between original design and accumulated repair dissolves into a single texture of warm metal and darker rust. Over time the workers’ boots have pressed faint trails through the cushions of moss; those trails now guide condensation downslope more efficiently than the original drains, so later crews simply deepen them rather than pave them over.

Nothing here reproduces in the old sense. The entire armature—concrete, vine, turbine, moth—persists by incremental substitution. When a weather station on a peak sheds its outer panels to corrosion, the moss that has already colonized the inner lattice becomes the new outer surface, and sensors are bolted directly into the living cushion. The highlands therefore do not contain life so much as they perform it: an ongoing, self-dampening exchange of load, moisture, and replacement that has no center and no final form, only the ceaseless adjustment of one layer taking the weight of the next.
