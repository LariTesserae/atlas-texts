In this chamber, what lives is not a crowd of creatures but a single, slowwoven tenancy of pale plants that have learned to measure time in the spacing of drops. They rise in tight ranks from every ledge and crack, their blades narrow and translucent at the edges, thickening toward a dull, waxy center that holds the faint metallic light like a film of ice. Each leaf is veined with the same white filaments that hang downward; when a filament touches water it thickens slightly and darkens, then splits, sending two new threads onward. The plants do not compete so much as inherit. Where one blade has already claimed a fissure, the next grows exactly beside it, using the first as both windbreak and ladder. Over centuries their overlapping layers have become a living map of the chamber’s every micro-climate: cooler on the north-facing wall where condensation lingers longest, thinner where a mineral seam still warms the stone by a fraction of a degree.

Beneath the leaves, in the black silt that has sifted down through the same slow drips, the roots form a second, hidden layer. They do not ramify wildly; they follow the channels already etched into the curved panel and the rim of the shallow bowl, treating the old alloy as simply another kind of stone. The upright rod has been claimed most completely. Stems have wound around it in a slow spiral, each new shoot settling into the groove left by the one before, until the metal is no longer visible and the rod now serves only as an internal scaffold for living tissue. The plants do not consume the artifacts; they accept them, the way a river accepts a fallen log and continues flowing around it for generations.

No animal moves here, yet the space is not empty of agency. The vegetation itself performs the only actions the chamber permits: the patient re-direction of moisture, the incremental extension of filament and blade, the quiet conversion of mineral trace and faint light into more of themselves. When a droplet finally releases, the fall is registered not as sound alone but as a minute redistribution of weight across dozens of waiting leaves. The plants lean, ever so slightly, toward that single point of impact; capillary films shift; a new path opens through the dense mat and continues downward. Then the waiting resumes.

The glow from the filaments in the stone remains constant because the plants have arranged themselves so that almost no new growth ever blocks it entirely. They need only the difference between shadow and lesser shadow. In this arrangement they persist, generation after generation, neither advancing nor retreating, content to deepen the stillness they already inhabit.
