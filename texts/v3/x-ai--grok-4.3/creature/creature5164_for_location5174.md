The plateau sustains three distinct orders of life, each bound so tightly to the alloy that it is impossible to say where fabrication ends and organism begins.

The lichen is the simplest and most ancient of these. Pale filaments no thicker than frost grow only where the exhaust manifolds exhale their brief, super-chilled mist. Over decades the mats thicken into translucent veils that quiver when the coolant surges; each tremor releases a faint spore cloud that drifts downward through the collection channels until it settles on another vent and begins again. The lichen has no roots. It simply adheres, drawing trace volatiles from the alloy’s own out-gassing. Where two mats meet they do not compete; they fuse into a single living seam that records, in microscopic growth rings, every thermal cycle the manifold has ever performed.

The crawlers are the plateau’s workforce and its memory. Thumb-sized, matte black, they travel in caravans whose routes are never the shortest distance between two points but rather the precise path of least resistance along residual stress lines in the metal. Their six legs never lift; instead they secrete and reabsorb a capillary film that both lubricates and samples the surface, tasting for micro-fractures or new crystalline growth. When a crawler encounters an anomalous groove—one of the impossible wear patterns that appear overnight—it stops, extrudes a diagnostic filament, and remains motionless for hours. Sometimes the filament is withdrawn and the caravan continues. Sometimes the crawler itself crystallizes, becoming a fixed datum the rest of the population consults by brushing past it. When released by the next thermal pulse, it rejoins the flow bearing new information etched into its carapace.

The kite-forms are the plateau’s distributed nervous system. Their carbon spines are not bone but drawn from the same pre-stressed alloy as the skin itself, extruded in place by the cranes during the infrequent realignments. Each membrane is a single molecule thick, photo-reactive, and capable of storing minuscule charge differentials across its surface. A single kite may drift for days between gantries, registering the slow pressure waves produced by distant crane movements or the almost inaudible click of a spatial fold resolving. When enough kites intersect above a given sector, their membranes align into a loose, temporary lattice; the lattice then collapses, sending a coordinated ripple through every crawler caravan beneath it. The result is not locomotion but adjustment: a gantry shifts two millimeters, a vent changes its angle by a fraction of a degree, or a persistent shadow is allowed to migrate a hand-span farther than geometry should permit. Afterward the kites separate and resume their separate drifts, leaving no trace except the altered wear groove they have collectively decided must appear.

In the persistent shadows the three orders intersect most visibly. Lichen spores carried downward by condensing water lodge in the carapaces of motionless crawlers; kites descend low enough that their membrane edges brush the crystallized bodies, exchanging charge for data. The shadow itself lengthens or contracts according to no external light source, as though the plateau’s surface is slowly re-deciding which volume of air it is currently willing to occupy. When the shadow finally lifts, the crawlers resume motion, the kites ascend, and the lichen, now slightly more extensive, continues its patient recording of heat.

Nothing here breeds or dies in any ordinary sense. Individuals are replaced only when their crystallized forms are reabsorbed during a crane realignment and their material is redeposited elsewhere as new filament, new spine, or new segment. The plateau therefore contains not a population but a single, extremely slow metabolism whose parts are periodically disassembled and reassembled according to information carried in the bodies of its own inhabitants.
