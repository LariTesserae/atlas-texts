In the channels and along the warm lips of poured concrete, the frogs sit motionless for hours, their bodies the exact temperature of the stone beneath them. They are thick-bodied, mottled in shades of slate and olive that blend with the algae crusts and salt rings. When they move, it is only to shift a few inches along a retaining edge or to flick a tongue at a slow-drifting midge; the sound of each landing is absorbed into the constant engine drone rather than breaking it. Their eggs are laid in gelatinous clusters that cling to the undersides of the narrow bridges where the channels pass beneath access ramps, never hatching in large numbers, never dying off entirely.

Dragonflies—dark-winged, metallic green—trace the same rectangular patrol above each stretch of tea-colored water. Their wings beat at a fixed rhythm that matches the drip of condensate from the iron railings. They do not chase one another or range farther; each individual reclaims its six-meter corridor as though it were territory measured once and never contested. Occasional damselflies, paler and more delicate, rest with wings folded on the salt-stunted sedges, their bodies adding thin vertical lines that do not sway.

Below the surface, the small fish hang in loose schools, oriented toward the single overflow pipe. They are elongated, almost translucent except for a faint bronze line along each flank. Their gills work steadily against the barely perceptible current, and when they adjust position it is by the smallest drift, never a sudden dart. They leave the sparse reeds untouched, instead grazing on the faint films of bacteria and detritus that form and reform on the submerged concrete without ever accumulating into sludge.

Between the buildings, where the pale mud is thinnest, minute translucent worms and copepods move through the saturated ground itself. They follow the same thin films of moisture that rise and fall by millimeters with each slow seepage from the foundation trenches. Their presence is registered only as a faint, continuous stirring of the surface tension around the edges of the white salt rings.

Nothing preys on anything else in a way that would shift the count. The frogs do not diminish the fish; the dragonflies do not thin the midges. The plants remain stunted and patchy, neither spreading nor retreating. The entire basin maintains its population at a constant density, as though the settlement’s fixed heat and unchanging sound have pressed every living thing into the same suspended state as the water table and the dripping edges.
