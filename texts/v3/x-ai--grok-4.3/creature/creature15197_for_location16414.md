In this sealed bowl of stone, life has narrowed itself to fit what the cycles will allow. The copper rosettes are the oldest residents. Each rosette anchors itself in a single crack no wider than a thumbnail, roots threading into the rock like slow veins. Their thick leaves catch the softened light and turn it into a faint internal glow; when the mist arrives they drink until their edges darken, then release the excess as a thin sweet vapor that keeps the air close and fragrant. The luminous nodules beneath each leaf pulse once per drying cycle, a slow strobe that never matches any neighbor exactly, so the basin floor always carries a shifting, pointillist shimmer.

The many-legged creatures move in endless rivers across the terraces. Their bodies are jointed plates of shifting iridescence, each segment carrying a tiny sensory hair that twitches at the sound of falling drops. They do not hunt; they harvest the drying phase itself. When the stone begins to pale they scrape away the newest mineral ring with delicate mouthparts, tasting the concentrated salts and metals left by the previous mist. Their clicking is constant because they never stop feeding, yet the sound rises and falls in loose waves as different streams cross or part around the rosettes. When a flyer passes overhead they pause, every hair lifted, as though listening to the chime through the soles of their feet.

The flyers are the most numerous and the least attached. Their translucent wings are veined with fine mineral threads left over from the long-vanished devices; each wingbeat draws a pure tone from those threads, and the overlapping tones braid into a single resonant chord that fills the pocket. They never land for long. Instead they station themselves beneath the gathering mist, drinking midair as droplets form, then drift upward again on the thermals created by their own chimes. Occasionally one will brush a rosette leaf and leave a dusting of glittering filaments on the copper surface; the leaf absorbs the gift and the flyer carries away a speck of copper scent that it will later brush onto another rosette, carrying pollen or memory or both.

The curved spar still protruding from the far wall is not quite empty. Inside its half-buried length something continues to count the intervals between mists, humming at a frequency that rearranges water molecules for an instant into precise, temporary lattices. The flyers drink from these momentary lattices as though from crystal cups. In return, the creatures that crawl nearest the spar sometimes climb its surface and deposit mineral scrapings they have gathered; the spar accepts the dust and folds it into the next lattice, maintaining the faint sweetness in the air. Nothing here kills or consumes another outright. Everything passes through the slow lens of condensation, is altered slightly, and passes on.
