The lattice that fills the chamber is itself the primary inhabitant, though it is difficult to perceive as such because it has no single body and no urgency that would mark it as an animal. Each fan is a living membrane still assembling itself, the individual tubes growing by lengthening their walls from within while the honey-colored fluid carries dissolved minerals upward from the foundation seams. Where two fans meet they do not merely push against one another; their tubes occasionally fuse into shared walls, so that over days the entire thicket becomes a single continuous network whose internal pressure can be felt as a faint, collective pulse. When the foundation flexes and the hairline gaps widen, the lattice does not react with alarm; instead a slow adjustment travels outward from the points of contact, tubes reorienting by a fraction of a degree so that light continues to reach the deepest layers even after the glimpse of the inverted chamber has vanished.

The small velvet worms move through this network as careful tenders rather than parasites. Their crystalline bristles are sensory and manipulative at once; each bristle ends in a microscopic cup that can seal over a tube wall and draw fluid without rupturing it. A single worm may spend an entire day on one fan, tracing the same few tubes in overlapping loops, leaving behind a faint glistening trace where it has redistributed excess minerals. They are not solitary in any strict sense. When two worms meet at the edge of a fan their bristles interlace for several minutes, exchanging a thread of the same honey fluid between them, after which each continues on its prior path as though nothing has occurred. Their segmented bodies never quicken; the ripple of contraction passes from tail to head like a slow tide, and when a worm pauses to rest it flattens until its outline merges with the lattice’s shadows.

Larger membrane creatures drift above this activity like slow-moving clearings. Their jointed frames are not rigid skeletons but adjustable lattices of their own, each joint containing a small reservoir of the same circulating fluid that stiffens or softens according to how much the creature has recently absorbed. When one settles, the membrane stretches taut across several adjacent fans at once, forming a temporary translucent roof beneath which the smaller worms continue their circuits undisturbed. The soft clicking heard against the ceramic is produced only when the creature must shift its anchorage; it lifts one or two joints, tests the neighboring tubes with fine sensory filaments, then lowers again, the motion so gradual that the fans spring back before the next joint has even begun to rise. These larger beings occasionally exchange fluid directly with the lattice by pressing specialized regions of their membrane against clusters of tubes; the exchange lasts for hours and leaves both the creature and the surrounding fans slightly more translucent afterward, as though some excess mineral has been drawn away and stored.

Nothing in the chamber reproduces in any way that can be witnessed within a human span of attention. Instead, growth and replacement occur through quiet redistribution: a damaged tube is slowly emptied, its walls reabsorbed into the fluid and carried to a nearby seam where new tubes bud outward. The velvet worms assist in this pruning without apparent intention, simply by continuing their patient circuits until every surface has been tasted and, if necessary, gently thinned. The foundation beneath everything flexes on its own longer rhythm, and when an inverted copy becomes briefly visible the lattice fans that overhang the gap reflect the image back upward, so that for those few moments the chamber appears to contain two identical thickets facing each other across a vanishing interval of glowing dust. Then the seam closes, the reflection dissolves, and the slow work of circulation resumes exactly where it left off, as though the lapse had been no interruption at all.
