The people of the hamlet are few—no more than a handful of families—and they have the look of having been pressed into the same shape as the stone around them. Their skin is pale to the point of translucence, not from any sickness but from the permanent film of moisture that keeps the sun from ever striking them directly; faint blue veins show at the temples and inner wrists. Hair, when it grows at all, is fine and lank, kept cropped close so it will not hold water against the scalp. They move with a deliberate economy, never lifting a foot higher than the shallow channels require, never raising a voice above the constant, soft percussion of seepage. Children learn early to read the faint tremors that travel up through the rock; a certain rhythm in the stone means it is safe to cross a new joint, another means the roof ropes must be checked before night.

They sleep in hammocks strung close to the ceiling beams, high enough that the worst of the condensation gathers below them. When they wake, the first task is always the same: wiping the inner faces of the stones with squares of reed cloth that are then wrung into the central drain. The cloth darkens quickly and must be replaced often; the discard pile behind the outermost house is a low mound of blackened fiber that the liverworts have already begun to colonize. Cooking is done on small stone platforms set over shallow braziers of dried reed; the smoke never rises far but instead layers against the overlapping eaves, turning the air inside each house into a slow-moving soup of mineral steam and the faint bitterness of scorched moss.

At intervals one of the adults climbs down a notched ladder to check the deeper channels. They carry a short, forked stick and move it gently through the water until it meets resistance; an eyeless fish, if it is quick enough, will sometimes be guided into a woven reed trap no larger than a hand. These fish are not eaten often—the flesh is almost without flavor—but their bones are saved and worked into needles or small hooks. The translucent salamanders are left alone; the children have learned that if too many are taken the gaps between stones begin to fill with a thin scum that smells sour and makes the mortar-moss rot faster.

The adults speak little during the day, not from any rule but because sound travels strangely here, flattened by the vapor and the constant small noises of water leaving stone. When they do speak it is usually to name a change: a new seep has opened above the western roof, the northern rope bridge has stretched another finger’s width, the slab settled again in the night. These observations are passed from one person to the next across the connected roofs until everyone has heard. In this way the entire settlement keeps a single, shared awareness of the rock still deciding what shape it will finally take.

At the outer margin, where the scarp drops away, a low stone bench has been set into the living rock. Someone sits there each evening, not to watch the marsh so much as to feel the cooler air that sometimes rises from the older ground beyond. The bench is never left empty for long; when one person stands, another is already crossing the nearest roof bridge to take the place. They sit with hands resting on the wet stone, feeling the faint, patient movement still rising from below, and they breathe the metallic air until the light begins to fail.
