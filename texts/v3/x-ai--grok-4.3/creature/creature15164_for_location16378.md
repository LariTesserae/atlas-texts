These beings drift and roll through the plateau’s corridors like half-remembered thoughts of a geometry that once belonged to dry air. Their forms never quite resolve into single bodies; each is a temporary agreement among many smaller units that may separate tomorrow or next century.

The lattices are the oldest agreement. Their calcareous rods grow and dissolve in slow counterpoint, so that an individual lattice may lengthen by an imperceptible fraction in one direction while shortening in another, reshaping its silhouette without ever breaking contact with the water it inhabits. As it turns on a faint current, it may briefly resemble a flattened polyhedron, then a ragged fan, then a torn page whose edges are still writing themselves. Inside the rods, microscopic chambers pulse with minute pressure changes that let the whole structure adjust buoyancy without expending motion. When two lattices meet, they do not touch so much as exchange a thin halo of dissolved carbonate; afterward each continues on a slightly altered heading, as if the brief overlap has rewritten the next page of its drift.

The spheres are more abrupt. Their filaments taste the stone only for the length of a single roll, then withdraw, leaving behind a faint polish that will be erased the next time a layer of water slides across the same place. Inside each dense core, the filaments are anchored to a lattice of their own, a rigid, spinning armature that records the sequence of ions it has tasted. When many spheres gather in one shallow concavity, their combined filaments form a temporary net that rasps the mineral with a sound too faint to register except as an increase in the low-frequency thrum that occasionally moves through the plateau. The spheres then disperse again, carrying away minute quantities of the stone’s memory in the form of newly arranged surface molecules.

The needle-swimmers are the most recent arrivals, yet they already behave as if they have always belonged. Each needle is a single, elongated crystal that grows its own slight curvature over hours, allowing it to steer within the spiral that temporarily contains it. When the spiral collapses, the needles do not scatter at random; most come to rest along the edges of existing concavities, their long axes aligned with the direction the water will next prefer. In that alignment they wait, vibrating at the same low frequency that occasionally travels through the mineral, until the next inversion lifts them again.

Over centuries these three manners of being rarely meet directly, yet each registers the passage of the others through small, lasting adjustments. A lattice that has crossed the path of many spheres develops a slightly sweeter internal chemistry. A concavity favored by resting needles becomes, for a few decades, the place where spheres most often pause to taste the stone. The water itself carries traces of all three, so that even the clearest layer above the plateau is never truly empty; it is only the place where the agreements among rods, filaments, and needles have not yet condensed into visible form.
