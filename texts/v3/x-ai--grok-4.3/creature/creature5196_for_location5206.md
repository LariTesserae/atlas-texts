The basin belongs to the stems. They are not a forest or a meadow; they are a single, slow architecture. The same thick, leathery growth that once crept across the silt has lifted itself into walls and ceilings, fusing along long seams until the original grid of streets and loading bays is readable again in living tissue. Where a warehouse stood, a continuous sheet of stem now bows outward and inward like a low lung, its inner surface damp and faintly veined. Where a foundry once cast iron, the stems have risen into hollow columns, their outer layers still cool while deeper tissues conduct a sluggish sap that rises after rain and falls again at dusk.

Inside these chambers the stems continue their quiet labor. Fine white rootlets have entered every seam of the old iron frames and turbines, not to devour them but to hold them. The metal no longer moves by its own power; it moves only when the surrounding tissue swells or contracts with moisture, producing the single, reluctant click that sometimes travels through an otherwise silent hall. The stems themselves do not speak, yet they register every change: a night of heavy fog draws them fractionally taller; a dry wind draws them fractionally inward. In the longest chambers, the collective shift can be felt as a minute alteration in air pressure, like the drawing of a very slow breath.

Between the structures the reeds and wiry grass follow their own measured claims. The reeds stand in loose files along the old lanes, each jointed stalk capped by a small cup that fills with condensate and tips at intervals, returning water to the silt. The grass forms low, pale mats that stop cleanly at the edges of the shallow pans, leaving the water surface free for the thin, branching films of algae. These films do not drift; they grow in place, following the faint tilt of the ground so that after a shower they briefly gleam in long, connected patterns before the water soaks away.

Nothing here hunts or flees. The only movement that crosses distances is the slow redistribution of moisture: stems taking in what the air gives, reeds releasing what the cups can no longer hold, silt darkening and softening beneath both. At midday the basin lies open and half-lit, its living walls beaded but motionless, its water pans reflective and still. Only in the long evening does the drip resume, steady and spaced, marking time at the pace the stems themselves require. The place is not empty. It is occupied in every plane and joint by the same patient, structural growth, content to maintain the old geometry at its own imperceptible speed.
