In this high basin, life moves at the pace of condensation and mineral deposition. The great plants are not a mere backdrop but the oldest residents, their broad leaves functioning less like foliage and more like slow, stationary bodies that have grown into position over centuries. Each plant maintains a deliberate spacing from its neighbors, roots flowing across stone in measured arcs that advance only after seasons of moisture accumulate. Their luminous veins are not decorative; they conduct a faint internal light generated by the steady warmth and the dissolved minerals carried in from the seeps. A single leaf thickens imperceptibly each year, its surface becoming a living catchment that channels mist and droplets toward the nearest root without ever allowing two plants to touch.

Beneath these leaves, the translucent slugs are the most mobile inhabitants, though mobility here is a relative notion. They follow fixed circuits along the undersides of the fronds, grazing on films of mineral-rich algae that form only where light filters through the veined leaves. Their trails, once dry, harden into faint crystalline ridges that the plants eventually absorb back through capillary action. The slugs themselves rarely descend to the pools; instead they linger in the perpetual mist, their bodies adjusting translucency to match the shifting green cast of daylight passing through the leaves above.

Subtler still are the thread-like colonies that inhabit the fissures themselves—networks of slow-growing lithophytes that extend from crack to crack, feeding directly on the seeping water and the faint warmth rising from deeper stone. Where two fissures approach each other, the colonies meet and merge into low, reef-like bridges no thicker than a fingernail, over which later droplets must leap. These colonies produce the basin’s deeper, almost inaudible tones: resonant vibrations that travel through the rock when pressure shifts and a new thread of water begins to move.

The air itself holds a faint, suspended microbiome of moisture-borne particles that only become visible when a droplet falls and momentarily clears a path through the mist. These particles settle on every surface, contributing to the slow silvering of stone edges and the gradual thickening of leaf veins. Nothing here breeds in quantity or speed. A new slug may appear once every several decades, hatched from a clutch laid in a damp seam; a new fissure colony might claim a fresh crack only after a century of seepage has widened it enough for roots to follow.

The basin sustains no predators or scavengers in the ordinary sense. When a leaf eventually senesces—after perhaps two centuries—it does so by gradually softening at the margins until it folds downward and its nutrients are reclaimed by the same roots that once nourished it. The resulting space is occupied not by rapid succession but by the patient widening of the nearest neighboring plant. In this manner the inhabitants maintain the emptiness as carefully as they maintain their own forms, each element of the basin—plant, slug, lithophyte, mist—participating in a single, protracted exchange of water, light, and mineral that has no need for haste or crowding.
