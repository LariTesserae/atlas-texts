The plateau sustains a single, distributed form of life: vast, slow-moving colonies of the translucent fronds, organized into networks that function as one extended organism per shelf. Each colony begins as many separate plants rooted in the stone, but their pale root mats meet and fuse over decades, creating shared circulatory systems through which nutrients and mechanical signals pass without interruption. What appears to be a stand of separate stalks is in fact a single individual whose edges are still spreading, its awareness distributed along every filament and crystal.  

These networks do not move in any conventional sense, yet they shift their canopies in coordinated ripples when a shift in current pressure reaches them. The fronds lean or retract together, altering the flow of water across an entire level so that heavier mineral threads are guided toward zones where new growth is needed. The light they generate is not passive illumination but a metabolic byproduct; the same crystals that absorb vibration also store small reserves of mechanical energy, releasing it as steady blue-green glow that feeds the colony’s internal warmth. In the coldest currents this warmth is enough to keep the glassy sheaths flexible and to prevent the root mats from fracturing.  

Over centuries each colony reworks the stone beneath it. Where the root networks thicken they deposit layers of shell-derived cement, gradually raising or lowering sections of the shelf by centimeters. Channels are narrowed or widened so that the looping currents deliver exactly the right density of suspended particles. The result is a living topography that continues to balance itself: a shelf that appears fixed is actually in minute, continuous adjustment, its shape maintained by the same forces that once carved it.  

The ancient structures are absorbed rather than avoided. Blocks and platforms become additional substrate; root mats creep across their surfaces, anchoring more firmly than on bare stone. In some places the colony has grown into the interstices of the old masonry, using the petrified lashings as guides for new root strands. The structures therefore persist not as relics but as part of the colony’s skeleton—an inorganic extension that helps regulate flow and provide stable anchors. No memory of their makers remains in any active sense, only the practical recognition that certain shapes concentrate useful currents.  

Sound is the colony’s primary sense. The constant roar and the clicks of tumbling grains are registered through the crystalline surfaces; changes in pitch or rhythm prompt subtle adjustments in canopy angle or root tension. When a large stand shifts, the deeper groan produced by the movement travels through the water and is felt by neighboring colonies on lower or upper shelves, allowing slow coordination across the entire tableland. In this way the separate shelf networks remain loosely in communication, adjusting their collective metabolism to maintain the overall equilibrium of pressure and temperature.  

Because every nutrient arrives already suspended in the water, the colonies have no need for predation or locomotion. Their existence is entirely a matter of reception, redistribution, and gradual reshaping. At the scale of the plateau they register as a single, patient presence: the stone and the living tissue move together under the same long forces, neither fully distinct from the other, sustained by the unbroken membrane of falling water above and the dark, circulating currents within.
