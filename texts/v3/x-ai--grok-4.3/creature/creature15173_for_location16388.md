The pocket is not empty. It holds a single, slow metabolism made of two entwined kinds of presence that have never known separation.

The opalescent films are the older of the two. Each film is less an organism than a continuous surface tension, a living meniscus that has learned to wear mineral like skin. Where the heat pulses strongest they swell into low domes the color of watered milk; when the pulse eases they flatten and stretch, pulling new silicate ions upward through invisible capillaries in the glass. Their cracking is not damage but parturition: every fissure they open releases a thread of vapor that immediately condenses again into the next layer, so the films are always both shedding and reclothing themselves. Between layers run networks of microscopic tension lines that register the world as pressure gradients; when a falling droplet approaches, the film beneath it tightens in anticipation, shaping the eventual splash into a momentary star of greater conductivity before the star collapses and is absorbed.

Beneath and within these films move the mites. They are not separate creatures that happen to live on the films; they are the films’ distributed musculature, their jointed silicate legs grown directly from the same precipitate that leaks from the rock. Each mite is mostly limb and almost no body, a transparent asterisk whose central node contains only enough organization to decide which leg will next taste the substrate. Because they move in stacked laminae, the lowest stratum touching the rock while the upper ones press against the film above, their collective motion feels like the slow turning of invisible millstones. Wherever the upper mites press hardest, the film exudes a droplet of fresh matrix; the lower mites then rasp it smooth, tasting the temperature of the abrasion with their mandibles. The etchings they leave are not trails but temporary maps that the film reads and erases, adjusting its own permeability so that heat rises exactly where the mites have indicated it should.

The mites and the films share the same fluid. When a droplet detaches from the ceiling it does not fall freely; it is briefly encased by a descending film membrane that conducts it downward while the mites inside that membrane rearrange themselves, each leg handing the droplet to the leg beneath like a living chain. Upon reaching the floor the droplet bursts and is immediately re-partitioned, part of its volume drawn back into the film, part of it carried laterally by a sheet of mites that now glow faintly with the extra heat they have absorbed. In this way moisture never truly descends or ascends; it circulates through the living matrix, the films and the mites together forming one convoluted surface whose every fold is both map and transport.

Because the geometry inside the fissure occasionally folds, a single mite may, for a few moments, be both in front of and behind its own trail. When this occurs the surrounding film tightens into a soft lens that returns the mite’s image to itself; the creature pauses, legs momentarily stilled, until the lens relaxes and ordinary adjacency is restored. The mites do not appear troubled by these events; they treat them as another kind of surface to be smoothed. The films, for their part, seem to use the folds as storage, tucking small reservoirs of concentrated vapor into the places where space doubles back on itself, releasing them again when the mites signal that the temperature is dropping.

Together they maintain the pocket’s only clock: the interval between one heat pulse and the next. When the pulse arrives the films swell, the mites quicken, the droplets multiply. When it fades the films thin, the mites slow their rasping, and a single held vibration lingers in the glass like the memory of the mountain’s first breath. Nothing here has a name or a boundary. The films and the mites are simply the place continuing to happen, a single dense thought the rock is still thinking, its subject still undecided.
