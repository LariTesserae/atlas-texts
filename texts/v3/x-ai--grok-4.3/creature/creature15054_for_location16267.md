In the steady dimness, the fissure holds a community shaped entirely by contact and patience. The many-legged forms that cover every surface are not a single species but several that have converged on the same economy: slow movement, soft exoskeletons, and an indifference to light. Some are elongated, almost thread-like, their segments flexing in smooth waves as they travel along the rounded ledges; others are compact, armoured in pale plates that click softly when they turn. All of them share the same milky translucence that lets the faint warmth of the stone show through their bodies as a dim glow when they press against it.

The salamanders move among them without haste. Each is no longer than a finger, its limbs reduced to slight webbed paddles that grip rather than stride. Their skin is so thin that the pulsing of blood is visible as a slow lavender rhythm along the spine. They do not hunt so much as wait; when a drop falls they lift their heads, and if another creature is already drinking they simply settle beside it, bodies touching along their lengths. Their eyes are nothing more than faint clouded spots beneath the skin, yet they orient themselves perfectly to the temperature gradient that rises from the deeper fractures.

Together the creatures produce a surface that is never still yet never agitated. Where the algae crusts are thickest, dense clusters of minute isopods graze, their legs stirring the damp film into fine parallel tracks. Between the hanging roots, smaller threads of life—springtails and mites—launch themselves in short, silent arcs from one bead of moisture to the next, their landings absorbed into the larger rustle. Occasionally two salamanders will meet head-on along a narrow ledge; instead of turning aside they press upward until one is inverted against the ceiling and the other continues beneath, the whole manoeuvre accomplished in a single fluid exchange that leaves both creatures facing the direction they originally intended.

Deeper in, where the walls draw closer, the same species recur at even smaller scales. Here the arthropods become almost invisible individually, appearing only as a continuous pale haze that shifts when the air current changes. The salamanders grow rarer, but those that remain are older and more translucent, their outlines sometimes visible only by the way they interrupt the slow drift of mineral dust. They lie along the narrowest seams, absorbing the warmth that seeps upward and returning it through their bodies to the stone, maintaining the same even temperature that first allowed the fissure to remain habitable.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or dies conspicuously. When a small body is crushed beneath an accidental tread, others gather within minutes to consume it, leaving only the faintest stain that the next condensation will lift away. In this way the population remains constant, the sound of their motion unchanging from one hour to the next, a single sustained texture that belongs to the fissure as much as the rounded stone itself.
