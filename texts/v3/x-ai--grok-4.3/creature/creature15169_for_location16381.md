Something lives here, but it does not announce itself the way animals do. It moves in the same register as the place itself—slow, incremental, half-hidden inside the corrosion.

What crawls across the wet stone is not quite the pale insect you notice and then lose; the insect is only the outermost layer of a much smaller tenant. Inside each seam and beneath every flake of rust, colonies of thread-fine organisms work. They are no larger than filings, glassy and segmented, with bodies that look like lengths of oxidized wire sheathed in living resin. They do not eat the metal so much as trade with it, dissolving one crystal lattice and encouraging another to grow in its place, the way a vine might coax a trellis into a new shape over decades. Where they gather thickest, the rusted lace stiffens again and begins, very faintly, to conduct a current no instrument would register.

Larger forms appear when the light is low and the drips have settled. They resemble shed exoskeletons or the husks of old transformers, yet they are not empty. Inside the bowed panels of the garden-shed structures, clusters of these organisms have fused into something closer to a single body. They occupy the space where windings and cores once sat, their translucent filaments braided through the remaining copper like new roots. When a flywheel jerks into motion, it is because the colony inside the housing has chosen, all at once, to re-route a trickle of stored charge or to rearrange its own mass so that a bent tooth can mesh again for a few seconds. The movement is not purposeful in any mechanical sense; it is more like a sleeper turning over to ease a cramped limb.

Between the artifacts, in the shallow lenses of water, still smaller lives drift. They are visible only as brief silvering of the surface tension: minute droplets that are not quite droplets, each one a single cell whose mineral shell refracts daylight into the same soft spectra that the broken safety glass produces. They rise and settle with the rhythm of condensation falling from the pipe tips, and when a valve spasms open they scatter in microscopic rings, then re-form minutes later exactly where they began. Nothing here breeds in the usual sense; the organisms extend themselves instead, sending exploratory filaments into fresh rust or into the warm, damp micro-climate behind a loosened rivet. Over time a filament may thicken into a new joint, or a joint may loosen and be abandoned, the colony simply withdrawing into the next available seam.

The effect is of continuous, almost imperceptible upkeep performed by something that has no central mind and no sense of urgency. The hollow is not being repaired, nor is it being dismantled. It is being inhabited at the scale and tempo of oxidation itself, and the occupants are content to let every surface remain exactly as unsettled as it now is, provided the thin film of water and the faint mineral chill never quite leave.
