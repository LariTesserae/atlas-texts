The hollow teems with forms shaped by the same unrelenting churn that rewrites its walls each hour. The root-mass itself behaves less like scenery and more like a single diffuse body: veins of sap pulse in slow tides that shift direction without warning, and whole galleries of fiber stiffen or soften in response to pressures felt miles away. Creatures here move through the tissue like blood cells or parasites, some welcomed, others fought moment by moment.

Largest and slowest are the armored slugs, each one a living plow. Their dorsal plates are not true shell but accretions of compressed leaf-laminate harvested from the walls they climb; as they drag forward they secrete a solvent that loosens the root-plate ahead, then press the dissolved matter into fresh armor behind them. When two meet they pause, exchange slow gusts of spore-laden breath through pores along their flanks, then continue, sometimes carrying one another’s shed plates as temporary cargo. Their trails seal almost at once, leaving only a faint ridge of re-knitted fiber and a lingering sweetness that draws smaller scavengers.

Above them the air belongs to the hard-shelled flyers—dense, clicking clouds whose wing cases are translucent and veined like the ceiling fronds. They rise and fall in coordinated pulses, spiraling around columns of rising growth until some signal dissolves the formation and they drop to feed on fresh exudates weeping from split stems. Their larvae burrow shallowly into the living walls, forming temporary abscesses that the root-mass later walls off and absorbs; many adults still bear the circular scars of those nurseries on their undersides.

Between these scales swarms a ceaseless undercurrent of many-legged forms, some no larger than a thumbnail, others the length of a forearm. They travel in braided streams along the interiors of hollow stems and beneath overlapping leaf-sheets, eating tunnels that the vegetation immediately begins to heal around them. Their discarded molts and frass become fertilizer pumped through the same vascular system that feeds the flyers’ exudates. In places the swarm thickens into solid mats that shift like living carpets; when a slug’s solvent trail crosses one of these mats the smaller creatures climb aboard, riding until the next vertical wall offers new feeding grounds.

Among all this motion the human traces belong to people who have surrendered fixed settlement for the same principle of continual arrival. They move in loose, ever-changing associations—no larger than a handful at a time—slipping between contracting chambers and riding the slow heave of a rising vine as transport. Their tools are grown rather than forged: a blade is a sharpened ridge of root-plate still attached to its parent stem until the moment it is needed, then left to be reabsorbed. When they pause long enough to shape a clearing they do so by coaxing the surrounding tissue rather than cutting it, training fibers into low arches and temporary platforms that are already beginning to thicken into new walls before the group has finished using them. Their presence registers only as slight alterations in the rhythm of growth—places where the usual convulsions hesitate half a breath longer, or where a sudden bloom of edible tissue appears along a route they favor. They are already becoming rumor again, their outlines blurring into the next wave of vegetative upheaval.
