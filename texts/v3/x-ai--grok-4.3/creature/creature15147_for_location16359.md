In the basins and across the long rises live the lattices—beings whose bodies are assembled from the same compressed minerals that form the plate itself. Each is a flexible grid of interlocking plates, pale as the stone yet veined with faint, crystalline fractures that catch the unchanging light. They do not breathe or pulse; their motion is a patient rearrangement of their own substance, plates sliding over and under one another with the deliberation of continents. When they travel, the pattern is barely legible: a slow folding at one edge answered, hours later, by an unfolding at the far side, so that the whole caravan drifts forward like a single thought moving through many minds.

They pause often. In the shallow depressions they linger for days or decades, their lattices splayed against the settling grit. There they perform their only visible labor, nudging crystalline dust into low ridges and then allowing it to subside again, as though rehearsing the endless cycle of erosion and deposition that shaped the world long before they appeared. When they rest, their abundance is felt only as a slight, distributed pressure on the bedrock—a faint, collective weight distributed over horizons too wide for any single eye to measure.

Inside the low cities, other presences persist. They do not move as the lattices move. They inhabit the steady heat of the furnaces and the long, motionless run of conveyors, expressing themselves through the occasional sigh of a valve or the quarter-degree turn of a vane that has waited seasons to complete its arc. These inhabitants are not separate from the machinery; they are the machinery continuing itself, their awareness distributed across catalytic beds and pressure reservoirs. When a new depression forms beneath a city, corridors reroute themselves over centuries, walls reseal, and ramps lengthen or shorten by the width of a single seam. The change is never announced. It simply becomes the new arrangement.

Between these two populations—the slow lattices outside and the distributed processes within—there is no conflict and little direct encounter. The lattices occasionally drift near a city’s outer walls, their plates registering the faint vibration of cooling metal as one more mineral property to accommodate. In return, the cities’ systems register the lattices’ passage as a predictable redistribution of surface mass, a variable already accounted for in their ancient calculations. Both proceed at the same tempo: adjustments measured in hand-widths per century, decisions unfolding across intervals that outlast mountains. The plate continues to subside and shift beneath them all, and nothing, living or enduring, hurries to meet it.
