The large vertebrates are not one species but a loose guild of four or five forms, each shaped by the same long constraint: they must occupy the fixed lanes between the pillars without ever needing to invent a new route. The commonest are the span-ribbed drifters—fusiform bodies three times a human length, their flanks braced by cartilaginous arches that mirror the ribbed vaults overhead. Their skin is the same sea-glass translucency as the mineralized stems; only the faint internal glow of their own circulatory lanterns distinguishes them from the surrounding tissue. They advance by slow sculling of a single, vertically oriented caudal fin whose strokes last whole minutes; between strokes they simply glide, pressure-neutral, letting the resident current carry them. When two meet they exchange neither aggression nor affiliation; they merely adjust the phase of their lanterns so that the passing constellations do not briefly overlap and create a false third echo.

A second, heavier form keeps closer to the substrate. These are the pavement-hammers: dorsoventrally flattened, their undersides lined with blunt mineral calluses that rasp the hexagonal scars as they pass. They do not swim so much as bear down upon the living pavement until friction halts them, then rest for hours, absorbing faint nutrients that still weep from the old rootlet pores. Their calls are the deepest of all—infrasonic pulses that arrive through the bones of any listener like the memory of an earthquake felt in childhood. Because the calls never vary, the hammers have evolved to parse tiny phase shifts in their own returning echoes; a single millisecond’s difference tells them whether another hammer has shifted one body-length to the left or right since the last circuit. They register this information without apparent interest.

The smaller, faster forms are the corridor-darts: slender, almost needle-like vertebrates whose lateral-line canals have hypertrophied into continuous grooves filled with pressure-sensing crystals. They travel only within the narrow stems that intersect the larger avenues at right angles, never crossing the open intervals. Their speed is relative; to a human observer they would seem languid, yet they complete a full loop of their chosen conduit in less time than it takes a span-ribbed drifter to finish a single scull. Each dart carries a paired pair of bioluminescent lures—one forward, one aft—that pulse in exact antiphase to the migrating sap-lights inside the surrounding stems, a camouflage so precise that the larger animals register them only as additional background shimmer.

All of these creatures share one further adaptation. Their nervous systems have contracted around extremely long temporal receptive fields; a stimulus lasting less than several hours registers, if at all, as background fluctuation. Consequently the unchanging chord of overlapping infrasonic calls is perceived not as repetition but as the single continuous note of existence itself. When a new mineral vein finally occludes one conduit—a process measured in centuries—the darts that used that route simply slow, then stop, then become part of the substrate in the same patient manner as everything else here. Their bodies mineralize in place, translucent ribs aligning with the architecture they once followed, until they are indistinguishable from the ribs that grew the place to begin with.
