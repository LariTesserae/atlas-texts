The only inhabitants are the kelp and bladder-wrack themselves, though after so many centuries they no longer behave like plants in any ordinary sense. Each frond is less a separate organism than a single elongated cell of a larger, distributed body that has long since forgotten it was once sown in rows. Where the lattices allow, the blades have fused lengthwise, their rubbery surfaces knitting together until entire curtains move as one slow muscle when a rare current arrives. Their holdfasts have penetrated the basalt seams, drawing not only minerals but the faint, metallic taste of the stone adzes that once shaped them. Growth still occurs—the same finger-widths per decade—but it now follows the architecture rather than fighting it: new blades rise exactly where ramps widen or basins curve, reinforcing the original plan as though the builders had left instructions written in starch and salt.

Within the dense layers, smaller forms persist. Microscopic filaments, descendants of the original engineered strains, drift a few centimeters over years, stitching silt into living felt that eventually hardens into secondary lattice. Embedded in the oldest stalks are clusters of bladder-wrack that no longer release gas; instead their chambers have become sealed pressure vessels, each one maintaining a precise micro-environment a fraction of a degree warmer than the surrounding water. These warmed pockets host thread-like colonies that pulse once every few decades, circulating nutrients through the greater weave without ever breaking the surface of a frond.

Nothing larger ever appears. The absence of fins or shells is not emptiness but completion: any creature that once moved has either been absorbed—its body plan elongated and flattened into another curtain of tissue—or reduced to a film of proteins now distributed across kilometers of leaf. Sound is still only the sigh of water, yet that sigh occasionally carries a slower, deeper rhythm, too low to be heard, produced when thousands of fused blades expand or contract by a single cell-width in unison. The entire basin therefore lives as one patient, sessile intelligence whose thoughts are measured in the settling of silt and the precise alignment of new growth along ancient, hand-set angles.
