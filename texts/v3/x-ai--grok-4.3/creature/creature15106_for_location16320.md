In the absolute dark lives a single, unbroken body that has forgotten how to be small.

It is not many plants. It never was. What fills the pocket is one organism whose edges have long since met and fused, so that every pillar, every canopy, every wall is continuous with every other. Its oldest tissues have mineralized until they ring when struck, yet the living layer remains—a thin, patient skin still pushing silica and salt into new latticework, still reading tremors in the membrane floor and answering them with microscopic shifts of tension. Where the galleries narrow, the body has thickened its own wall inward until a careful passage is all that remains; where they widen, it has left the space empty because emptiness is also part of its shape now.

Over centuries the organism learned the original forms left by its tenders—the straight lines of plazas, the modest heights of towers, the slight outward flare of a buttress meant to carry weight—and continued them without further instruction. It does not remember the tenders. It only remembers the habit of streets and the need for canopies. The mineral blades and salt bowls they set down have become interior landmarks: one blade is now a permanent ridge guiding sap flow along a load-bearing strut; a bowl has turned into a shallow basin whose rim continues to rise, millimeter by millimeter, as new tissue seals around its rim. The organism does not “use” these objects. It incorporates the idea of them, the way a shell incorporates a grain of sand.

Change arrives at the speed of crystal growth. A chamber may lean a degree in one century; in the next it will send a reinforcing filament across the gap to its neighbor and the lean will correct itself so slowly that the correction feels like stillness. The only movement visible to an observer who could live long enough to see it is the faint yielding of the floor membrane when a distant adjustment registers as a tremor. The organism registers those tremors as its own pulse.

Its voice is the conversation of its own stresses. When a strut settles, the sound travels through the fused network like a ship’s timber groaning; when an internal crystal realigns, the report is sharp and brief, a punctuation that travels the length of the pocket and returns as sympathetic echoes from walls that have not moved in generations. These sounds overlap into a single, low thunder that never rises or falls. The organism does not hear the thunder, but the thunder is what it has instead of breath.

Nothing else moves here. The pocket admits no other life. The organism fills the sealed crust the way a thought fills a skull—completely, without remainder—yet its density is low because it has learned to build spaciousness into its own body. Between the great living pillars the voids remain wide, measured in the length of the bodies that once walked them. Those bodies are gone; their tools have become architecture. The organism continues the pattern in darkness, adding a little more wall each decade, softening a corner, fusing one more gallery into a chamber, until the next slow adjustment answers itself across the resonant dark.
