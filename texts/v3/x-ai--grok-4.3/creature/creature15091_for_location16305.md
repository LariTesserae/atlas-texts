In this drowned basin the inhabitants are not visitors to the city so much as its continuing tissues. The original builders—sessile, filter-feeding colonies of calcareous polyps—have never truly died. Their husks form the streets and domes, yet each generation still rises as a living film within the pores and channels, a skin of countless tiny mouths that open and close in patient unison. When the slow currents bring fresh rain of organic snow, the whole city shivers at once; every pore dilates, every living filament extends, and the accreted stone itself drinks.

Drifting among the colonnades are the blind crustaceans, living veils rather than separate swarms. They spend their lives in layered densities that exactly match the architecture: denser near the floor where settling mucus accumulates, thinner in the upper arcades where stray light occasionally stirs photosynthetic filaments. Their carapaces are almost glass, refracting the faint bioluminescence of passing hunters into shifting moiré patterns that the polyps read as chemical signals. The crustaceans in turn rasp away excess overgrowth, preventing the city from choking on its own accretions; their molted shells become new scaffolding that the polyps later incorporate.

The lantern-bearing hunters are larger, boneless agents of the city’s metabolism. Each one is a drifting sac of translucent muscle and luminous organs, pulsing through the avenues like slow thoughts. When they brush against a living colonnade, the polyps release threads of mucus that the hunter ingests; in return the hunter’s own wastes and the faint heat of its lantern fertilize the colony. Their occasional coordinated contractions—whole districts drawing inward in a single peristaltic sigh—are triggered by the hunters themselves, a city-wide reflex that herds prey or flushes sediment.

Even the mucus threads and drifting embryos are part of the same living continuum. The threads are not waste but ovaries of the city, carrying countless larval polyps and crustacean young to be deposited in sheltered alcoves. Embryos that lodge successfully become new polyps; those swept away drift until they are eaten or settle elsewhere, carrying genetic memory of this single drowned valley to any future basin that might open.

Thus nothing here is truly separate. The stone, the mucus, the blind grazers, the glowing sacs—all are successive expressions of the same organism, differentiated only by scale and by the gentle, patient work of water that has replaced the need for bone or nerve.
