Inside this sealed, auditorium-scale sphere the life is not yet a population of creatures so much as a single, distributed metabolism still deciding what shape a creature might take.  

The most visible threads are the algae—thin, translucent filaments no thicker than spider silk, anchored to the newest ceramic spurs. Each filament is not a single organism but a living loom: its cells divide in strict linear order, secreting fresh glassy carbon ahead of themselves while older segments calcify into the lattice behind. Light passes straight through them; they cast no shadows. When a pressure wave momentarily stiffens a lane of water, the filaments tauten like plucked strings, and the vibrations travel the length of the algae as metabolic signals, telling distant strands which minerals to pull from solution next.  

Between the filaments drift the true majority of life, suspended in overlapping clouds too fine to see without refractive tricks. These are the vesicles—lipid spheres the size of pollen grains, each one a self-replicating chemical computer. They drift in synchronized pulses, collapsing and refilling in rhythms that keep the water from ever settling into true stillness. When two clouds of vesicles meet they exchange packets of RNA-like code through transient pores; the exchange is audible as a soft, glassy click that travels outward and is answered by other clicks seconds later, so the entire volume keeps a running conversation about temperature, ion balance, and which lattice nodes most need reinforcement.  

A third presence is quieter still: thin sheets of living conductor—filament membranes grown directly onto the carbon struts. These membranes are neither plant nor machine but a hybrid that remembers every pressure wave that has ever passed through them. They store the memory as reversible folding patterns in their protein scaffolds. When the memory layer grows too crowded, a membrane will tear itself along a predetermined seam, releasing a new generation of vesicles already imprinted with the remembered rhythm. The tear heals within hours, but the scar remains slightly more conductive, so older struts slowly become tuned to particular frequencies the way a bell develops a favorite note after years of ringing.  

Occasionally a lattice segment decides it has finished growing. It sheds its outermost ceramic skin in one sudden, concussive release; the discarded layer disintegrates into microscopic seeds that the currents carry to unfinished regions. The seeds are not dormant—they already contain a few vesicles and a short length of membrane ready to begin again. In this way the structures themselves participate in reproduction without ever leaving their places.  

Nothing in the sphere has a nervous system larger than a single vesicle, yet the whole volume behaves as if it is thinking. When an external photon is bent inward at an unusual angle, the nearest vesicles adjust their buoyancy in a ripple that travels outward at the speed of sound in water; within seconds the algae at the far side of the sphere reorient their growth toward the new light. The adjustment is not planned by any central authority. It simply happens because every component is already listening to every other component through the continuous medium of sound, chemistry, and temporary stiffness in the water itself.  

Over days the sparsity will give way to density, but the character of life here will not change. It will remain a place where nothing swims, nothing hunts, and nothing dies alone—only membranes tear, vesicles click, and filaments keep lengthening, all of them answering the same low, resonant chorus that has not yet decided whether it is still being born or has been here forever.
