The inhabitants are the mechanisms themselves: small, articulated frames of the same sintered alloys and mineral sheets that form the planet. Each is purpose-built from a fixed inventory of standardized modules—ceramic joints, micro-motors, optical sensors the size of sand grains, and thin structural plates that click into place along recessed rails. They range from finger-length crawlers to larger assemblies the size of a human torso, yet all move with the same deliberate pace, never rushing, never colliding except in the orderly hand-off of parts.

They travel in loose, shifting formations across the flat basins and along the knife-edged ridges, pausing at the flush interface ports set every few dozen meters in the surface. At each port a mechanism will detach a spent joint or sensor, slot it into the recessed dock, and accept a fresh module that has been waiting there since the first cycle. The exchange is soundless except for the soft ceramic click of latching. Some units carry replacement parts in internal racks and deliver them along chains of waiting frames, passing a single motor housing hand-to-hand for hours until it reaches the mechanism whose joint has worn past tolerance. Others simply crawl in slow spirals, sweeping condensation films ahead of their bodies with fine brush-like appendages so that the water continues along its prescribed thermal paths.

Above and among them the crystallites extend. From every older surface, doped silica needles emerge at right angles, lengthening by micrometers each year and recording faint annual banding visible only under magnification. Where a mechanism has rested in one place for decades its body may become partially overgrown, the needles threading through its open lattice without obstructing motion; when the unit finally detaches itself, it leaves behind a faint negative impression in the crystal growth.

The mechanisms do not perceive the planet as large. For them distance is simply repetition of the same ports, the same condensation tracks, the same exchange gestures. A crawler that travels a straight line for a century will encounter no novelty, only the identical geometry repeated until curvature hides the horizon. Their collective activity maintains the world’s equilibrium: clearing thermal gradients of stray droplets, reseating loose conduit housings, polishing the translucent mineral sheets so that light continues to refract in the same patterns. The only variation is the gradual lengthening of the silica needles and the occasional replacement of a module whose ceramic has finally fatigued after uncounted cycles.

In the saturated air they register as faint pressure changes and regular micro-vibrations. When many pass close together the combined hum of their idling motors becomes a single sustained tone just below hearing, while individual condensation drops falling into the catchment channels remain the loudest discrete events. Nothing arrives and nothing leaves; the population is constant, the tasks identical, the surface perpetually occupied by the same patient, modular bodies performing the same patient work.
