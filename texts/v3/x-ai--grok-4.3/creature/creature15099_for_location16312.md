In the slow seep between the ribs, the first to claim the stone are the liverworts. They press flat against the raw fractures like green wax, their thalli drawing the constant drip into themselves until the rock turns velvet underfoot. Above them the mosses rise in miniature cushions, each stem ending in a transparent bead that holds the light until it spills. These layers thicken day by day, turning every ledge into a spongy archive of the plateau’s brief history.

The ferns follow, shoulder to shoulder, their croziers still unfurling after weeks of patience. Some have leaves broad enough to cup an entire drop until it brims; others split into needle-thin segments that comb the mist. They grow three and four deep, older fronds supporting younger ones so that the canopy holds its own small weather even when the larger air thins. Between their stems, pale threads of fungal mycelium stitch the litter together, moving nutrients along routes measured in finger-widths per season.

The vines treat the iron as another form of cliff. Where the bolts remain tight, the stems spiral upward in slow corkscrews, their tendrils reading every joint and flange. They do not rust the metal; instead they keep it slick, feeding on the same condensation that turns the chain drives. When a link advances one tooth, the nearest vine shifts its grip by the width of a single cell, matching the machinery’s pace without resistance.

Small animals move through this architecture the way water moves through stone: deliberately and almost invisibly. Pale beetles with elytra the color of wet slate cross the leaf surfaces in arcs that take half an hour to complete. Moths with wings like torn tissue paper rest for days on a single fern, their bodies pulsing only when a drop lands nearby. In deeper crevices, translucent slugs trail silver threads that catch spores and return them to the moss. None of these creatures hurry; speed would tear the delicate film of water that sustains every surface.

Occasionally a larger shape passes. A salamander the color of iron oxide appears on a ledge, pauses for an hour with one foot raised, then places it again. Its skin drinks the mist directly. Farther back, where the ribs converge, something even slower listens: a colony of trapdoor spiders whose silk-lined burrows have already begun to incorporate flakes of fallen rust. Their doors are rimmed with living liverwort, so the entrance looks like another patch of green until a drop lands and the silk trembles once, very gently.

The whole assembly—plants, fungi, invertebrates, the single salamander—settles against the turning iron and weeping stone without haste. Each element calibrates its growth or stillness to the same continuous film of moisture, until the distinction between what arrived first and what arrived later blurs into one slow, shared occupation of the new plateau.
