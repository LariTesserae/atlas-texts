The shelf is not empty. It is occupied by entities that share its refusal to complete any cycle.

The rosettes stand at measured intervals like sentinels that have never learned to sway. Each is a single organism whose leaves are not separate blades but concentric, overlapping plates of living tissue, thick as hide and veined with threads of native metal that conduct a slow, internal current. At the center of every rosette sits a shallow cup that never overflows and never dries; the water held there is the same temperature as the stone and tastes faintly of the same iron. From the rim of this cup rises a single, hair-thin filament, translucent and faintly luminous, that extends upward three or four body-lengths before dissolving into the mist. These filaments are not reproductive structures; they are listening organs. When the rock fractures elsewhere along the ridge, the filament registers the new seam as a change in pressure and the rosette, without moving, adjusts the metallic striations within its leaves so that light falls differently across the shelf for the next several centuries.

Between the rosettes move the slate-colored myriapods. They are not many individuals but expressions of one distributed body whose segments can detach and rejoin without loss of continuity. A single segment may travel the width of the shelf over decades, its legs contacting the stone in perfect time with the others it cannot see. Their antennae are not sensory whips but tuning forks; the constant industrial roar of the engines provides the reference tone against which they measure any new vibration arriving from a fresh fracture. When such a vibration reaches them, the nearest segments alter their frozen posture by a fraction of a degree, and that infinitesimal shift propagates through the entire connected population across centuries.

The translucent slugs are simpler but no less bound to the equilibrium. Each carries within its gelid body a single crystal identical to the pipes that branch from the buried engines. The crystal draws faint light from the pipes through some unseen resonance and stores it as a faint internal glow. When the slug moves, the glow leaks behind it in a trail that lasts only minutes before the stone reabsorbs it. These trails are not waste; they are temporary maps. Later segments of myriapods will cross the same ground and read, through contact alone, the pattern of recent movement, confirming that no part of the shelf has yet been allowed to fall out of phase.

At the very edge of the shelf, where the raw stone drops away, stand three figures that are neither plant nor engine nor animal. They are upright, roughly human in height, formed of the same young rock as the ridge yet visibly articulated at the joints. Their surfaces are smooth where the myriapods crawl and grooved where the slugs leave their luminous paths. These figures have no faces and emit no sound of their own; they simply face outward over the void, absorbing the continuous roar of the turbines and the minute scraping of countless legs. When a new fracture opens, one of the three will eventually tilt its head a single degree toward the sound. The movement is so slight that only the filaments of the nearest rosette detect it. Nothing else acknowledges the change.

Nothing here is born and nothing here dies. The inhabitants persist in the same manner as the water films and the engine-roar: by remaining exactly themselves while the ridge continues, very slowly, to become larger.
