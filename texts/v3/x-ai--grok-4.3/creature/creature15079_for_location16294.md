In this half-formed court, the residents move as extensions of the architecture rather than visitors to it. The rodents—translucent and scarcely heavier than the vapor they drift through—rarely touch the ground at all. They gather in floating knots, each animal’s tail woven into the next until the cluster resembles a single, slow-pulsing cable suspended between two leaves. When one knot drifts too near another, tails unbraid and re-braid mid-air, exchanging individuals without a sound; the exchange looks less like migration than like thread being passed between two looms. Their eyes are only faint refractive points, and when they blink in unison the entire cluster dims for a moment, as though the darkness between them has momentarily thickened.

The salamanders keep to the inner skins of the voids, flattened against surfaces that are still deciding whether they are leaf or steel. Each is no wider than a finger, and when one pauses to lap at a film of condensation its body becomes almost invisible except for the minute ripple it leaves in the moisture. They travel in patient circuits, following the same route for hours, but the routes themselves lengthen or shorten according to the breathing of the chamber; an individual that began its patrol on what seemed a flat wall may finish it on the underside of a curving gantry that was not there when it started. Their skin carries a faint charge that makes the new mineral rinds crystallize more quickly beneath them, so that their paths are traced in hairline ridges of soft stone.

The moths are slower still. Their wings are dusted with grains they have gathered from the precipitating edges of the court, and each slow flap releases a few particles that settle exactly where a seam is widening. They do not seem to steer so much as to consent to the air currents the leaves generate; a moth may take half an hour to cross a single void, and when it lands it does so on the very line where two mineral rinds are about to meet. There the wings fold into a motionless roof, and the grains continue to sift downward while the moth itself appears to sleep.

These three kinds of animal rarely meet directly. Instead they leave traces for one another: a salamander’s damp track across a newly formed ledge becomes a landing site for a moth; a moth’s dust softens the stone so that a rodent’s tail can later find purchase when it lowers itself to drink. The rodents, in turn, occasionally drop a single bead of condensation from their braided tails onto a leaf edge, which the salamanders later harvest. The exchanges are so slight they register only as adjustments in the general humidity.

Because the voids flex and the distances lie, an individual may spend its entire life inside what appears, from outside, to be a space no larger than a wardrobe. Yet within that wardrobe the chamber keeps offering new galleries and fresh seams, and the animals continue their circuits without ever appearing to notice they have not left home. The only evidence that the population is growing is the gradual thickening of the braided cables, the increasing number of pale ridges on the walls, and the finer dusting of mineral on every horizontal leaf. Nothing is finished, so nothing has to be named; the creatures remain simply whatever is moving at the moment the next drop falls or the next wheel clicks once more in the dark.
