In this place the dominant inhabitants are not animals at all but the immense, slow architectures of vegetation that have already begun to think like organisms with intentions. The thick olive and copper fronds function as both body and dwelling; their internal canals pulse with faintly luminous sap that draws light from the surface shafts and carries it downward into dim alcoves where smaller filaments take root inside the hollow stems themselves. These inner threads are not parasites so much as extensions of a single shared metabolism, glowing softer and greener the deeper they sink, until entire vertical galleries are veined with cool, living lanterns.

Drifting among the uppermost buoyant mats are the bell-shaped grazers, each one a translucent envelope of membrane stretched over ribs of cartilage that flex only once or twice in an hour. Their slow contractions draw water through fine internal sieves, harvesting the motes of organic matter that settle like perpetual snow. Because the mats themselves rise and subside on the same long rhythm, the bells rarely need to swim; they simply adjust their buoyancy to remain inside whichever layer currently offers the richest rain of particles, letting the architecture do most of the work of positioning them.

Clinging to the undersides of the interlocking plate-leaves are the thread filterers, so attenuated that a single individual can span the width of several drifting ceilings. Their bodies are little more than knotted nerve cords sheathed in cilia that beat in perfect, minute waves, drawing water through the narrow gaps between plates. When a ceiling slowly fissures and reforms, the threads simply detach one end, drift with the new current for a few body-lengths, and re-anchor; the rearrangement is so gradual that they treat the shifting architecture as weather rather than catastrophe.

Smaller still, and visible only as a continuous faint scintillation within the sap, are colonies of microbes that have colonized the light-conducting tubes. They arrange themselves in chromatic gradients along the inner walls, turning each pale stalk into a living prism that splits incoming shafts into the precise wavelengths their host frond prefers. In return the stalks thicken and stiffen around them, creating permanent galleries whose walls are half plant tissue, half microbial metropolis.

All of these forms move at the same geological tempo. When two large fronds press together and fuse along a margin, any bell grazer caught between them simply compresses its envelope and waits, sometimes for hours, until the plates ease apart again. The thread filterers ride the temporary turbulence like passengers on a very slow tide. Nothing here flees or pursues; presence is registered mainly by a slight local dimming of the internal light or a momentary change in the drift of organic snow. Over days the plateau continues its imperceptible breathing, corridors narrowing and widening, galleries brightening or darkening as the collective metabolism shifts, and every inhabitant—whether frond, bell, thread, or luminous colony—remains exactly where the living architecture last placed it, still becoming together.
