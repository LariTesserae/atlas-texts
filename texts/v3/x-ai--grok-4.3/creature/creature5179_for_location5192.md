The hollow is not inhabited so much as it is occupied from within, the way a lung is occupied by its own alveolar tissue. What lives here is a single, sprawling plant body whose architecture is both its own shelter and its own digestive tract. The rope-like stems and wide fronds interlock at every scale because they were never truly separate; cytoplasmic bridges stitch them into one continuous symplast, a shared cytoplasm that flows, however slowly, from the outermost frond pressed against dripping dolomite to the deepest pillar bracing the low ceiling. Water does not merely wet this body; it is drawn through living channels, filtered, enriched with trace minerals leached from unfinished rock, and redistributed according to metabolic rhythms measured in days. The persistent film on every surface is therefore not condensation alone but the visible edge of an internal circulation that never stops.

Embedded in that same body are the pale, furred animals. They do not move through the lattice so much as they are carried within it; each tunnel they press is a temporary airway kept patent only as long as their passage requires. Behind them the plant cells gently reseal, the disruption already being metabolized into new cytoplasmic connections. The animals’ subsonic calls propagate as pressure waves through fluid-filled cells rather than air, so that a single long groan registers first as a faint tightening in the stems themselves—an adjustment of turgor—before the sound reaches any ear. In this way the creatures serve as both passengers and low-frequency sensors, their bodies registering minute shifts in the dolomite overhead or in the slow thickening of load-bearing pillars. Their fur, translucent and fine, is itself interwoven with thread-like hyphae that extend from the surrounding stems; the “fur” is as much plant as animal, a living interface that conducts nutrients and signals in both directions.

Nothing here reproduces in the hurried sense of flowering or laying eggs. Instead the entire mass expands by cell division that adds a few hundredths of a millimeter per day to stem girth or frond thickness. The fracturing of dolomite is therefore not violence but accommodation: as the lattice pushes outward it reabsorbs the released mineral ions directly into new cell walls, converting the very rock that once confined it into structural reinforcement. Over human lifetimes the ceiling lowers imperceptibly while the floor rises; the distinction between plant and substrate is already meaningless in places, where mineral crystals remain suspended like inclusions inside translucent stem tissue.

Light arrives only from the slow, violet-grey pulses inside the veins. These pulses travel along the same cytoplasmic continuum, so a change in one chamber is registered, after many hours, as a corresponding shift in bioluminescence elsewhere. The animals orient themselves to these long rhythms, inflating their vocal bladders in phase with the faint glow or withdrawing deeper when it dims. In the intervals between pulses the hollow returns to near-darkness and the only motion is the deliberate fall of droplets from one leaf surface to another, each impact sending a minute hydrostatic ripple through the connected tissues.

Because the lattice is still deciding its final shape, chambers can close or new ones open according to slow gradients of turgor and mineral availability. An observer who remains long enough begins to sense that the low ceiling is not fixed overhead but is simply the current upper limit of the same living volume that extends downward through every pillar and frond. Floor, wall, and ceiling are therefore not locations but momentary densities within a single, cold, breathing tissue whose original genetic designers—if they ever existed as separate beings—are now dispersed into the continuous cytoplasm itself.
