The people of the ridge move like extensions of the growth around them—lithe, earth-toned figures whose bare feet know every spring of root mat and every slick stone terrace. Their skin carries the faint green stain of crushed leaf and the pale bloom of constant moisture; hair is cropped short or braided with strips of bark that have begun to sprout tiny rootlets where they touch the scalp. They speak in low, overlapping murmurs that blend with the drip and the insect drone, never raising their voices above the constant background. Children are indistinguishable from the architecture at first glance: small bodies wrapped in living sashes of supple vine that adjust their own grip as the child climbs, learning early which stems can bear weight and which must be left to thicken. The elders, marked by calluses that run in curving lines down their arms from decades of guiding new growth, spend long stretches simply listening—palm pressed to a wall, head tilted—to learn where a chamber has grown too tight or where a lattice needs another splice of fresh runner.

Animals share the same habits of belonging. The quick-furred climbers, no larger than a house cat, have elongated forelimbs and gripping pads that leave faint resinous prints on the upper lattices; they descend only at dusk to drink from the stone basins and to drop half-eaten fruits whose seeds take root in the leaf mold below. The tiny birds—plumage the exact muted green of the photosynthesizing walls—nest inside hollowed nodes of the oldest vines; their eggs are patterned like water droplets on broad leaves. Even the insects seem less like visitors than circulating blood: the iridescent beetles follow regular patrol routes along the vine walls, scraping away excess algae; the larger cicadas time their heaviest chorus to the moment when afternoon rain begins to slacken, as if the sound itself helps the excess water drain away.

Nothing here is truly wild or truly tame. When a new family needs space, a pair of young people will coax two parallel vines to arc toward each other over several seasons, then weave sapling cross-braces between them; within a year the structure has thickened and darkened, and the couple’s first child will be born inside a chamber whose walls still remember the touch of human hands. The hamlet breathes with them: vents open in warm spells, close during cold nights; water channels reroute themselves when roots shift. The people, in turn, have grown subtle adaptations—a slight webbing between the outer toes that helps them cross flooded lanes, an instinctive reach for the exact spot on a leaf where condensation is thickest. Together they form a single slow metabolism, neither fully human nor fully forest, that has learned how to stay cool, damp, and alive on the side of a ridge that never quite dries.
