The people who live along the ridge call themselves the Settled, though the name is half-ironic; nothing here has finished settling. They move through the scaffolds and cantilevered sheds with the same unhurried patience as the mills themselves, their limbs long and slightly bowed from a lifetime of leaning into the constant tilt of new stone. Most wear layered coats of woven mica-fiber and condenser silk that bead water automatically and turn nearly transparent when wet, revealing pale skin mapped with faint blue branching veins—marks left by the low, constant voltage that threads through the copper braids of the city. Their eyes have adapted to the perpetual half-light; the irises are wide and almost colorless, rimmed with a thin gold that catches the blue lanterns like a second reflection.

They do not build homes so much as claim pockets between platforms where the rock has already fractured into shallow alcoves. Inside these spaces the walls are still streaked with the pale quartz of their making. Families string hammocks from the same rivets that hold the scaffolds; children sleep suspended above the slow-turning gears, rocked by vibrations too deep and slow for adult nerves to register. Meals are taken on narrow ledges where fern gardens have been coaxed into edible varieties—sharp, metallic-tasting fronds that grow only on iron-stained stone. The grazers are milked rather than hunted; their six-legged gait produces a thin, luminous fluid that tastes of ozone and is fermented into a drink that keeps the body warm through the long, never-quite-dark nights.

The Settled do not fight the mountain’s ongoing birth. When a new fracture opens they halt work for hours or days, waiting to see whether the fresh ledge will hold scaffolds or merely become another fern garden. Some among them—called listeners—press their ears to the raw stone for entire shifts, reading the deep groaning for signs of imminent collapse or unexpected stability. They carry no clocks; time is measured by the rotation of the house-sized gears and by the hatching cycles of the luminous larvae, whose collective ticking rises and falls like a second pulse beneath the ridge’s bass.

A smaller population lives lower, clinging to the undersides of the largest platforms. These are the Carapace Folk, fewer and more reclusive, who have learned to share space with the beetle-turbine creatures whose vibrations they augment rather than resist. Their skin is thicker, almost chitinous in patches, and they communicate in subsonic hums that travel through the iron itself. They harvest the condensation films for the floating larvae, sorting the luminous dust into slow-burning lamps and into a paste that seals new fractures before the mountain can decide to widen them. At the edges of their territory, where platform meets raw col, the two groups meet without ceremony, trading vials of larva paste for bundles of fern fiber and brief exchanges of information carried in the timing of a sigh.

The larval swarms themselves are tended rather than exterminated; the Settled have learned that when the humming thins too far the condensation ceases to gather and the small fern gardens wither. In return the swarms seem to linger longer over human skin than over bare stone, as though tasting the faint metallic salts the people carry in their blood. Occasionally a particularly dense cloud of them will follow a single listener for an entire shift, orbiting their head like a living halo before dispersing again into the mist.

Nothing here is hurried except the rare, catastrophic birth of new cliff faces, and even then the silence that follows lasts longer than the roar. The Settled have grown to prefer it that way. Their gestures are economical, their speech sparse and spaced like the measured clicks of the grazers’ hooves. They have learned, without ever stating it aloud, that the ridge is still deciding what shape it will finally take, and that any life grafted onto it must be willing to wait out the decision.
