The plateau’s true residents are not the relics of its buried engines nor the water that keeps returning to them. They are the generations that have grown across and between those fixed pulses.

The mat-plants themselves are not individuals. They form slow, contractile webs whose vein-ribbed leaves rise and subside like a single diaphragm, drawing the constant film of condensate toward the warmest seams in the rock. When something heavy crosses them, the leaves do not merely glow; they shift their internal currents so that the light travels ahead of the disturbance, a brief corridor of pale green that closes again once the pressure has passed. Their roots do more than drink. Certain thread-like laterals actually enter the seams around the catalytic cores, tasting dissolved metals and carrying trace amounts back to the surface where other mats can use them to thicken their spongy stems. In places the stems have fused into low, rubbery arches that shelter the intakes of the engines from silt. The plants did not plan this; they simply kept the channels open season after season until the channels began to answer.

Among the mats move the first animals that learned to read the same language. The slowest are the shelf-grazers: broad, low domes of dense fur so fine and water-laden that they appear almost liquid until disturbed. Their bodies contain no eyes, only paired cushions of pressure-sensitive tissue along the belly that register every shift in the trembling stone. As they feed they produce a continual soft vibration, less a call than a rolling purr that the leaves interpret and amplify for a few seconds, marking safe, mineral-rich zones. When they rest they settle into slight depressions they have worn over centuries, depressions that now guide runoff directly to the root webs beneath them.

Faster, segmented forms patrol the same carpet. These carry overlapping plates of a dark, flexible chitin that conducts the engine thrum better than flesh. Their forward edges bear rows of fine sensory pits that flare open when the ground’s resonance changes; a grazing mat-web answers one frequency, a wounded or heavy body answers another. They move with sudden straight-line bursts followed by long stillness, and their only consistent sound is the wet click of plates resetting after each lunge. They do not chase so much as arrive where the pressure signature has already told them something edible or vulnerable has paused.

Larger, rarer shapes cross both. They are the ones whose calls carry through the stone itself. Their bodies are mostly fluid-filled sacs suspended within loose cages of mineralized cartilage, buoyed by the very warmth the engines release. When they exhale, the vapor they release carries not only their own metabolic signature but a faint metallic tang drawn from the vents they deliberately seek. The smaller creatures give way long before the larger ones arrive; the mat-webs dim their light in the precise corridor the great shapes will travel, conserving energy and revealing a clear path. Once the passage is complete the leaves brighten again, and the whole surface resumes its slow exchange of heat and motion.

Nothing here breeds in the ordinary sense. The mat-webs bud new runners wherever a grazer has left compressed warmth and nitrogen. The chitin-plated hunters split when their plates can no longer accommodate the metals they have sequestered from their prey. The fluid-bodied giants simply thin and divide over decades, each new segment drifting away along the faint gradient that leads toward fresher vents. The plateau does not need more than this measured replication; its darkness and continuous warmth already supply the surplus that elsewhere would be spent on eyes, flight, or fear.

The only true stillness belongs to the catalytic cores themselves, and even they are no longer separate from the life they once powered. Their exhaust has become one more exhalation among many, indistinguishable in the dark from the breath of the fluid giants or the slow opening of a mat-web’s newest vein.
