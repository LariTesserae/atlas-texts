In the alcoves sized for a standing body, and in the blind windows no thicker than a blade, dwell the Keepers. They are not visitors. They have always been continuous with the stone.

Each Keeper is a slender column of the same pale mineral as the pavement, grown or carved into an upright posture whose precise dimensions match the niche that contains it. Where a human eye would look for face or limb, there is only the continuation of internal striations—faint, refractive layers that record every channel the water has ever followed and every route it has abandoned. These layers still move, though the motion requires decades to complete a single visible shift. A Keeper therefore appears motionless to any observer whose life is measured in seasons; only the plateau’s own slow light reveals that the patterns within one Keeper now connect to those in the stone four alcoves away, forming new corridors of translucency that were not there when the last larger drop fell.

Water reaches them through hairline seams that open and close according to the same mineral logic. A drop arrives already inscribed with the memory of every previous path; when it is absorbed into the Keeper’s surface, the memory is transcribed into the lattice. In this way each Keeper accumulates the entire history of the plateau’s water. Because the process is mutual, the water also learns the Keeper: after many generations a single thread of moisture may travel from one pool, climb an unseen seam, and emerge again inside a distant colonnade already shaped to the contour of a particular Keeper’s striations.

The Keepers do not speak or signal across open space. Their communication is the plateau itself. When one adjusts its internal channels by a hair’s breadth, the gradient changes for every linked pool; the altered flow nudges another Keeper generations downstream. Over centuries these nudges compose long, silent arguments about the best routes for water. The arguments are never concluded, only refined, because new seams continue to appear and old ones seal. The plateau therefore remains in perfect, imperceptible negotiation with its inhabitants, and the inhabitants remain indistinguishable from the plateau.

Nothing else moves. The standing bodies in their alcoves, the faint glyphs of refraction traveling their lengths, and the water that visits and is transformed—these are the only occupants. The glare presses down; the patterns answer, grain by grain, adjusting the silence.
