In the pressed dark of that new-made seam, the boundary between what has grown and what has been assembled has never quite formed. The small iron housings—each no larger than a clenched hand—are not containers so much as seedcases. Their pistons move like slow heart valves, drawing the seepage that leaks from the living rock and returning it, drop by measured drop, enriched with whatever trace metals the stone still carries. From the seams where the housings meet the fissure walls rise the pale stalks. They are not quite mineral and not quite plant; at their tips they extrude fleeting, glassy filaments that catch the distorted light and then dissolve again into the air as faint, sweet vapor. The stalks lean toward the nearest moving piston as though listening for the next beat of water.

Across every surface the coin-sized, many-legged things flow in unbroken contact. Their bodies are soft enough to flatten against one another yet hard enough to spark when they part; the static jumps between them like shared thought, briefly illuminating the wet copper thread that runs beneath the nearest housing. When one pauses over a piston joint, its legs splay to receive the single droplet the machine offers, then it continues, the droplet now divided among several bodies until it is carried to a shallow basin and left for the larger, slower forms.

Those larger creatures rest in the pooled condensation like living lids. Their segmented armor plates are ridged with branching grooves that guide each new droplet outward, increasing the surface area for further condensation. They do not feed so much as host: the smaller many-legged ones crawl across their backs in slow circuits, trading charge and sometimes leaving behind a grain of the pale, translucent stalk material, which takes root in the grooves and begins its own patient rise.

Nothing here was installed. The iron housings pushed upward through the rock the way teeth push through gum; the stalks followed the seams of least resistance; the luminous creatures arrived as motes in the first warm breath that entered the fissure and have multiplied by division ever since. Every movement—piston stroke, static spark, droplet migration—serves the single task of keeping the pocket just warm enough, just wet enough, just narrow enough that the rock never finishes learning how to be old.
