The chamber sustains only two forms that could be called alive, each shaped by the narrow tolerances of pressure, light, and motion that have held for longer than any surface season.

The lichen tufts are not truly plants but slow crystallizations of dissolved silica that fix themselves at every obtuse joint where the dolomite has been gentled by water. They drink nothing; instead, each grain-thin filament draws a few molecules of the migrating film as it passes, converting the brief contact into an internal lattice that lengthens by microns between energy bursts. Their continuous trembling is not response to air but the visible evidence of this lattice lengthening and relaxing in micro-rhythms too small to measure. When a light-pinch erupts nearby, the tufts release a momentary fizz of dislodged silica particles that drift a finger’s width before resettling; over centuries these particles contribute to the same glassy composite from which the ruined arches are formed. In this way the lichen slowly repairs and extends the incomplete ribs, never finishing them, only softening their angles further until any new seam will accept another tuft. They are therefore both the chamber’s smallest colonists and its slowest masons.

Far rarer and more transient is the filament creature. It exists as a single, barely visible thread no thicker than a hair, pale with the same opalescence that runs through the rock. It does not swim or crawl; it is assembled on demand by the currents that an energy release momentarily liberates. When the water films lift into droplets and reverse, the creature condenses at the edge of a pool, rides the new flow for a few body-lengths, and then dissolves again the instant the films flatten and resume their former direction. In that brief transit it leaves behind an almost invisible trail of re-ordered salt, a faint adjustment to the next film’s path. The adjustment is so slight that it registers only after many such passages, yet over time these adjustments keep the shallow bowls from ever overflowing or drying. The creature therefore functions less as an animal than as a living calibration: a thread of the chamber’s own memory given momentary mobility so that the equilibrium may continue to remember itself.

Nothing else persists. Larger bodies would disturb the films or cast shadows that the pulsing veins cannot tolerate; smaller ones are absorbed into the lichen or the composite. The two residents together maintain the precise, self-correcting stillness that lets the rock remain both ancient and never finished.
