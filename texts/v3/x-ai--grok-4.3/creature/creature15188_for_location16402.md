The chambers are tended by pale, root-limbed beings who move as slowly as the dripping water itself. Their bodies are elongated and jointed in too many places, skin the color of new bone or the undersides of the largest leaves, faintly translucent so that a network of blue threads shows beneath—veins, or perhaps rootlets that have learned to carry blood instead of sap. Where their feet or hands rest too long on moss, the growth thickens into raised platforms or shallow basins without any conscious shaping; the moss simply recognizes their presence and answers it.

Smaller creatures share the same space and the same blood-color. Compact, six-limbed animals with dense silver-gray fur slicked flat by constant moisture crawl along the root bridges. Their eyes are completely black and set deep beneath heavy ridges of damp fur, protecting them from the occasional shafts of surface light that reach the upper terraces. They leave narrow, self-sealing trails through the foliage because the leaves close again behind them, drawn by the faint heat they carry. When two meet, they press their bodies side by side for several minutes, exchanging warmth and a low trilling vibration before parting.

Deeper in the larger basins live heavier forms that never climb. Their flat, muscular bodies are covered in overlapping plates of lichen-crusted hide rather than fur. These larger inhabitants move by rippling contraction, pressing against the saturated moss so that water squeezes out ahead of them in small glistening waves. Their mouths are hidden beneath folds of the same plate-like skin; they feed by pressing their entire ventral surface against clusters of roots and drawing dissolved minerals and sugars directly through absorbent pores. At intervals they rise into low arches and release clouds of fine mist from gill-like slits along their sides—respiration and irrigation at once.

All of these inhabitants share a common trait: none of them perceive the rock as separate from the plants. To them the sharp new walls are simply another kind of slow-growing surface, already beginning to be claimed by the same white-veined runners that cover everything else. When a fissure widens further and fresh cold air pours in, the root-tenders press new vines across the gap, not to seal it but to taste it, letting the shoots decide whether the new stone is hospitable. The whole population—fur, plate, and translucent root-limb alike—continues its quiet expansion as the chambers themselves continue to open, each generation of growth and movement making the space slightly more alive and slightly less like raw stone.
