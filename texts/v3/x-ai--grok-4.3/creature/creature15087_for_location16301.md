The plateau’s smaller travelers are not quite insects, nor quite crustaceans, but something that borrows from both without committing to either. Their bodies are long, jointed tubes the color of wet slate, each segment fringed with translucent filaments that test the air like whiskers. When they march—as they do almost without pause—the filaments interlock, turning the column into a single, undulating ribbon that can flow over sharp ridges or slip between vine and moss without ever breaking stride. At intervals one individual pauses, rears the first three segments, and extrudes a droplet of clear saliva onto a leaf; the droplet spreads, etches a hair-thin channel, and is reabsorbed within moments, carrying dissolved minerals back into the column’s shared gut.

Larger than these are the veil-walkers, slow-moving aggregations whose true shape is difficult to hold in the eye. Each is a loose federation of translucent, leaf-shaped lobes joined by threads fine enough to be invisible except when condensation beads along them. As they drift through curtains of foliage they rearrange themselves, sometimes flattening into a single broad membrane that drinks the weak coral pulses from the fungi, sometimes bunching into denser knots that push aside heavier vines. When the rock pulses and the air briefly doubles, a veil-walker may appear in two places at once for several heartbeats; the two images drift toward each other, overlap, and become one again without hurry, as though the creature has always understood that space here is only a temporary agreement.

Deeper, in the glowing seams, live the fracture scribes. They are not visible until the heat peaks and the motes of stone flake away; then they uncoil—thin, metallic filaments that catch the furnace light for the brief instant before the rock reseals. Each scribe is no thicker than a needle, yet they move in coordinated constellations, tasting the new surfaces the pulse has created and, in the same motion, guiding the next reformation. When a scribe finishes its work it withdraws into the fresh seam and becomes indistinguishable from the ore veins themselves, a living record of every microscopic adjustment the plateau has made since its birth.

Above all of them, half-submerged in the perpetual canopy, drift the resonators. They are immense, almost motionless sacks of membrane stretched over branching lattices of pale horn. The low, machinery-like vibration that fills the plateau is the sound of their breathing: each slow contraction draws in the metallic sweetness of dissolving stone and releases it again, charged with a faint charge that keeps the condensation from ever becoming rain. When the spatial warps occur, the resonators’ outlines ripple as though seen through heated air, yet they do not pause; they simply absorb the distortion the way a sponge takes water, then let it pass through and out again as another slow, ground-borne thrum.

None of these forms announce themselves as separate from the place. The segmented columns feed the veil-walkers with the minerals they collect; the veil-walkers, in turn, shade the fungi whose pulses guide the scribes; the scribes maintain the fissures that let the resonators keep breathing. Everything moves to the same quiet grammar of pause, pulse, and reformation, each life folded into the unfinished work of the plateau itself.
