In the phosphorescent half-light, the basin’s dominant life is a lattice of broad-leaved photophores that rise in staggered canopies, each leaf a slow pump of internal chemistry. Their veins carry a steady drizzle of condensed droplets that fall from one tier to the next until the lowest fronds release them into the root mesh like a private rain. When a leaf edge is torn, pale sap wells at once, hardening into a flexible scab within minutes; the wounded plant then flexes an entire stalk a few finger-widths aside, dragging its anchoring roots through the loose rubble so that the gap closes before the scent of the injury can spread far.

Among these moving stems live uncountable thin-winged clatterers whose wing membranes are the same slick texture as the leaves they brush. They do not fly in straight lines; instead they ricochet from one vein to another, each impact releasing a soft chime that travels through the whole stand. When disturbed, whole clouds of them lift at once, the sound like wet parchment being torn in overlapping sheets. Their bodies are translucent enough that the leaf-light shines through them, turning them momentarily into floating green scripts before they settle again.

Larger and slower are the soft-bodied herds that travel in rippling fronts across the ridges. Each individual is little more than a muscled tube cased in mucus; together they advance like a single poured liquid, mouths rasping in unison so that the vegetation ahead disappears beneath a churning froth of digestion. In their wake the ground is left glazed and steaming, already beginning to sprout new rootlets that fish the discarded fiber for nutrients. The herds themselves are never still; individuals on the trailing edge are continually shouldered forward again by those arriving from behind, so that the mass appears to breathe as it moves.

Below the surface churn runs a quieter traffic of colonial excavators. Their burrow mouths are ringed with stacked pellets of chewed stem and grit, each pellet angled so that warm mist condenses along the inner slope and runs inward to cisterns the animals keep filled. When a fresh basalt spine blocks their path they scrape at it with mandibles until a powder of black silt drifts down; this silt is gathered into new pellets and added to the growing causeways that thread between spines. The tunnels they abandon are quickly claimed by stridulators whose legs scrape the walls in rising and falling waves, the vibration traveling through the connected root lattice so that distant plants twitch in answer.

Everywhere the motion feeds on itself. A wave of soft herds clears a swath; within hours new stems elbow sideways to occupy the space, their roots prying stones loose that the burrowers immediately incorporate. A fallen clatterer is swarmed by smaller scavengers whose chewing produces a fine mist of fragments that settle as glistening points on the nearest leaves. Nothing pauses long enough to harden into permanence. The entire population—plants that drag their own roots, animals whose migrations last only as long as the vegetation ahead—exists as a single unsettled surface in which every organism’s small, constant adjustments keep the basin from ever repeating the same arrangement twice.
