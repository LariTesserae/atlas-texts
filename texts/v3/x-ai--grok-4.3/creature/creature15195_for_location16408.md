In the slow hours between the pumps' clanks, the long-legged invertebrates thicken into visible clouds. They are not one species but a shifting league of them: some no larger than eyelashes, their bodies pale as the calcite they graze; others the length of a finger, jointed legs splayed across the mineral rinds like living calligraphy. They move in the water film itself, skating on surface tension or pausing to rasp at the newest, softest layers of deposition. Where the sodium beams drift through mist, their outlines appear as brief, silver-threaded fractures in the light; between the beams they vanish again, leaving only the sensation that the ledges are never still.

The translucent fish keep a deeper rhythm. In the rust-colored sumps they hang suspended, bodies lit from within by faint, shifting glows that may be bacterial symbiotes or merely the last refraction of the distant fixtures. When a pump housing releases its next low vibration, the fish turn as one, a single collective twitch that sends fine sediment rising in veils. They feed on the constant rain of fragments—flaked calcite, rust dust, the occasional shed exoskeleton of an invertebrate that has finished its portion of the ledge. Their calls are pressure changes more than notes: brief compressions of water that travel through connected pools and return altered by the stone.

Between these animals the bacterial mats and thread algae maintain their own slow negotiation. The mats form at the warmest seeps, sulfur-yellow and slightly rubbery, pulsing with micro-currents generated by their own gas release. The algae trace the edges like faint script, advancing and retreating by millimeters with each decade of temperature fluctuation. Where both meet the industrial relics, they colonize the interior of housings and the undersides of flywheels, turning abandoned steel into living substrate. Condensation drips from these growths in measured intervals, feeding the pools below.

Unseen but implied by the crowding is a finer life: films of microbes suspended in every droplet, carried along fracture lines and re-deposited on fresh calcite skins. They ride the slow heave of the floor and the vibration of the machines, participating in the same patient conversion that turns unfinished excavation into living rock. Nothing arrives or departs quickly. Each organism, each layer, each sediment particle occupies its place for long enough that the distinction between occupant and architecture begins to blur. The chamber is not empty of life; it is overfull with forms that have learned to move at the speed of mineral growth.
