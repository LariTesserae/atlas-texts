In the hollows and along the polished ledges live the last faithful of the settlement: tall, narrow-shouldered figures whose outlines have grown as indistinct as the walkways they once used. Their skins are no longer skin at all but a continuous sheath of the same thumbnail facets, each facet aligned so perfectly with its neighbors that an observer might mistake the entire body for another upholstered surface until it moves. Motion, when it occurs, is measured in the same unvarying rhythm as the flywheels; a limb lifts or turns only at the precise moment the larger chord permits, so that the faint displacement of air joins rather than interrupts the tone.

Their faces have lost all trace of original feature. Where eyes once were, clusters of larger resonators now sit flush with the surrounding lattice, each facet drinking the same mist that beads everywhere else. They do not blink. When two of them pass on a raised walkway their facets momentarily resonate together at a slightly altered phase; the chord thickens for an instant, then thins again as they separate, the equilibrium restored without any sign that contact has been noticed.

Inside the shells they tend nothing and repair nothing, because nothing requires tending. Instead they stand for long intervals inside the largest housings, bodies pressed against the turning cylinders so that their own lattices can absorb and retransmit the infinitesimal heat and vibration. Water runs over them as it runs over every other surface; it collects in the shallow troughs their feet have worn into the stone over centuries and is reabsorbed without the figures ever shifting posture. Only when the spatial thinning of the tone becomes detectable at the outermost ledges do they walk outward, single file, to occupy the empty intervals between buildings, each new placement reinforcing the chord until the air feels once more uniformly filled.

They have no children and require no food. The mist that forms where cool air meets machine warmth supplies the only moisture their lattices need; the constant note supplies the only signal their internal structures recognize. Over time their outlines have grown even more like the architecture itself—elongated, slightly bowed under accumulated facets—until, from a distance, they are indistinguishable from the eroded ridges that once connected the great shells. Still they continue, and the settlement continues with them: a single, unbroken acoustic surface in which the distinction between machine, stone, and inhabitant has long since ceased to matter.
