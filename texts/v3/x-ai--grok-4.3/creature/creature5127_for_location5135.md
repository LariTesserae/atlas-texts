The plain is not inhabited. It is occupied from within, its every seam and capillary already claimed by forms that condense and shear as naturally as the rotors themselves.

In the wet galleries between plates live the shard-beetles, though beetle is only a borrowed word for what moves there. They are small, translucent lattices of stacked mica, each facet edged with living quartz abrasives that renew themselves whenever a leg-tip fractures. They never rest; they file. The grooves they cut weep mineral-laden water that immediately recrystallizes into fresh plates, so the beetles are always walking across yesterday’s work. When a mat of them lifts and glides to a new gap, the sound is a single prolonged sigh of ground glass. Individual beetles left behind do not die. They simply wait for the next shifting sheet to arrive and are incorporated without ceremony.

Between the rotors drift the suspended-droplet slugs. Each is a loose sack of iron-rich condensate held in tension by faint, shifting magnetic domains. When two meet they do not consume; they trade charge. A brief violet arc jumps between them, equalizing their internal potentials, and the air goes metallic for several seconds afterward. The exchange sometimes leaves one slug larger and one smaller. The smaller simply drifts until it meets another; the larger may split later, birthing a new, trembling droplet that grows its own membrane in minutes. Their waste—a pale, gritty slurry—falls in threads that are promptly scavenged by the beetles or drawn back into the rotors’ capillary jets.

Larger, rarer motions occur where the plain’s memory glitches. A rotor-field reverses for three or four turns, its bearings shrieking. Any droplet-slug caught across the shear plane is cleanly divided; each half seals within moments, the new edges already extruding fresh membranes. The beetles caught in the same reversal are crushed into paste that the weeping water quickly re-mineralizes into new plates. Nothing is lost, only redistributed. The reversed rotors then snap back to their original direction, and the grinding continues as though the interruption had been imagined.

Occasionally the detached shadows do not rejoin. They linger, drifting a handspan above the plates, still shaped like the rotor or crystal they left. These shadow-forms are cold to the touch and produce no sound of their own, yet the grinding of the plain grows momentarily louder wherever one passes, as though the machinery must compensate for the missing mass. When such a shadow finally dissolves—after hours or days—it leaves behind a faint film of extra condensate that the nearest beetles immediately begin to score into new grooves.

All of this occurs at once and continuously. Beetles file, droplets exchange charge, rotors turn and sometimes forget their direction, shadows drift and either return or thin into nothing. The only constant is the rearrangement: every creature, every mineral surface, every lingering echo is already where it must be, and the plain has no need to expand because nothing has ever been missing.
