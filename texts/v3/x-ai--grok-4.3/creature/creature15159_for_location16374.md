The inhabitants of this place move as if the rock itself has learned to dream.  

The smallest are pale, jointed things no longer than a finger, their translucent bodies threaded with faint silver veins that flex when they breathe. They travel in loose, drifting shoals across the bare salt corridors, their many legs never quite touching the ground at once; instead they ride the thinnest films of vapor rising from the fissures, tasting every molecule with the fine hairs along their undersides. Where the mineral mats grow thick they burrow only shallowly, just deep enough to leave a shallow trough that later fills with new crystal growth. Their herds are not fixed; a group may spend a century crossing a single basin, pausing for generations wherever a fresh vent opens. When two herds meet they do not fight or mingle; they simply pass through one another, the way mist passes through mist.

Larger than these, and far quieter, are the ceiling-gliders. Their wings are not skin but flattened lattices of the same filaments that carpet the floor, grown translucent and almost weightless. They never land. Instead they circle the upper vaults in slow, patient spirals, their undersides brushing the mineral mats with each pass and shaking loose microscopic spores that drift downward like invisible snow. The spores take root wherever they settle, extending the mats by another hair’s breadth over decades. The gliders themselves are almost entirely hollow; only a single dark filament runs the length of each wing, carrying nutrients gathered from the metallic air. They breed by detaching small, seed-like fragments from their own wing-edges. These fragments fall slowly, sometimes taking a human lifetime to reach the floor, and become new gliders if they land on an active vent.

Between and beneath both these kinds move still other forms, less easily seen. There are tubular creatures that live entirely inside the older, deeper crusts, their bodies no thicker than threads, pumping mineral-laden fluid from one chamber to another over centuries. Their slow peristalsis is what generates the faint metallic scent that never leaves the air. In the widest basins, clusters of sessile, disk-shaped organisms lie half-buried in the salt, their upper surfaces polished mirror-bright by the passage of countless wings overhead. They do not move, yet the sound that fills the halls is partly theirs: a continuous low vibration produced by the collective flexing of their internal crystal lattices, synchronized across thousands of individuals so that the entire floor seems to hum in response to changes in temperature miles above.

All of these populations share one rhythm. Their generations are not counted in years but in the widening or narrowing of the natural vents that thread the ceiling. When a vent closes, an entire herd may simply stop moving for decades, their metabolism slowing until they are almost indistinguishable from the mineral nodules around them. When a new vent opens—perhaps after a century—they awaken together, the sound of their renewed respiration rising like a distant tide until it merges once more with the unbroken drone that has never truly ceased.

Nothing here builds. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. The life that remains is only the final, patient occupation of a space that finished becoming long ago, still occupied by bodies that have learned to continue the work of stone.
