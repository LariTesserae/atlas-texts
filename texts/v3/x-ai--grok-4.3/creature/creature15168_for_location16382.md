The only living things here are small, translucent organisms no larger than a thumbnail. They press themselves flat against the sheltered undersides of the columns where the constant dripping leaves a faint, chalky residue. Their bodies are little more than watery films bounded by a glassy cuticle the same smoky color as the stone, so that even when they are present they register mostly as a slight thickening of the light. They do not crawl in any ordinary sense; instead they inch forward by extending and retracting minute crystalline spines, each movement timed to the arrival of a fresh droplet so that the shift is masked by the falling water.  

Between feedings they simply wait, flattened and motionless, while new layers of mineral film settle over them. The film is their only food. They dissolve it with an almost imperceptible secretion and draw the dissolved salts inward through the same spines they use for locomotion. Because the supply of residue is renewed at the same unvarying rate as their consumption, a single individual may remain on one ledge for decades without ever exposing itself to the open air or to the direct fall of drops.  

Most columns carry no trace of them at all. The few that do support perhaps three or four animals spaced so far apart that their territories never meet. When two happen to settle on the same formation they orient themselves along separate seams of the polygonal ground cover and never interact. Their stillness is so complete that the only evidence of their presence is an occasional, perfectly circular clearing in the mineral film—each clearing the exact diameter of one animal’s body and centered beneath a ledge where no drop has struck in many days.  

These clearings are the sole record the creatures leave. Everything else about the plateau—the weeping stone, the standing lenses, the unbroken hiss—continues exactly as before.
