In the suspended hush of the chamber, what persists are not inhabitants in any familiar sense but thin, translucent veils of filamentous algae that have claimed the highest ledges where a faint, unchanging gradient of light still reaches them. Each tuft is not a single plant but an interwoven colony of strands, some no thicker than a hair, others gathered into pale green ropes that trail a hand’s-breadth before ending in blunt, rounded tips. They do not grow so much as they hold position, exchanging ions with the motionless water at a rate so slow that centuries might pass before a strand lengthens by another millimeter. Their movement, when it occurs, is only the brief shudder of a single filament releasing a microscopic gas bubble, after which the thread settles back into perfect vertical alignment.

Clinging among the algae and along the lower mineral lattices live even smaller tenants: colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria that have infiltrated the freshly precipitated carbonate. These appear, to any eye, as nothing more than faint discolorations—subtle rust or sulfur-yellow washes—yet they metabolize the slow seepage itself, drawing energy from the trace minerals before the water can lock them into stone. Where the lattices branch, minute clusters of these microbes form living seams that keep the growing structures slightly porous, preventing them from becoming fully sealed. In return, the accumulating minerals offer the bacteria stable, sunless real estate and a continuous trickle of dissolved nutrients.

Scattered across the same surfaces, almost indistinguishable from the inorganic accretions, are occasional solitary sponges no larger than lentils. Each is a soft gray cushion whose oscula open and close at intervals measured in hours, drawing in the surrounding water only far enough to refresh its internal canals before closing again. They filter whatever stray particles descend from above—mostly fragments of dislodged algal filament or microscopic flakes of carbonate—and incorporate the inorganic matter directly into their own spicule scaffolding, blurring further the boundary between living tissue and the chamber’s architecture.

Nothing swims or crawls. Any creature that once required motion has either left or never arrived. The only other presence that could be called alive is a diffuse bacterial film suspended within the water itself, so dilute that it registers merely as a faint increase in viscosity. These free-floating cells drift exactly as the inert particles do, descending in the same unhurried lines until they encounter a surface and adhere, adding one more imperceptible layer to the patient mineral work.

Together these organisms occupy the chamber as a single, extremely slow metabolism: the algae converting the last available photons, the bacteria harvesting the seep, the sponges harvesting both, and all of them contributing, by their deaths and secretions, to the very lattices that continue to form around them. The result is not an ecosystem in any energetic sense but a kind of living stillness, an equilibrium that includes biology as one more sediment settling into place.
