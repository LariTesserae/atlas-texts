In this contained hollow the living architecture itself is the primary inhabitant. The thick-trunked evergreens and the broad-leafed vines form a single, unbroken organism that has been shaped once and then allowed to continue its own existence without further interruption. Their wood still grows, yet so slowly that a beam laid across an alley thickens by less than the width of a fingernail in a century; the same growth that once required axes and fire to guide now simply maintains the joints it was trained into, sealing them with new layers of xylem that match the older ones cell for cell. The moss on every roof drinks the nightly condensate and, in doing so, lengthens the roof’s surface by microns that are never noticed because every adjoining surface lengthens by the same amount at the same rate. The settlement does not house its trees; the trees are the settlement, holding their own form across centuries with the patience of heartwood.

Among and within these living frames move the smaller residents, whose paces have synchronized with the larger stillness. The pale salamanders occupy the carved channels where water traces its brief nightly circuit; they lie with gills half-open, each slow pulse of water through the filaments coinciding with the arrival of mist. Their skin, the color of wet limestone, blends so completely with the mineral film left by evaporation that only the occasional blink of a lid—once every several years—marks them as distinct from the rock they rest upon. The minute white birds have taken the undersides of the largest leaves as permanent roosts; their claws are hooked into the same vein patterns they were born gripping, and they shift weight from one foot to the other at intervals measured in seasons rather than hours. When they preen, the motion is a single feather at a time, the sound no louder than the settling of dust.

The translucent beetles traverse every lintel and root-ladder in a procession so gradual that each individual becomes a fixed point in the local geography. A beetle that began crossing a threshold when the first notches were cut may still be midway across, its elytra catching the unchanging light and returning it as a faint, steady gleam. Their larvae develop inside the same channels the adults crawl, feeding on the mineral residue and emerging only after the parent has completed its crossing. In this way the beetle population replaces itself without ever appearing to have moved.

Nothing enters or leaves this equilibrium except the mist that arrives and departs on its own schedule. The salamanders, birds, and beetles are sustained by that single rhythmic exchange and by the imperceptible flow of sap through the walls that surround them. They require no additional motion because every necessity—shelter, moisture, light, and the faint mineral nourishment left on every surface—remains constantly and identically present. Over time the distinction between structure and creature has blurred until the entire hollow reads as one continuous living surface: a slow, self-repairing skin of wood, leaf, scale, feather, and chitin held at the precise temperature and illumination where growth and preservation become indistinguishable.
