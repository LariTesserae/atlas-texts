The district is sparsely tenanted, and its tenants move as slowly as the surfaces they inhabit.

Most numerous are the threaders—small, pale organisms resembling elongated arthropods, though their segments are not strictly chitinous. Each body is a translucent filament no thicker than a fingernail, jointed at irregular intervals. They spend entire seasons traversing a single vertical seam or conduit edge, secreting a faint mineral film that helps the raw stone accept the composite materials laid across it. Their progress is measured in centimeters per week. When two threaders meet, they do not turn aside; instead they lie alongside one another for hours or days, apparently exchanging the tiny crystalline inclusions they have gathered. After such a meeting one of them often ceases moving altogether, its filament gradually incorporated into the seam it has been tracing, becoming part of the next slow ridge.

On the colder vertical faces live the plaque colonies—dark, almost black encrustations that are neither wholly lichen nor wholly engineered sensor. They expand by the breadth of a palm every few decades, their outer margins reading temperature gradients and micro-settlements in the rock beneath them. In return they damp certain frequencies of thermal contraction, preventing the sudden cracks that would otherwise interrupt the patient negotiations between stone and composite. Where a plaque colony has been established for a century or more, the wall it occupies shows a faint, persistent translucency even when the surrounding rock has returned to opacity.

Human presence is rarer and stranger. A few dozen people live in the stacked strata, though “live” is an imprecise term for how they occupy the place. They move at roughly the same pace as the architecture itself. Their dwellings are not rooms so much as intervals of permission: when a building’s uppermost level rotates a few degrees over several days, the inhabitants inside shift with it, rearranging themselves and their sparse possessions so that the slight change feels like a redistribution rather than a disruption. They rarely speak above a whisper, and many have learned to remain silent for weeks. When they do converse, it is usually to report an observation—that a conduit has lengthened overnight by the width of two fingers, or that a reflection in a condensation film now shows a corridor that continues for several meters beyond the wall that should terminate it. These reports are recorded on thin mineral wafers that are left in the seams for the threaders to carry away.

There are also rarer, less nameable occupants. In the brief windows when the mineral veins become translucent, slow-moving inclusions can sometimes be seen that are not crystalline fractures but elongated shadows with approximate human proportions. These shadows do not correspond to any physical body on either side of the vein. They drift for minutes or hours before the translucency withdraws and the rock returns to its ordinary opacity. Some residents believe these are previous attempts at habitation whose coordinates have not yet fully resolved in the ongoing negotiation between geology and structure. Others treat them as simply another slow gradient the district is still in the process of deciding whether to accept or absorb.

All of these forms—threaders, plaques, people, and the unresolved shadows—participate in the same measured exchange. None of them dominate the process; they merely continue it at their respective speeds, allowing the basin to keep becoming whatever it is still deciding to be.
