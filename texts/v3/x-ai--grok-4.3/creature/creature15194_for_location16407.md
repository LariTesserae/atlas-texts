No separate creatures move through this tissue-world, because nothing has remained separate for long. What persists instead are extensions and specializations of the same continuum, grown into forms that still register, at the very edge of perception, as distinct.

Along the inner walls of the low vaults, certain folds have thickened into opaque, lens-shaped bodies that hang motionless from the ceiling like inverted puddles. These gather what little light filters through the layers above and focus it downward onto clusters of thinner, almost translucent tubules beneath them. The focused light is not used for growth in the ordinary sense; it maintains a constant, minute difference in pressure between one side of each tubule and the other. The difference never increases enough to push fluid onward, yet it never equalizes. The lens-bodies and their receiving tubules therefore exist in a state of perpetual, infinitesimal tension whose only outward sign is a faint, steady vibration too small to be felt except as a change in the way condensation beads form and release.

Deeper, where the stacked strata have fused into solid masses hundreds of body-lengths thick, older vessels have narrowed until they are little more than hair-fine threads. Inside these threads, columns of cells have rearranged themselves into long, spiraled chains. Each cell in a chain touches its neighbors at only three points, leaving most of its membrane free. Because the chains are anchored at both ends to the larger, pressurized conduits, any slow pulse that travels through the main system causes the free membranes to flex slightly. The flexing changes the shape of the microscopic spaces between the cells, which in turn alters the rate at which dissolved compounds can diffuse in or out. Over days or centuries the change is imperceptible, yet it is enough to keep each chain chemically distinct from the tissue that surrounds it. The chains do not reproduce; they simply remain, their identity preserved by the fact that the surrounding mass cannot afford to dissolve them without also dissolving the pressure balance that sustains its own conduits.

On the angular crests where the surface is still sharp, the tissue has produced rigid, outward-growing spines no thicker than a finger. These spines do not branch. At their tips, however, the cells have died and their walls have mineralized, forming minute, glassy caps that catch and split the sliding films of water. Beneath each cap a single living cell remains, protected from direct contact with the air. That cell receives its nutrients entirely through the spine from the main mass below. Its only activity is to secrete a slow, continuous thread of new wall material that lengthens the spine by a fraction of its own diameter each year. The thread never branches or turns; it simply continues, so that older spines stand as straight, translucent needles whose length records the exact interval since the mass first unfolded.

Between these spines, in the shallow basins, the fringed mats that meet overhead have produced downward-growing curtains of the same material. The curtains do not reach the floor. Instead they stop short, their lower edges dissolving into a haze of individual cells suspended in the thin film of water that coats every surface. These cells remain attached to one another by the faintest strands of cytoplasm. When a droplet detaches from the edge of a curtain and falls, it carries a few of the suspended cells with it. Upon reaching the reservoir below, the cells rejoin the film and begin to secrete new strands, restoring the curtain’s lower margin to its previous contour. The process repeats without variation; the curtain’s height and the reservoir’s level therefore stay constant even while the material that constitutes both is continuously exchanged.

Throughout the entire volume, the original distinction between “growth” and “maintenance” has collapsed. Every structure described above continues to enlarge, yet the enlargement is exactly offset by the incorporation of its oldest parts into the surrounding matrix. The lens-bodies thicken by the same amount their supporting tubules are absorbed; the spiraled chains lengthen by the same increment that older conduit walls are lignified and rendered inert; the spines extend by the same distance their glassy caps are gradually overgrown and smoothed. The result is a population of forms that remain visible as forms only because they are always in the process of disappearing into the larger equilibrium that produced them.
