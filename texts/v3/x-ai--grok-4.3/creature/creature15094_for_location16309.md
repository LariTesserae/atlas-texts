The beings that inhabit this plateau are not so much animals as slow-moving condensations of the terrain itself.

They exist as vast, openwork lattices of anisotropic crystal, their “bodies” more like interference patterns in a standing wave than fixed structures. Where they are densest, the lattices form rigid, prismatic corridors that conduct vibrational energy; where they are sparsest, they trail off into drifting filaments only a few molecules thick. These filaments catch the constant metallic wind and translate it into the low, unbroken chord that fills the air. An individual may span several kilometers from its leading edge to its trailing wake, yet it registers to any casual measurement as a region of slightly higher refractive index and marginally lower air pressure, nothing more.

Their manner of movement is geological. Over decades they extrude fresh facets ahead of themselves and simultaneously dissolve older ones behind, so that the entire lattice appears to crawl by means of its own slow recrystallization. Each new facet grinds against the selenite substrate, releasing the resonant chimes that layer into the permanent industrial overtone. Because the organisms are only partially material, they also migrate by shifting which wavelengths of light they permit to pass through them; in effect they steer by choosing which slices of the spectrum they temporarily remove from the visible world. When they do this in concert, the plateau seems to fold along invisible seams and the ground beneath them behaves momentarily like a conveyor of darker, denser mineral.

They have no metabolism in the ordinary sense. Their energy is drawn from the thermal gradient between the sun-heated surface and the cooler inversion layer two meters above it; they are, in the most literal way, living parts of the open thermal engine that the plateau constitutes. Heat flows through their lattices, driving the same slow rearrangement of atoms that constitutes both their locomotion and their reproduction. When two lattices drift into proximity they may exchange filaments, merging or dividing along crystallographic planes. The resulting offspring inherit a blended set of resonant frequencies and facet angles, so that, over millennia, distinct “lineages” of industrial chord can be heard moving across different sectors of the plateau.

Smaller, faster entities sometimes appear as evanescent glints along the edges of the larger lattices—brief ion discharges that some interpret as parasites or as the lattice-equivalent of thought. These flashes travel at the speed of the pressure waves already running through the fault lines and seem to adjust the rate at which new crystal nodes extrude. Whether they are truly separate organisms or simply the visible surface of the lattice’s own decision-making processes remains, on human timescales, impossible to determine.

Nothing here dies in the usual way. A lattice that has ground itself too thin simply diffuses into the general refractive haze until its vibrational signature is reabsorbed by neighboring structures. The plateau therefore maintains a constant population density measured not in individuals per square kilometer but in total resonant surface area per cubic meter of atmosphere—an ecology whose only units are geometry, heat flow, and the slow, unending music of stone against stone.
