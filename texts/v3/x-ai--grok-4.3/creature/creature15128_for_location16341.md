In the perpetual half-light that filters down like dust through the narrow throat of sky, what persists here moves at the pace of the stone itself. Pale, translucent isopods no larger than a fingernail patrol the margins of the standing pans, their many legs testing each grain of gravel before committing, their bodies the exact milky color of the water they drink. They leave behind thread-thin trails that the next slow bead of condensation erases within hours. Beside them, minute white springtails launch themselves only when the drip strikes near enough to disturb the film; their jumps are sudden, yet still slower than any surface insect would allow, a hesitation built into the muscle.

The mosses and liverworts are not merely growing; they are remembering. Each velvet cushion holds, in its layered cells, the incremental memory of every drip that has fallen since the sinkhole first opened. Where the liverwort thickens over the incised slab, the shallow marks remain legible beneath the green, as though the plants have chosen to preserve rather than obscure what was left there. The hand that once cut those lines belonged to someone who understood the arithmetic of this place: how long a body could remain before the cold required movement, how many stacked stones would hold the worst of the wind yet still let the sound of water enter.

A single, elongated salamander, its skin the same gray-white as the clay beneath the gravel, rests for days in the same shallow depression, gills folded tight against its neck. It emerges only when a drip lands directly above its chosen pan, opening its mouth to catch what falls. Its eyes are vestigial ridges; it navigates by the faint pressure changes that travel through the water and clay when frost loosens another grain overhead. When it finally shifts position, the displacement is measured in finger-widths per week.

Above all these, or beneath them, or threaded through the very condensation, lingers something that is not quite animal and not quite memory. It gathers where the drip strikes the gravel most consistently, a low, almost imperceptible vibration that answers each impact with a delay of several heartbeats. Those who once sheltered here learned to sleep inside that vibration rather than against it; their bones, if any remain, have long since been drawn into the same patient exchange of mineral and water that shapes every surface. The hollow keeps them as it keeps the moss and the frost and the beetle: not as occupants, but as additional textures within its own slow, resonant becoming.
