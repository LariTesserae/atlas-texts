What lives in the cleft is mostly green and slow, tuned to the intermittency of water and the stingy ration of light. The ferns are the oldest residents; their rhizomes have threaded into every fracture wide enough to accept a thread, prying the stone by micrometers each season. Each frond is a single broad plane that can pivot at the base, angling itself toward the brightest sliver of sky visible from its ledge. When a droplet lands on the upper surface it beads for a moment, then the whole leaf tilts a fraction and the water runs along the midrib to the tip, where it falls onto the next lower plant. The ferns therefore act as both canopy and irrigation system, passing moisture downward in a staggered relay.

Between the fern trunks grows a dense understorey of thinner stems whose leaves are reduced to narrow straps or scales. These plants do not compete for sunlight so much as for the brief intervals when the air is saturated; their stomata open only while the humidity pulse lasts, then clamp shut again to conserve what they have drawn. Their roots are short, mostly exploratory mats that cling to the flakes of rock still attached to the walls. When one of those flakes loosens and drops, the mat tears but does not die; the plant simply re-roots on whatever surface it lands, whether stone or the back of another leaf.

Smaller life moves in the perpetual half-light. Pale woodlice the size of thumbnails travel along the undersides of the larger fronds, grazing the film of algae that forms wherever a leaf stays wet long enough. They leave faint silvery trails that dry within minutes. Minute springtails launch themselves from the moss hummocks whenever a drop strikes nearby; their jumps are soundless but visible as brief white flickers against the darker foliage. On the rock faces themselves, encrusting lichens in shades of dull olive and graphite grow in overlapping lobes, each lobe only a few millimeters across yet old enough to have survived several rock-fall events. Between the lichen patches, solitary snails no larger than peppercorns press their shells into shallow depressions, emerging only when condensation re-forms.

Nothing larger than a finger disturbs the mass for long. The occasional small lizard that wanders in from the outer slopes soon turns back; the cleft offers too little open ground for its preferred ambush tactics, and the constant low-level noise masks the tiny movements of its prey. The plants themselves close the space again within minutes, their stems springing back into the gaps the lizard’s passage created. In this way the inhabitants reinforce the same provisional architecture that shaped the cleft: growth fills every newly opened fissure, drops replace themselves on the same leaves, and the whole assembly continues its slow, muffled rearrangement.
