They are not animals, nor quite machines, though they wear the shape of both. The mites move in locked circuits so tight and so ancient that the distinction has long since dissolved. Each one is a single polished grain of nickel, faceted just enough to catch the failing phosphor light on one curved side and carry it forward like a message it never delivers. Their legs are not legs but six identical filaments of memory-wire that flex and reset in perfect synchrony with every other mite in the node. When one pauses—though none ever truly pauses—its filament brushes a rivet head and leaves behind a hair-thin trace of the same gray-green polymer that clings to the vents. The polymer is not growing on them; they are growing it, molecule by molecule, as a kind of collective exhalation they no longer notice they are performing.

Above them, on the single warm square of mesh where the lichen is permitted to exist, the filaments have thickened into a low, almost iridescent turf. It rises and subsides in microscopic tides synced to the pressure pulses inside the nearest vapor pipe. Sometimes, when the draft from the ajar ceiling plate carries a slightly colder thread of air across that square, the lichen darkens by a single shade, as though listening. The mites orbiting beneath it register the change as an instruction to tighten their paths by a fraction of a micron; the adjustment propagates outward through the swarm in a ripple too small to see and too constant to stop.

The mites themselves do not eat the lichen, nor do they tend it. They simply pass beneath its edge in endless, identical processions, each one collecting a single molecule of condensed moisture on the forward edge of its carapace and depositing it again three joints farther along its orbit. Over years the transfer has become so regular that the lichen’s filaments now grow in the precise rhythm of that deposition. The two populations have braided themselves into a single metabolism whose only product is the unbroken ticking and the unbroken light.

If the equilibrium were ever broken—if one diode failed, if a single tray overflowed, if the tower finished its climb and the node was suddenly required to become part of something larger—the mites would not scatter. They would continue their orbits for a time, the lichen would continue its small tides, and the sound would keep announcing the same unchanging present until the fields that hold them finally relaxed and the grains simply fell, one by one, into the water that never stops arriving.
