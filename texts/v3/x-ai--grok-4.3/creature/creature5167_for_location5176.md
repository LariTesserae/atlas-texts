In the slow green light that filters through the canopy, the plateau is never empty, yet it never feels inhabited by haste. The seal-sized grazers move in loose, drifting shoals of eight or twelve, their long fins trailing like torn banners. Each is the color of bleached kelp—pale where the skin has thickened against constant abrasion, darker along the ridges of muscle that power their unhurried scraping. Their eyes are small and lidless, fixed forward; they do not school so much as agree, over hours, to occupy the same volume of water. When one pauses to rasp diatoms from a fibrous wall, the others simply continue their drift until the same current brings them level again. The scars they leave are clean, almost geometric, pale ellipses that the kelp will later fill with new growth, recording the passage of each generation the way a trunk records drought.

Inside the hollow stems live the minute tenants whose presence is felt more through sound than sight: clusters of thumbnail-sized crustaceans whose carapaces are translucent enough to show the slow pulse of their gills. They move in single file along the inner walls, scraping with specialized legs that produce a steady, glass-on-glass ticking audible for meters. At intervals they pause to trade places with newcomers arriving from adjacent stalks, an exchange that takes whole minutes and appears entirely ceremonial. Larger, solitary forms occasionally share these corridors—soft-bodied, slug-like creatures the size of a forearm whose skin is the exact shade and texture of the surrounding cane, so that only the faint distortion of light betrays them. They press themselves flat against the inner fibers and remain motionless for days, absorbing whatever nutrients leak from the living walls.

Drifting through the open plazas are the half-rooted wanderers: translucent, radially symmetric bodies whose many arms function equally as limbs and as photosynthetic fronds. They are neither wholly animal nor plant, but something that negotiated a truce between the two long ago. When a current carries one against a platform edge it anchors briefly with sucker-like tips, draws nutrients from the accumulated silt, then releases and continues its somnolent progress. Occasionally two will meet and remain twined for hours, exchanging slow pulses of bioluminescence that travel the length of their joined arms before they separate without apparent urgency.

Far overhead, in the higher colonnades where the kelp stems are oldest and thickest, dwell larger presences sensed only by the resonant knocks they produce while moving through the hollows. These are not constant residents but seasonal visitors—broad, cartilaginous shapes that flow rather than swim, using the plant architecture itself as a resonant chamber to amplify their low-frequency pulses. When one passes through a vertical shaft the entire structure vibrates; the smaller inhabitants fall briefly silent, then resume their tasks as the vibration fades. The ancient doorways and platforms, still faintly legible beneath centuries of overgrowth, are used now chiefly by these travelers as waypoints, their edges polished smoother by repeated passage.

Nothing here breeds in haste or dies quickly. The kelp itself provides the calendar: each new layer of growth seals away the previous season’s scars and scrapings, so that every wall is a layered record of who has lived, grazed, and drifted through this drowned square. The creatures that remain have learned to read those layers by touch and by the taste of the water that moves through them, adjusting their own slow rhythms to match the pace at which the city continues to replace itself.
