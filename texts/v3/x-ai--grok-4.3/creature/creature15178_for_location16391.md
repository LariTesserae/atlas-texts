The plateau sustains a handful of human inhabitants whose lives have slowed to match the stone. They move in loose, widely spaced groups, never more than six or eight visible at once, each person dressed in layered, salt-stiffened cloth that has taken on the same bone-grey of the rock. Their work is mostly watching: they walk the iron walkways at the same pace as the water in the grooves, pausing for long stretches where a pipe has sweated through or where a chimney leans a fraction farther than it did the season before. Repairs, when they happen, consist of tightening a hoop or clearing a single channel with a hooked iron rod; the engines themselves are never stopped.

These people sleep in the long sheds, not in separate quarters but on narrow ledges welded high along the inner walls so that the slow swing of the flywheels passes beneath them like a tide. They eat what condenses. Wide, shallow pans set beneath the dripping ledges collect a thin mineral broth that they sip directly; the pale crusts on the gravel supply a drier, almost tasteless salt they crumble into the same liquid. Speech is rare and never raised above the constant mechanical murmur. When they do speak, it is usually to note a measurement—a chimney’s new angle, the exact minute a droplet has taken to fall from one ledge to the next—then the observation is left to settle into the general sound.

Among the smaller seams lives a different order of life entirely. In the cooler, darker fissures between the chimneys, translucent creatures the size of a thumb move through thin films of water. They are neither insect nor amphibian but something that combines both, their bodies almost entirely clear except for a faint internal thread of darker tissue that pulses once for every three revolutions of the nearest engine. They emerge only when the temperature drops a precise degree at night, feeding on the mineral film left behind by the seeping water. The workers rarely disturb them; a single careless boot would collapse an entire seam, and the loss would be felt years later in a change to the local condensation pattern.

Nothing here breeds quickly or travels far. The human population replaces itself by the same patient arithmetic as the settling of a factory roof: a birth or a departure registers only as a slight adjustment in who walks which stretch of walkway on a given night. Over decades the distinction between the living and the built blurs further. A worker who dies on shift is sometimes left where he fell; the slow accumulation of mineral crust eventually incorporates the cloth and bone into a new low ridge that redirects a trickle of water. The engines continue without pause, and the next walker simply steps over the new formation, noting its height in the same quiet tone used for every other gradual change.
