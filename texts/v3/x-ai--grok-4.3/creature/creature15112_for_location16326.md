In the latticework, the bore-worms are not single animals but a phase, a habit of matter that sometimes gathers enough continuity to move. They enter the tubes as films of slightly denser fluid, no thicker than breath on glass, and only after they have ridden an updraft for several cycles do they condense into the pale, ribbed shapes that leave the spiraling trails. Inside a trunk they travel for days without hurry, rasping the inner walls with microscopic crystalline teeth grown from condensed minerals they scavenge from the rain. What they excrete is not waste but a temporary glue that seals micro-fractures until the next surge of growth pries them open again. When two such films meet inside the same tube they do not fight; they simply share the direction and continue, their combined trail forking only when the tube itself divides.

The drifting spheres are their eventual form, or one of its forms. After a bore-worm film has moved through a sufficient length of tube it gathers at a node, thickens, and extrudes the sphere outward through a pore that heals behind it. Inside the sphere the remains of the film continue a slower version of the same journey, circulating in a closed loop. Some spheres drift until they lodge in a pool or a crevice; others rise until they reach a ceiling fracture and freeze there, becoming temporary lenses that bend the white light into narrow, shifting beams for a few weeks before they thaw and fall. When a sphere ruptures, its contents do not spill as liquid but unfold into another film, already oriented, already beginning to seek the nearest current of air or water.

Larger, rarer signs appear only after a lattice section collapses. In the sudden exposure of older, denser tissue, something else wakes. It is not a worm and not a sphere but a spreading opacity, a slow cloud of cells that had been suspended in the plant’s own sap. These cells multiply along the broken edges, feeding on the released moisture and on the brief drop in temperature. Within hours they begin to knit a temporary skin across the wound; within days the skin has differentiated into new plates and spines. The process looks like ordinary regrowth, yet the exact angles of the new plates match the fracture lines of previous collapses in distant parts of the hollow, as though the information travels through the same updrafts that carry the spheres. Observers who have lingered long enough to notice this pattern begin to suspect that the entire architecture is one enormous, very slow recording surface.

Among the human remnants the living system has already performed its quiet incorporation. The braided fiber has been drawn into the plant tubes and re-extruded as reinforcing strands inside newer growth; the bone needles have dissolved and reappeared as calcium-rich nodes where spines meet. The low ring of fitted stone is now half plant column, half mineral memory. No new tools have been made because none are required; any sufficiently patient visitor eventually discovers that a sharpened spine and a length of living tube can be coaxed to perform the same tasks more efficiently than the old bone and fiber ever did. The last deliberate marks left by earlier people were attempts to widen a light-shaft. The plant has since narrowed that same shaft again, not in hostility but because the new constriction produces a steadier, colder beam that favors the formation of spheres. The architecture is therefore not hostile to visitors; it is simply older than any distinction between host and guest.
