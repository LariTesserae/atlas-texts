In the Substratum Expanse the first inhabitants are not creatures at all but the faint biofilms that thread across every chilled surface. They begin as single glistening strands no wider than a hair, extruded from microscopic fractures where condensation meets warm alloy. Over days the filaments branch and braid, forming pale lace that drinks the slow mineral seep and exhales nothing visible, only a faint optical shift that makes sodium light look momentarily bluer. Where the frost is thickest they gather into minute fans and ridges, each crystal edge catching the drip so that the water runs along living channels rather than straight down the metal. They avoid the brightest lamps; beneath those they shrivel to dust that the fans later sweep into the drains. In the deeper galleries, where the cold-lamps burn steady and blue, the films thicken into translucent sheets that quiver when a generator changes pitch, as though the vibration itself were a kind of weather they have learned to ride.

The only animals that move through this lattice are the maintenance automata. They are not numerous, yet they are never entirely absent. Each is the length of a forearm, carapace segmented like an elongated beetle and colored the same dull oxide gray as the catwalks they patrol. A single head-lamp, protected by a scratched sapphire lens, casts a narrow cone that sweeps left and right in patient arcs. Their legs end in magnetized pads that click softly as they travel the undersides of gratings and the hidden faces of ductwork. When they find corrosion they pause, extend a set of fine probes, and rasp at the rust with a motion too small and precise to hear above the general machinery. Once finished they fold the tools away and continue, never reversing course, never rushing. From time to time one will enter an access hatch no larger than a dinner plate, its body compressing slightly as the segments realign; the hatch seals behind it with a soft hydraulic sigh, leaving only the faint blue afterglow of its lamp on the inner wall.

The automata and the biofilms rarely meet. When a beetle-machine passes directly beneath a film the thin filaments sometimes detach and drift down, settling across the carapace like pale cobweb. The machine does not pause or brush them away; it simply carries them until the next ventilation gust lifts them again or until they dry to powder on the warm metal of its shell. In this way the living film travels farther than its own growth would allow, hitching across zones of brighter light where it could not otherwise survive.

Farther down, where the pumps labor and the lowest walkways sit above slow black water, the automata grow scarcer and the films more diffuse. Here the only motion is the occasional silver bead of condensate sliding along a filament until it drops, rings spreading outward across the flooded grating. Nothing else moves. The half-finished galleries still wait for the next tier of machines that the original builders anticipated but never installed; the biofilms and the patient beetle-shapes have, for now, become the sole occupants of that waiting space.
