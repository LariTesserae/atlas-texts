The city’s inhabitants are not visitors to its mineral lattice; they are the lattice’s slower, repeating thought. They condense where the water films are thickest and the vibrational gradients steepest, emerging first as a faint opalescence along the seam-lines, then thickening into bodies whose composition matches the surrounding silicates so precisely that the distinction between wall and occupant is legible only by motion.

The smallest of them are not discrete animals but traveling precipitates—thread-thin filaments that slide through the capillary fissures like living solder. They carry charge differentials from one crystal domain to another, equalizing stresses before the next scheduled shear. When two filaments meet they do not reproduce so much as braid, exchanging silicate ions until one filament lengthens and the other shortens; the shorter one dissolves back into the wall, its pattern of charge now preserved in the longer. Their passage leaves momentary rainbow fringes where the water film is momentarily disturbed, then the lattice reclaims its smoothness.

The segmented forms that follow the condensation channels are longer-lived and more visibly animal. Their jointed carapaces are grown in place rather than assembled; new segments bud at the tail end while older ones at the head are slowly resorbed and their mineral mass returned to the city. Each segment is tuned to a narrow band of the city’s resonant frequencies; when a distant actuator array begins to shift a block, the creatures align themselves along the seams like living dampers, their bodies flexing in counter-phase to bleed off unwanted vibrations. They move in trains of dozens, nose to tail, the lead individual testing the film thickness with forward-facing crystalline tufts, the rest simply following the altered surface tension.

Larger still are the reef-like drifters that cross the broader plazas. Their undersides are not limbs but continuous, faintly glowing cushions of ionized gas held in place by micro-currents drawn from the power lattice itself. They do not steer so much as yield: when an entire district begins its daily tilt, the reef settles into the new slope, accumulating fresh condensate until its mass increases enough to trigger a slow, stately lift toward a different thermal gradient. Their upper surfaces are never still; they extrude temporary stalagmites and cupolas that catch airborne dust and stray metal ions, then reabsorb the new growth hours later, redistributing the material according to some internal ledger of structural need. Smaller segmented forms sometimes ride on these temporary protrusions, harvesting ions the reefs have already concentrated.

These creatures do not die in any ordinary sense. When a segment, filament, or entire reef can no longer match the city’s shifting frequencies, it simply slows, stops, and is overgrown by the same repair swarms that maintain the walls. Within hours its atoms have been redistributed into the lattice or into the bodies of newer arrivals. The boundary between “individual” and “city” therefore remains permeable in both directions: a drifting reef may seed an entire new block of tubules; a wall that has accumulated too many micro-fractures may soften and bud a fresh generation of segmented forms.

What emerges, then, is not a population living inside an environment but a single, self-rewriting mineral organism whose visible fauna are simply the expressions that move more quickly than its structural mass. The city rearranges itself; the creatures are the visible signature of that rearrangement, the parts that still retain the memory of having once been distinct.
