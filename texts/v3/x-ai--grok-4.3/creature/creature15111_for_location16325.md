In that high cirque the life is small, quick, and tightly fitted to the stone. The herds that cross the moss are not quite insects and not quite lizards; each animal is no larger than a thumb joint, armored in plates the same pale gray as the rock they cross. Their six legs end in chitinous hooks that find purchase even on the slickest ice lenses. They move in single file, each creature stepping exactly where the one ahead has stepped, so that over years the cushions of moss develop narrow, polished trails no wider than a finger. When a raptor drops, the herd freezes in place; only the faint rasping of their feet against the herb rosettes gives them away.

Above them the white raptors are less birds than extensions of the downdrafts themselves. Their feathers carry the same faint crystalline sheen as fresh frost on granite. They do not nest in the cirque but arrive each morning on the same currents that bring the ice fragments, wheel once or twice to read the pattern of movement below, then either strike or lift away. Their kills are neat: a sudden compression of air, a scatter of down, and the herd reforms around the empty space as if the loss were already accounted for in their route.

The beings who built the low stone rings are smaller still, and rarer. They stand no higher than the rings themselves when they are present, their limbs jointed in ways that let them flatten against the rock until they seem part of its light and shadow. Their skin is translucent at the edges, taking on whatever color lies beneath them—sometimes the bruised cobalt of a shadow, sometimes the burning white of sunlit stone. They come only when the pools have thawed and the herbs are at their fullest, staying perhaps twelve days, perhaps twenty. During that time they mend the rings, replace cracked slabs, and stack fresh stones where the old ones have sloughed away. They do not speak aloud; instead they leave patterns pressed into the moss—circles within circles, always oriented toward the three horns—that the next group will read and alter by a single line or a single gap.

Between visits the rings belong again to the moss and to the small herds that shelter inside them when the wind screams over the rim. The beings themselves retreat lower, carrying away only the memory of the place and leaving the cirque to its regular detonations of falling ice and the soft, continuous work of condensation returning to the rock.
