The inhabitants of the plateau are few in number but deeply entangled with its substance. They call themselves the Pruners, though the name is more a description of habit than a true identity. They are tall, slow-moving figures whose skin has taken on the same muted chlorophyll sheen as the older callus layers, as if pigment and cell wall have begun to trade places over generations. Their joints carry the faint raised ridges of living wood, and the lines of their palms continue without interruption into the grain of the tool-handles they never set down. When they stand still for more than a few minutes, thin filaments of cambium rise from the plazas beneath their bare feet and braid gently around their ankles—an intimacy they neither encourage nor prevent.

These people do not build so much as conduct. They move along the glowing sap channels of the clearings with the careful gait of gardeners walking among seedlings, pausing to press their palms against a trunk and feel the pressure changes inside. Their settlements are not clusters of dwellings but concentrations of attention: they gather where a new conduit is budding, or where an older rail joint has begun to drift out of true. With hooked blades of resin-hardened thorn they slice away excess growth, always leaving a thin living rind that will seal the wound. The cut surfaces weep a sweet, milky fluid that the Pruners collect in cupped leaves and drink; it sharpens their senses to the plateau’s slow rhythms and, over decades, lengthens their lives.

Most of their labor is listening. They press ears and chests to the woody ribs of the stalk-towers to hear the low, grinding music of expanding xylem. A change in pitch or a new harmonic tells them where a machine-cavity has narrowed and must be encouraged open again, or where a pocket of trapped condensation risks rotting the interior scaffolding. When a fold in reality appears along an old rail line, the Pruners do not approach it. They simply mark the location with a ring of living stakes and wait for the plateau itself to grow around the disturbance, sealing it like a scar.

Their only companions are the great translucent slugs. At dusk the Pruners leave shallow basins of the same milky fluid near the edges of the clearings. The slugs arrive without haste, their bodies a shifting map of internal currents that glow faintly when they feed. The Pruners watch them the way other peoples watch weather: as slow, reliable indicators of the health of surfaces. Occasionally a Pruner will climb onto a slug’s back for transport across a wide clearing; the creature’s movement is so gradual that the rider can prune a rail joint or seal a budding conduit during the crossing without interrupting the journey.

At night the Pruners sleep inside the hollow trunks, curled against the warm, respiring walls. Their breath mingles with the humid exhaust of the resin engines, and the city’s ceaseless, minute adjustments continue around them. In dreams they sometimes feel the plateau thinking—not in words, but in the long pressure changes that run from root plate to highest stalk. When they wake they rise and continue their work, each new cut or graft another small refinement in the organism they have agreed, without ever signing a treaty, to become.
