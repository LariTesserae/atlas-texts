In the basin’s steady light, a small population of people moves through long intervals of stillness. They are few—perhaps a dozen households whose numbers have not changed in living memory—and they inhabit the stone compounds without crowding them. Their skin and the woven cloth they wear take on the same pale, damp-warm tone as the rock and the mist; at a distance they can seem extensions of the walls until a slow shift of posture gives them away.

They work little and speak less. Water is gathered only when it has already pooled in the shallow clay basins they set beneath the eaves; no one digs new channels or widens existing ones. The same flat roofs that catch the mist also serve as sleeping platforms on warmer nights. Inside the rooms the air stays cool and mineral, and most of the furnishings are simply more of the same fitted stone, left smooth by use rather than by tools. Leather hinges creak only rarely; doors are left open for weeks at a time so the mist can pass through.

Children, when they appear, do so singly and at wide intervals. They grow slowly, spending long stretches sitting on the stone ledges above the standing water, watching the surface tension of a drop elongate and release. Their play is quiet: moving a single pebble from one shallow depression to another, or arranging fallen leaves so the next seep of water will carry them along a chosen path. No one teaches them to be silent; the basin itself supplies the instruction.

Older inhabitants spend entire days on the terraces, not in idleness but in an attentive watching that keeps the scattered pattern of shrubs and grasses exactly as it has always been. When a new seedling threatens to join an existing stand too closely, someone will gently lift and replant it farther off, or simply shade it with a flat stone until it withers. They do not speak of conservation; they speak, when they speak at all, of not adding weight to what already holds its place.

The effect is a human presence that registers more as weather than as event. Footsteps on the fitted paths are infrequent and soft, absorbed by the saturated earth between the stones. At dusk or in deeper mist a figure may stand motionless for so long that the faint film of water on a roof begins to drip again, the sound marking time rather than interruption. In this manner the people remain inside the same unaltered balance that contains the rock, the water, and the isolated green stands—present, unchanging, and almost indistinguishable from the quiet they inhabit.
