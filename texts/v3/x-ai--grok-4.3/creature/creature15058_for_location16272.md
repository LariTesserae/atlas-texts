The chamber is inhabited by a single species of small, segmented arthropod whose entire existence is given over to the maintenance of an unchanging order. Each individual is no longer than a thumb joint, its body divided into eleven articulated plates of a pale, almost glassy chitin that catches the sourceless glow and returns it as a faint internal luster. Their legs are fine and numerous—twenty-two in all—ending in hooked tarsi that strike the sulfur-veined crystal plates with the precise, unvarying rhythm responsible for the perpetual chiming. They possess no eyes; instead, each segment bears a row of minute sensory pits that register the exact distance to the next creature ahead and behind, allowing them to keep their fixed routes without collision or deviation for decades at a stretch.

These creatures neither eat nor excrete in any conventional sense. Their internal chemistry is reduced to a slow, closed loop in which trace minerals drawn from the air and from the plates are simply rearranged and redeposited along the edges of their own exoskeletons, thickening them by imperceptible increments each year. When one finally stops moving, its body is left untouched for a time. Then a neighboring individual deviates from its route just long enough to grasp the husk with its forward legs, carry it to one of the blade-like mineral ridges, and deposit it there. Over an interval too slow to observe directly, the husk is absorbed into the ridge and recrystallizes as another translucent plate identical to those already present, its sulfur veins aligning with the existing mosaic. The number of living individuals and the number of plates therefore remain constant; only their identities rotate through geological time.

Their motion is not random. Each follows one of several thousand invisible corridors etched into the chamber by the cumulative wear of countless prior passages. Where two corridors cross, the creatures time their arrivals so that neither pauses; one rises slightly on its rear legs while the other passes beneath, the exchange registered only as a brief brightening of the metallic note. The low stone cylinders serve as fixed landmarks within this geometry. The creatures climb them occasionally, not to rest but to realign the angle of their bodies before descending again, the worn tops preserving the faint pressure of limbs that have performed this adjustment for an age.

The few artifacts left among the cylinders—an obsidian flake, the rigid braid, the shallow bowl—are never touched. The creatures skirt them exactly as they skirt the bare stretches of basalt, maintaining the deliberate emptiness the chamber requires. In this way the pocket sustains a population that is both crowded and perfectly sparse, loud and perfectly still, its inhabitants neither young nor old, only present, their chiming continuing without fatigue or memory or hope of change.
