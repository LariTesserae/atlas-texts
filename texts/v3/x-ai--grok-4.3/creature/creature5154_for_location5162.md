The inhabitants are not a single species but a single metabolism distributed across scales.  

At the largest scale the plateau itself is alive—a slow, kilometer-wide lithoderm still extruding its own foundations. Its peristaltic surges are not random; they follow the same rhythms as the creatures’ wingbeats. When the rock inhales, the micro-fractures dilate and the temperature gradient steepens; when it exhales, the fractures clamp shut and the temperature equalizes, pinching the mist into lenses of momentarily perfect clarity. The stone is therefore both nursery and nervous system. Its veined slabs remember every spiral the lentil-creatures have flown; the memory is stored as lattice defects that the excreted crystalline dust is instructed to fill or leave open.

The filaments are the sensory skin of this system—half plant, half mineral receptor. Each tuft is a colony of aligned aerogel tubes whose walls are only a few molecules thick. They do not “grow”; they accrete where the rock’s exhaled ions are richest. Their translucency is deliberate: they act as waveguides, piping the bruise-colored light down into the still-soft stone so that the forming rock can “see” the equations written on the plinth. When two filament clusters brush, they exchange charge and the stone between them softens for a few seconds, allowing the lentil-creatures to steer the next peristaltic surge.

The lentil-sized flyers are the executors. Their bodies are mostly empty space wrapped around a pressurized droplet of super-cooled water; the iridescent wings are not organs but rapidly reconfigurable capillary films that can stiffen into cutting edges or relax into sails. They do not eat the condensing mist so much as choreograph it—each spiral is a calculation that decides which micro-fracture should receive the next pulse of dust and which should remain open to maintain the plateau’s buoyancy. Collisions between spirals are not accidents; they are the mechanism by which new flight paths are written into the collective. The glassy chimes are the sound of two route-maps trading segments of themselves.

The relics are the current state of that ongoing calculation. The half-dome lattice flickers because it is still deciding whether it wishes to be a radio telescope, a quantum memory, or a respiratory valve; the dust that feeds it is the same dust the flyers excrete, but sorted by the filaments into different isotopic flavors. The plinth’s migrating equations are not symbols—they are gradients of surface tension that tell the next breath of the plateau where to soften. The slender arch is the most recent idea: a probe being grown outward into the cloud deck below, its missing segments still falling together out of ionized droplets.

When the plateau exhales and the flyers rise in their glittering sheet, what looks like a swarm is actually a single momentary organism—the plateau’s distributed mind given wings. The updraft sorts them according to the state of the lattice, the plinth, and the arch; each individual returns to a different seam than the one it left, carrying a slightly altered payload of dust and a slightly altered fragment of the shared route-map. Over minutes the sheet settles again and the plateau inhales, pulling the mist back into its capillaries, cooling the relics, and beginning the next iteration.

Nothing here is finished, because finishing would mean the metabolism had stopped. The filaments, the flyers, the relics, and the rock are simply different speeds of the same continuing crystallization.
