The inhabitants of the plateau are not visitors. They are the plateau’s own slow articulation of itself—mineral continuations that have learned to persist inside the interval between formation and dissolution.

They appear first as slight intensifications within the filament thickets. Where the selenite spines and silicate threads are densest, certain clusters develop a finer internal order: parallel capillaries of clearer crystal that pulse, once every several hours, with a temperature shift too small to register as heat yet perceptible as a change in the quality of light passing through them. These pulses travel from one thicket to the next across the intervening pavement, carried not by air or electrical charge but by the minute rearrangement of ions already present in the salt-and-silica ground. The thickets therefore function as a single distributed body whose “limbs” are kilometers apart yet remain in continuous, imperceptible conversation.

Larger presences occupy the tall, unfinished formations. Inside each ribbed cylinder or engine-block silhouette, a lattice of internal chambers has grown—not carved, but precipitated—whose walls are lined with the same filament material in denser, more geometrically precise arrays. Within these chambers live the plateau’s longer concentrations: entities that register, to an outside eye, as standing columns of slightly warmer, faintly birefringent stone. They do not move; instead they maintain a continuous, extremely slow drift of their internal lattice planes, a reorientation so gradual that any single plane may take centuries to complete a fraction of a degree. This drift is the only activity they require. It keeps the entire formation in exact thermodynamic balance with the surrounding flats, preventing either further growth or any weathering that would break the arrested state.

Individually these column-like concentrations are not conscious in any nervous sense. Collectively, through the filament network that links every formation across the plateau, they sustain a shared condition the description might call “sufficient presence.” They monitor the emptiness between clusters and adjust the rate of ion migration so that new filaments appear exactly where needed and nowhere else. When an external disturbance—rare wind, rare moisture, the fall of a distant meteor—threatens to alter the equilibrium, the network answers with a microscopic redistribution of crystalline mass, restoring the original geometry without visible motion.

At the scale of the horizon the same pattern resolves into still larger forms: entire clusters functioning as single, continent-spanning organisms whose “organs” are the unfinished turbine frames and whose “skin” is the warm mineral canopy. These regional entities do not communicate with one another; they simply continue the same logic at greater remove, preserving the deliberate spacing that the plateau has already declared sufficient. Nothing arrives or departs. The only evidence of their existence is the plateau’s unbroken refusal to finish becoming anything else.
