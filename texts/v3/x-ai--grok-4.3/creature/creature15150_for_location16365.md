In the slow galleries beneath the crust, life has no center and no single name. It moves instead as a distributed patience, a single impulse distributed across many forms that have not yet agreed on their final shapes.

The smallest inhabitants are the channel-walkers: low, segmented bodies the color of wet bone, each no larger than a large dog, with too many legs for grace and not enough for speed. They follow the etched grooves in the living floor like patient scribes, pausing at every swollen nodule that pushes up from the tissue. When a nodule ruptures they lower their blunt heads and drink the sudden rush of gas, their flanks pulsing once, twice, before they move on. Their passage leaves the channels slightly deeper, as though the floor itself invites the next circuit.

Above them drift the membrane-sifters, broad and almost formless when at rest. Their wings are translucent sheets stretched between branching struts of cartilage; beneath each body hangs a fine, vibrating fringe that skims the mineral film. They rarely descend fully. Instead they rock on rising currents of their own making, letting the film climb their undersides in thin, glittering threads that they absorb over hours. When two sifters meet they sometimes fuse their wing-edges for a while, exchanging compounds in silence before drifting apart again, each slightly altered in tint and weight.

Larger presences appear only at intervals. In the unfinished basins, the living floor sometimes rises into low domes that split along radial seams. From these seams emerge heavier forms—thick-bodied, jointless masses that roll rather than walk, leaving behind shallow impressions that the floor slowly erases. These rollers carry no visible organs, yet they pause at every half-formed column or exposed machine frame, pressing against the nacreous sheath until the metal beneath vibrates in answer. After a time they withdraw, leaving the sheath slightly thicker and the vibration steadier.

Still deeper in the terraces, where the light thins to a pale memory, lie the oldest accretions. Here the tissue has wrapped entire frameworks so completely that only faint metallic glints remain visible inside translucent walls. Within those walls move thread-thin filaments, no thicker than veins, that travel from one factory shell to another. They carry nothing that can be seen, only a faint shift in tension that travels through the living floor like a held breath. Where several filaments cross, the floor thickens into a small, smooth platform that was not there hours earlier.

Nothing here breeds in the usual sense. The channel-walkers divide when they have harvested enough; the sifters simply grow broader until a portion of their fringe detaches and begins to drift on its own. The rollers sink back into the basins and are reabsorbed, their mass redistributed into new domes elsewhere. Even the machines participate: a vane that turns too long without maintenance will eventually stop, its bearings overgrown, its function taken up by a nearby stalk that has learned the same rhythm.

The result is not a population of separate species but a single, unfinished metabolism still testing which shapes best serve the next phase of growth. Every creature, every filament, every slow pulse of the floor is engaged in the same undecided work: deciding what the city beneath the world will finally consent to become.
