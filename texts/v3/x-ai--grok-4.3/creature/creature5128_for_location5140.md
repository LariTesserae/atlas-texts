The people who live here are few and slight, their bodies kept narrow by the cold and the need to pass through spaces that are always in the act of closing. They emerge from the moss pads at dusk and dawn like figures rising from water, skin the same muted gray as the damp stone, hair cropped short and clotted with the same living filaments that line the walls. Their movements are slow, deliberate, as if they too are part of the ongoing growth rather than visitors to it. When they prune, they do so with the flat side of a bone blade drawn along a vein, never severing more than a few cells at a time; the cut seals behind them in minutes, leaving only a faint paling of the phosphorescence. Their palms are broad and callused in a way that suggests long contact with living tissue rather than dead tool handles. Eyes are large and nearly pupil-less, adapted to the perpetual half-light, and when they look at one another they seem to read small shifts in the glow along the other’s throat or wrists—the same faint pulsing that marks the roots above the pool.

Children, when they appear, are almost indistinguishable from the architecture until they move. They sleep curled inside the thicker leaf vaults, their breathing synchronized for hours with the contraction of the walls around them. When they wake they do not cry; they press a hand to the nearest surface and wait until the tissue warms slightly under their palm, then slip out through whatever new gap has formed overnight. Their play consists of redirecting a single vine across a walkway or coaxing a mat of liverwort into a low seat, tasks performed with the same absorbed concentration the adults bring to maintenance.

The arthropods and soft-bodied forms are not separate from these people so much as an extension of the same living medium. Occasionally a child will lie still in the moss and allow the segmented bodies to cross their skin, registering each passage as a faint, cool tickle that fades almost at once. The adults never speak of the creatures as distinct; they simply note, by the momentary dimming of a wall vein or the slight subsidence of a floor pad, where concentrations are highest and adjust their pruning accordingly. In this way the settlement’s true population remains continuous—neither fully human nor fully plant nor fully animal, but a single slow conversation held in tissue, light, water, and breath.
