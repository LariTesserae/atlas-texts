The plateau is home to almost nothing that moves in the ordinary way, yet it is not empty. What persists there is a quiet confederacy of three kinds of life, each shaped by the same residual lattice that once arranged the rock.

The cushions are the oldest residents. Their blood-colored fronds are not leaves in the usual sense but flattened colonies of a single slow organism that has learned to hoard metal from the weeping stone. The silver threads inside them are literal conductors; during the brief dusk glow they carry a faint charge that synchronizes every cushion across the plateau so that their internal rhythms rise and fall together. They do not reproduce by seed or spore. Instead, when a cushion grows too large for its pocket of dust, it slowly extrudes a thread of itself across the bare basalt. The thread follows the faint etched lines until it reaches empty ground, where it settles and begins to darken into a new cushion. The process can take years. In the meantime the parent shrinks slightly, as though the plateau itself were pruning excess.

The thread-thin creatures are more recent arrivals, drawn upward from deeper, wetter fissures by the constant seepage. Each is a single transparent filament of muscle and gut, no thicker than a hair, yet they segment themselves into three or four jointed portions that allow them to flex like a slow whip. They drink not by sipping but by pressing their entire length against a veil of water, absorbing it through microscopic pores along their sides. The ripples they leave behind are not accidental; the creatures’ passage imprints a temporary tension in the surface tension of the water, and this tension travels for a time as a standing wave that the lattice amplifies rather than damps. Other creatures, still smaller and unseen, ride the ripple outward to colonize fresh seepage planes.

Between these two visible tenants moves the lattice itself, or what remains of it. It is not alive in the cellular sense, but it maintains preferences. It dislikes crowding, excess heat, and standing water; it likes perfect spheres, regular spacing, and the particular spectrum of light that leaks through its altered sky. When conditions stray too far from these preferences, the lattice exerts a gentle pressure—sometimes on the stone, sometimes on the air, sometimes on the very surfaces of the plants—until balance returns. The small translucent creatures seem to have learned to read these pressures; their drinking schedule coincides exactly with the moments when the lattice is most permissive. The cushions, in turn, have grown veins whose metallic load can briefly reinforce the lattice’s own patterns, so that each dusk the plateau reinforces itself for another night.

Nothing else arrives. Birds that stray near the cliff feel an abrupt downdraft and turn aside. Larger animals sense the altered edges of the sunlight and keep to the valleys below. The plateau remains what it has become: a few square paces of deliberate weather, tended by three slow collaborators who have no name for one another and yet keep the same hours.
