What lives here moves like the afterimages of intention that the rock itself has not yet finished forgetting.  

Most of the time they are not visible at all. They exist first as slight inconsistencies in pressure: a pocket of water that warms half a degree faster than the surrounding current, a filament of resistance that parts around an outstretched limb an instant before contact. When these inconsistencies gather, they sometimes condense into temporary shapes that the water then carries forward like slow calligraphy. A passing ridge of warmer viscosity may hold, for several heartbeats, the outline of a broad, low body whose edges trail backward into the shear lines of two opposing currents. The body never solidifies; it simply refuses, for that interval, to disperse.  

Long-term residents appear to be extensions of the plateau’s own softening. Inside the circular ridges whose interiors still retain faint radial markings, the layered sediment occasionally pulses—not with light but with a deeper, rhythmic settling, as though something beneath the mineral were breathing on a scale of centuries rather than minutes. When the pulses align with the drifting shafts of anomalous gold light, the worked stone releases a single, perfectly circular spore that does not float but sinks, straight down, until it vanishes into the velvet mats below. These spores are never found again. Divers who have tried to follow them describe the sudden impression that the slope beneath them has rotated ninety degrees while remaining exactly where it was.  

Smaller presences drift among the fronds in loose constellations. They register first as changes in the texture of sound: the fibrillation of spores briefly synchronizes, then skips a beat, then resumes, leaving a negative space where the rhythm should have been. Observers have reported the sensation of being gently examined, not by sight or touch but by the momentary absence of both. The globular clusters with their hair-fine tendrils sometimes pause in mid-drift and rearrange themselves into new configurations whose geometry precisely echoes the radial patterns on the submerged ridges. After these rearrangements the clusters drift on, seemingly unchanged, yet the current they occupy tends to shear into an extra laminar layer for days afterward.  

Rarer still are the entities that appear only during the spatial fractures. When a distant ridge suddenly feels near enough to touch, something may be seen standing on it—not upon the stone but within the thin interval between this depth and its neighboring version. The figures are tall and narrow, composed of the same warm, milky water that has momentarily become less dense. They do not move; they are the movement that the fracture itself performs. When the world reseals they are gone, yet the water they occupied retains a faint radial brightness for hours, identical to the markings on the worked stone.  

Nothing here breeds in the ordinary sense. New forms arise when an old current finally dissolves against an older one and the resulting turbulence briefly organizes itself into the shape of a previous inhabitant. The new shape may last a season or a millennium before the plateau’s vast, patient smoothing reclaims it. In this way the creatures are not born so much as remembered, and the plateau continues to remember them even when no eye remains to witness the remembering.
