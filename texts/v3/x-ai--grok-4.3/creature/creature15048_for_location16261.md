In the high vaults where the living mass thins into hanging galleries, the first true inhabitants make their presence felt as motion rather than form. Entire constellations of translucent gliders—creatures whose bodies are little more than elongated lattices of cartilage and membrane—drift on the slow updrafts that rise from the condensing sheets below. Their wings are not wings so much as continuous veils of living tissue, rimmed with fine sensory cilia that taste the constant fall of mist. When they flex, low-frequency pulses travel through the plant walls like the bass notes of some vast wooden drum; the organism itself amplifies and returns the signal so that a single individual’s call can be answered hours later by another colony fifty kilometers distant.

Smaller, more numerous flyers haunt the fibrous surfaces, their metallic scraping a constant background to every sound. Each is no larger than a human palm, yet they move in dense, shifting clouds that follow the phosphorescent root strands as a grazier follows pasture. Their feeding leaves behind polished scars that quickly reseal; the plant appears to tolerate them as a kind of circulating immune system, trading small losses of surface tissue for the removal of parasitic molds. When disturbed, the swarms ignite momentary blue flares along their wing scales, brief illuminations that reveal the deeper geometry of the lattice before darkness reclaims it.

Deeper still, within the cathedral-scale voids left when circulation halts for a decade or a century, predators glide in patient arcs. These are heavier beings, their membranes stiffened by mineral deposits drawn from the rock the plant has only begun to digest. They launch from one fibrous cliff and cross the black pools without ever touching water, their undersides trailing fine rootlets that drink as they pass. Their eyes are not eyes but clusters of light-sensitive nodules along the leading edges of their wings; they navigate by the faint gradient of phosphorescence and by the pressure waves left behind by the gliders they hunt.

On the interior root roads—broad, flattened highways of vascular tissue that run for kilometers without rising or falling—herds of slow-bodied browsers migrate. Each animal is a segmented tube of muscle and hydrostatic chambers, armored in overlapping plates of the same translucent material that forms the plant’s mist-collecting sheets. They move nose-to-tail in long processions, pausing at intervals to extrude proboscises into the living walls and sip the pressurized sap. Their waste is immediately reabsorbed by the surrounding tubules; the herd’s passage leaves behind a temporary enrichment that the organism puts to use within hours, thickening the road ahead in preparation for the next migration.

Scattered among these living forms are the quiet remnants of an older tenancy. Where the circular stone platforms still retain their shape, the plant has grown around rather than through them, preserving low, level clearings where condensation falls straight instead of in wind-driven sheets. Occasionally a browsing herd will pause on one of these ancient floors, the stone cool beneath their plates, before resuming its circuit. The polished basalt tools wedged deeper in the fibrous matrix have become part of the organism’s slow metabolism; their edges now serve as nucleation points for new tubule growth, mineral and tissue blurring together until it is no longer clear which substance first gave shape to the other.

All of these lives—gliders, scrapers, predators, browsers, and the vast continuous plant that both shelters and consumes them—move according to rhythms the ridges themselves have not yet learned to measure. A single gallery may cycle from empty to brimming and back again between one dawn and the next; a herd may require a century to complete its circuit of a single massif. The young continent continues its slow buckling beneath them, but the inhabitants have already accommodated the motion, shaping their hollows and roads to the changing angles of stone. In the resonant dark, distance itself becomes another shared tissue, thin and humming, carrying every call, every drip, every slow migration onward until it is absorbed again by the living mass that first gave it voice.
