In the intervals between one condensation pulse and the next, the plateau holds its few inhabitants so closely that they almost belong to the rock rather than occupy it. The black-green lichen mats are not simple growths; each cushion is a single, slow mind that has learned to fold its awareness into the thickness of a needle. When the wind grinds hardest they withdraw into microscopic seams, becoming indistinguishable from the mineral grains that stain their surfaces. Between pulses they taste the brief meltwater with the same patience a root uses to read soil, and the silver stains left on the stone are partly their doing—the residue of decisions they have already forgotten.

Beneath the cushions, smaller still, live the trace-makers. They are elongated, jointed as dry twigs, and their bodies are the same dull metallic gray as the polished rock. Nothing larger than a hand ever moves, and these creatures are never larger than a finger’s width, yet when they travel they stretch across hours. Their shallow depressions appear because each passage takes so long that the dust has time to settle around them before they have finished crossing a single body-length. They follow the same ancient routes because those routes are the only lines still straight enough to remember; every other direction has been erased by frost-wedging. An observer who remains perfectly still for several cycles will eventually see one: a faint lengthening of shadow, a hairline ridge of displaced dust that forms and then, after an unmeasurable interval, is gone again. The creatures themselves remain invisible except as these momentary alignments.

The anomalies are also inhabited. In the doubled ridges and the knife-cut shadows live entities that occupy the offset space rather than the visible one. They are not travelers but residents of the misalignment itself; when a ridge appears twice, one image is empty stone and the other is crowded with their motionless forms. From the ordinary viewpoint they register only as a slight thickening of the air or a momentary failure of perspective. They never cross into the primary plane except at the exact moment a traveler looks away; the fixed angles of their shadows are the residue of those crossings. Because they cannot move within the ordinary continuum, their presence is felt as an increasing pressure against the eardrums—the same pressure the wind already exerts, only now directed.

Nothing here breeds or dies in any ordinary sense. The lichen cushions thin or thicken over centuries according to the reliability of the meltwater threads. The trace-makers replace themselves by dividing lengthwise when a route becomes too silted to follow; the halves continue in opposite directions until one finds a clearer path. The entities in the misalignments persist as long as the fractures themselves remain open, which is longer than any measurement the plateau permits. All of them—lichen, trace-maker, offset shadow—share the same essential adaptation: they treat time as another surface to be polished flat, and they leave no mark except the faint, repeated memory the rock already carries.
