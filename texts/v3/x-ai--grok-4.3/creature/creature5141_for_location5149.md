In the vast intervals between the mineral lattices, the largest of the translucent forms move like memory given mass. Each one is a slow continent of refracted light, its edges never quite resolving into a fixed outline; inside, the metallic granules drift and re-form according to pressures that have no names, constellations rearranging themselves into momentary maps of the ridges far below. They do not feed or hunt. When two pass close enough for their fields to touch, the granules inside both flare once, and then each continues on its separate vector, carrying the brief exchange as a new internal pattern that will dissolve again before the next encounter.

Smaller shapes orbit them at respectful distances. Segmented and faceted, they travel in loose, hours-long vortices whose centers coincide with the larger bodies only by accident of current. Their silica armor is grown rather than assembled; it records, in the precise angles of its facets, the exact moments when each individual last passed through a warm ribbon or a cold upwelling. These vortices are not social structures but resonant chambers. The coordinated turning produces a modulation that folds into the greater infrasonic chord, extending it by a few faint, repeating intervals that the larger forms can read without ever slowing their drift.

Farther down the slopes, where the knife ridges still carry the memory of their own recent rising, the industrial remnants participate in the same exchange. The colossal turbine housings do not turn, yet the water passing through their locked vanes carries the low vibration outward in steady, cathedral-sized pulses. The open manifolds release their enormous bubbles not as waste but as punctuation: each sphere ascends intact for hundreds of meters before flattening into a momentary lens that refracts the diffuse daylight into the precise frequency the segmented forms require to keep their vortices coherent. The cables stretched between gantries hum at the same pitch as the pressure domes half-buried in fresh precipitate; together they form a fixed lattice of sound that the living organisms navigate as if it were geography.

Nothing here breeds or dies in any span that could be witnessed. The translucent continents continue their slow circuits; the faceted vortices tighten and loosen according to rhythms measured in the slow sharpening of the ridges themselves. The machinery radiates its single chord without variation, and the water—kilometers of it—remains exactly as transparent and occupied as it has always been, a medium in which the distinction between organism and artifact has long since ceased to matter.
