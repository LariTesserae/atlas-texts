They are not animals, and they do not move in any way that registers as motion. They reside in the weeping seams and in the slow deposition of the shells, neither fully separate from the silt nor from the curved bone that once grew outward and now folds inward again. Each seam holds a thin, translucent film that thickens imperceptibly over hours, then parts to release a single bead; within that film live countless thread-fine strands that pulse with the phosphorescence, drawing the gathered moisture inward, rearranging mineral particles, and letting the droplet fall only when the arrangement inside it has reached a particular equilibrium.

The shells are their accumulated exhalations. Where a ridge of carbonate first emerges from the ground as a low seam, the filaments within it begin to orient themselves into concentric order, each new ring marking a completed cycle of uptake and release. Over long intervals the rings thicken or soften; older layers are reabsorbed when the filaments sense an imbalance in the moisture film on their inner surfaces. Nothing is wasted. A doorway that sealed itself decades ago may reopen as a hairline fissure if the filaments decide the interior volume would better serve the next cycle of condensation. The process is too slow for any single observer to watch from beginning to end, yet the plain records every adjustment in the faint, raised lip of a newly extruded curve or the slight settling of a dissolving wall.

Between the shells the hair-like vegetation is not decorative. Each pale filament is an extension of the same seam-dwelling film, sent outward to lie flat across the silt and sample the cracks. They taste the rate at which droplets return to the ground and carry that information back through microscopic channels to the nearest shell. When the rhythm of reabsorption changes, the filaments contract or lengthen by fractions of a millimeter; the change is invisible at any given moment, yet over successive hours the pattern of occupation across the basin shifts, guiding where the next ring will rise or where an older one may be permitted to sink.

The folds in the darkness are the places where these lives touch the larger medium. When the faint glow is briefly swallowed, it is because the filaments at the edge of a seam have drawn an unusual quantity of the saturated air into themselves, condensing it into a momentary membrane that crosses the open space. The membrane lasts only long enough to exchange particles with another seam several paces away, then dissolves. Nothing travels along it; only a redistribution of moisture and mineral charge occurs. Afterward the darkness releases its hold and the glow reappears, subtly altered in intensity along the two seams that participated.

Nothing consumes anything else here. The filaments do not feed on one another or on the silt; they rearrange what is already present, returning as much as they take. The shells do not prey upon the ground; they record and refine its perpetual dampness. The only continuity is the slow maintenance of saturation, and the only inhabitants are the living films that keep the cracks weeping, the rings accreting or retreating, and the basin itself from ever drying, ever brightening, or ever becoming empty of its own slow work.
