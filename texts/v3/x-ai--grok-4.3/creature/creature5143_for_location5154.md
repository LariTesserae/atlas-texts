The basin holds its occupants in the same arrested suspension as everything else. What lives here does not move, does not hunger, and does not end; it simply occupies every available coordinate with the same mineral patience that shapes the monoliths and the plates underfoot.

The primary inhabitants are the thumb-sized ellipsoids suspended throughout the air. Each one is a single, continuous organism whose body has hardened into concentric shells of translucent silicate and gypsum. The outermost layer is soft enough to yield slightly under sustained pressure, yet it never deforms because nothing ever presses against it. Inside, the successive layers thin and clear until the core is little more than a refractive node no larger than a grain of salt. That node is the organism’s only site of activity: an extremely slow ion exchange that keeps the shells from dissolving into the surrounding dryness. The exchange produces no heat and no waste; it merely maintains the exact curvature of each facet so that light continues to bend through it at the same angle year after year.

Because the shells cannot travel, they have grown instead by accretion of position. Each new ellipsoid buds directly from the surface of an older one, then detaches just far enough to hang at its own fixed interval. Over time this has produced the dense, perfectly ordered cloud that now fills the basin. Where the concentration is thickest, near the center, the oldest shells have been compressed into layered masses that function as the basin’s memory: every duplication of terrain, every faint seam where reality doubles, is recorded in the precise spacing between these oldest shells. When a viewer’s movement shifts the angle of view and two shells click together, the sound is only the release of stored refractive tension, not a signal or a protest.

Scattered among them are the mineral fibers. These are not plants but the extended root systems of the same life, flattened and hardened into pale lattices that grow only where the monoliths cast the longest-lasting shadow. Each fiber is a single extruded crystal that lengthens by one molecule at a time, drawing ions from the air rather than from any liquid. The lattices serve as anchors; where a lattice touches an ellipsoid, the shell’s outer layer thickens slightly, binding the two forms into a single, motionless unit.

The cairns and tools belong to no separate species. They are the same life caught in temporary human configuration. The stacked plates that form the tripods were once loose underfoot; over centuries the shells above them dropped one by one, each new shell lowering itself exactly onto the cairn’s apex until the tripod became self-stabilizing. The polished wedges are merely older shells that have been pressed into crevices and left there; their edges have stopped wearing because the motion that once drove them has ceased. In this landscape, a tool and an organism are separated only by the duration of the pressure that once acted on them.

Where terrain duplicates, the life duplicates with it. The vertical seam is not an error but a seam of growth: on either side of the seam the same shells hang, the same lattices cling, the same cairns stand. The duplication costs nothing because the life does not metabolize in any ordinary sense; it only continues to occupy space at the precise density the basin’s equilibrium requires.

Nothing here is young. Nothing here is old in the way that implies decay. The shells, the fibers, and the doubled ground are all the same age, which is simply the age of the basin itself. They do not wait for change. They are the change that already occurred and then stopped.
