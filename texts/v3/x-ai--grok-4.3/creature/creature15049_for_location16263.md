The mites are the oldest residents, though they have no sense of age. They are no larger than a grain of sand, their bodies the same milky translucence as the condensation they follow. Over centuries they have lost every trace of pigment and eye, every trait that once allowed haste; what remains is a soft, segmented tube that drinks the thin film of water and excretes a faint crystalline trail behind it. They move in single-file processions along the undersides of pipes, pausing for hours at each joint where a droplet might eventually form. When the droplet falls they scatter for a moment, then re-form their lines as if nothing had happened. Their rustling is not communication but the sound of countless microscopic feet testing the same damp seam again and again.

The thread-like growths are younger, though still older than any surface language. They begin where a pipe sags just enough to create shadow and a slight temperature drop; there they anchor as a single translucent filament no thicker than a human hair. Over decades the filament branches, each new shoot seeking the next rivet or seam where metal sweats. They do not photosynthesize. Instead they feed on the slow exhalation of warmth that leaks from the engine deeper in, drawing trace minerals from the alloy itself and leaving behind a faint etching of corrosion that looks, at certain angles, like script. Clusters never touch one another. They grow until their outer filaments reach the faint green glow of a phosphor wafer; then they stop, as if the light itself were a boundary they have learned not to cross.

Deeper still, in the standing water cupped inside the depressions of abandoned machine bases, something even slower persists: a pale, gelatinous sheet that spreads across the surface tension of the water. It is almost invisible until a falling droplet disturbs it, at which point it contracts into a tight lens, holds for several minutes, then relaxes again. The sheet absorbs nothing but the occasional mite that loses its footing; when this happens the body dissolves over weeks, leaving only a darker mote suspended in the gel. No one watches these disseminations. They simply complete themselves between one condensation cycle and the next.

All of them—the mites, the filaments, the sheets—move at the same tempo as the belts and pistons around them. Heat rises, moisture gathers, metal cools by a fraction of a degree, and the creatures adjust by the width of a single cell. They have no predators, no seasons, and no memory of any other place. Their continued presence is the only proof that the crypt is not yet finished with its purpose, whatever that purpose has become.
