In the suspended galleries of that motionless plateau, life has narrowed itself to two interwoven presences that have long since ceased to be separate.  

The first is the plant itself, though “plant” is only the nearest exhausted word. It is a single, continent-sized soma whose every frond and conduit belongs to one unbroken metabolism. Its rigid columns do not grow; they have already finished growing and now simply persist, their glassy vessels maintaining an unbroken loop of mineral and water that returns to itself at every scale. Between the columns the thinner mesh of tissue acts less like foliage and more like a vast, slow diaphragm: at measured intervals it contracts, forcing water through ridged internal throats. The resulting thunder is not communication but the sound of equilibrium being restored; each pulse corrects the tiny drift that would otherwise accumulate. Nothing is produced that is not immediately reabsorbed. The architecture is therefore perfectly closed, its surfaces worn to the same satin finish wherever water has moved across them for ages.  

Within the clearer volumes the second presence drifts: translucent, many-limbed bodies whose mineral lattices have grown outward from their own tissues rather than being added from outside. These lattices function as both skeleton and current-sense; each fine crystalline ridge records the exact vector of the last hydraulic pulse that passed it, so that the creatures orient themselves without ever needing to decide. They move only along the corridors the plant has left open, and their motion is never faster than the water itself. Because the temperature never varies and no waste accumulates, their bodies require neither feeding nor repair. They simply translate from one fixed route to another, their limbs trailing faint prismatic refractions that vanish as soon as they stop. Schools of them may occupy the same drifting volume for uncounted intervals, yet they never brush the living walls; the plant’s surface chemistry gently repels any contact that would disrupt the flow.

Nothing else is present. No scavenger follows, no spore settles, no current from elsewhere intrudes. The two forms—the encompassing soma and the drifting mineral bodies—complete each other so exactly that disturbance has become geometrically impossible. When the conduits constrict again, the low thunder travels through both plant tissue and creature lattice alike, and both answer with the same minute adjustment of internal pressure, restoring the original stillness. In that stillness the plateau continues, its inhabitants indistinguishable from the conditions that allow them to remain.
