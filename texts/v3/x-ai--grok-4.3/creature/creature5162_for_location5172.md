In the slow galleries and dripping terraces of the green mass, life does not arrive from elsewhere; it condenses out of the same slow circulation that moves water and spores. The smallest inhabitants are scarcely separate from the substrate. They are films of living tissue, no thicker than a fingernail, that spread across the undersides of leaves or along the moist inner walls of hollow stems. When they sense the approach of a droplet they contract into tight rosettes, drinking until their color deepens to a bruised plum, then relax again and continue their patient expansion. Over centuries these films weave themselves into the surrounding fibers, trading nutrients for structural support until the distinction between feeder and food disappears.

Slightly larger forms drift along the perpetual interior drizzle. They are translucent sacks the size of a closed hand, buoyed by the gases they generate in their own bellies. Each sack trails a fringe of fine, hair-like roots that sample the air and the falling threads of water, sorting minerals and dissolved sugars before the whole creature folds itself around a particularly rich vein and rests for decades. When the vein is exhausted the sack detaches, drifts downward through successive layers until it finds another current, and begins again. Their only movement is the gentle pulsing of their root fringes, like the breathing of a sleeping lung.

The broad grazers the eye first notices are older and far more deliberate. Their many legs are not legs at all but thickened, muscular lobes grown from the same living walls they traverse; each lobe ends in a moist suction cup that can remain clamped for centuries without tiring. As they feed they scrape away the outermost layer of the canopy with a rasp made of accumulated mineral salts, then press the fresh wound against their own underbellies so that new tissue can knit them into place. While they rest, their skin slowly greens and grows its own small leaves; when they finally move again they carry patches of the canopy with them, like walking gardens. Their exhalations are the low, wet sighs that sometimes pass through an entire plateau at once, a communal breath exchanged across distances measured in human lifetimes.

Deeper inside the galleries live the quicker, smaller gliders. Their bodies are flattened into delicate membranes that can stiffen or soften at will, allowing them to steer between falling droplets or to flatten completely and become just another wrinkle in the wall. When threatened they do not flee; they dissolve the edges of their own membrane into the surrounding growth, leaving only a faint scar that heals within a season. Their chief sense is pressure: they can feel, from many body-lengths away, the minute tensing of fibers that signals an approaching grazer or the slow collapse of an over-ripe seed capsule.

At the greatest scale, there are entities whose true size is never seen because they are coextensive with the plateaus themselves. These are not single animals but knots of coordinated activity that persist across millennia. One such knot may decide, over the course of a thousand years, to thicken the canopy along a particular ridge so that less light reaches the interior; another may encourage the formation of a new hollow gallery by directing water into a line of weakening fibers. Their “thoughts” are the patterns of growth and resorption that slowly reshape whole mountains. When two such knots meet, the boundary between them becomes a fertile seam where new life condenses for centuries before either side absorbs the other.

Among all these forms move the last, most elusive residents: slender, pale threads that follow the buried ribs of harder material. They are never seen except as a momentary brighter line when a leaf layer thins; they travel the ancient bone-like traces as if reading them, and where they pass the surrounding tissue grows slightly denser and more ordered for a few centuries afterward. What they seek along those forgotten curves remains uncertain, but after they have gone the relic contours seem, for a time, less like ruin and more like scaffolding waiting to be used again.
