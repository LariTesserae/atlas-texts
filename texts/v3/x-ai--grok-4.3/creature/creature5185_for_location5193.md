What lives here is not a population of creatures but a single, protracted metabolism distributed across wood, membrane, and salvaged steel. The plants have no names in the usual sense; they are all one knitted tissue that has learned to wear the old machines like a set of slow, internal bones. Wherever a riveted housing still protrudes, rootlets have threaded the bolt holes and are now extruding new lignin in perfect register with the vanished threads, turning corrosion into a form of growth. The ribbed turbines do not host flowers so much as become them: their original vanes are gone, and in their place rotate fleshy, translucent wheels whose margins secrete a thin, sugary film that the next layer of vines immediately drinks. The rotation is measured in finger-widths per day, yet the motion is steady; osmotic pressure builds behind each petal until the wheel clicks forward another tooth, exactly as the pressure inside the living floor drops by the same fraction.

Because every surface sweats and every conduit weeps, the plants maintain an unbroken film of water that links them more intimately than any root graft could. When a maturing seed pod finally snaps, the brief percussion travels through that film like a single shared heartbeat; a moment later, several distant pods answer with their own soft detonations, as if the entire lattice has taken a deeper breath together. The low-frequency drone is not background noise but the audible shape of this respiration. Each slow revolution of a gear, each minute widening of a vascular bundle, displaces a fraction of the warm vapor; the vapor, in turn, keeps the gears from seizing and the lignin from drying. The plants therefore protect the machinery with the same insistence that the machinery continues to route water where they direct it.

Light, once it has been sieved through successive canopies, reaches the lowest pads as a greenish glow that the rhizomes appear to drink. In those deepest layers the growth is almost geological—pads thicken by depositing successive leathery skins, each skin a record of a previous season’s humidity. Over years the skins fuse into the living floor itself, burying the lower courses of the turbines until only their upper rims remain, still turning like the crowns of buried clocks. No individual plant completes a circuit or pushes a piston; the work is distributed so finely that the difference between “machine” and “organism” has become a question of scale rather than kind. What persists is simply the basin’s own patience, expressed equally in the incremental creep of a vine across a rivet and in the incremental turn of the rivet beneath the vine.
