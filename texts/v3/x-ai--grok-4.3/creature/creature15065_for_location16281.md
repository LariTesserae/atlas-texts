In this slow basin the inhabitants are not separate from the stone so much as its way of continuing itself. The tube-creatures—pale as diluted milk and no thicker than a quill—follow the weeping seams like the threads of a single, distributed nervous system. Each one carries inside it a darker filament of concentrated ore that pulses, once every several minutes, with a minute electrical twitch; the twitch nudges water molecules along the creature’s length, drawing fresh carbonate into the mouth and expelling spent flakes behind. When two tubes meet at a junction they briefly press together, exchanging a bead of fluid that carries both mineral salts and the memory of which channels are already claimed. After the exchange they separate again without hurry, each returning to its own seam, the rock closing over the meeting place so smoothly that no trace remains except a slightly brighter line of crystal.

Above them the flat, many-legged discs drift and settle like slow leaves. Their faint tea-colored glow is not constant; it brightens for a few heartbeats whenever a drop strikes the stone directly beneath them, then fades again. The light seems to serve as a map they read with their undersides: where the glow lingers longest, the carbonate is newest and most easily grazed. When two discs cross paths they exchange a single soft click—more vibration than sound—that travels through the thin water film and is answered by the stone itself as a brief answering ripple in the nearest fissure. The click appears to mark territory already worked and therefore not to be revisited for several days.

Deeper in the matrix, where the crystalline plates press against the half-buried turbine, other forms exist that are neither wholly creature nor wholly rock. Thin veins of translucent mineral sometimes detach from the plates and crawl, millimeter by millimeter, across the bronze vanes; once they reach a gear tooth they wrap around it and harden again, effectively lengthening the tooth by a fraction. The gear then advances one more click under the drip, and the vein detaches, returning to the plate as if nothing had occurred. These veins are the basin’s way of remembering movement when its own slow chemistry cannot supply it.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense. Instead, separation and fusion occur continuously at the scale of a single crystal face. A tube may split lengthwise when it reaches a widening fissure, each half sealing its cut edges with fresh carbonate before gliding onward. A disc may dim and settle until its legs are indistinguishable from the stone, only to brighten again hours later with two smaller, identical glows moving away from the place it rested. The machinery participates without intention: a carbonate-encrusted gear records each passage by the faint polish left on its tooth, and that polish in turn guides the next vein or tube that crosses it.

All of these motions remain within a few body-lengths of one another yet never collide, because the basin adjusts its own geometry at the same pace. A new ridge rises by the width of a hair, a tube lengthens by the same amount in the opposite direction, and the space between them stays open because both processes are expressions of the same continuous redistribution of dissolved stone. The result is less a population of distinct animals than a single, extremely slow metabolism whose organs happen to be readable as creatures when light and attention move quickly enough to catch them.
