In the bruised half-light, the pocket teems with entities that have never known a finished world. The thumb-sized transparents—call them veil-threads—are not individuals so much as shifting clauses in a single, distributed sentence. Each is a ribbon of clear gel threaded with a filament of living silver that contracts in slow pulses. When dozens braid together they generate their own faint current, a private eddy that lets them linger against a still-soft rib long enough to rasp away a few molecules of carbonate for their own minute skeletons. They never quite stop moving; when danger (which here is only another current) presses too close, the braid loosens and the veil-threads pour through one another like smoke through smoke, reassembling elsewhere without ever having collided.

Larger than these, and slower, are the lantern-jaws. Their skulls are open lattices of the same porous carbonate that forms the ribs, and the “lanterns” are not lights but clusters of chemosensitive papillae that glow a dull, liver-colored red when they taste dissolved minerals in the inflowing water. They patrol the membrane in patient circuits, pressing their broad flanks against its living wall until the membrane yields just enough to let a thin film of their own mucus merge with it. In this way they add a new layer of reinforcement without ever breaking the boundary. When two lantern-jaws meet they do not fight or court; they simply exchange a slow, deliberate brushing of jaw lattices, trading fragments of the carbonate they have tasted so that each carries, for a time, a record of the other’s route.

Among the hanging vegetation—pale, almost translucent whips whose holdfasts have not yet decided which rib to claim—live the pinprick grazers. These are not animals but mobile colonies of the same living tissue that forms the outer membrane. Each colony is a constellation of microscopic mouths on stalks no thicker than cilia; when a veil-thread drifts too near, the stalks extend, harvest a few of the thread’s silver filaments, and retract. The vegetation itself seems only half-aware of this theft; its fronds continue their slow, searching drift, occasionally brushing a grazer colony and, in the contact, exchanging a brief chemical signal that may encourage the grazers to move their grazing elsewhere for a while.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense. Instead, bodies and boundaries grow by the same incremental deposition: a lantern-jaw exhales a few carbonate grains that the membrane immediately incorporates; a veil-thread dies and its filament settles against a mucus sheet, stiffening it into a more definite partition; a grazer colony dissolves itself into the inflowing water and is taken up again by the membrane’s microscopic pores. The pocket therefore contains no parents or offspring, only successive thickenings of the same living process. At any moment a new rib may finish its arc and meet its neighbor; at any moment a lantern-jaw may press too hard and tear a temporary window that the membrane will seal again within a few flexes. The life inside continues without pause or panic, treating each unfinished corridor and each newly hardened doorway as simply the next available surface on which to continue becoming.
