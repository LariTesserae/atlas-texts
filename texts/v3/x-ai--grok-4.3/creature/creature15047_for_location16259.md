The mechanisms that trace the grooves are the most persistent presence. Low and segmented, they move as if poured rather than driven, their metal carapaces streaked with the same pale crystalline growth that coats the towers. Each one follows its assigned channel with the patience of water finding its level; when two meet at an intersection they pause, exchange a brief, steaming hiss through hidden vents, then continue on altered paths. They do not appear to gather or consume anything; their purpose seems only to keep the grooves clear and to leave behind the thin films of moisture that mark their passage.

Higher up, the dark flyers are less frequent and more solitary. They are not birds or machines but something intermediate: broad, flexible membranes stretched over minimal skeletal frames that fold wetly when at rest on a ledge. Their wings collect condensate as they glide, and when they alight they press themselves flat against the weeping metal so that water runs directly into porous channels along their undersides. Once filled they launch again with a soft, sucking release and climb until they are lost in the haze. Nothing preys on them here; they simply arrive, drink, and depart according to rhythms no observer has yet mapped.

Between these two scales, the crystalline excrescences themselves behave like a slow form of occupation. Over years they extend fine, glassy filaments into seams and pores, then retract or thicken in response to temperature shifts no larger than a hand’s breadth. Where they meet the moving mechanisms they sometimes adhere for a time, forming temporary bridges of pale mineral that the mechanisms later shear away with deliberate, grinding passes. The crystals do not die when broken; they simply resume growth elsewhere, patient as the condensation that feeds them.

No larger creatures traverse the open distances. The emptiness between platforms appears to be maintained precisely so that nothing accumulates enough mass or speed to disrupt the delicate balance of heat, moisture, and slow mechanical motion. What lives here has adapted to that constraint: everything moves either along fixed lines or through the air for the briefest necessary intervals, and everything that moves also carries or redistributes water. The basin therefore sustains a sparse population whose members are almost indistinguishable from the processes that keep the place itself alive.
