In the wide intervals between the towers, the true residents move without ever claiming the plazas outright. They are pale, elongated forms—neither plant nor animal—that drift just above the mineral film, their bodies little more than translucent ribbons of bundled filaments. Each ribbon is no wider than a finger and can stretch for several meters before contracting again, tasting dissolved salts and warmth with every slow pulse. When two ribbons meet at the edge of a shallow pool they briefly braid together, exchanging microscopic motes, then separate without haste, resuming their solitary circuits across the empty stone.

These filaments are not the only life. Within the rubbery leaves of the isolated stands hangs a finer presence: clusters of bead-like organisms no larger than pollen grains. They glow the same soft green as the canopies themselves and migrate along the undersides of fronds in patient caravans, feeding on the condensation that gathers there. When a mist thickens, the beads release tiny clouds of spores that spiral downward and settle into the hairline channels, where they will eventually harden into new mineral film and begin the cycle once more.

Deeper still, inside the living rock, microscopic threads knit the fresh stone together. They are the reason the ground continues to adjust height and why entire plazas can tilt by millimeters over the course of a day. These threads sense the weight of any filament that passes above them and quietly redistribute mass, nudging a hovering walkway a fraction higher or easing a tower’s new level into alignment. Their work registers only as the faint collective sigh that sometimes accompanies the unfolding of architecture.

Occasionally, larger shapes appear at the farthest edges of visibility. They resemble the filaments but carry thicker cores and move in long, elliptical arcs that skirt the clusters of towers. They never enter the plazas while any light remains perceptible; they arrive only when the mist withdraws and the pools have evaporated, sliding across the dried mineral glaze to harvest the concentrated salts left behind. By the time the next condensation begins they have already retreated to the shadowed intervals where vegetation stands apart, leaving the plazas as empty and unmarked as before.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or travels in groups. Each form—ribbon, bead, thread, or core—maintains long stretches of solitary adjustment, responding to moisture, warmth, and the slow breathing of the stone itself. The city accommodates them because it is built from the same patient material, still learning which spaces to leave open and which to fold inward as the quiet cycles continue.
