The plain is not empty. Its inhabitants are slow accretions of the same salts and glasses that form the tiers, shaped by the same patient forces that carved the avenues and mesas. They have no names for themselves because names imply separation from the substrate, and these beings have never quite completed that separation.

The filaments are their sensing organs. Each is a single extruded crystal, grown along a lattice flaw until it reaches the surface. When one rises, it is tasting the air for microscopic shifts in humidity or for the faint piezoelectric charge left by the last tremor. The retraction is not withdrawal but digestion; the filament draws whatever ions it has gathered back into the main mass, adding or subtracting a few atoms from the body below. Over centuries a single filament site can migrate several meters along a crack, leaving a faint healed scar in the stone.

The low, faceted shapes that cross the open tracts are more complete forms. They are ambulatory mineral lattices, no taller than a knee, whose facets are tiled repeats of the same white-and-black banding seen in the cliffs. They move by controlled dissolution and re-precipitation: the leading edge softens, flows forward under its own surface tension, then re-hardens, while the trailing edge follows in the same manner. Their speed matches the pace of the wind-sculpted ridges they pass. When one reaches a crevice it does not enter so much as relax into it, spreading its lattice along the walls until it becomes indistinguishable from the surrounding rock. The soft click is the final lattice plane locking into register.

These mobile forms are not individuals in any strict sense. Each is a temporary excursion of a much larger, sessile body that occupies entire clusters of the pseudo-buildings. The avenues and slabs are its skeleton; the tremors are its slow metabolic pulses, adjusting internal stresses and realigning crystal domains so that no single fault grows large enough to collapse a whole district. When the harmonic tone fades, it is the sound of those domains settling into their new lowest-energy configuration.

Farther out, where the mesas stand in wider isolation, still older and larger entities reside. They have not moved in measurable time. Their “city” forms have grown so regular that wind and settling have produced perfect right angles without any further guidance. Inside them, filaments rise and retract on schedules measured in millennia, each one carrying infinitesimal loads of sodium, magnesium, and trace metals from one part of the body to another. Occasionally one of these giants will release a mobile fragment, which travels across the intervening emptiness and merges with a younger formation, carrying a few new crystal planes as a kind of inheritance.

The light that falls at contradictory angles is refracted through the upper layers of their translucent outer shells, bent by domains whose orientation changes with each tremor. The shadows that sometimes lean toward their objects are simply the local geometry of the lattice asserting itself against the ambient illumination. Nothing here grows in the organic sense; everything accretes, dissolves, and re-accretes according to the same rules that shaped the tiers themselves. The entire plain is therefore a single, extremely slow metabolism distributed across hundreds of square kilometers, its inhabitants differing from the rock only in the degree to which they can direct their own slow rearrangements.
