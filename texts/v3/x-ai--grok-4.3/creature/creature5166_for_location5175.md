The Hollow is kept by a people no taller than a child’s forearm when they stand upright, though they seldom do. Their bodies are compact and low to the ground, covered in a fine, downy fur the color of dried river clay that grows thickest along the spine and thins to almost nothing on the palms and soles. The same hands that once raised the sapling lattice now move constantly beneath the half-light: six-fingered, broad-knuckled, each finger ending in a blunt, inward-curving claw suited for both prying loose a tile and cradling a water drop without breaking its surface tension. Their eyes are small and recessed, milky at the edges yet quick to catch the shift of shadow when a beetle pauses; they navigate more by the vibration of the floor tiles underfoot and the changing pitch of dripping water than by sight alone.

They live in loose, overlapping clusters rather than families—sometimes six or seven share a single timber platform for a season, then drift apart when the vessels of stored roots begin to empty. Each individual carries a small pouch of stripped bark slung across the chest; inside are tools no longer than a finger: a sliver of obsidian for scraping lichen, a thorn needle threaded with rawhide, a flat stone that fits the palm exactly for tamping fresh clay into a crack. When they move they travel in loose, spiraling processions that mirror the flight of the winged insects above the water pockets, pausing often to press an ear or a cheek to the mosaic and listen to the deeper breathing of the walls.

Their maintenance of the chamber is not worship or even deliberate craft; it is simply the condition under which they remain alive inside it. A loosened sapling pole is re-lashed before it can sag further because the sound of its creaking disturbs the rhythm they keep in their own chests. A cracked tile is patched with smaller fragments because water that pools too deeply begins to drown the root-threads the people also harvest and dry. They do not plant or herd; instead they harvest what arrives—gathering the pale root mats when they grow long enough to lift without tearing the lichen, collecting the husks the grass tufts shed, and following the endless lines of beetles to learn which grout channels still hold moisture. The lizards they tolerate, and sometimes stroke along the back with one careful claw before letting them flick away; the lizards in turn keep the winged insects from settling too thickly on sleeping bodies.

At certain hours, when the shaft of light from the apex strikes the central mosaic, most of the people withdraw to the edges of the chamber and lie flat on the tiles, bellies pressed to the cool clay, eyes half-closed. They do not sleep so much as listen—to the condensation forming overhead, to the slow expansion of the sapling lattice, to the almost imperceptible shift of water moving through hidden channels in the walls. When the light fades they rise again without ceremony and resume their small, steady traffic across the floor, each footstep pressing the dust and seed husks once more into the grout, keeping the Hollow exactly as it has always been: a place too crowded to be empty and too carefully tended to be wild.
