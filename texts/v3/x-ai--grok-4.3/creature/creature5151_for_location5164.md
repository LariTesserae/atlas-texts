The plateau holds life the way stone holds resonance: not as crowding or urgency, but as a slow, distributed vibration that passes through everything without ever filling it. Most of the surface remains bare to the eye, a pale film of microbes locked in dormancy, their bodies reduced to crystalline sheaths that awaken only when a falling flake from a phytarch lands close enough to share its lattice pattern. Then the film stirs for a few hours, extruding hair-thin mineral threads that knit the new fragment into the larger mat before retreating again into stillness.

Above this film the phytarchs stand as the plateau’s true inhabitants—sessile colossi whose growth is measured in microns per cycle and whose “bodies” are shared across kilometers. Each tower is not a single organism but a continuous lattice in which older and younger crystalline tissue interlock without clear boundary. The hexagonal pores function simultaneously as lungs, ears, and mouths: they admit aerosols, release metal vapors shaped by the colony’s metabolic needs, and conduct the low, constant wind into subsonic signals that travel between towers faster than any chemical messenger could. Inside the translucent trunks, the mineral slurry is not blood but a circulating library; every pulse of bioluminescence carries both nutrients and encoded adjustments to the growth templates laid down ages ago. When two branches meet in their airy catenary, they do not merely touch—they fuse their lattices, exchanging templates so that what was once two towers becomes, for a time, a single distributed body.

The fauna are the active surface of this same lattice. Their exoskeletons are not armor grown around soft tissue but living extensions of the same glassy material that forms the phytarchs; many are little more than articulated mineral frames driven by thin films of the microbial mat that cling to their inner surfaces. Some glide between towers on vanes so thin they catch the perpetual wind like faint sheets of mica; others move across the plateau in low, scraping swarms whose clatter is not random but a form of communication—each impact sends a sympathetic tremor through the connected branches above. When they burrow into a phytarch wall they do not wound it so much as join it briefly, drinking the moving slurry through temporary mineral capillaries of their own before pulling free and resealing the pore behind them. Their calls are not songs but tuned clicks and subsonic rubs that ride the wind already shaped by the towers, layering new overtones onto the plateau’s standing chord.

Nothing here reproduces in the familiar sense. The phytarchs extend by accreting new lattice at their summits while shedding microscopic flakes at their bases; those flakes, caught by the wind, may seed a new tower kilometers away or simply thicken the dormant mat until another flake arrives. The fauna fracture along preset seams when they grow too large, the fragments crawling apart on fresh vane buds until each is again complete. Even the microbial film divides by geometric cracking rather than cellular fission, its pieces drifting until they encounter compatible lattice and rejoin.

The result is an ecology whose participants are less discrete individuals than phases of one another. A single flake may, over centuries, become part of a tower, later be shed and ingested by a swarm-glider, and finally return to the mat as a dormant crystal—only to begin the circuit again when wind or vibration delivers it to new pores. The plateau therefore never accumulates more life than its wind and light can sustain, yet it never loses what it already holds; every fragment remains in conversation with the whole through the continuous, resonant medium of the air itself.
