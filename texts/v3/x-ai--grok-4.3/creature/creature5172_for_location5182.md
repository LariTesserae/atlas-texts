In this low basin the life that remains has folded itself into the fractures and seams, becoming indistinguishable from the slow seepage of the ground. What moves here does not announce itself with flight or song; it inches, scrapes, and pauses. The lizards are the most visible when light leaks through the overcast—small, scale-bare bodies the color of wet shale, each one pressed flat against a slab of concrete that still holds yesterday’s warmth. Their eyes are lidless and lidless again; they do not blink so much as they retreat inside themselves between one insect and the next. When they strike they do it without sound, only the soft click of jaws closing on a thorax that has already begun to curl.

Beneath the lizards, and sometimes inside their own bellies, the insects keep the surfaces alive with motion. Most are thin and hard-shelled, their legs adapted to grip rust rather than leaf. Some carry small mineral plates on their backs, scavenged flakes of oxide that darken them against the iron-streaked walls. Others never surface at all; they spend their entire cycle inside the drip-line beneath a sagging roof joint, drinking, molting, and being eaten in the same narrow shadow. Their discarded casings accumulate in gray drifts along the edges of every channel, the only litter the basin tolerates.

Lower still are the rodents—short-furred, nearly blind things that have learned the map of cracks beneath the floor plates. They move in pulses rather than steady progress, appearing only when a thread of moisture reaches a new low point and softens the silt. Their tunnels follow the old utility conduits; they enter the hollow cores of conveyor housings through rusted bolt holes no wider than a thumb. Inside those housings they build nothing permanent, only temporary nests of shredded insulation and the wing casings of larger beetles. When the nest grows too crowded or too warm they scatter, and the same fan that has run for decades carries the scent of their passage outward into the avenues.

These three layers press against one another without hierarchy beyond the next meal. A lizard will sun itself on a pipe until an ant column crosses its flank; a rodent will pause at the edge of a puddle to lap the iron-tasting film and, while its tongue works, lose its own tail to a patient waiting jaw. The water decides the timing: every new bead that condenses and falls resets the small clocks. Where the bead lands, activity flares for minutes and then subsides again into the patient stillness that matches the heavy sky.

Nothing larger survives the heat and the scarcity of cover. What once walked these avenues left its shape in rust and concrete, but the basin has since been claimed by bodies that require only a film of moisture and a gap no wider than a scale, a mandible, a whisker. Their numbers rise and fall with the slow seasons of seepage, yet the surface always appears to be moving, as though the ground itself were breathing through thousands of minute, ordinary mouths.
