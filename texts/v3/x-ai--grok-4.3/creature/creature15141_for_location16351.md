The chamber is home to the slowfolk, a loose colony of soft-bodied creatures that have grown into the vegetation rather than simply dwelling inside it. They are not quite animals and not quite plants, but something that occupies the seam between the two: elongated, flattened forms threaded through the root mats like pale, translucent grubs. Each is no larger than a forearm at full stretch, with a damp, veined hide the color of new willow. Where they rest against stems or stone they put out fine, hair-thin filaments that drink the constant film of water and trade minerals back into the living walls.

They move only when the drip-pattern around them shifts or when an engine’s low pulse changes its rhythm. A single slowfolk will take the better part of an hour to cross the width of a leaf, its body contracting and relaxing in long, tidal waves. As it travels it leaves a faint silver trail of mucus that the surrounding vines absorb; within a day or two the plant thickens at that exact spot, reinforcing a sagging arch or sealing a hairline crack in an old pipe. The creatures do not appear to coordinate, yet the effect is deliberate: platforms remain level, reservoirs stay clear, and the rusted metal brackets never quite lose their last grip on the stone.

Most stay half-buried, only the dorsal ridge visible, pulsing gently with the warmth rising from the floor vents. A few, older ones, have fused entirely with the substrate. Their outlines show as slightly raised, leaf-shaped bosses on the thicker platforms; from these quiet nodes thin runners run outward in every direction, carrying the faint chemical signals that tell younger slowfolk where moisture is pooling or where an engine belt has begun to slacken. When one of these elders finally stops moving, the vines simply grow over it, incorporating its shape into the next layer of living floor.

They share the space with nothing larger than themselves. No birds or surface insects reach this depth. The only other motion belongs to the condensation itself and to the occasional wandering droplet that rolls along a filament until a slowfolk intercepts it, drinks, and continues its patient circuit. In return the colony keeps the haze breathable and the engines from overheating or seizing. The whole arrangement feels less like tenancy than like a single, very large organism that has learned, over centuries, how to include both root and rusted steel inside its own slow metabolism.
