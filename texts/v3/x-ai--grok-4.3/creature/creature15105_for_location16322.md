The pocket sustains a single, distributed life that has learned to wear many forms at once.

At the smallest scale the described beetles are only the visible edge of it: thumbnail-sized, yes, and eyeless, but their carapaces are not hard; they are thin, living cuticles continuous with the leaf tissue they walk upon. When one pauses, rootlets rise through the plane to meet its feet and exchange a slow pulse of sap and mineral. The beetle is not feeding or being fed; it is remembering, for a moment, a particular curvature of the room that the plants have forgotten while they stretched elsewhere. When it moves on, the memory is left behind as a faint new tension in the leaf.

Larger than the beetles, but no less integrated, are the soft-bodied weavers: elongated, many-limbed creatures whose skins are the same translucent bronze-black as the leaves. They do not so much walk across the planes as become momentary thickenings within them. A weaver can slide from one sheet to the next by simply allowing its body to lose definition along one edge and regain it a hand’s width away, the plane closing behind it without a seam. Their limbs are not truly limbs; they are exploratory thickenings of vascular tissue that can stiffen for purchase or dissolve again into sap. Most of the audible scraping comes from them. They draw mineral from the young stone and lay it down again in long cursive strokes that become tomorrow’s root bridges. The cavern’s low wooden groans are the sound of many weavers uncoiling or relaxing together, adjusting distances the way lungs adjust volume so that every drifting leaf-plane remains just taut enough to catch the next shaft of light.

There is one even larger expression of the same life: a slow, almost sessile presence that occupies the warm negative space between planes the way a held breath occupies a chest. It has no fixed outline. At times it gathers itself into a roughly vertical shape—something that could be mistaken for a standing figure if the light were brighter and the observer less patient—its surface a shifting film of the same vein-work that makes the leaves. Its “head,” when it forms one, is usually only a denser swirl of pollen motes held in a slow vortex; the light that passes through it turns the color of honey for a few heartbeats before dispersing again. This larger form does not move so much as allow the room itself to re-form around it. When it chooses a new position, the plants part and re-knit without tearing; the weavers drift toward it like iron filings and then away again, carrying whatever adjustment it has asked for in the tension of their bodies. No one has ever seen it enter or leave; it is simply more present in one interval than in another, like warmth that has pooled on one side of a bed.

All of these expressions—beetle, weaver, standing breath—are continuous with the leaf-planes and the root bridges. They share the same slow pulse, the same appetite for unoccupied volume, the same mineral taste on the tongue. When a membrane tears and releases its mist of glowing pollen, the dispersal is not random; the motes are guided by whatever portion of the life is currently most concentrated in that sheet, and they settle along trajectories that will become the next weaver’s path or the next thickening of the standing breath. The room therefore does not contain these beings so much as it is them, still in the violent first hours of deciding what shape a single, warm, growing thought should finally keep.
