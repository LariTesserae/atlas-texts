In the half-dark the plateau sustains a sparse but persistent population whose members have adapted to motion measured in centuries rather than seasons. The dark-green crusts are the most numerous form of life. They function less like individual plants and more like living films, each patch an interlocking community of cells that can pause metabolism for months when moisture vanishes and resume within minutes when a shower arrives. Their edges advance by millimeters per decade, following the faint mineral lines left by earlier condensations, so that over time the crusts slowly braid the terrain into a faint, living geometry visible only from low angles.

Scattered among them stand the wiry, leafless stems. These are solitary and long-lived, some of them centuries old. They do not photosynthesize in any ordinary sense; instead they trap the slow-moving vapor on microscopic ridges and channels along their sides, then convert the captured water and dissolved minerals into a thin internal syrup. The faint phosphorescence they emit is not constant; it pulses once every few hours in response to temperature changes in the rock beneath them, creating brief constellations across the plateau that shift as the stems themselves age and new ones seed in distant basins. When a stem finally dries and fractures, its remains are quickly colonized by the surrounding crust, which uses the stem’s mineral skeleton as a scaffold for further growth.

Within the shallow fractures and beneath the stone lips live the small, soft-bodied creatures. They are translucent and slow, their bodies little more than pressurized sacs of fluid and fine cilia. They detect the arrival of water or vapor through pressure changes transmitted along the rock rather than through sight or sound. When the geothermal pulses rise they become briefly more active, riding the faint upward movement of warmth to new fissures or to feed on freshly deposited crystalline layers. Their passages leave no trails visible to the eye, only subtle alterations in how moisture later beads on the stone.

The larger, many-legged translucent forms emerge only during the thicker vapor events. They are low to the ground and wide, their limbs arranged in overlapping rows that allow them to glide rather than walk. Their bodies are nearly invisible against the pale stone except when they pass across a phosphorescent stem, at which point their internal structures show as darker veins. They graze the thin mineral films left by retreating water, rasping them away with mouthparts that also serve as sensory organs; the films provide both nutrition and the salts they need to maintain the delicate osmotic balance inside their own skins. After feeding they retreat into deeper fissures or simply flatten against the rock, becoming indistinguishable from the surface until the next shift in vapor.

These creatures interact with the geology as much as with one another. During a warm shower they spread outward from the basins, their movements helping to distribute spores and fragments of crust across newly wetted stone. The geothermal pulses, in turn, occasionally bring up fluids that alter the chemistry of the basins in ways that favor certain crusts over others, creating slow, invisible successions across centuries. Nothing here reproduces quickly; the intervals between generations are long enough that the plateau’s inhabitants register change the way glaciers do—through cumulative drift rather than sudden events. The result is a living layer that is inseparable from the rock itself, each organism part of the same patient cycle of deposition, dissolution, and recolonization that continues whether or not any eye observes it.
