The enclave belongs first to the smallest. Across every wet plane crawl and drift populations of thumbnail-sized creatures whose bodies are scarcely more than sacs of clear gel crossed by faint radial threads. They drink the perpetual film of condensation, pulling it through their skins the way a sponge draws water upward, then excrete it again as even finer droplets that catch the light and vanish. In the narrow channels between terraces they sometimes gather by the hundreds, forming slow, glistening rafts that inch along until a shift in temperature breaks them apart; the individual creatures then drift free again, rising on invisible thermals until they catch on an outcrop or land, with a momentary tickle, on any exposed skin.

Larger by a factor of ten are the gliders. Their six legs are little more than articulated spines; the real body is the taut, translucent membrane stretched between them. They launch from lip to lip without sound, the only indication of their passage a brief flicker of reflected light where a wing-membrane momentarily aligns with the sun. They prey on the smaller translucent beings, snatching them in flight and folding their legs beneath their bodies to consume them while still hovering. When sated they press themselves flat against the stone, membranes sealing against the cold, and remain motionless for hours while frost crystals form along the cartilage ribs and later melt away.

Deeper still live the crystalline worms. They never surface; instead they etch networks of hair-fine galleries just beneath the outer skin of the rock. Their bodies are segmented rods of cloudy quartz-like matter that flex by dissolving and reforming mineral bonds. As they advance they leave behind walls of re-frozen moisture arranged in branching, feather-shaped patterns that catch sunlight and throw miniature rainbows across the interior chambers. At night, when the temperature drops further, these traceries contract and the rock itself emits a single audible note—a dry, high click—as microscopic seams reopen.

Nothing larger than a glider moves here now. The original carvers left behind only the stone they shaped and the faint, perpetual scent of old bone tools turned to lime. Their descendants, if any remain, have become indistinguishable from the other inhabitants: pale, silent, and continually reworking the same surfaces they once believed finished. The effect is that the settlement feels both empty and overflowing at once—every terrace and vault humming with microscopic traffic, every carved line still migrating by fraction after fraction under the combined labor of frost, worm, and wing.
