The valley-organism sustains itself through a ceaseless internal parliament of tissues, each region a distinct polity that votes with its shape. Along the basal terraces, broad mats of photosynthetic parenchyma argue continuously with the vascular bundles above them; the parenchyma thicken and flatten to admit more light, while the bundles thicken in reply, forcing new channels that reroute water before the leaves can monopolize it. These negotiations produce the audible low roar, a constant basso of expanding walls and sliding cell plates.

Higher, where the shelf-like canopies interlock, entire districts of collenchyma act as living scaffolding. Their cells remain slightly un-lignified so they can still elongate when traffic—meaning a new surge of water or a shift in symbiotic pressure—demands it. Between these scaffolds run the braided aerial roots that function as both roadway and gut: their cortical layers absorb stray nutrients from condensation films and, when the films are insufficient, release digestive enzymes that break down any slow-moving detritus the valley has already begun to envelop. The roots therefore serve as the valley’s distributed mouth and its circulatory police, redirecting resources toward whichever quarter happens to be expanding fastest that hour.

Inside the succulent reservoirs the atmosphere is different: warmer, thicker, and faintly contractile. These chambers operate like slow lungs or communal hearts; their inner epidermises rhythmically tense and relax, not to draw breath but to squeeze stored water into the xylem on a schedule only the valley keeps. Occasionally a reservoir will seal itself off from the rest of the system, fermenting its own small atmosphere until pressure forces a rupture and a new fountain appears on some upper terrace. Such births are one of the few events that momentarily silence the surrounding growth-roar.

At every scale, smaller participants maintain continuity. Sheets of parenchyma occasionally bud off autonomous runner-like strands that crawl along existing surfaces until they locate a fresh fissure; there they insinuate themselves and become the next corridor or plaza. Meanwhile, the very oldest buttress trunks house nested cambia that remember previous configurations; when a newer layer begins to overgrow them, these buried meristems sometimes reawaken and push woody counter-protrusions upward, like memories surfacing through skin. Nothing is ever discarded; it is only repositioned.

Thus the population of the valley is the valley. Its citizens are gradients of turgor, fronts of meristematic activity, and slow osmotic decisions that resolve, over hours or afternoons, into the architecture an outsider would mistake for terrain.
