The inhabitants of the plateau are not so much organisms as slow accretions of persistence. They do not occupy space so much as they have become intervals within it—pauses in the long decline of a world that has refused, for reasons no longer legible, to finish ending.

The lithophytes form the baseline presence. They are not “mats” in the usual sense but living seams, each one a single colonial membrane no thicker than a sheet of mica, threaded through hairline fractures that may have opened during the last crustal tremor or may date to the plateau’s first cooling. Their color is not truly black or green but an absence that swallows light; under the knife-edge of a shadow they vanish, and only when a dust veil crosses the sun do they briefly phosphoresce, a dull emerald pulse that lasts a few seconds and then subsides. They draw nothing from above. Every atom they require is leached, ion by painstaking ion, from the fused silicates beneath them. Their growth is measured in centuries per millimeter, yet they are the most numerous thing that still answers to the name “life.”

Larger forms appear only when the tremors align. The flattened, city-block-sized membranes—sometimes called starfish by travelers who need a word—drift upward from the rock like oil rising through water. Their bodies are not flesh but pressurized silicate films enclosing a single, vast vacuole of trace gases and mineral suspensions. They press themselves flat against the surface for decades or centuries, tasting the slow exhalation of the stone. When conditions briefly allow, they lift a few centimeters, telescope their edges into radial arms, and glide at walking speed across the plain. Movement leaves no track; the dust simply parts and closes again. After a time measured in the half-life of certain isotopes, they settle, deflate, and are reabsorbed, leaving only a faint circular paling in the rock that fades within another century.

Between these scales lie intermediate presences that straddle the border between organism and process. Certain filaments that extrude after tremors do not die when they harden. They remain conductive, carrying minute electrical potentials generated by the daily thermal flex of the plateau itself. Where these filaments intersect the relics—those half-melted lattices of exotic alloy—something like a nervous system appears. The lattices do not think, exactly; they maintain standing gradients of gravitic tension that translate, over geologic time, into slight biases in which way dust veils drift or which fissures widen. The lithophytes that colonize such junctions grow differently: their membranes develop microscopic pleats that align with the local field lines, and the resulting colonies can, across millennia, nudge an entire ridge a few meters higher or lower than surrounding rock. In this way the plateau’s “metabolism” is not purely tectonic; it is coaxed by the living residue of its own engineering past.

Above all of this the dust veils themselves behave like a single, continent-scale organism whose cells are individual mineral grains. When two veils cross, the crystalline pattering is not random; it is the sound of information exchanged at the scale of lattice defects. Grains that have passed through the wake of a starfish membrane carry different charge distributions than those that have only circled above sterile pavement. Over time these differences steer the veils into new closed loops whose geometry slowly reshapes the plateau’s microtopography. The veils therefore function as both atmosphere and collective memory, preserving the faint signatures of every creature and relic they have touched since the last great erosion.

Nothing here reproduces quickly enough to be witnessed. Nothing migrates with intent. Yet the plateau is not empty. It is occupied by a single, distributed metabolism whose components—lithophyte seams, starfish membranes, conductive filaments, gravitic relics, and circulating dust—exchange matter and minute decision at rates that make geological epochs feel conversational. The only constant is the horizon that never arrives and the knowledge that, somewhere beneath one’s feet, another ridge is already deciding whether to rise or to sink.
