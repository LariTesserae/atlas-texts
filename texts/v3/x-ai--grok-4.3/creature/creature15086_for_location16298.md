In this sealed basin, the life that persists does so as an unbroken saturation rather than as separate beings moving through space. The salamanders lie pressed to the stone like translucent scripts, their bodies so flattened that the slow seepage of water passes through the thin webbing between their ribs before continuing downward. Their eyes are not absent but recessed and clouded, registering only the minute shifts in moisture that reach them from above. They do not hunt or hide; they simply occupy the same palm-sized film of rock for intervals too long to measure, their gills opening and closing in a rhythm that matches the interval between falling drops.

The minute crabs are even less distinct as individuals. Pale to the point of near-invisibility, they gather where veins of water trace the undersides of leaves, drifting in loose constellations rather than walking. Their legs trail behind them like fine hairs stirred by condensation alone. When two clusters meet, they do not contend; they simply interpenetrate, each continuing its slow lateral drift as though the other had never been there. Their shells carry flecks of the same pale stone that forms the rim, so that they seem less like visitors than like extensions of the weathering itself.

The slugs are the most diffuse presence. Their bodies lengthen and contract in such gradual pulses that the movement registers only as a continuous glistening that travels the same routes hour after hour. Where they pass, they leave behind not slime but a faint reordering of existing moisture, a redistribution so slight that the moss beneath them shows no sign of having absorbed or rejected anything new. They are numerous enough that any small volume of air contains parts of several at once, yet each appears solitary because no two ever occupy precisely the same leaf-edge at the same moment.

These forms do not feed upon one another or upon the vegetation in any visible way. Instead they move through the intervals between fronds and moss cushions as though the plants themselves were already shaped to accommodate them without displacement. The salamanders rest where the stone weeps most steadily; the crabs gather where the light, already thinned, reaches farthest before failing; the slugs follow the veins that carry water outward from the rock face. Their placements are not chosen but inherited, renewed each time a body withdraws into stillness and another assumes its exact outline moments or centuries later.

Nothing here breeds or dies in any observable cycle. The translucent flesh of a salamander does not grow thinner or thicker; the pale clusters of crabs neither multiply nor scatter; the slugs maintain the same soft weight whether they have traveled the length of one leaf or remained nearly motionless for an age. Their numbers remain constant because nothing ever arrives or departs. The basin holds them as it holds the water and the stone and the filtered light: as parts of one finished density that cannot increase or diminish without ceasing to be itself.

At intervals too faint to be called sound, the combined friction of their movements merges with the descent of drops and the settling of spores. The result is not silence but a single, continuous low pressure of presence that fills the nave from rim to floor and never lifts.
