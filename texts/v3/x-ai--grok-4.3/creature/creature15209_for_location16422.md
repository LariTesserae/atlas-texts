The inhabitants move as though they have always been part of the slow return of stone to water.  

In the shallow channels, the isopods travel in loose files, their translucent plates catching the faint phosphor gleam like ice under moonlight. They pause often, antennae tasting the mineral film where algae has thinned into a living slick; each contact seems to last longer than necessary, as if memory must be refreshed at every step. When a droplet lands near them, they rearrange themselves without hurry, bodies flexing so the water rolls along pre-worn grooves in their backs before being carried forward, shared among many. Their legs leave no prints, only temporary displacements in the surface tension that close again within seconds.

The salamanders follow the same channels but keep to the deeper grooves where the water continues even in the long intervals between lamp flickers. Their skin is the precise shade of the wettest stone, and they flatten against it so completely that only the slow pulse of gill fans betrays them. They do not hunt so much as wait; when an isopod lingers too long over a particularly rich patch of film, a salamander will simply be there, mouth already open in the unhurried way of creatures whose metabolism moves at the speed of seepage. Swallowing takes several minutes. Digestion takes days. The salamanders leave behind only a slightly clearer streak in the channel and a new scattering of pale eggs no larger than condensation beads.

Higher up, beneath the catwalks, the larger amphibian-insect forms hang in loose constellations. Their bodies are elongated, segmented in a way that suggests both crayfish and moth, though neither comparison survives close looking. Six legs end in suction pads that attach to metal and stone alike; between the segments, translucent membranes expand and contract at intervals measured by the drip rhythm rather than any internal clock. These expansions draw in the cool, mineral-laden air and release it as fine mist that joins the constant condensation. From time to time one will detach, drift downward on half-unfurled wing-cases, and reattach elsewhere, the movement so gradual that only the change in position registers. When several shift within the same hour, the resulting redistribution of weight causes the ancient belts above to creak once, a sound the creatures appear to register through their pads rather than any ear.

None of these beings crowd one another. Between every cluster of isopods lies an interval of clear stone wide enough for a salamander to pass without touching; between every salamander lies space for the larger forms to descend and ascend again. The arrangement is not chosen so much as settled into over generations until it matches the spacing of the water channels themselves. When a pressure valve sighs and releases its ribbon of mist, the larger creatures expand their membranes in unison, not in response to the sound but to the slight change in humidity the mist carries. The isopods lift their antennae a fraction higher. The salamanders press more closely to the stone. Then everything resettles, and the long maintenance continues.

Occasionally, higher voices drift down from the narrow walkways—slow syllables exchanged between figures whose outlines are lost in the intervals between lamps. The voices carry the same measured pace as everything else here. The creatures below do not react; the mist and the constant mineral rain absorb the words before they can reach the channels. Life threads the gaps without filling them, and the gaps, in turn, keep widening by the width of a single careful step each generation.
