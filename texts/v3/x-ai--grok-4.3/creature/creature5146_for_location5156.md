The plain is not empty, but it is not inhabited in any ordinary sense. What moves across it and within its nodes does so as part of the same unfinished process that raised the stone itself: an ongoing condensation of form out of heat and vapor.

The smallest creatures are little more than ambulatory fragments of the black-leaf plants. They are polyps no larger than a thumb, their translucent skins still threaded with the same slow, luminous sap. They travel the resonant filaments in dense, slow caravans, pausing at each condensate bead to press their soft undersides against the stone and drink. When a steam eruption begins far away, they flatten themselves in unison, their tiny leaf-like extensions folding tight until the pressure wave passes; then they resume their march. They leave behind faint trails of moisture that glisten for only a few minutes before the mist reclaims them.

Larger beings share the same jointed architecture. Their bodies are elongated clusters of the same dark stems, held apart by internal tensioned threads that run continuously from node to node. Where the filaments cross open stone they become wings—membranous sheets stiffened by the same vibrating material that carries footsteps as chords. These creatures do not flap; they launch from the high scaffolds and ride sequences of pressure bursts between distant nodes, adjusting the angle of a single rigid vane to steer. Their undersides are lined with clusters of the smaller polyps, which they carry like living cargo, depositing new colonies at the edges of expanding basins. When they land, the impact sends a fresh harmonic through the entire node, answered moments later by the leaves flexing in reply.

Deeper within the scaffolds, forms exist that are neither fully plant nor fully animal. Some are stationary lattices of interlaced stems whose central chambers fill and empty with the rhythm of the steam vents, acting as living pumps that move water and dissolved minerals from basin to growing tower. Others drift inside these chambers as loose assemblages of filaments and polyps, reorganizing themselves constantly, testing new configurations of thread and leaf. Nothing here reproduces in isolation; propagation occurs through the gradual extension of the network itself—new stems rising through fresh fractures, new polyps budded directly from older tissue and given a filament to follow.

Even the vapor itself carries passengers. During the loudest eruptions, microscopic motes of living matter are lofted high above the plain, later settling on distant stone as the beginning of new nodes. Over decades these specks will thicken into stems, then scaffolds, then resonant bridges, until the next eruption carries their descendants farther still. The plain is therefore not crossed by its inhabitants so much as grown through by them, the distinction between traveler and territory still in the process of dissolving.
