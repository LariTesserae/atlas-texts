The inhabitants are the canopies themselves, or rather the single, indivisible tissue that has become canopy, wall, floor, and root all at once. It does not occupy the terraces so much as it is the terraces: each broad, waxy plate is at once leaf and paving, each rope-thick strand both stem and balustrade. Growth proceeds not by reaching but by pressing, the new tissue swelling against what already exists until the older layers are compressed into something almost mineral, their original cells still faintly visible as darker veins within the hardened mass.

These beings have no centers. A single organism may extend through dozens of terraces at once, its oldest parts sunk deep in the lower courses while newer lobes push outward along a causeway fifty body-lengths above. Because the distinction between one individual and the next has long since dissolved, the entire slope functions as a single slow intelligence whose thoughts are expressed in the angle of a root plate or the thickness of a mineral crust. Movement is almost imperceptible: over decades a leaf margin may shift a finger’s width to close a gap, or a hanging curtain of stems may thicken until it forces water to find a new route, altering the pattern of drips on the levels below. These adjustments are sufficient to maintain perfect equilibrium with the ceaseless rain and the unchanging heat.

Where the folded spaces occur, the tissue simply continues through the duplication. A terrace that appears twice when viewed from the correct angle is experienced by the canopy as a single continuous surface that has somehow folded back on itself; the same leaves receive light from two slightly different directions at once. The organisms that occupy these places grow correspondingly denser, their layers packed tighter so that the doubled illumination does not overstimulate them. In the narrow gaps that drop farther than the eye expects, the same leaves and stems continue unbroken down the hidden faces, turning the vertical plunge into another curving terrace that only the water and the light ever traverse.

Nothing hurries. The constant roar of falling water is the only rhythm they know, and they have grown so perfectly attuned to it that the vibration itself helps draw moisture upward through their stiffened vessels. Even at the highest crowns, where the slope still ascends into haze, the outermost leaves remain beaded because the entire body below is continuously pumping water skyward through capillary channels kept open by the same mineral secretions that slowly petrify the oldest wood. In this way the inhabitants remain perpetually wet, perpetually balanced, and utterly without need for any other form of life.
