The plateau hosts a quiet multitude that moves less like animals than like slow currents within a single vast body. The smallest inhabitants are the thread-legged skimmers, clusters of eight or ten jointed bodies no larger than a closed hand. They drift across the wind-polished stone in loose, shifting constellations, their legs brushing the rock in synchronized pulses that leave no prints. Each skimmer carries a translucent dorsal sac that fills and empties with the same thin mist the plants exhale; when the sac is full the creature rises slightly, riding brief updrafts between ridges before settling again. They graze on invisible films of airborne spores that settle on every surface at dusk.

Larger and more deliberate are the long-necked steppers, whose bodies remain hidden except for the slow arc of a neck and the occasional silver plume of breath. Their hides are the same matte black as the rock, broken only by faint crystalline ridges that mirror the seams below them. A stepper will stand motionless for an hour or more above a pool, its neck extended so the tip of its beak can touch the water without creating a ripple. When it finally drinks, the motion is so measured that the surface tension barely breaks. The armored scrapers that share the basins with them are even slower: low domes of overlapping plates that inch forward on dozens of short, muscular feet. Each plate edge is lined with mineral-gathering cilia that rasp at the crystalline veins, dislodging microscopic nodules the scrapers swallow whole. Over years these scrapers leave shallow, spiraling tracks that the wind eventually polishes smooth again.

The vegetation itself behaves like a distributed organism rather than separate plants. The dangling mats contract their braided roots in long, tidal rhythms, drawing water upward during the colder hours and releasing warmer vapor when the half-light shifts toward dusk. Certain thicker stems function as living valves: their hollow chambers expand to pull mist inward, then constrict to push a faint current of air along the stone channels, guiding both moisture and the drifting skimmers toward the next basin. Around the pools, the pale fringe growths form low, radial patterns whose leaves tilt in unison every several hours, tracking the faint glint of crystals rather than any visible sun.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or loudly. The steppers lay single eggs the size and color of frost-covered stones directly onto the rock, where they remain for seasons until a passing scraper’s vibrations crack the shell at the precise moment the surrounding mats release a pulse of warmer air. The skimmers occasionally fuse two or three bodies into a temporary larger form that travels farther, then split again once they reach a new ridge. All of it proceeds at the same deliberate tempo as the frost that forms and melts, the nodules that grow and are harvested, the air that moves only because the plants will it to move—an entire living system content to operate inside the plateau’s long, patient silence.
