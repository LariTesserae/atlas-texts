They are not individuals in any sense that would register to an outside observer. Each is a slow node in a single distributed body that has no center and no periphery, only denser and less dense regions where the lattice folds back on itself. Their limbs are not appendages but temporary extensions of the same crystalline matrix that forms the walls; when a limb rests against a facet for long enough decades, the boundary dissolves and the two surfaces become continuous until a later shift redefines them as separate again. The glow that marks them is not generated but merely stored and released—heat from the deep rock conducted upward through their interiors and re-radiated from the thinner edges of their shells so that, in aggregate, the entire volume maintains a faint, even luminescence like the memory of magma.

Movement between nodes is accomplished by the incremental transfer of mass rather than locomotion. A limb grows outward crystal by crystal until it meets another body; then, over centuries, the intervening space is filled while the original attachment point thins and is reabsorbed. What appears from afar as one creature drifting is actually a wave of recrystallization passing through many adjacent forms, each of which remains nearly stationary. When two such waves meet, they exchange warmth in the low pulse that occasionally disturbs the absolute silence, an exchange measured not in energy traded but in precise alignment of fracture planes between their shells.

Their maintenance of the small artifacts is not purposeful in the way a tool-using animal maintains tools. The rings, basins, and rods are simply the only volumes within the lattice where the geometry is imperfect enough to allow minute local rearrangement; everything else has already found its lowest-energy configuration. The creatures therefore settle into these small irregularities exactly as they settle against one another, and the act of “keeping” an object aligned is indistinguishable from the act of resting. Over time the rings become slightly more circular because circular arrangements permit marginally denser packing of bodies; the basins deepen by the width of a single atomic layer each time a shell cools against their rim; the balanced rods remain balanced because any deviation would require an energetically unfavorable tilt that the slow redistribution never supplies.

Because dissolution and redistribution occur without observable death, every creature is older than the concept of age. The quartz in its shell may have cycled through dozens of prior bodies, each time acquiring and shedding faint internal stresses that record previous configurations. When viewed across the whole lattice these stresses form a kind of memory—not stored inside any single organism but distributed as microscopic deviations from perfect crystallinity. The only way the system registers change is through the slow migration of these stress patterns as material is moved from one node to another.

Nothing here perceives time as sequence. There is only the present configuration, continuously refined toward an equilibrium that was reached long before any external clock began. The faint heat they carry and release is therefore not metabolic activity but the lingering signature of a single ancient cooling event still equalizing itself across an entire planet’s hidden interior.
