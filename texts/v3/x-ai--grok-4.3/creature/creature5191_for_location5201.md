The plateau’s true population is not the moving shapes one first notices—though their numbers are legion—but the slower, deeper orders of life that have folded themselves into the rock and iron over uncounted cycles. What moves in the open is only the surface of the arrangement.

The crawlers are not individuals so much as distributed appetites. Each is a low, segmented plate perhaps three paces wide and twice as long, the color and texture of cooled lava. Their backs carry shallow channels that funnel condensate toward the head; the thousands of thin bristles that line their undersides are not simple brushes but living sieves. They taste the films they gather and, by some consensus no single crawler fully directs, alter their route when the residue tastes too metallic or too alkaline. In places where a new pipe gallery has begun to weep, the files thicken into dark, milling drifts. When the files have passed, the stone is left faintly scored, as if polished by countless slow tongues. At intervals the crawlers pause, pile upon one another in temporary reefs, and exchange packets of half-digested condensate through everted mouthparts. The exchange is silent, but the drumming of their legs against one another travels through the rock like a heartbeat felt at a distance.

Above them the lenticular fliers are less bodies than condensed thermals given temporary coherence. Their silica wings are grown rather than assembled; new individuals bud from older ones along fracture lines that heal only after the fledgling has spiraled away. They do not hunt or forage in any ordinary sense. Instead they ride the rising breath of the foundries, spiraling in schools whose internal order shifts with barometric pressure. When a school passes through a drifting bank of cooler mist, their wings ring like distant chimes. The sound is not communication but simple resonance; the fliers keep formation by reading the minute turbulence each wing leaves in the shared medium of steam and air. Occasionally an individual drops out of the gyre, wings darkening as it descends, and settles on a ledge to deposit a single glassy egg no larger than a lentil. The egg remains there for seasons, fed only by the same condensate films the crawlers harvest, until it cracks and releases a translucent larva that must immediately catch an updraft or fall to the lower tiers and be consumed by the gathers below.

In the pipework and cooling channels live the true substrate of the plateau’s metabolism. The bead-sized gatherers are not a single species but a shifting consortium of armored myriapods and silica-shelled mollusks so intermingled that the eye cannot separate them. They move in densities that turn the interior surfaces of pipes into living linings. Their waste products are crystalline scales that progressively narrow the ducts; when the narrowing becomes critical, a wave of larger, specialized individuals moves through, rasping the accretions loose and carrying them to the outer ledges where the crawlers and fliers can reach them. The entire network pulses with a slow peristalsis that matches the cycle of the great presses far below. When a press descends, pressure waves travel upward through the galleries; every gatherer halts for several heartbeats, then resumes with renewed feeding. The pattern is older than any single colony.

Yet even these three forms are only the most visible expressions. Between the rivets and the pores of the stone exist films of organisms so thin they register only as slight alterations in the color of condensate when it evaporates. They change the rate at which iron rusts, the conductivity of certain alloys, the precise tone a dropped rivet makes when it strikes a girder three tiers down. The city’s machines, for their part, have become unwitting hosts: their vibration patterns have been shaped over generations by the constant presence of the gatherer colonies, just as the colonies have been shaped by the machines’ exhalations of heat and metal vapor. The result is an equilibrium in which neither the builders’ remnants nor the later arrivals can be said to “use” the other; each has lengthened the other’s persistence.

At night, when the mist grows thickest and the foundries pause between shifts, the entire vertical city seems to breathe as one organism. The crawlers stop their drumming. The fliers descend to rest on every available ledge, wings overlapping like scales. The gatherers slow to a near standstill, their collective movement audible only as a faint, continuous hiss inside the pipes. For those few hours the plateau holds its single, distributed voice in abeyance, and the rock itself—layered, bored, inhabited—remains the only permanent resident.
