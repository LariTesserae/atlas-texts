The small amphibians are the plateau’s most consistent residents. They are nearly flat, their bodies no thicker than a pressed leaf, and their skin carries the same matte gray-green as the basin rims they cling to. When the wind falls even for a few seconds they rise on stubby, translucent legs and drink from the pooled condensation, throats pulsing once or twice before they flatten again. Their eyes are lidless and the same color as the water; only the faintest darker rim betrays them.

Larger, unseen creatures leave the three-toed prints. The tracks appear overnight in the finest dust and vanish by the next gust. From the spacing and depth they belong to something heavy and low to the ground, its stride long enough to cross between vegetation pockets without ever staying in the open. Sometimes a single broad swish mark trails the prints, as though a tail or wing-edge drags for balance when the wind leans hard. No one has seen them feed or rest; the only evidence they still cross the plateau is the occasional line of prints that begins at one leaf-mat and ends at another without ever showing the animal between.

The vegetation itself moves. The broad leaves are not rooted in soil but in thin cracks where dust has gathered; when a dust-devil passes they lift at the edges like a single living hide and settle again facing a slightly different direction. The wrist-thick vines that climb the monoliths do not grow upward past a certain height; instead they thicken at the base and send new runners sideways across the stone, knitting one pocket of growth to the next. Their blue veins pulse faintly in the moving light, brighter after rain, dimmer when the wind has scoured the moisture away.

Among these shifting mats are the only other persistent signs of life: the low stone rings and the obsidian blade. The rings are never complete; each is a broken circuit of flat stones set edge-to-edge, half-sunk under leaf mold and dust. Inside one of them the blade stands upright, its dark glass etched with the same spirals that appear on the petroglyphs elsewhere. The spirals on the rocks are nearly erased, yet the blade’s edge remains sharp enough to sever a vine without effort. Nothing grows over the blade. The leaf-mats stop short of it, leaving a narrow circle of bare stone.

Nothing else that could be called a person or an animal ever stays long enough to be named. The wind and the vibration keep every living thing either hidden or in constant, low motion, so that the plateau always appears empty while remaining unmistakably occupied.
