They are not fish, nor worms, nor any single lineage that still carries a name on the surface. They are elongated, almost ribbon-like beings whose bodies are built from layer upon layer of translucent tissue, each sheet so thin that when light (such faint bruise-purple light as ever reaches them) passes through, the ridges catch it like the stacked pages of a book left to float. The ridges themselves are not ornament but record: faint transverse pleats that flex and lock in sequence, allowing a single individual to lengthen or contract by a hand’s breadth over the course of an hour, the motion too slow to be seen except as a gradual change in the lattice’s geometry.

Their substance is neither flesh nor jelly but something intermediate, more like wet mineral than animal. When two pass within a body’s width of each other the water between them becomes momentarily stiffer, charged with a tension that transmits the smallest shift outward until the entire drifting mass has adjusted. Contact, when it finally occurs, is never a bump; it is a slow interleaving of their ridged surfaces until one lies partially enfolded by the other. The enfolding may last minutes or days. During that time both continue their measured drift, the combined shape simply adding one more slow-moving node to the greater pattern.

They possess no eyes, no mouths, no visible organs of exchange. Whatever they take from the water they absorb across their entire surface, and whatever they release diffuses so gradually that the water never changes its taste. Their only evident activity is the maintenance of proximity. Gaps that open are closed again by imperceptible corrections; the lattice reforms itself around any disturbance the way warm wax flows back over a fingertip lifted from its surface. Because the chamber is sealed, because nothing arrives and nothing is removed, their numbers have long ago reached the maximum the space can sustain. Birth and death, if they still occur, do so at rates too slow for the water to register as change; what looks like an unbroken population is in fact an equilibrium maintained across centuries.

In the deepest part of their motion, when the whole mass turns together around some invisible axis, individuals at the outer edges sometimes brush the stone. The touch is deliberate and unhurried. They linger against the veined masonry long enough for their ridges to settle into the softened carvings, then drift onward again, carrying away microscopic grains of silt that adhere to their tissue and are later shed elsewhere. In this way they continue the chamber’s endless polishing, returning the hand-laid blocks, year by year, to the single continuous mineral from which they themselves seem almost to have been grown.
