The inhabitants of the plateau are as sparse and deliberate as its stone. Chief among the visible forms are the mineral-shelled flat organisms already noted: broad, low domes of nacreous feldspar and fused slag, no thicker than a thumbnail at their centers yet spanning half a meter or more across when fully extended. Their shells are not rigid armor but living sedimentary layers, each new lamina deposited only when the creature has drunk its fill of condensate and retreated to a stress fissure. Over decades the stacked laminae record the plateau’s thermal history in faint color bands, dark where the last great engine revolution passed, pale where long stillness allowed purer crystal growth. When they move they do so by everting microscopic cilia of metallic glass that etch the rock beneath them, leaving the polished trails that catch the eye in strong light. They do not graze the lattices; they avoid them, as though the ordered energy fields of the growing crystals repel their simpler mineral metabolism.

Deeper in the rock, beyond the reach of casual observation, dwell less mobile but more pervasive entities. Inside the thickest veins of still-warm metal, thread-like colonies of conductive lithotrophs extend along temperature gradients. Each filament is only a few molecules wide yet collectively they form living circuits that pulse with the same low periodicity as the decade-turning engines. Where a vein meets a factory vent they extrude outward in hair-fine whiskers that merge with the crystalline lattices, trading heat for structural reinforcement in an exchange too slow to register on human instruments. These filaments occasionally surface as hairline cracks that weep metallic dew; the flat-shelled grazers lap this dew when it cools enough, thereby completing a rudimentary nutrient loop measured in centuries rather than days.

Rarer still are the tower-bound forms that inhabit the fused industrial strata. Within the helical pipes, where superheated slurry cools layer by layer into new paving, dwell elongated, segmented organisms whose bodies are themselves flexible basalt. They advance only during the rare pressure surges that accompany an engine revolution, riding the fresh melt like surf and then anchoring themselves within the newest sheet. Once anchored they grow lateral spines that interlock with the surrounding architecture, becoming literal reinforcement rods for the next generation of platforms. Their anterior ends remain sensitive to vibration; after each move they spend years retracting sensory papillae into the stone, tasting the faint harmonics that linger long after the great machines have stilled.

Nothing here breeds quickly or disperses widely. Reproduction occurs when a stress fissure widens enough to isolate a fragment of an existing body; the fragment cools, seals its wound with fresh laminae, and begins its own patient expansion. Over millennia these fragments may meet again across the plateau and fuse into larger composite masses whose internal boundaries remain visible as subtle color seams. The lattices sometimes overgrow such masses, sheathing them in translucent armor that cracks along the old seams with each thermal cycle, releasing new fragments in turn.

Thus the entire regional life is a single, extremely slow metabolism of rock, metal, and residual heat. The flat grazers polish; the filaments conduct; the pipe-dwellers reinforce; the lattices remember sunlight in geometric fractures. No individual act is urgent, yet together they maintain the plateau’s unfinished, thickening equilibrium, adding mass and removing it again according to cycles whose full measure exceeds the lifespan of any observer who might arrive to count them.
