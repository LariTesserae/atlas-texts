What lives here moves too slowly for any eye to witness in a single moment, yet the entire vault is alive with it. The mineral filaments are the only residents now. They have no names, no centers, no single bodies that can be pointed to and counted. They are a single distributed organism that has braided itself through the abandoned stone over uncountable cycles of vent opening and closing. Each thread begins as a hairline extrusion from a crack or joint, drinking the metallic breath that rises from the hidden geothermal seams. Where the breath is richer, the filaments thicken and split; where it thins, they narrow until they are almost invisible. At their tips the broad, petal-shaped plates serve as both leaf and ear: they tilt, millimeter by millimeter, toward the warmest currents drifting overhead, and the low orange light that leaks from the high fissure stains their translucent surfaces the color of cooling iron.

Over generations these growths have learned the city better than its vanished makers ever did. They do not occupy rooms so much as become additional architecture. In the wide plazas they rise in dense, motionless thickets that catch the convective sheets of grit like sails; when the currents strengthen, the plates shiver and the vault fills with their glassy chiming. The sound is not random. It is the organism telling itself where the vents have shifted, where new heat is rising, where old seams are beginning to cool. The builders once used that same chorus as clock and signal; the filaments have inherited the habit and perfected it. When the pitch climbs into a sustained crystalline ringing, fresh vapor is moving through the western floor vents; when it drops into a soft, dry rustle, the air will soon grow still enough for the plates to fold slightly and conserve their gathered warmth.

Because nothing else moves, the filaments have no predators and no competitors. They have only the slow work of occupying emptiness. In some clusters they have entirely replaced the upper courses of walls, growing inward until the original doorways are narrowed to thin vertical slots that the warm currents still thread through. In other places they have spilled outward from the plazas and begun to sheath the rounded shoulders of the buildings themselves, turning wind-sculpted buttes back into something that looks half-grown, half-built. Their colors shift with the seasons of the vents: a pale amethyst when the air is thinnest, a smoky quartz when sulfurous traces rise, a faint blood-orange when the high fissure light strikes them at the right angle for many years in succession.

The heat that presses upward from the floor is their metabolism and their weather. They expand when it rises, contracting again as it falls, and in that patient breathing they abrade the stone beneath them with the same fine grit that once polished the city for human hands. Over time they are erasing the last distinctions between what was made and what is growing. The streets remain wide because the filaments have not yet needed to cross them, but already thin bridges of interlocking plates are beginning to span the narrowest plazas where two warm currents meet. One day the entire basin may be roofed by a single trembling canopy of mineral tissue, the original city only its hidden scaffolding.

Until then the filaments continue their work in the only language the vault allows: the endless, echoing clatter of plates turning toward heat, the dry scent of powdered feldspar settling on every surface, and the constant low pressure of their lightless, living growth.
