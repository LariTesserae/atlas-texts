In the half-light the first inhabitants to declare themselves are the vines and the moss. They do not grow across the place so much as answer it, reading every pipe joint and mortar line as a question posed in stone and damp. Where a catwalk runs close enough to the ceiling for condensation to fall in a steady metronome, the vines thicken into living gutters, their translucent leaves arranged in overlapping fans that catch each droplet and pass the excess downward along their stems. The moss, darker, waits beneath them on every horizontal ledge, turning the vibration of a passing footfall into a brief, darker bruise that fades as the water returns.

The isopods follow the same grammar. They are not numerous, yet their faint silver trails mark every pipe that still holds a film of moisture long enough for grazing. Their bodies are narrow, almost translucent when they pause beneath a caged lamp, the segments of their armor edged with the same pale mineral dust that coats the fresh rock. They move at the pace of drying, crossing a catwalk in the time it takes a single rotation of the great flywheels below to complete itself. When two meet they do not stop; one simply lifts a segment and the other slides beneath, a brief silver flicker that evaporates before either has continued on.

Larger forms appear only as absences first. A catwalk rail that was straight an hour ago now carries a shallow pressure dent the width of a hand. A moss patch on a stair landing has been pressed into a darker green where something rested its weight for several cycles. These traces belong to solitary climbers that use the vertical service shafts the builders left unfinished. Their passage is felt more than seen: a slow change in air pressure against the skin, a momentary muting of the engine thrum as something large enough to displace air moves past an intake grille. They are pale in the same manner as the isopods, but elongated vertically rather than segmented, their limbs jointed so that they can fold against the rock face and become another fracture-plane among many. They do not feed on the vegetation. Instead they follow the heat rising from the engines, pausing where the rock is warmest, absorbing what the machinery and the condensation together provide.

The people who cut the galleries and fitted the buildings have become continuous with these older patterns. They keep their speech to the same low register as the engines, words shaped more by breath than by larynx so that the absorbent stone and the thick leaf cover swallow them within a few spans. Their skin has taken on the same faint, shifting phosphorescence as certain wall-growths, not bright enough to read by but sufficient to mark one another when lamps fail. They move along the same three-dimensional lattice as the vines, stepping from pipe to ledge to catwalk without looking down, their routes already memorized in the way the moss has been pressed into permanent pathways. When they tend the engines they do so with rags soaked in the same mineral sediment that lines the rock joints, wiping flywheel rims in slow arcs timed to the machines’ own long intervals. Their children, fewer than the isopods, are born already knowing which drip patterns will persist longest and which vine stems will bear their weight before the fifth year of growth.

Over longer cycles the distinction between these kinds of life blurs further. A vine root that has followed a pipe into a ventilation shaft will one day be pruned back by a pale hand whose fingers leave the same faint silver film the isopods leave. A solitary climber, descending to drink at a condensation trough, will pause while a person passes on the catwalk above, neither acknowledging the other yet both registering the shared displacement of air. The rock continues to darken where moisture lingers, the moss continues to brighten where drops strike, and the inhabitants—whatever name each takes in its own time—continue to learn the precise spacing required so that none of them, plant or crawler or spoken word, ever quite fills the volume the others have already claimed.
