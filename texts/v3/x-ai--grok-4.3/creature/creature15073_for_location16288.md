These are the fracture-wardens. Each one is a forearm-length slab of living mineral, vitreous and segmented, its edges beveled into low, serrated flanges that catch the slow water like the vanes of an anemometer. The plating is not armor so much as an extension of the ridge itself: the same pale, opalescent silicates that climb the walls are grown directly from their dorsal seams, so that an individual drifting past a spire often seems to trail a ghost-image of the formation it has just left. Between the plates run hair-thin fissures that contain the creatures’ only visible organs—threaded veins of conductive salts that pulse once every few minutes with a cool, blue-white spark. When two wardens meet, or when one presses against a living spire, these veins align for an instant and the spark jumps, fusing a new glassy weld that stiffens both bodies and rock alike.

They do not swim so much as translate. A warden will fix itself to a surface with three or four ventral hooks, then push forward in a single, deliberate rasp that lasts nearly a minute, leaving behind a hair-width groove whose walls are already re-mineralizing. In open water they drift in loose, three-dimensional schools, their bodies oriented at slight angles to one another so that the entire mass resembles a slowly rotating prism field. Collisions are constant but never abrupt; when two plates glance together they linger, exchanging the minute electrical suture before drifting apart, each slightly more rigid for the contact.

Their feeding is indistinguishable from maintenance. Wherever a fresh fracture has exposed raw ions, a warden settles and works its anterior margin across the surface in long, meditative passes. The scraping dissolves a molecular layer of the matrix, which the creature absorbs through the same conductive seams that carry its welding current. The waste it extrudes is not detritus but a supersaturated solution that the next slow current carries to another surface, where it re-precipitates within the hour. Thus the population continuously redistributes the very substance from which it is built.

Bioluminescence is faint and constant, a pale seam-light that does not pulse with alarm or courtship but simply marks the locations of recent welds. In the dense lanes between spires these seams create a shifting net of blue-white threads, each thread recording a minute repair performed seconds or minutes earlier. The light is steady enough that the entire chamber appears to be threaded with a faint, living circuitry whose brightness increases almost imperceptibly with every tidal pressure change.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense. When a warden’s plates grow too thick or its conductive seams too numerous, it simply ceases translating and settles into a crevice, becoming another fixed spire. New individuals emerge from the matrix itself where two such retired wardens stand close together, their fused surfaces weeping a colloidal seed that hardens into a mobile larva within a single slow circuit of the water. The larva’s first act is to rasp a groove between its two parents, thereby joining the living chorus of sound and current that never quite ceases in the cathedral throat.
