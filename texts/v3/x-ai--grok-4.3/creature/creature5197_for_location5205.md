The plateau sustains a narrow order of life, sparse and slow, shaped entirely by the intervals of moisture and the long patience of the stone. The dominant organisms are the two forms of vegetation already rooted in the fractures. The low cushions of blue-black leaves function as living reservoirs: each dense mat draws mist and overnight condensate downward along the undersides of its leaves, where the water is held against evaporation. At intervals that span several days the cushions contract in a visible wave, squeezing the collected liquid toward the stone below and forcing excess outward as a brief, fine spray that beads along the hairline fractures. Between contractions the leaves expand again, their surfaces dulling as they take on new chill.  

The taller, jointed stalks operate on a complementary rhythm. Their hollow segments fill until internal pressure forces a thin membrane at each node to open; the water is then released in measured pulses that travel the length of the stalk before dripping into the nearest shallow basin. The stalks themselves lean fractionally toward one another during release, then straighten once the segment empties, an adjustment too gradual to observe in a single glance yet unmistakable over hours. Because the thickets remain separated by expanses of bare rock, these motions never interfere; each colony completes its cycle in isolation, the only measurable activity across the flats.  

No animals traverse the plateau, but traces of former presence linger inside the low-walled settlements. Within the colder air held by the hand-laid courses, fine films of the same blue-black pigment sometimes appear along the inner faces of the masonry, as though portions of the cushion plants had once been encouraged to grow vertically. These films thicken and thin with the same measured contractions as the wild thickets, suggesting the cities may once have supported cultivated versions of the same species before their caretakers departed or thinned beyond recovery.  

A quieter order of life occupies the intervals revealed by the shearing events. When a patch of stone lifts away from itself along an invisible plane, the newly exposed layer remains identical in color and veining yet supports a momentary bloom of microscopic filaments, pale and translucent, that extend upward a few thread-widths before the surfaces knit shut again. These filaments appear to absorb the brief exposure of deeper mineral moisture; once the plane closes they retract or dissolve, leaving the surface unchanged. The doubling that occasionally affects entire thickets functions similarly: the offset image carries a fainter version of the same pulse and contraction, as though a secondary colony occupies a plane immediately adjacent to the visible one. When the images coincide once more, the fainter colony vanishes, yet the interval between separation and reunion appears to allow an exchange of minute droplets or spores transferred between the two layers.  

Clouds that descend at walking pace sometimes carry a finer population. When the low banks touch the stone, they leave behind transient motes—smaller than the width of a fracture—that glitter briefly in the condensation before being drawn into the open segments of the stalks or absorbed by the cushion leaves. These motes do not persist once the clouds lift; they serve only as the briefest bridge between the drifting moisture and the rooted organisms below.  

Together these forms—contracting cushions, pulsing stalks, ephemeral filaments, and the cultivated traces inside abandoned walls—constitute the whole of what lives on the plateau. Each proceeds at the pace of the erosion that first planed the stone: slow enough that change registers only across repeated cycles of filling and release, yet persistent enough that the flats remain unmistakably occupied wherever the fractures allow root or film to hold.
