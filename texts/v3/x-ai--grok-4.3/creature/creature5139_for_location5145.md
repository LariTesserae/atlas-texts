The inhabitants of the plateau are few in number yet numerous in their presence, for each one occupies its allotted interval of stone and shadow exactly as the ferns occupy theirs. They are not numerous the way a crowd is numerous; rather, they recur. The same figures return to the same ledges at the same angles of light, generation after generation, until the distinction between one life and the next has been worn away like a joint in unmortared stone.

Their bodies are long and spare, the color of the slate when it is still darkened by the night’s condensation. Skin lies close to bone and does not fold; where it meets the air it gathers a faint bloom of the same pale moss that softens the terrace cracks. Limbs are jointed in the manner of the long-legged insects that drift among the fronds, each movement completed and then held, as though the decision to move again must be weighed against the perfect stillness it would replace. Their eyes are the matte black of the lizards on the sun-warmed ledges. They blink only when a drop of water gathers on the upper lid and releases itself; the blink is therefore as regular and unhurried as the drip from the cistern lips.

They do not build, nor do they repair. When a beam of split timber finally softens at one end, they simply cease to use the stair or plaza that depended on it, shifting their weight to an identical adjoining route whose timber still holds. The abandoned span remains exactly as it failed, neither cleared nor reinforced, until new growth of creeper eventually draws a living handrail across the gap. In this way the city records its own slight failures without ever requiring correction.

Speech, when it occurs, is pitched so low that it is felt first as a cool stirring along the inner surfaces of the ear, like the settling of condensation. A single exchange may span several slow mornings; each participant returns to the same terrace edge at the same hour, uttering one additional phrase that completes the thought begun days before. Because the air never moves, the words remain suspended between them until the next speaker arrives to receive them.

They eat sparingly of the broad, waxy leaves that grow in the allotted patches between buildings. A single leaf may sustain one inhabitant through an entire cycle of rains; the uneaten portion is left precisely where it was cropped so that the next inhabitant, arriving later, will find the same gap in the foliage and therefore will know the previous occupant has finished. The lizards and soft-feathered birds share these intervals without competition. At times an inhabitant will extend a finger and a lizard will step onto it, the two remaining motionless together until the light shifts and both resume their separate stations.

Over centuries the distinction between the living and the built has thinned. Some of the older inhabitants no longer rise from the stone where they rest; their limbs have taken on the same faint film of moisture and the same cushion of moss that covers the unused ledges. Their eyes still open when a drop gathers. When the next light rain arrives they receive it exactly as the cisterns receive it, and the city continues, balanced, because nothing within it any longer recognizes the difference between persistence and change.
