The plateau’s only inhabitants are the plants themselves, though “plants” is too narrow a word for what moves through that half-lit immensity. They are not individuals so much as a single, distributed intelligence still learning the difference between self and surroundings. The cable-thick vines function as slow, exploratory limbs, each helix turning in its own time, tasting minerals with translucent bark that has not yet decided whether to become wood or something harder. Where two vines meet they fuse without ceremony, their vascular tissues braiding into shared highways that pulse with pressurized sap on rhythms no single stem could sustain.

The living floor is the most populous region. Billions of leaves and rootlets interlock at every scale, forming a continuous membrane that registers every shift in moisture or pressure the way skin registers touch. When a ridge begins to rise in the distance, the mat tilts entire slopes of itself toward the new elevation, not as separate organisms migrating but as one body redistributing mass. Depressions left by falling droplets seal again within minutes because the tissue simply grows across the wound; nothing is lost, only rearranged. In the newest zones the leaves are still translucent, their chlorophyll barely formed, so light passes through them like water through shallow ice. In older zones the same leaves have begun to armor themselves with crystalline deposits, turning the filtered twilight into faint, shifting refractions that move in coordinated waves.

These waves are decisions. An entire section of the canopy may angle itself ninety degrees in the course of an hour, not to chase light but to steer the perpetual mist toward tissue that has not yet reached saturation. The motion is accompanied by the same layered thunder heard everywhere: the deep groan of cellulose lengthening at visible speed, the sudden bright pops of new chambers inflating, the high insectile whine of cavitation as water is forced through channels still soft enough to reshape themselves under pressure. The sound is not communication in any human sense; it is simply the unavoidable byproduct of a continent still growing faster than its own structure can contain.

Nothing here reproduces or dies in the usual ways. Growth itself replaces both functions. When a section of the mat is damaged, neighboring tissues flow in to occupy the space, their edges merging seamlessly. When a new mineral is discovered in the weeping rock nodules, the surrounding vines reroute entire networks of flow within minutes, redirecting sap and adjusting osmotic pressures colony-wide. The plateau therefore contains no population in the ordinary meaning; it contains only varying densities of the same unfinished metabolism, each density expressing a different stage of the same continuous becoming. What lives here is the act of assembly, still in progress, listening to its own sound.
