In this basin the life is slow, patient, and oddly continuous, as though everything here has learned to keep time with the engines rather than with seasons or sun.  

The largest residents are the vines. They are not the delicate creepers that soften old ruins; these are thick, almost woody things whose stems push between rivets and wind around valve wheels until the metal can no longer turn without their permission. Their leaves are broad and bluish, always filmed with the same condensation that beads on the pipes. At night the leaves trap the faint orange glow of the sodium lamps, so that each walkway looks like a dim green tunnel roofed in living glass. The vines do not seem to mind the vibration of the flywheels; they simply grow around the motion, thickening where the throb is strongest.

Smaller life moves in the intervals between structures. The herons are the most visible—tall, gray, and deliberate. They wade the shallow pools that never quite drain, stepping around the bubbles rising from vent pipes as if the released air were a normal part of the water. When they strike, it is not at fish but at pale, translucent salamanders that live in the sediment stirred up by the pumps. The lizards, rust-colored and quick, live on the pipes themselves, pressing their bellies against warm iron during the cooler hours and darting after midges. The midges form low, rotating clouds above every condensation pool, rising and falling in time with the drip from the eaves. At dusk the moths arrive, large and pale, their wings brushing the lamps in soft, repeated collisions that leave a faint dust on the glass.

Underground, in the loam that never dries, the life is mostly root and fungus. Pale mycelial threads follow the buried pipes, feeding on the constant seep of mineral-rich water and on whatever organic film the condensation carries downward. The reeds and ferns that grow in the open ground are shorter and tougher than they should be; their roots have learned to tolerate the faint tremor that travels through the soil from every reciprocating engine.

There are also the people, though they are few and move like the rest of the basin’s inhabitants. They appear at irregular intervals from low doors in the brick halls, wearing the same moss-softened clothes as the roofs above them. Their tasks are quiet: they tighten a valve here, clear a clogged screen there, then stand for a while watching the herons before returning inside. They do not seem to own the place so much as keep appointment with it. When they sleep, the sodium lamps stay on and the machinery continues without them, the sound never rising or falling, only persisting.

Nothing hurries. The lizards, the vines, the moths, the herons, the unattended engines, even the slow erosion of the young ridges—all share the same measured tempo. In this basin, life and mechanism have arrived at the same conclusion: keep turning, keep growing, keep the water moving, and do not ask the work to end.
