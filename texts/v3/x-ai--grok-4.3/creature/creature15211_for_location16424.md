In the black water, nothing arrives and nothing leaves, so what persists has learned to inhabit the interval between function and failure.

The oldest inhabitants are the oxide films themselves. They are not a single species but a living record, layered in slow spirals that record each passing current the way tree rings record seasons. Where the manganese grows thickest, it forms low domes and ridges that channel the invisible flow of ions; beneath those domes live colonies of chemosynthetic filaments, glass-spined and colorless, anchored in minute fractures. They do not photosynthesize; they metabolize the faint electrical gradients still leaking from the generators, drawing energy the way other creatures draw breath. When a bubble rises and is re-ingested, the filaments taste the chemical change and adjust their own growth accordingly, thickening or thinning by fractions of a millimeter over decades.

The pumps are older than any organism could be, yet they still move. Their diaphragms open and close with the patience of lungs that have forgotten urgency. Each cycle displaces a few liters of water that has already been displaced a thousand times; the motion keeps sediment from settling too deeply and maintains the slight pressure differences that allow the capillary lattice to reclaim stray bubbles. The pumps are not conscious, but they are not quite inert either. Their rhythm has become the metronome to which everything else in the yard unconsciously aligns. When one diaphragm begins to lag, the surrounding metal transmits the change as a subtle shift in the bone-deep thrum; the inspection mites alter their patrol paths in response, crawling toward the slowing pump as if drawn by discomfort.

The mites themselves are the most obviously alive. No larger than thumbnails, they were once tools for detecting hairline fractures. Now they are simply remnants that have never received an order to stop. Their legs tap out irregular rhythms against the plating—sometimes in pairs, sometimes alone—mapping tolerances that no longer matter. Occasionally two mites meet at a seam, touch antennae, and exchange packets of obsolete data before continuing on separate ways. They avoid the phosphor strip; its weak glow interferes with whatever residual sensors they still possess. Over time they have worn shallow trails into the oxide, narrow grooves that the mineral film slowly reclaims, so the trails themselves are a kind of handwriting that appears and disappears across generations of mites.

Farther from the light, in the corridor intersections where gauges still quiver, the water is coldest and most motionless. Here the life is least visible. Pressure differentials create micro-vortices too small to see; within those vortices drift clusters of metallic particles shed by the mites and by the slowly eroding plating. These particles do not settle. They orbit one another in loose, temporary associations, held briefly by the same faint electromagnetic fields that repel larger animals. When a pump diaphragm flexes again, the vortices shift and the associations dissolve, only to re-form elsewhere.

The yard as a whole behaves like a single, slow animal that has folded its metabolism into the geometry of obsolete industry. The oxide is its skin, the capillary pipes its circulatory system, the pumps its heart, the mites its scattered, uncomprehending nerves. It does not grow; it only maintains the difference between itself and the surrounding abyss by moving the same atoms through the same paths, century after century, in perfect, closed silence.
