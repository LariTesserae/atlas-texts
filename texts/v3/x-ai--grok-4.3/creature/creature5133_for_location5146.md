The highland shelf is not merely occupied; it is the body. Its true residents are extensions of the same slow metabolism that has replaced geology with tissue.

The mat itself is one organism of continental scale, though it no longer remembers being singular. Its will is distributed across billions of nodes—vascular junctions that decide, without central authority, when a ridge should suddenly age decades in seconds or when a continent of stems should rotate another fraction of a degree. These decisions travel as pressure waves through the hollow pipes, arriving as faint tremors felt by anything standing on the surface. The mat does not sleep; it only drifts between intensities of attention. During its deeper phases entire belts of tissue fall into a slack, translucent state, their bioluminescent veins dimming to a twilight blue while the rest of the shelf continues its grinding work around them.

Within and upon this body live two distinct orders of creatures.

The smaller ones are the cloud-dwellers: iridescent, multi-winged motes that move in synchronized veils rather than swarms. They are not individuals so much as temporary expressions of the mist itself—each one a condensed droplet of the living spores the mat exhales. They drink the weeping surfaces and, in doing so, carry genetic fragments from one vascular pipe to another, performing a pollination so ancient it predates flowers. When they die they simply dissolve back into the constant condensation, their wings softening into new droplets that rejoin the cycle.

The larger residents are the translucent grazers. They are not quite animals and not quite organs. Their soft bodies are composed of the same vascular materials as the mat, yet they possess enough independence to migrate across the open belts between the great plant-continents. They feed by pressing their ventral surfaces against the mat and drawing fluid and nutrients directly through permeable skin. When they vocalize, the sound is not produced by lungs but by sudden contractions of internal bladders that also serve as hydrostatic skeletons. Their barking calls are therefore both communication and structural adjustment; each cry temporarily stiffens their bodies so they can cross the slicker, less supportive zones. Some of them have lived so long and grown so large that their passage leaves permanent troughs that the mat has learned to accommodate with raised, reinforced borders.

There are also rarer, stranger presences. Occasionally a section of the mat will accelerate its growth so violently that it extrudes a temporary skyscraper of spiraled cellulose in minutes. These structures last only hours before collapsing, yet during their brief existence they are colonized by dense clusters of the cloud-dwellers and visited by the grazers, who climb their flanks to drink from the furious new weeping points at the summit. When the tower falls, it does not die; its substance is reabsorbed and its accumulated experience distributed back through the mat’s deeper fiber bundles.

Nothing here is truly separate from the living ground. Even the strange temporal folds—where a falling droplet pauses or a sound arrives before its cause—are disturbances in the mat’s own distributed awareness, moments when too many growth fronts interact and briefly overload the local substrate. In those instants the grazers freeze, the cloud-dwellers flicker out of phase, and the rotating stems stutter in their endless, patient turning.

The shelf remains unfinished because its residents—mat, cloud, and grazer alike—are still deciding what they are becoming.
