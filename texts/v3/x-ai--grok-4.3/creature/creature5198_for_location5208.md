In the layered canopies the first sustained presence belongs to the frond-walkers: elongated, low-slung beings whose bodies are more membrane than flesh, stretched taut across the overlapping leaves like living bridges. Their dozens of jointed legs end in adhesive pads that never puncture the wax; instead they sip the constant thin film of condensation, drawing both moisture and dissolved minerals into translucent channels that pulse visibly beneath their skin. When they move they do so by rippling their entire length in slow waves, each wave timed to the breathing rhythm of the flowers beneath them. Over years a single walker will wear a faint path across the canopy, a groove that the plants then reinforce with extra veins of supporting tissue, turning the creature’s habitual routes into permanent living causeways.

Farther down, where the root mats meet the weeping rock, clusters of cave-limpets adhere in dense colonies. Each is a flattened spiral of chitin and mineral, its inner whorls filled with symbiotic algae that glow the same faint blue-white as the ancient conduits. The limpets seal themselves against the stone during the warmest hours, then crack open at dusk to extend feathery gill-fans that drink the rising metallic vapor. Their excretions are alkaline; over decades they soften the rock just enough to deepen the channels the water already follows, so that every new generation of limpets inherits a slightly more intricate network of private rivulets. In places where the platforms still carry their hair-thin light, the limpets have congregated in greatest numbers; their combined glow strengthens the old luminescence rather than competing with it.

The armored gliders are not solitary. Each carries a permanent under-crew of thread-mites that live in the seams of its wing membrane. The mites harvest flakes of shed armor and in return keep the membrane free of the heavier molds that would otherwise weigh it down. When a glider lands on a mesa edge it spreads its wings flat against the warm stone for days at a time, becoming a temporary extension of the canopy itself. During these rests, smaller browsers climb aboard to graze on the thread-mite larvae, an arrangement the glider tolerates because the browsers’ grazing stimulates fresh membrane growth exactly where the old growth is thinnest.

Deeper still, within the steaming basins, live the slow sirens: palm-sized, radially symmetric animals whose bodies are mostly water held in transparent sacs. They drift just beneath the surface in loose constellations, their internal organs arranged like the spokes of a wheel that turns once per day. When a flower overhead releases its vapor cloud, the sirens rise and spin, converting the metallic compounds into threads of crystalline salt that they trail behind them like faint comets. The threads sink, seed the basin floor with new mineral, and eventually become part of the same fused platforms that already exist. No siren ever lives long enough to see the platform it helps build reach maturity; their colonies measure time in the widening rings of salt they leave behind.

At the very margins of the open air, where the vapor thins into true haze, drift the veil-nets: colonies of colonial organisms that behave like single translucent sheets many meters across. Each net is woven from thousands of filament-thin animals whose bodies contain microscopic lenses. By day the lenses focus sunlight into pinpoint heat that causes the net to ripple and tack against the thermals; by night the same lenses gather starlight and re-radiate it as a soft, directionless glow. The nets never touch the canopy or the stone; they remain suspended in the boundary layer, feeding on airborne spores and the occasional stray vapor plume. Occasionally a glider will coast straight through a veil-net, tearing it into drifting fragments that each begin to grow into new sheets within a single season.

Nothing here is transient. Even the shortest-lived insect in the resonant swarms is part of a lineage whose collective memory is stored in the changing pitch of their drone, a frequency that has shifted only a few notes since the first luminous platforms were laid. The mesas themselves are occupied rather than merely inhabited; the rock, the water, the plants, and the animals form a single metabolism whose heartbeat is measured in centuries. Any newcomer—whether a stray spore or a fallen fragment of smoother tower—simply joins the same slow circulation and, in time, forgets it was ever separate.
