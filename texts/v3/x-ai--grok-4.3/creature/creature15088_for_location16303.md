In the high silence between rock and water, what lives here does so without hurry or hunger. The plants themselves are the oldest inhabitants, though they barely seem to move. Their spongy domes and ridges breathe through seams too fine to see, drawing the mist straight into the seams between one cushion and the next. Each dome is both individual and part of a larger, loose collective; a single plant may extend its pale filaments only the width of a fingernail in a year, yet the whole mat shifts and settles like a slow tide, closing gaps, lifting slightly where water pressure builds beneath, then subsiding again. Inside these living ridges the temperature stays even and cool, and small pockets of air remain trapped for decades, growing slightly more acidic each season from the slow exhalations of the plants.

The rare insects are little more than moving droplets. Translucent bodies no larger than a grain of sand drift between the cushions on wings that do not beat so much as drift on the constant micro-currents of mist. They lay eggs in the shallow lenses of standing water atop the plants; the eggs themselves remain for years, developing at the same rate as the stone itself weathers. When an adult insect dies, its body dissolves into the mat within hours, adding almost nothing measurable, yet the plants have learned to wait for exactly this faint addition.

Farther down, where the concrete housings interrupt the green, the flywheels turn in their own kind of life. They do not think or intend, yet their motion is so steady and so ancient that it has become part of the mountain’s own rhythm. Water enters them at a pressure that never varies; the wheels answer by pushing the same water onward through stone channels carved before the plants arrived. In the long intervals between one full rotation and the next, condensation gathers on the metal spokes, runs down, and is carried away again. The concrete itself has begun to soften at the edges, its surface etched by the same slow chemistry that softens the rock plates above it. Cables trail outward like exploratory roots, half-dissolved into the living mats, carrying only the faint memory of current.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or competes fiercely. The spaces of open stone between the ridges are crossed only by vapor and by the occasional insect that wanders too far and is never replaced. The rock continues its own slow lifting; the plants continue their own slow spreading; the wheels continue their own slow turning. All three meet at the edges where concrete meets growth, where water meets light, where sound becomes the only constant motion. Over centuries the balance may tilt a little—more green, slightly less roar, a fraction less raw exposure—but the manner of living will remain the same: patient, dispersed, and almost entirely without haste.
