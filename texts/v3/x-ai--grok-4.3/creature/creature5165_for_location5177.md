In the slow breathing of the chamber, life does not announce itself with movement so much as with persistence. The vines and fungi have become a single organism stretched across centuries, each shelf and frond trading warmth and water with its neighbors until the distinction between root and mycelium blurs. Where the ceiling drips most steadily, the canopy thickens into layered canopies that catch the falling beads and redirect them along channels of their own making, so that certain basins receive a measured trickle while others remain almost dry. The blue threads beneath the fronds brighten when moisture arrives, then dim again, a quiet pulse that seems to follow the rhythm of the heat rising from the cracks below.

The pale, many-legged grazers are more numerous than they first appear. They do not simply slide along leaves; they linger where the condensation has gathered into thin films, rasping at the algae with tiny mouthparts that leave those silver trails. The trails themselves are not waste but a temporary scaffold: other, even smaller organisms follow them, feeding on the residue before the glow fades. Overhead the translucent gliders seldom flap. Instead they drift on the warm updrafts, their flat bodies catching the faintest currents so that they circle for hours without touching anything. Only when a drop falls near them do their edges ripple, adjusting course by a fraction.

In the steaming basins the amphibians remain so still that their outlines dissolve against the rock. Their skin is nearly the same temperature as the water, and they adjust their buoyancy with minute shifts of internal gas so that only their eyes and the tops of their heads remain above the surface. When one lunges, it is not hunger that decides the moment but the precise alignment of ripples; the others in the cluster do not react, as though each strike belongs to its own private sequence. Deeper in the foliage the larger segmented creatures move even more sparingly. Their bodies are jointed like old stonework, and they pause for long intervals with segments pressed against the warmer rock, absorbing heat before resuming their slow progress along routes that avoid the basins entirely.

The ancient stones have been absorbed so thoroughly that they now serve the living mass. Roots have threaded through the fitted circle until the stones function as anchors rather than walls. The upright slab, its carvings long ago softened into vague undulations, collects a steady film of water that the vines follow like a path. Nothing here grows around the remnants; the remnants have simply become another layer in the same slow exchange of mineral and tissue.

Light and heat move through the chamber as a single current. When a glider’s wing or a grazer’s trail disturbs a vine, the glow rises not as alarm but as a brief increase in local vapor, feeding the condensation above so that the next drops fall a little sooner. The metallic taste in the air is the constant exhalation of all these processes together—rock warming, fungi respiring, water cycling through membranes that have learned to use one another. Nothing hurries. Even the rare sound of claw against stone arrives already softened by layers of leaf and mist, as though the chamber itself decides which disturbances are permitted to travel.
