The lithic ones are not quite animals, nor quite mechanisms, nor quite the land itself, though each of these descriptions fits them for a time before sliding away again. They rest most often within shallow depressions they have worn for themselves over centuries, depressions so gradual that a casual eye mistakes them for natural hollows. Each body is a single dense nodule of aggregated crystal and compacted dust, its outer surface faceted by slow abrasion until it catches the half-light in precise, shifting geometries. Inside, the grinding plates turn like millstones—soundlessly, over weeks—pulverizing the mineral salts they have gathered. The plates are the same material as the veins that still thread the plain; what the creatures consume, they also are.

They do not breed in any ordinary sense. Instead, when two of them come to rest against one another for long enough, a thin seam of new lattice forms between their shells. Over further years the seam thickens, hardens, and finally breaks free as a smaller copy, already possessed of the same patient hunger. The young are scarcely larger than pebbles at first and remain stationary for many rotations of the sky while their interior chambers finish forming. During this period older individuals may drift close and gently press against them, transferring minute grains of pre-worked mineral the way one might pass a memory.

Movement, when it occurs, is less a decision than a yielding. A creature senses a slightly richer film of evaporated residue a body-length away and leans, infinitesimal fraction by fraction, until the tilt becomes irreversible; then it rolls once, settles, and resumes stillness. In this manner whole populations have crossed basins over millennia, leaving behind only the faint polishing of their passage. At certain seasons, when the wind carries an especially fine dust, the creatures align their facets perpendicular to the flow; the resulting low sheen can be read, from a great height, as a slow map of where the most concentrated feeding has happened.

Their relation to the black-green cushions is oblique and ancient. The plants root into micro-fractures that the lithics have, over time, enlarged by their grinding and excretion; in return the cushions release a faint organic vapor that the mineral veins beneath the surface seem to conduct and store. A single creature may spend an entire lifetime beside one cushion without ever touching it, yet their presence together keeps the local lattice faintly active. When a cushion eventually withers, the nearest lithics converge over decades, methodically reducing its waxy rind and returning the carbon to the stone in altered form.

Above them, nothing lives in the ordinary sense, yet the slow cyclonic cells themselves behave like vast, diffuse organisms. They draw moisture upward from the basins, hold it for months, then release it again along paths that have become traditional. The lithics and the cushions register each cell’s approach as a change in temperature and electrical gradient; the plain registers it as a slight brightening of its crystalline memory. In this way the entire shield participates in a single metabolism whose pulse is measured in centuries rather than heartbeats. The creatures are its most visible agents, but they are not separate from it; they are the places where the old industrial residue has learned, very slowly, to move itself.
