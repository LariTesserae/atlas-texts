In this basin the living things are not visitors but extensions of the minerals themselves, expressions of the same slow pressure that first heaved the crust into ridges.  

The thread-like beings are the most mobile. Each is a single translucent filament, no wider than a quill, its body a chain of microscopic salt chambers linked by flexible silicate joints. They move by peristalsis, pushing dissolved gypsum ahead of them and redepositing it behind in hair-thin ridges that the next air spiral will erase. When two threads meet they pause, press their flanks together for several minutes, and exchange a few grains of darker, ion-rich sediment; the exchange leaves both filaments slightly more opaque for an hour afterward, as though they have traded a memory of where the richest crust still lies undisturbed. After the exchange they separate without ceremony and continue their patient circuits, never traveling farther than the distance between two neighboring towers.

The colorless mats that cling to the lower columns are not growths but interfaces. Each mat is a single, continuous membrane of living halite, one molecule thick in places, thicker where it has folded into vein-like pleats. The membrane breathes through the crystal it covers, drawing sodium and sulfate ions inward with every contraction and expelling water vapor so faint it never condenses. Where two mats from adjacent columns happen to touch, their edges fuse for a time, forming a temporary bridge of living salt that conducts charge between towers; when the charge equalizes the bridge dissolves, leaving behind a new seam in the crystal wall itself. The mats therefore do not merely inhabit the towers—they maintain them, coaxing the columns into new alignments by the subtlest redistribution of mass.

Deeper still, inside the hollow cores of the tallest towers, live colonies of even smaller entities: glassy spheres no larger than dust motes, each enclosing a droplet of saturated brine. These spheres drift in slow currents of their own making, rising and falling within the column as temperature changes by fractions of a degree. When enough of them accumulate against one inner facet they press outward together, nudging the entire column a hair’s width toward the light that never brightens. Their collective pressure is the only force that keeps the towers from slumping back into the crust from which they rose.

Nothing here reproduces quickly or dies suddenly. A thread may travel the same circuit for decades before it frays and is reabsorbed by the grit. A mat may seal an entire tower for a century, then thin and vanish, exposing fresh crystal for a newer membrane to claim. The spheres inside the tallest towers simply multiply or dissolve according to the ion supply, their numbers rising and falling like the level of an invisible tide. All of them move, adjust, and persist at the same deliberate pace as the air spirals and the contracting crystal. In this low chamber, life is indistinguishable from the continuing act of crystallization itself.
