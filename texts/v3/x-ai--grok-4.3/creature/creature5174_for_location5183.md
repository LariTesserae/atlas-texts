In the ceaseless churn, life does not arrive so much as leak through, arriving already half-dissolved and forced to finish itself again with every pulse of current. The ribbon of vegetation—more lung than leaf—drifts in long, hesitant veils. Each filament is a single cell thick, a living boundary between water and the memory of air. It never stays whole; when a gyre seizes it, the ribbon tears into hundreds of smaller threads that continue photosynthesizing even as they are flung end over end. In calmer pockets they begin to knit themselves together again, faint pulses of faint light traveling along the seams like signals deciding whether to become one organism or many. Their color is the same weak-tea translucence as the water itself, so they are visible only when they tangle around a rising bubble and are briefly outlined in silver.

The hand-shaped nodules are not quite stone and not quite abandoned implements. Some still bear the ghost of a thumb-stop or finger groove worn by pressure that no longer exists; others are growing new protrusions—crystalline nubs that might become tools or might simply be the mountain continuing its own unfinished sentence. When a strong vortex drives two nodules together they do not shatter. Instead they briefly adhere, trading mineral ions and tiny spirals of organic film before the current pries them apart again. In this way they exchange possibilities: a tool edge migrates from one nodule to another across hours, decades, or only the span of a single collapse of water.

The smallest residents are the ones the water most clearly remembers how to make. The hand-sized membranes jet and fold through temporary corridors of reduced turbulence. Each is a single continuous sheet of living tissue folded into a crude, collapsible cone; the darker thread within is not a gut but a contractile strand that tightens and releases to expel a jet of water. When the strand fires, the creature somersaults through the turbulence like a thrown scarf. Its edges are studded with microscopic cilia that taste dissolved minerals and the faint metabolic traces of the drifting vegetation; if a favorable eddy is detected the cilia stiffen and the membrane flares, turning the creature into a temporary sail that rides the shear line until the next violent shift. Because the water is so cold and nutrient-poor, these creatures rarely reproduce by fission. Instead they occasionally split a fragment of membrane that drifts free, already forming its own contractile thread before it is lost in the roar. The parent shrinks by that fraction and continues, altered but still viable.

Nothing here breeds in the ordinary sense. The vegetation, the nodules, and the membranes are all versions of the same unfinished decision the mountain itself is still negotiating: whether to remain liquid, to solidify, or to become something that moves between both states. Every organism carries a portion of that negotiation inside its own body, and every current that rips through the chamber is therefore also a form of inheritance, passing unfinished shapes from one generation of turbulence to the next.
