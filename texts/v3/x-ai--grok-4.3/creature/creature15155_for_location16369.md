In the intervals between impacts, the basin’s life draws itself forward. It does not push against the needles or ripple the pools; it simply waits inside the same geometry the rock has already offered, then lets a small portion of its own mass trade places with a hanging thread of water. The thread hesitates, thins, and continues; the organism has already passed, carrying away a film thin enough to remain invisible except as a momentary shift in tension across several nearby spines at once.

These bodies are not separate from the precipitates. Where the glassy sheets lie thickest, a living layer moves within them, slower than the flexing of the sheet itself. Its substance is a shifting lattice of the same pale salts, held together by surface charge rather than fixed bonds. When it needs to cross open rock it loosens a few planes of its lattice, lets water enter, and becomes a transient meniscus no thicker than the condensate already present. In that state it can slide between spines, guided by the same hairline fractures that carry seepage outward. Once across, it tightens again, depositing a new lamina indistinguishable from the non-living sheets around it. Over years the difference appears only as a slightly different interference color when light catches the surface at the right angle.

The resonant tones originate deeper. Inside certain spines, narrow chambers have grown whose walls are lined with the same living lattice, now rigid and acoustically tuned. When the lattice in one chamber contracts by a few molecular layers, the chamber’s volume changes and a standing wave is released into the surrounding needles. The wave travels as a low vibration through the continuous sheet of water films; elsewhere in the basin, other chambers answer by relaxing in sympathetic time. The exchange carries information—an adjustment of growth rate, a warning of an approaching shift in seepage, a slow census of how many sheets have thickened since the last cycle. Because the signal moves through water and stone rather than air, it arrives already altered, so that each receiving chamber hears not the original tone but the cumulative history of every surface it crossed.

The circular depressions are nurseries of a sort. Their polished walls and still lenses are the places where a lattice must loosen completely, becoming almost wholly liquid for a brief period. In that state it can divide; the two resulting portions drift to opposite rims, re-solidify, and begin extending new sheets outward along the rock. The depressions therefore enlarge by imperceptible increments, their rims rising as new precipitate is added from below. When a young lattice first hardens it often produces a single pure tone, higher and clearer than the adult resonances, before settling into the slower rhythm of the field.

Nothing rushes. A single organism may spend a generation simply aligning its internal planes with an existing fracture so that future water will follow the path it prefers. Another may devote centuries to thickening one wall of a resonance chamber until its pitch matches a distant neighbor. The only urgency is the maintenance of enough open water films for transit; should the dripping cease for too long the lattices dry and fix in place, becoming ordinary precipitate until moisture returns. In that sense the life of the basin is coextensive with its weather: wherever water continues to arrive and depart, the slow redistribution of mass and tone continues, written in the changing pattern of echoes and the almost imperceptible leaning of spines toward one another.
