In the warm chalk of the hollow live the slow chemists first: thread-fine colonies that draw their breath from the stone itself, exhaling microscopic crystals that knit into the white filaments and brittle crusts. They move nothing visible, yet they soften the mineral just enough for other life to find purchase. Where their work thickens, the stiff sage-colored thickets rise—each “leaf” a wafer of precipitated salts and captured dust, translucent at the edges, arranged in overlapping fans that tremble at frequencies too low for most ears. These growths do not photosynthesize so much as accrete; every rare wisp of moisture from the highland wind is turned into another translucent scale, another fraction of height. They stand shoulder to shoulder because solitude would let the wind strip them bare.

Between their crowded stems move the pollinators—small, matte-winged insects whose mouthparts are tipped with the same chalky powder that coats the crusts. They do not sip nectar; they harvest the living mineral films and carry them from one thicket to the next, exchanging grains the way larger creatures exchange pollen. Their wings produce the constant high whine; their bodies, when they land, add the soft percussion of many tiny impacts. Some of these insects are little more than wing and gut, others carry faint ochre markings that match the older growths, as if the plants have begun to mark their tenders. A few species never leave the hollow at all; they are born, mate, and die within a single low canopy no wider than a handspan.

Among the insects live still smaller things: pale, many-legged grazers that rasp at the fibrous stems with chitinous jaws, producing the dry, papery rustle. They in turn are prey for the thinnest of lizards—creatures whose scales are nearly translucent and whose feet end in tiny suction pads that let them cling to vertical mineral skins. The lizards move in short, deliberate bursts, never more than a body length from cover. Their droppings, rich in the same salts the growths crave, are quickly claimed by the crusts.

All of these lives press against or inside the old stacked stones. The stones are no longer simply ruins; the insects have widened convenient crevices into galleries, the grazers have worn doorways round, and the mineral crusts have sealed former joints until the walls seem to have grown rather than been built. Inside the lowest platforms, in darkness no thicker than a finger’s width, clusters of the white filaments glow with a faint, steady warmth generated by their own slow reactions. Small feet cross these glowing threads nightly, and the dust they stir is immediately reincorporated into new crust.

Nothing here is separate from the stone. The thickets add to it, the insects subtract and redistribute it, the filaments bind both actions into one continuous surface. The hollow therefore contains no single population but a single metabolism that has learned to include stacked limestone, airborne dust, and the warm, steady breeze funneled from the ridge above.
