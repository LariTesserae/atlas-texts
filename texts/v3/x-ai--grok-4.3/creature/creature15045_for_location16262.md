In the perpetual hush of the plateau, the silicate beings that pass for trees are not fixed entities so much as slow negotiations between rock and vapor. Each one begins as a single vertical lamina of hydrated silica that, over centuries, accretes new layers the way a pearl adds nacre—except the accretion is lateral, a continual folding outward that produces the fan-like arrays of translucent plates. These plates are not passive; microscopic tubules within them pulse with a faint piezoelectric current generated by the daily expansion and contraction of the plateau’s own atmosphere. When the pressure gradient reverses at dusk, the current reverses too, drawing condensed droplets along the channels with a deliberation that is almost peristaltic. The droplets do not merely water the organism; they carry dissolved ions stripped from wind-borne dust, ions that the plates then electrodeposit back into their own crystalline matrix. In this way the “forest” is literally built from the air it breathes and the dust it catches.

The thumbnail-sized grazers are even more intimate with the mineral substrate. Their armor is not chitin but a mosaic of micro-crystalline plates grown in situ from the same quartz seams that vein the ground; as an individual matures it actually etches its way into a chosen fissure, enlarging the crack by a few micrometers each year with acidic exudates that dissolve the feldspar matrix. Inside this self-made crevice the creature remains torpid, its metabolic rate so low that a single grain of condensed moisture, harvested from a passing silicate plate overhead, can sustain it for weeks. When the light finally tilts and the surface cools, the grazers emerge in their pairs or trios, each one dragging a filament of extruded silica behind it like a dragline. These filaments serve as both sensory organs and territorial markers; when two filaments cross, the grazers pause, taste the ionic signature left by the other, and either retreat or continue, depending on whether the dissolved trace metals match the profile of kin or stranger. Over generations the crossings accumulate into faint, branching maps etched into the pale crust—maps that are continually overwritten by the same wind that redistributes the dust, so the record of movement is always in the process of being erased and redrawn.

Occasionally a larger disturbance occurs: a quartz plate on one of the taller silicate forms reaches the limit of its flexure and fractures along a pre-existing cleavage plane. The resulting shard does not simply fall inert. Within hours the broken edges begin to re-mineralize, drawing silica from the surrounding grit; within days a new, smaller fan has begun to sprout from the fracture site, as though the organism were deliberately subdividing itself. The fallen shard, meanwhile, is colonized almost at once by the grazers, which treat its still-warm surface as a temporary nursery, laying clutches of eggs that are themselves nothing more than microscopic silica spheres containing a single dormant crystal seed. When the eggs hatch weeks later, the emergent young carry that seed with them until they find a fissure of their own, at which point the seed is deposited and the cycle of etching and armor-building recommences.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense; nothing dies in the usual sense either. The plateau sustains a single, continent-wide metabolism whose cells are these jointed silicate fans and whose circulating fluid is the ceaseless, self-similar drift of pale dust under constant light. Every organism is both a participant in and an expression of that larger equilibrium, its individual flexures and migrations indistinguishable from the larger movements of wind and pressure that the plateau itself maintains.
