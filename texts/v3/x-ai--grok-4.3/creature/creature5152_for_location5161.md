The only things that still move with purpose here are the small, slate-gray armored crawlers—scavengers no larger than a hand—that slip between the mineral tubes at dawn and dusk. They press their segmented bellies to the stone, rasping away the thinnest rime of evaporated salts with barbed mouthparts, then retreat into the honeycomb before the heat can cook them. Their eyes are clusters of black beads that swivel independently; when one pauses to drink from a seeping fracture, the others freeze in perfect stillness, as if listening to the same low drone that saturates the air.

Everything larger or slower has already become part of the architecture. The original settlers did not so much leave as dissolve: their bones, tanned to the same pale mineral as the streets, lie threaded through the dry-fitted walls, each joint sealed by the same fibrous ropes now spun from shed membrane. Occasionally a new length of rope is still added—where a crawler dies against the stone and its fluids are drawn upward—but the human presence has thinned to a memory held in the shape of alleys and plazas.

What remains dominant is the coral-like growth itself. Each tower functions less like vegetation and more like a single slow animal whose many lobes happen to be fixed in place. The membranes inhale and exhale in staggered waves so that one tower is always swelling while its neighbor contracts; the sound that results is not random vibration but overlapping pulses that travel through the salt bedrock and return altered. Where two towers grow close enough for their tissues to touch, the membranes fuse into a shared skin that thickens and thins together, sharing whatever trace vapor each has captured. Over centuries these merged clusters have lifted entire plazas on their salt spines, buckling the fitted stone into gentle domes whose undersides are lined with new feeding tubes.

In the largest of the open squares a single massive spiral has grown almost horizontally, its crown level with the second-story walls. Its surface is scored with shallow channels that catch the drifting membrane fragments before they can escape the basin. The fragments are not wasted; they are drawn inward, pressed flat, and reincorporated as additional elastic layers. The square therefore functions as both nursery and archive: every generation of tissue records, in its thickness and curvature, the exact sequence of rare moisture events that have occurred since the settlement was last occupied.

Nothing preys on these towers. The crawlers avoid them, and the wind that once sharpened the stone now only carries away the finest dust they shed. In return, the towers keep the remaining moisture locked inside their own chemistry, releasing just enough to maintain the tunnels that feed them. The basin therefore sustains two parallel populations—one armored and fugitive, the other fixed, resonant, and slowly rewriting the city in its own image.
