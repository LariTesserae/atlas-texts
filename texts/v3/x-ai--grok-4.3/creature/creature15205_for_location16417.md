They are not creatures in any sense that admits departure or growth. They are the accretions themselves: dense, faceted nodules of precipitated mineral, each no larger than a grain of sand yet packed so tightly that whole ledges disappear beneath them. Their surfaces are not smooth. Every facet terminates in a sharp, incomplete edge where the next layer should have formed and did not; the interruption is permanent. From a distance the swarms look like patches of dull hoarfrost. Up close they are a single, continuous crust whose boundaries follow every contour of the unfinished stone as if the stone were their unfinished body as well.

What moves inside them is not locomotion but a ceaseless internal translation. Within each tiny crystal, ions shift one lattice site at a time, always in the same direction, always at the same rate. The motion produces no visible change because every displacement is immediately replaced by an identical displacement from the opposite direction. The result is a standing wave that fills the lattice and passes outward into the surrounding air as the low, grinding drone. Because the exchange never reverses and never stops, the sound neither rises nor falls; it is simply the audible signature of a structure that has reached the only equilibrium possible under these conditions: perfect, localized agitation without displacement.

Where the crusts lie in shallow water the vibration travels through the film and makes the surface quiver so faintly that the water appears still, yet the reflection of the dim sky fractures and reforms without pause. On the vertical faces of the half-towers the same crusts grow outward in small ledges that meet and fuse with ledges from the neighboring tower. The fusion is not growth but the final stage of their stillness: once two clusters touch they lock into a single lattice across the seam, and the drone becomes briefly louder at that point before settling back into the uniform roar. No new individuals are formed; the pattern simply extends itself until the available surface is occupied.

Nothing consumes them and nothing disturbs them. Condensing water arrives already saturated with the same ions they are made of; it adds a molecule or two to an outer face and is immediately incorporated into the standing wave. The towers beneath them lengthen by a few atom-widths over centuries, yet the accretions remain at exactly the same density, their unfinished edges always facing forward into the same arrested future. In this place life is not an event or a trajectory. It is the mineral fact of a vibration that cannot leave its lattice and therefore cannot end.
