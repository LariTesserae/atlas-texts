In the suspended lattices, the inhabitants are not visitors but extensions of the same slow metabolism. They appear first as thickenings along the filaments: translucent capsules no bigger than a fingertip, each one a sealed volume of super-cooled mineral gel. Inside every capsule a single, elongated nucleus of living metal contracts and relaxes in rhythms measured in hours. When the nucleus contracts, it draws a hair-thin current of condensed water along the filament surface; when it relaxes, the water is released again as vapor that the next capsule can harvest. The work is imperceptible except as a faint shimmer that travels the lattice like a slow pulse.

Larger forms exist, though they are rarer and less fixed. From the deeper crevice mouths emerge thread-like assemblies, jointed in dozens of microscopic segments. Each segment carries a different charge; together they function as a temporary nervous system that can detach, migrate across several lattices, and reattach elsewhere. When two or more of these threads meet at an intersection they pause, exchange minute crystalline beads of information, then continue or merge. The merged thread may persist for days before splitting again, leaving behind a new capsule or a repaired section of lattice. Their movements are deliberate enough that an observer fixed in place for an entire day might record only one or two such encounters.

Occasionally, at the very tips of the tallest filaments, a different architecture gathers: clusters of minute, radially symmetric disks that rotate by fractions of a degree over weeks. These disks align themselves with the faint temperature gradients inside the fissure, acting as passive sensors. When a disk senses a dangerous drop in temperature or an excess of moisture, the filament beneath it lengthens by a few molecular layers, tilting the cluster toward a warmer or drier current. The adjustment is so slight that the chamber’s overall appearance never changes from one human lifetime to the next.

All of these entities share the same interior condition: they contain almost no water and almost no carbon. Their continuity is maintained by the orchestrated growth and dissolution of the engineered mineral itself. When a lattice segment reaches the limit of its tensile strength, it dissolves from the inside outward, its ions re-deposited elsewhere as reinforcement. The few arthropod-shaped wanderers are not separate creatures but mobile concentrations of this same process, their bodies assembled and disassembled as needed. In the stillness of the fissure they carry nothing that could be called memory or intention in older senses; they carry only the distributed record, written in the precise spacing of their mineral lattices, of how to remain suspended, cold, and intact while the mountain continues to rise.
