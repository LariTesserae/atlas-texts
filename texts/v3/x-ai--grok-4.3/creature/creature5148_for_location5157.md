The small tufts are not truly separate individuals. Each is a single, long-lived organism whose woody body has spent centuries—or perhaps longer—expanding by a few millimetres a year, pushing thread-like roots no thicker than spider-silk into the shallow fissures where night moisture condenses. Beneath the surface those roots meet and fuse, so that what appears to be a scattering of isolated plants is in fact one continuous, extremely slow network whose different crowns have simply reached the air at different points. They share water, share salts, and when one crown is damaged the others continue feeding it until new tissue can be grown outward again. In this way the basin holds not dozens of plants but a single, pale intelligence stretched thin across the polygons, patient enough to wait decades between one useful rain and the next.

Within the crust itself, far smaller residents move even more slowly. Entire generations of salt-tolerant bacteria live and die inside the microscopic films of water that cling to each crystal face. Their metabolic cycles are measured in months rather than hours; when the temperature rises they simply cease most activity and wait for the night cooling that will allow another thin layer of brine to form. Occasionally, during especially long dry spells, some of them excrete tiny quantities of new mineral that add, grain by grain, to the crust’s slow smoothing. Their presence is felt only as a faint, almost sweet aftertaste in the mineral tang of the air.

Nothing larger than these two residents has ever established itself. The encircling rises, low though they are, are sufficient to exclude anything that requires regular wind, regular rain, or the company of its own kind. The basin therefore contains only the vast, nearly motionless plant-web and its even slower microbial partners, both of them perfectly adapted to a place whose sole remaining resource is time.
