In the Verdant Expanse the living things are not visitors to the voids but extensions of their emptiness, shaped by the same slow negotiations of tension and mist that hold the lattice aloft. The builders’ descendants move least of all. They are tall, spare figures whose skin has taken on the same fibrous matte as the lianas they tend—pale green threaded with brown, almost indistinguishable from the terraces when they stand still. Their limbs are long and jointed in extra places, allowing them to hang from a single braided strand for days while they coax new fibers across an empty span. They carry the old stone adzes not as weapons but as calibration tools; each notch they cut is felt, rather than heard, as a minute shift in the lattice’s internal pressure. Over generations these notches accumulate into a silent record that the plants themselves read and absorb.

They rarely gather in numbers. When two descendants meet at a crossing, they communicate by leaning their weight in precise patterns against the same strand, sending ripples that travel for kilometers and return as faint tremors underfoot. Their speech, if it can be called speech, is a choreography of pauses: one will remain motionless until the other’s posture has finished its question. Between these exchanges entire districts may exhale, lifting both figures several body-lengths on a warm updraft before setting them down again.

Among the open air drift the armored moths, though “armored” is only how their membranes appear when they fold. Unfolded, each wing is a single translucent sheet finer than any woven fabric, veined with channels that carry the same condensate the lianas weep. The moths do not flap so much as steer, catching the micro-currents that rise between levels. When enough of them gather, their collective wing-beats produce a low, continuous pressure that the descendants can walk upon for a few steps, like a temporary terrace of living air.

Larger than the moths and quieter than thought are the long-limbed gliders. Their bodies are little more than articulated spines and sail-like membranes stretched between six or seven joints. They do not hunt; they filter. As they coast, fine root-hairs along the leading edges of their wings drink the mist and convert it, slowly, into the oils that keep their membranes flexible. A colony will sometimes align itself across a void for an entire day, forming a living diaphragm that modulates the flow of warmer air upward. The descendants read these alignments as seasonal calendars.

Heaviest and most deliberate are the leaf-scute herbivores. Their bodies are low domes of overlapping plates, each plate a living leaf stiffened with silica. They never alight on the lattice. Instead they extend long, prehensile tongues—hollow and muscular—into the vascular bundles of the thickest strands, drinking without piercing. When a herd passes, the only evidence is a soft, rhythmic tightening and release felt through the soles of any descendant standing nearby, like a distant heartbeat shared across kilometers of wood.

Occasionally, at the places where two exhale-rivers collide and the air thins into momentary rainbow, a fourth kind of presence appears. These are not animals in the usual sense but transient condensations of the lattice’s own memory: brief, half-material shapes that walk the same suspended causeways the descendants use, wearing the outlines of earlier builders. They last only until the next droplet falls, at which point they dissolve back into chlorophyll-gold haze. The descendants treat these visitations with the same patient attention they give to new splices—neither welcoming nor preventing them, only noting the slight change in grain that remains after they are gone.

Nothing here reproduces quickly. Reproduction itself is an act of maintenance. When a descendant dies, the body is left suspended from a chosen strand; over decades the lianas grow through it, incorporating bone and fiber into a new splice whose tension exactly replaces what the living person once contributed. The gliders and moths perform the same function in miniature, their casings and wings becoming the next season’s seed vessels. The voids remain empty not because nothing lives in them, but because everything that lives here has learned to occupy emptiness as a form of architecture.
