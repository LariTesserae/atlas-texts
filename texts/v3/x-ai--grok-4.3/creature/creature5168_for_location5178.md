The basin is home to a single, slow metabolism rather than separate populations. What moves through it most steadily is the continuous exchange between plant tissue and the residue of former industry: vines thicken around axles until torque becomes growth, and the gears, in turn, shear just enough living wood to keep their teeth free. The people who remain are not distinct from this process. They are low, broad-shouldered figures whose skin has taken on the same warm, slightly tacky texture as the older leaves; they sleep in hammocks woven directly into the structural vines and wake only when the drip rhythm changes or when a certain low harmonic fades from the thrum of the belt-houses. Their work is almost indistinguishable from the vegetation’s own: they stroke condensation from the undersides of root bridges with the same patient sweep a vine uses to re-anchor itself after a shift in the peat.

Among them move the thinner, quicker presences that still answer to the old names of lizard and snail. The lizards are pale as peeled root and no longer hunt; they press themselves into the narrow gaps between brick and vine where temperature gradients are steepest, and they sip the condensate that runs down the casings of the engines. The snails are larger than memory suggests, their shells grown translucent and veined with the same green lattice that holds the walls. At night their trails glow faintly with the same bioluminescent film that coats every wet surface the engines touch.

Nothing else is numerous. Small, jointed things the size of thumbs drift in the slow channels, more plant than animal, their bodies simple rafts of buoyant stem and fine cilia that steer them toward the warmest upwellings. Birds are absent; the canopy admits too little sky. In their place a single species of pale, long-legged flyer—more moth than bird—emerges at the dimmest hours, its wings brushing the high leaves so lightly that only the change in drip pattern reveals its passage.

All of these lives keep the same measured interval. When one of the old belt-driven pumps labors, the people and the thicker vines lean toward it together, adding their slight weight to the housing until the mechanism eases. When the channels shift a few centimeters over years, the settlement reshapes with them: platforms are left behind, new ones grown from the same vines that once framed the engines. The basin therefore contains no true visitors, only continuing participants in a single, warm, patient exchange that has already outlasted the original purpose of the machinery it still turns.
