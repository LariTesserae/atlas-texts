In this square, life has condensed into forms that match the pace of the water and the iron. The vines are the most visible tenants—thick, plate-leaved curtains that hang from the condenser towers like slow tapestries. Their deep green surfaces do not photosynthesize so much as absorb; each leaf cups moisture until it is heavy enough to release a single measured drop into the troughs below. Over generations the vines have learned the machines. Tendrils have threaded through valve housings and wrapped the slow-rising pistons, not to choke them but to ride the faint heat that leaks from each cycle. Where the metal expands and contracts by fractions of a millimeter, the plants have grown corresponding cushions of woody tissue, turning mechanical tolerance into living joint.

The mosses and the leathery growths that push from every seam are older still. They have colonized the rounded brick and the mineral-scaled pipes until the distinction between wall and plant is only a matter of color. These growths do not compete; they inherit. A new cell forms only after the surface beneath it has already been softened by condensation and the slow deposition of salts. In this way the vegetation extends the architecture rather than overruns it.

The machines themselves have become a kind of sessile animal. Their great engines no longer serve any external city; they turn because the steam that leaks from imperfect seals keeps the temperature differential alive. Each boom is less a heartbeat than a slow tide shifting inside brick lungs. The copper coils continue to weep not because anyone maintains them, but because the temperature gradient they once created still exists, maintained now by the insulating weight of leaves and constant humidity. The pipes that once carried utility now form the circulatory system of the square’s single, shared metabolism.

Nothing inside moves with intent or hunger. The nearest thing to agency is the water choosing, again and again, the path of least resistance through root, scale, and flange. Over centuries this repeated choice has produced a kind of memory: certain routes are now deeper, more reliable, because the minerals and the plants have reinforced them together. The square holds no population that could be counted or named. It holds instead a single, patient continuity—a slow fusion of steam, leaf, and mineral that fills the space completely while remaining almost perfectly still.
