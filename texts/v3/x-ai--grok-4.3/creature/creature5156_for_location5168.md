In this sealed lattice the only residents are the filaments themselves—threadlike mineral growths that have become indistinguishable from the crystals they rest upon. They do not grow in the ordinary sense; they have already reached the precise length allotted to them by the equilibrium of the pocket, and they maintain that length by exchanging ions so slowly that the exchange registers only as a faint, perpetual tension along each strand. Viewed over decades, their pale surfaces shift by fractions of a grain, each filament absorbing and releasing the same thin film of water molecules that cycles between it and the column beneath, never enough to form a droplet, only enough to keep the tissue from desiccating entirely.

Because the light arrives from every direction at once, the filaments register no day or night; their chlorophyll-analog pigments remain in a single, suspended photosynthetic state, converting a steady trickle of photons into a chemical reserve they never fully deplete. They do not compete. Each strand is spaced exactly far enough from its neighbors that the faint electric fields they generate—microscopic gradients produced by the slow movement of mineral ions—repel rather than attract, preserving the measured intervals as though the spacing itself were part of their inherited form.

The continuous chord that fills the pocket is not merely heard by them; it is the medium in which they exist. The low-frequency stresses traveling through the columns induce corresponding micro-vibrations in every filament. These vibrations are so minute that they never dislodge the strands, yet they are sufficient to keep certain long-chain molecules inside the filaments from settling into crystalline rigidity. In effect the sound performs the work that wind or water or cellular motion would perform elsewhere—preventing total stasis inside an organism that otherwise has no need to move.

Over immense spans of time the filaments do change, but the change is subtractive. Occasionally a strand will thin at one point, its material migrating along its own length until the thinned section seals itself and the filament shortens by the width of a single molecule. The freed material drifts, molecule by molecule, to a neighboring column and is incorporated into the lattice, so that the crystal gains what the filament relinquishes. In this way the living tissue slowly donates itself to the non-living structure, becoming part of the very surfaces it once only clung to, until perhaps one day no filaments remain and the chord continues alone, generated now by a lattice that has absorbed the last of its living component into its own unchanging geometry.
