In the seams where the basalt still cools, colonies of thread-thin organisms drift with the weeping water. They are neither plant nor mineral but slow accretions of crystalline filaments that bind droplets into temporary bodies, each one a shifting lens that focuses the diffuse light long enough to draw a faint chemical spark. When the next fracture opens they unravel, seeding the next pool, so that life here is less a matter of survival than of continuous redistribution.

Higher, the silver-green mats thicken into low domes that trap pockets of warmer vapor beneath their felted surfaces. Inside each dome a single, spindle-shaped creature moves through the lichen like a living needle, turning the mat from within so that fresh growth faces the constant moisture while older tissue is pressed against the rock to draw residual heat. These inner tenders are nearly invisible; only the slight, rhythmic lifting of the mat betrays their presence, and even that motion stops when the larger, broad-footed walkers pass.

Those walkers are not single animals but loose republics of jointed plates that can separate and rejoin. Each plate carries its own fringe of sensory cilia that taste dissolved minerals in the fault-line pools. When a new extrusion of basalt appears they migrate toward it in sluggish procession, their overlapping feet leaving behind temporary gardens of compressed lichen that will later be reclaimed by the gliders as launch pads. The walkers never hurry; their pace matches the continent’s own, and when two republics meet they simply trade a few plates before moving on, the exchange marked only by a brief brightening of the cilia.

From the ledges of the towers the furred gliders launch in pairs or alone. Their membranes are translucent when stretched, veined with the same glassy filaments that still extrude from the mantle below; moisture condenses along those veins until the glider becomes a small, drifting lens itself. They do not hunt or court in any ordinary sense. Instead they ride the updrafts for spans that can last weeks, occasionally descending to brush the surface of a walker-republic or to sip from a dome’s trapped vapor. Their silence is not stealth but economy: sound, like weight, is something this unfinished place can still shed.

Embedded within the same towers are quieter presences. Where the lattices reconfigure overnight, slender, almost liquid forms flow along the new alignments. They leave no prints. They are drawn to stresses in the rock the way the gliders are drawn to updrafts, and they adjust tension only by settling their own weight into the lattice joints. When a fracture threatens, they gather there until the crystalline report sounds and the filaments lock; then they disperse again, thinner than before, carrying the memory of that lock into the next adjustment. To an observer they would appear as moving shadows in the constant mist, yet the city’s machines register them as part of the same data stream that governs the towers themselves.

At every scale, nothing is finished. The spindles inside the mats will one day become the next generation of plates on a walker’s back; a glider membrane may eventually seed a new dome elsewhere; the lattice-flowing presences may thin until they are indistinguishable from the water that endlessly condenses and falls. The continent continues its slow extrusion, and the life upon and within it continues to arrive at shapes that remain provisional, held together only by the same low-pressure sigh that never quite becomes wind.
