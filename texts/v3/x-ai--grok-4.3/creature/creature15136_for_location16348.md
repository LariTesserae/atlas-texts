In the intervals between the veils, when the steam thins to a single transparent film, the first signs of occupation become visible as faint pressure marks left on the still-tacky panels. They are not footprints exactly, but shallow radial impressions, each the diameter of a palm, arranged in slow, deliberate arcs that suggest a body turning in place over many minutes rather than walking. Where these marks overlap the gutters, the condensate gathers more thickly, as though something has pressed the moisture into temporary basins before lifting away.

These traces belong to entities that form and un-form in sympathy with the towers themselves. Each is a loose aggregation of fired ceramic shards, still warm at the core, held in rough suspension by surface tension and the constant film of condensation. From a distance they register as low, irregular cairns no taller than a kneeling figure; closer, the arrangement reveals itself as a temporary body: larger plates serving as a kind of torso or carapace, narrower strips flexing along what might be limbs, all of it clicking softly into new alignments with every shift. The seams between shards are sealed not by staples but by the same iron-red glaze that weeps from the towers during their slow extrusions; when a limb reconfigures, excess glaze runs downward in thin, smoking threads that harden into new staples mid-fall.

They move, when they move at all, by softening the ceramic beneath them and letting the ground decide the next placement. A limb will sink a few finger-widths, the panel beneath it loses cohesion, and the shard sinks with it; moments later the same shard rises elsewhere as fresh material wells up from the depression left behind. The effect is less like walking than like the ground itself offering successive resting points, each one briefly accepted before being refused again. Because the surface is never entirely solid, their passage produces no lasting trail; every mark is overwritten within minutes by the next exhalation of steam or the next settling of a tower.

Heat and moisture pass directly through them. When a pocket of trapped warmth rises beneath a cluster of shards, the glaze softens and the entire assembly slumps into a flatter, more diffuse shape, its edges blurring into the surrounding haze. When the steam sheets draw together and condense heavily, droplets bead along every exposed edge and are drawn inward; the shards drink the water, cooling fractionally, and the body grows momentarily more rigid, its angles sharpening until the next thermal shift arrives. In this way their outlines remain provisional, always one condensation event or one kiln breath away from a new posture.

At times several of these gatherings drift near one another. When they do, the intervening ceramic panels soften in irregular bands between them, and the slow extrusion of new material occurs simultaneously in two or three places, as though the ground were answering a shared pressure. The resulting towers lean toward one another at complementary angles, and for a while the space between becomes a kind of temporary corridor defined only by the alignment of their leaning walls. Once the steam veils close again, the corridor is lost; the next time it opens, the towers may have sunk or shifted orientation, and the ceramic gatherings are already elsewhere, or have merged at their edges into a single, larger, more slowly rearranging form.

No sound accompanies these encounters beyond the usual high ringing of cooling glaze. The entities themselves emit nothing; their only trace is the faint, continuous ticking of shards seating themselves against one another and the occasional soft report when a newly formed staple takes the weight of a reconfiguring limb. They leave no residue of purpose or hunger, only the evidence that something continues to test the unfinished surface, accepting each new panel long enough to register its temperature and moisture before moving on to the next.
