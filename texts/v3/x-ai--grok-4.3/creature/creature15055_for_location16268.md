The highland is one organism wearing the shape of a forest.  

Its body is the mesh of trunks and vines and root-mats, all pressing shoulder-to-shoulder so that no single stem can be said to stand alone. The low-crowned trees are its broad, slow-moving leaves; each one lives for centuries and moves only by the width of a finger each year, shouldering its neighbors into a perpetual, gentle compression. Between them hang the cable-thick vines, which are not parasites but the forest’s own circulatory threads, drawing and returning the mineral broth that leaks from the weeping rock. Where two vine systems touch they exchange warmth and dissolved salts in a handshake no larger than a palm, just enough to keep the perpetual mist from icing over.

Mosses and liverworts form the living skin. They are not scattered cushions but a continuous film that coats every surface the light can reach, shifting in slow tides of blue-green and slate as one patch slowly overgrows another. The same film lines the interior of every hollow trunk and coats the undersides of the great leaf-mats; inside these cavities the air never moves and the chemistry stays constant for decades. Fallen needles and leaves do not rot so much as cure, pressed into leathery, faintly metallic sheets that the forest re-absorbs through thread-fine rootlets. The whole place smells of cold stone and iron filings because the aerosols the leaves release are not perfumes but the exhaust of that same slow digestion.

Animals move through the organism the way mites move through moss: small enough that their passage registers only as a temporary rearrangement. The fist-sized, many-legged browsers follow the vine corridors like traffic along established roads; they pare away single cells from the living skin without ever breaking its weave. The larger herbivores are low, broad-flanked and almost sessile; they slide their mouths between leaves the way a tongue slides between teeth, taking only what the canopy can spare. Their droppings are dry wafers that the moss reclaims within days.

Predators are absences rather than presences. A stretch of vine that was occupied moments earlier is now empty; something has withdrawn upward or sideways into a deeper fold of canopy, carrying one of the slow herbivores with it. The temperature keeps all metabolisms deliberate, so even violence is muffled and efficient: a brief compression of stems, then stillness again. The forest registers the loss as a minute redistribution of weight and moisture, nothing more.

Light filters through mist and leaf in a perpetual half-presence that never brightens or dims enough to mark hours. In that steady dim the forest breathes on a scale measured in centuries: water rises along one set of capillaries and descends along another; faint warmth pools where root-masses meet; aerosols rise and glint and settle again. The plateau itself is the slow heave of this single body across the rock, the hills no longer stone but the rounded contours of an enormous, patient lung.
