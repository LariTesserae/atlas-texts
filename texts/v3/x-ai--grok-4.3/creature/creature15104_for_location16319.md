The basin holds a population shaped entirely by its own equilibrium. Most visible are the many small invertebrates that occupy every surface and every interior channel. They move constantly yet never disrupt the steady drone. Pale moths with wings thin as the membrane sheets themselves drift between the high supports, their bodies catching the filtered light so that they appear only as momentary glints. Beneath them, dark beetles the size of thumbnails patrol the root-lips and the shallow water pockets, their legs creating fine concentric ripples that die out before reaching the next droplet fall. Smaller still, thrips and mites swarm in the grooves of the older wood, feeding on the slow seep of sap that leaks where the vascular walls flex. Their activity adds a finer, higher layer to the overall sound, a continual dry rustling that blends with the deeper mechanical pulse until it becomes indistinguishable from it.

Larger forms exist but remain harder to locate. Slender, translucent salamanders the color of wet root bark live inside the hollow trunks, entering and leaving through cracks no wider than a finger. They press themselves flat against the inner walls to drink the condensation that runs downward, then withdraw again into the darkness. Occasionally one will cross the open air between two trunks, moving with a slow, undulating crawl that seems almost plant-like until the tail flicks. On the membrane floors, clusters of pale, soft-bodied millipedes gather at the folds where water collects longest. They do not feed on the plant matter itself so much as on the thin film of algae and bacteria that grows there, their many legs producing a soft, continuous scraping that rises and falls with the temperature of each breath that condenses above them.

Even the air itself supports life. Midges form loose, stationary clouds in the voids, each swarm holding its position for hours while individual flies rise and sink within it. The cloud as a whole never disperses because new insects are always hatching from the egg masses laid along the damp root ridges. Their wingbeats contribute the highest, most insistent note in the constant roar. Nothing preys on them in quantity; any sudden increase in their numbers is met by a corresponding rise in the number of web-spinning spiders that stretch irregular nets between distant supports, the silk itself faintly luminous and beaded with the same condensation that runs everywhere else.

All of these creatures share the same constraint: nothing here grows large enough or moves quickly enough to alter the architecture. The colossal trunks and membranes were already mature before any of the present generation hatched or pupated. The animals therefore treat the living structure as permanent weather, navigating its pulses, its dripping intervals, and its cool, unchanging drafts as fixed features of the space rather than as resources that might be depleted. Their combined presence fills the absences without ever crowding them. The basin remains mostly empty air and steady sound, occupied by life that has learned to occupy very little.
