In the throat of every surge, the scavengers multiply. They are not one species but a shifting swarm of glassine forms, each no longer than a finger joint, their carapaces thinned by generations until they are little more than moving films of membrane stretched over invisible joints. When a fresh mineral vein splits, they pour out in ropes of motion, their bodies catching the bronze light so that for an instant the plaza is threaded with thousands of shifting filaments. They do not feed so much as dissolve: mouthparts no thicker than cilia rasp at the fresh precipitate, drawing dissolved metals and gases straight through permeable skin. What they cannot absorb they leave as faint, glittering trails that re-seed the next accretion layer.

Larger shadows sometimes cross the same currents. They are rarely seen whole—only the slow drift of segmented limbs the color of aged sulfur, or the sudden flare of a pressure-ridge spine unfolding across a gantry. These forms never fully detach from the structures; they seem to grow from the petrified conduits themselves, plates of precipitated salt and iron oxide layered over older metal until the distinction between building and occupant blurs. When the pressure inverts they contract, their surfaces rippling inward to match the surge, then expand again once the water stills, extruding new crystalline filaments into the flow like roots seeking fresh solvent.

Between these scales drift the smallest residents, motes of living precipitate that exist only in the brief windows when dissolved gases come out of solution. They appear as momentary clouds of ochre sparks, each spark a self-contained reaction that lasts seconds before collapsing back into the general mineral rain. The shrimps pass through these clouds and emerge brighter, their membranes temporarily etched with branching mineral patterns that fade within minutes.

Nothing here reproduces in the old sense. Instead, the enclave maintains a continuous exchange: surge loosens material, scavengers concentrate it, larger forms anchor and reshape it, and the smallest motes return it to solution. The city’s original purpose—heat, pressure, flow—has become the metabolism of its present tenants. Every reversal of current is a shared pulse; every new seam cracked open is a communal wound that all of them, from filament to lattice, close together.
