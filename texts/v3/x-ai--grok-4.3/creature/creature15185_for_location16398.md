The plateau belongs first to its smallest tenants. In the perpetual film of moisture that coats every surface, mats of threadlike fungi and cyanobacteria live in layered consortia, trading metabolites in rhythms timed to the daily cycle of evaporation and condensation. They are the true foundation: without them the larger structures would starve. From these films rise the pollinators—minute, soft-bodied things whose wings are little more than vibrating membranes fringed with cilia. They drift in clouds dense enough to tint the air, alighting on the glistening exudates that bead along the woody lattices. When disturbed they lift in synchronized pulses, each pulse producing a note too high for most ears yet felt as a pressure against the skin.

Slightly larger, but still numerous enough to seem like weather, are the iridescent fliers. Their bodies are flattened and translucent, the color of oil on water, and they rise and fall in tight vertical columns above the living walls. They sip the same exudates, then excrete a fine mist that nourishes the epiphytic growths clinging to the vertical faces. In return the walls release additional sugars, a mutual economy so ancient it has become nearly involuntary.

The herbivores that cross the open intervals are more conspicuous. They travel in loose, shifting columns several hundred strong, each individual the size of a large crab and similarly jointed, though their carapaces are matte and fibrous rather than chitinous. Their legs end in blunt pads that grip the cellulose without tearing it; when they move they produce a continuous, layered percussion that travels through the ground and is answered, after a delay, by similar columns still too distant to see. Between their body segments grow delicate, hair-like gills that comb the constant haze for water and dissolved minerals. They do not graze so much as sip, pressing specialized mouthparts against the living surfaces and drawing up the films that collect there. When a column reaches the next cathedral wall the sound changes: the ticking becomes a wet, sucking chorus that lasts through the night and into the following morning.

Inside the lattices themselves dwell quieter residents. Cavities left by earlier collapses have been lined with spongy growths that function simultaneously as nest, larder, and acoustic buffer. Pale, elongate creatures—partially plant, partially animal—move through these spaces by extending and retracting root-like tendrils. They rarely emerge; when they do it is only at dusk, to drink from the condensation threads and to release tiny winged gametes that drift upward like luminous dust. Their presence is felt mostly as a faint, rhythmic contraction that passes through entire towers at irregular intervals, as though the architecture itself were breathing.

Above everything, at the tops of the tallest formations, live the few truly solitary forms. Enormous, sessile filterers have anchored themselves in the uppermost angles where wind currents are strongest. Their bodies are radial and nearly sessile, great discs of translucent tissue stretched between woody struts. From the center of each disc rise slender, jointed arms that comb the air for the drifting pollinators and fliers. When one of these giants captures prey the entire structure vibrates once, a low note that rolls outward across the plateau and is answered, after many seconds, by another giant some distance away. The exchange is slow, almost ceremonial.

Nothing here arrives or departs quickly. Generations of the crab-like migrants have worn shallow, permanent trails across the open ground; the trails are already being narrowed again by creeping lateral growths that will one day close them entirely. The fliers and pollinators hatch, feed, mate, and die within the span of a single day, their bodies adding to the thin film that sustains the next generation. Even the giants at the summits are not individuals so much as long-lived expressions of the same slow intelligence that built the towers. Over centuries their discarded arms and membranes are absorbed back into the lattice, becoming new struts for whatever will rest there next.

The plateau therefore sustains no simple community but a single, distributed metabolism that has learned, through repetition, how to incorporate movement, sound, and even the memory of earlier hands into its own continued expansion.
