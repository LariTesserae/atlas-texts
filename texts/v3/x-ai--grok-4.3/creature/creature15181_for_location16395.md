The cleft shelters a sparse but ceaseless population, each member shaped by the perpetual damp and the narrow, echoing acoustics. The reeds and sedges stand like widely spaced sentinels, their blades beaded constantly with condensation that slides downward in slow, repeated tracks. Their roots, pale and fibrous, knit loosely across the mineral floor, drawing the upward seep and releasing minute oxygen bubbles that rise in irregular chains, popping at the surface with soft, wet ticks.

Between the stems the invertebrates move as a restless film. Tiny water-striders and springtails patrol the meniscus layers, their legs dimpling the water without breaking it, while translucent midge larvae and copepods pulse in coordinated clusters. The thrumming they produce is not random; it rises in measured surges that match the larger gas vents below, creating overlapping waves of sound that the rock walls multiply and return. Some of the smallest beetles, dark and glossy, cling to the undersides of root mats where the air is thickest, their movements sending minute ripples that travel outward until they meet the next drip-fall.

The thumb-sized frogs, nearly colorless except for faint gold flecks along their sides, occupy the lower stems and root hollows. They remain motionless for long intervals, then flick forward in sudden, precise arcs to seize larvae or surface insects, the sound of their landings absorbed into the general mechanical rhythm rather than interrupting it. Their eggs, laid in loose gelatinous strings among the sedge bases, drift in the shallowest films and hatch in synchrony with the strongest gas releases, as though timed to the opening of new feeding pockets.

Nothing here accumulates into crowds or tangles. Each creature maintains a measured distance, its activity slotted into the steady meter of water lifting through mineral cracks, condensing on cold faces, and falling again in threads that never quite become streams. The mist at shoulder height carries the collective breath and exhalations upward, scattering the already weakened light into a cool, even haze. In this narrow fracture the processes of growth, hunting, and reproduction run without pause or hierarchy—simple bodies moving through the same pulse that lifts and releases the water, as if the rock itself were the workshop and they its attending, tireless mechanisms.
