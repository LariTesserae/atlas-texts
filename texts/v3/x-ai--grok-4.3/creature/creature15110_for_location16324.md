The basin’s occupants do not arrive or depart; they simply thicken in place, each generation already half-rooted before it finishes growing.  

Along the lowest seams of fresh clay, the translucent amphibians rest in such numbers that the tea-colored pools appear to breathe. Their skins are little more than water held in tension, stretched over ridged skeletons that flex with every slow capillary pulse. When the constant drip from above lands on them, they flatten rather than flinch, letting the new moisture slide through microscopic pores straight into the dark substrate beneath them. In this way they become temporary extensions of the ground itself, softening its surface so the next thin layer of mineral can settle without cracking.

Higher, where the pale clay blisters have only just stopped steaming, clusters of jointed arms emerge in patient spirals. Each arm is the same milky color as the cooling extrusion it clutches, and each ends in a fringe of root-hairs identical to those on the undersides of the rubbery fronds. When the arms retract, they do not pull back into hiding; they simply shorten, folding their segments into the new rock so that the distinction between limb and substrate vanishes within minutes. What remains visible is only the next ring of arms already reaching outward, testing the air for the next drop of condensate.

Between these layers the many-legged forms travel in slow, overlapping trails. Their segments glow the steady orange of cooling glass only when they move; when they halt they darken to the exact shade of the vines they cross, becoming another texture in the canopy. They feed by pressing their undersides against the constant film of water on every surface, drawing it inward through thousands of hair-thin channels until their bodies swell and the excess is exuded again as fine mist that feeds the insects hovering below them.

The insects themselves never settle. The smallest drift in vertical columns that match the rising vapor; the larger ones drift horizontally through the same columns, their wing edges frayed into combs that harvest both water and the microscopic bodies of the smaller cloud. None of them mate in the usual sense; instead they exchange fragments of wing or leg when they touch, so the next generation carries traits gathered from hundreds of brief collisions rather than from any single pair.

At the very edges of vision, where the greenish dusk thickens into solid shadow, segmented bodies larger than any of the others coil through the densest vines. Their orange glow is intermittent and internal, pulsing only when they press against the living canopy; in those moments the vines themselves brighten for a few heartbeats, as though the light is being lent rather than produced. These larger forms never fully emerge. They remain half-sheathed in foliage, their movements indistinguishable from the slow sway of the fronds, so that the basin appears to rearrange itself only when one stops attending to any single layer and lets the whole mass shift at once.

Every creature here maintains the same density at every hour because nothing is ever added or removed; each population is simply the current expression of a single, continuous metabolism that includes the cooling clays, the condensing vapor, and the unhardened stone. When a new glassy blister rises, the nearest arms are already gripping it before the surface dulls; when a pool evaporates, the amphibians settled there have already flattened into the next depression. The life of the basin does not inhabit the place so much as complete its unfinished sentences, turning each fresh extrusion of rock into another damp, many-limbed clause in an arrangement that has no first or final draft.
