The plateau belongs first to the growth itself. The colossal mats are not passive cover; they are the resident power, a single slow intelligence of fiber and vein that has learned to roof the world. Each vaulted canopy drinks the night-mist directly through its upper skin and pipes the surplus downward in measured tricklings that fill the cupped leaves below like cisterns. When a storm passes, the living ceilings flex, shedding excess water in deliberate sheets rather than letting it batter the stems beneath. Where older sections finally collapse under their own weight, the fallen mass is not wasted; new shoots tunnel through it within days, converting the slump into fresh scaffolding. The plants therefore maintain the only permanent architecture here—walls that breathe, floors that steer water, ceilings that shift with the light.

Through and upon this architecture move the insects, heavy and lacquered, each the size of a doubled fist. They travel established runways along the thickest stems, their wings producing a ceaseless layered drone that rises and falls with temperature. At dusk the largest swarms lift together for a brief, thunderous circuit before settling again; the vibration loosens capsules of pollen and spores that the updrafts then carry across the clearings. The insects do not sting or bite in any conventional sense; instead they press their abdomens against the leaf veins to sip surplus sap, and in return their exhalations seed tiny, fast-growing nodules that help seal wounds in the canopy.

Larger grazers follow the same stem roads but at a slower cadence. These segmented herbivores move in loose, rope-like processions, each body sprouting a living pelt of symbiotic moss that photosynthesizes while the animal feeds. Their mouthparts are blunt scoops that lift strips of the rubbery outer bark without piercing the deeper vascular channels, so the road remains intact behind them. When they drink from the leaf-pools they do so with long, slow siphons that minimize disturbance; the resulting ripples carry chemical signals that travel through the plant tissue itself, alerting distant sections of canopy to close their stomata against the day’s coming heat.

Predators announce themselves only by absence. The usual layered drone of wings and the wet percussion of grazing fall abruptly silent across a stretch of stems; the subsonic thrum that replaces it travels through the living floor like a held note in bone. What descends is seldom seen for more than a blurred moment: a flattened, many-limbed shape that unfolds from a crevice in the architecture, seizes a grazer at a joint, and folds away again. The plants respond to these events by tightening the nearest vascular channels, starving the damaged section so that decay cannot spread.

Scattered through the same lattice are the older traces—low, single-story frames of fitted stone and woven fiber whose roofs have long since been replaced by living canopy. The people who raised them appear to have lived in the same dispersed rhythm as the present fauna: small groups moving between clearings, never numerous enough to leave continuous settlement. Their obsidian tools still lie where vines lifted them; the blades are worn to crescent shapes from long use scraping bark, not from cutting wood or flesh. Clay vessels remain half-buried in pockets of loam, their interiors stained with residues that suggest storage of condensed mist rather than cooked food. The growth has absorbed these remnants without erasing them; stems grow through the stone gaps, turning each abandoned frame into a trellis rather than a ruin.

Nothing here remains motionless. Leaves tilt toward the filtered bronze light throughout the day; water pulses along capillary grooves in time with the cooling and warming of the canopy; entire insect clouds rise and settle in visible waves whose rhythm matches the daily appearance and disappearance of mist. The only rule observed by every living thing—plant, insect, grazer, predator, and the vanished makers of stone—is that whatever cannot bend or be absorbed is eventually overgrown.
