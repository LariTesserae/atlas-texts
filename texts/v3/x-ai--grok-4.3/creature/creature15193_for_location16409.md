What lives here does not arrive or depart. It condenses, like dew, from the same continuous tissue that lifts the plateau above the clouds. In the hanging bladders and the vascular walls beneath them, certain regions of the organism have learned to detach without dying. They become small, translucent bodies—neither quite seed nor quite cell—whose interiors remain in constant conversation with the larger body through threads of living sap. These bodies drift or crawl according to gradients of pressure and sweetness. When one of them brushes against a membrane floor, the floor softens, admits it, and reseals, carrying the small shape deeper into galleries that only exist for the minutes it needs them. There it feeds on concentrated sugars and, in return, releases minute adjustments of hormone and waste that nudge tomorrow’s growth a fraction of a degree toward or away from light.

Larger patterns move above these travelers. Along the braided vine scaffolds, a slow peristaltic ripple sometimes runs in both directions at once; where the opposing waves meet, a temporary knot forms, tightens, and extrudes a cluster of new root-callus shaped like a many-limbed star. The star does not detach. It remains anchored, pulsing, until its own edges begin to wither and pull the surrounding tissue into fresh alignments. From a distance these knots look like punctuation marks left by an absent writer; up close they are entire miniature cities whose “buildings” are the same organism momentarily specialized into walls, ramps, and light-absorbing surfaces. When their work is finished they collapse back into undifferentiated flesh, their materials already on their way to serve elsewhere.

Inside the deepest fermentation chambers, still stranger residents persist. Long, thread-like entities—more fluid than solid—slide through superheated sap like living distillation columns. Their bodies are sequences of semi-permeable sacs; each sac condenses a different volatile compound and passes it onward or stores it until a pressure wave from above signals release. These thread-forms never leave their chambers, yet their metabolic choices propagate upward through the entire plateau, deciding which new tower will stiffen first or which basin membrane will be allowed to thin until it sings in the wind.

Nothing here reproduces in the manner animals once did. Instead, patterns replicate. A certain spiral of growth that proves useful in one ridge is remembered by the vascular flows and offered again, days later, in another ridge half a day’s walk away. The small drifting bodies carry fragments of these successful patterns like migrating libraries; when two such bodies meet inside the same gallery they may merge for a time, trading patterns before separating again. The plateau therefore contains countless partial copies of itself, each copy incomplete, each incompleteness an experiment that may be kept, altered, or discarded.

Over centuries, these experiments have produced regions that behave almost like separate intentions. One basin now consistently grows flatter and quieter than its neighbors, as though it has chosen to become a reservoir of calm rather than a site of constant rebuilding. Another ridge repeats the same three towers every few weeks, varying only their angles, as though rehearsing a single unresolved question. These habits are not commanded from any center; they arise wherever enough small bodies and thread-forms happen to reinforce one another. The plateau remains unfinished because its residents have not yet finished deciding what they, collectively, prefer to become.
