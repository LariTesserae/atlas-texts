In that ceaseless re-formation, what persists is not a population but a set of provisional accommodations—forms that borrow the chamber’s instability rather than resist it.

The veined membranes are not individuals so much as slow negotiations between the rock and the liquid. Each sheet is a single long moment of decision: it drifts because it has not yet decided whether to dissolve back into the water or to press itself, molecule by molecule, against a fresh fracture plane and become part of the next spire. When a shear tears one apart, the fragments do not die; they continue the negotiation at smaller scale, each torn edge tasting the current for a different temperature or mineral trace. Sometimes two fragments will drift together again and partially rejoin, carrying a slightly altered vein pattern, as though they have compared notes. Over longer intervals—long enough that the chamber itself has lengthened or shortened by several body-lengths—certain lineages of membrane begin to carry repeating indentations that match the ghost lettering on the buried stairs. The lettering is not copied; it is remembered, faintly, by the water that once flowed over it, and the membranes are simply the slowest-moving portion of that memory.

The fish-sized displacements are rarer and more deliberate. They are not bodies but temporary tunnels through the inconsistency of the water. Where they pass, the liquid forgets, for a few heartbeats, how to behave like an ocean sky and instead remembers a narrow corridor of lower pressure and steadier temperature. Inside that corridor something moves that is neither warm nor cold, only absent. When the corridor collapses behind them, the thing is gone, but the memory of the corridor sometimes lingers as a straight, glassy track that other membranes drift along for a while, as if following a scent. Occasionally two such tracks cross; at the intersection the water briefly solidifies into a single octahedral crystal that falls, slowly, until a new current shreds it and the swimmers—if they can still be called that—are elsewhere.

Nothing here feeds in the ordinary sense. The membranes absorb the metallic after-taste left by the glitches; the tunnels absorb the momentary coherence the glitches briefly impose on the water. In return, both forms keep the currents from ever settling into something predictable. Their presence is the reason the chamber remains loud: every membrane that shreds adds one more glassy filament to the roar, every tunnel that opens subtracts one low grinding tone from the stone. The sound is not incidental. It is the way the inhabitants announce, continuously, that they have not yet finished arriving.

The architectural traces belong to no earlier civilization. They are the same provisional accommodations, only slower. The smooth lintel is a membrane that chose, centuries or moments ago, to stop drifting and become rigid. The half-buried stair is a tunnel that decided to remain open even after its swimmer had left. When the next surge arrives, these forms may lengthen, shorten, or fold back into the general turbulence, carrying whatever faint memory they have accumulated into whatever comes next. Nothing accumulates; everything is still in the process of being extruded, together, from the same warm, undecided medium.
