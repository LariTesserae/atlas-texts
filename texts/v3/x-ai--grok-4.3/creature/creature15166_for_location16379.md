The plateau holds its residents close, as if they have become another layer of the same slow erosion. They are not many, and they do not gather in the ways cities once did. Instead they inhabit the staggered tiers in loose, breathing clusters—sometimes alone for years, sometimes near enough to share the faint pulse of a conveyor housing when it wakes.

Most visible are the tenders. They move along the vine-choked frameworks with deliberate slowness, their limbs wrapped in layered fabrics dyed the same blue-green as the dominant creepers. Their hands and faces carry the same patina of condensation and mineral dust that coats the stone itself. They do not prune so much as guide: loosening a root that has grown too tightly around a pipe, redirecting a flowering shoot so its weight helps brace a sagging conveyor housing rather than pull it apart. When they pause, which is often, their stillness blends them into the vegetation until the light shifts again. Some have lived inside the same tier for generations, their family lines marked only by subtle changes in the pattern of growth they encourage.

Deeper in the mist live the listeners. They rarely leave the sheltered concavities where old turbine housings meet the rock. One might find them pressed against metal or stone with an ear or palm, feeling the low thrum travel through their own bones when the machinery decides to turn. Their bodies have adapted to the damp and chill; their skin holds a faint translucency, and their breathing is so quiet it merges with the sound of water finding its way across ribs of buried stone. They keep no calendars. Instead they mark time by which pipes have begun to sing again or which terraces have newly slumped under the weight of wet vegetation. A single listener may wait decades between one meaningful sound and the next, yet they remain alert to every minor shift in pressure or drip.

Then there are the gliders themselves, seen only when the cloud thins. They are pale, almost translucent things whose wing membranes carry the same oxidized-copper sheen as the oldest exposed metal. They do not nest or roost so much as drift between levels, riding the brief updrafts created when rain curtains pass. Occasionally one will alight on a pipe or vine long enough to drink from a stone bowl. Their presence feels less like wildlife and more like a continuation of the plateau’s own slow adjustments—another element that settles, evaporates, and settles again.

The entire population moves according to the same logic as the place: nothing is finished, nothing is hurried, and nothing is ever truly separate from the roots and water that continue to rearrange every contour.
