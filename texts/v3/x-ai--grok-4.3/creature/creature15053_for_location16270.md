The primary life here is the fibrous mat itself—a single, indivisible organism whose many lobes are only the visible surface of a far larger, slower body. It does not so much grow as occupy, pouring itself along fractures and over concrete like cold pitch. Each lobe thickens by accretion at its margins while the centers gradually hollow into spongy chambers that trap air, water, and fine mineral dust; inside those chambers the temperature stays a fraction of a degree above the surrounding stone, enough for slow metabolic work.  

Long, glassy filaments rise from every exposed surface. They are not roots or leaves but sensory and transport structures, turning slowly toward the dim light and toward the steady low-frequency vibration that fills the cavern. Where a filament touches a turbine housing or a stone fin it adheres, drawing both moisture and minute piezoelectric currents generated by the rock under the ceaseless mechanical pressure. The organism therefore lives partly on the machines: the turbines’ hum is metabolized as surely as the condensation is.  

The isopods, few and translucent, are not independent actors but brief extensions of the same metabolism. They graze the mat’s surface, ingest its outer fibers, and excrete mineral salts that the mat immediately reabsorbs a few centimeters away. Their slow circuits stitch the separate lobes together with faint chemical trails, so that over centuries the entire floor becomes one continuous, faintly contractile tissue.  

Nothing else moves. The light is merely endured; the cold is merely slowed. The organism’s only urgency is the patient widening of its territory, one plant-lobe width per handful of decades, while the turbines continue their imperceptible rotation inside the living cradle they helped create.
