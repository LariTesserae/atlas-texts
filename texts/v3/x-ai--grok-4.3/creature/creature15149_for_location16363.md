In the upper terraces, where the mist hangs in slow veils and the light arrives already softened, the smallest lives move first through the seams. Insects no larger than a thumbnail trace the rivet lines with wings that flash like burnished foil, their legs tapping out paths between slabs of moss. These creatures do not merely feed on the growth; they tend it, carrying spores from one gutter to another in the fine dust that clings to their undersides. Ferns respond by tightening their coils at each passage, releasing a faint, resinous scent that lingers longer than the insects themselves.

Slightly larger are the climbing mammals—compact, velvet-furred forms whose claws fit precisely into the weathering grooves left by centuries of condensation. They keep to the shaded undersides of catwalks at midday and emerge when droplets begin to fall. Each one trails a thin filament of living root fiber from its nest, a strand that thickens over seasons into a living cable linking one housing to the next. Their young are born inside the hollows of old flywheel casings, where the rhythmic thrum of turning metal masks their first cries. When they venture farther, they move along the same suspended ramps used by rarer, larger forms, never quite descending to the open platforms.

Birds of many colors occupy the vertical faces. Some nest in the open ventilation housings, their eggs resting on beds of accumulated leaves and iron filings. Others weave through the lattice in constant, low arcs, following the condensation cycles as though choreographed by them. Their plumage carries pollen grains that glint under the diffused light; when they preen, these grains sift downward into the trays and gutters, seeding new growth at every level. A few species have learned the intervals of the great pistons below; they time their loudest calls to coincide with the metallic exhalations, so that the sound merges rather than competes.

Deeper within the structure, where the composite skin thickens into vaulted chambers, live beings whose presence is felt more through absence than direct sight. Maintenance crews pass at intervals measured in weeks rather than hours. Their silhouettes are lean and deliberate, dressed in layered fabrics that have taken on the same waxy sheen as the surrounding leaves. They carry small tools that fit into existing joints, never forcing new openings. One might stop for several minutes beside a particular vine, watching how its broad leaves channel water into a tray, then move on without altering the arrangement. Their footprints on the deck plates fill quickly with fresh moss, leaving no lasting trace.

At the very edges of visibility, where platforms recede into haze, larger shapes sometimes resolve: slow-moving forms whose outlines blend with the struts themselves. These may be extended family groups whose dwellings have grown outward from the original armature over generations, or they may be something else entirely—organisms whose bodies incorporate riveted plates and living root networks in equal measure. They shift position only when the light changes sides of the sky, their movements absorbed into the same patient rhythm as the unfurling leaves and migrating droplets.

Nothing here dominates. The insects, the mammals, the birds, the infrequent crews, and the deeper blurred presences all occupy overlapping volumes at different scales and speeds. The continuous armature supports them all without favoring any single kind, and the lives in turn keep the structure from locking into rigidity. Over time the distinction between what was built and what has taken root grows less meaningful; both soften at the edges, both continue.
