In the seams between the compacted silt and the pale stones, a thin population of scale-insects lives almost entirely within the gray-green cushions. Each insect is no larger than a lentil, its body flattened and ridged so that it matches the leathered surface of the leaves exactly. During the brief windows when the shallow pans still hold water, the insects descend in slow procession along the stems, their legs moving with the deliberate pace of something that has learned never to waste moisture on haste. They drink until their abdomens swell to translucent globes, then retreat again to the protected hollows inside the toughest leaves, where they remain motionless for days while the warmth reclaims the pans.

Beneath the hummocks, in the narrow fissures that reach a few finger-widths into the compacted ground, small clusters of pale, thread-like fungi persist. They do not form fruiting bodies; instead they exist as a pale, branching mycelium that binds the thinnest soil particles into stable pellets. When a condensing droplet falls and lingers, the mycelium extends microscopic hyphae upward through the soil surface, absorbing what it can before the droplet disappears. The pellets they create are left behind as slightly darker crumbs that, over many cycles, help the cushions retain a fraction more moisture than the bare ground around them.

Within the temporary water itself, during the short interval after each wetting, a scattering of transparent, disk-shaped organisms drift. They are neither plant nor animal in any ordinary sense—more a colonial film that separates and reforms. Each disk is thinner than paper and carries a faint greenish tinge only when viewed against stone. They absorb dissolved minerals quickly, then encyst as the water recedes, leaving behind the same faint rings already noted on the basin floor. The cysts remain dormant in the silt until the next condensation event restarts the cycle.

Nothing here hunts or flees. The scale-insects occasionally lose a few individuals to the rare, desiccating gust that slips over the rim, yet the cushions regenerate them over years rather than seasons. The fungal pellets accumulate so gradually that the linear marks half-buried in the silt are, in places, slightly elevated by their presence, though the difference is perceptible only as a change in texture under a fingertip. The disk-colonies leave no trace once they encyst except the mineral ghosts they help create.

All of these forms share the same measured tempo: growth measured in the interval between one rain and the next, movement measured in the time it takes a droplet to bead and vanish. They do not disturb the held-note silence because their activities produce no audible friction against the stones or the air. The basin therefore continues to read as empty while remaining, at its scale, fully inhabited.
