The space holds its few inhabitants the way an unfinished sentence holds its words—provisionally, without insisting they stay. The translucent arthropods are the most numerous, though “numerous” is an overstatement; at any moment perhaps a dozen drift through the folds. Their legs do not so much stroke the air as test whether resistance is still required. When one pauses, its body flattens until it is almost invisible against the even light, a mere thickening of transparency. Another will approach it from what looks like an impossible angle, phase-shifting the final distance so that for an instant two bodies occupy overlapping space. They do not fight or mate or feed; they exchange faint ripples of temperature, each creature cooling slightly where they intersect, then drifting apart again, the memory of contact already fading from their milk-colored carapaces.

The salamander—there is only one—is older than the arthropods and far less willing to be seen. Its camouflage is not static. When it remains motionless for several minutes the stone-grain pattern on its skin begins to sync with the ridge beneath it, the two textures sliding into identical alignment until only its slow breathing gives it away. Each blink resets the pattern, as though the creature is periodically forced to remember it is still an animal and not yet mineral. During the water-cycle moments, when a lens evaporates and the light brightens by a fraction, the salamander lifts its head and lets its tongue taste the suddenly sharper air. What it tastes is not moisture but possibility; the change registers on its tongue as a faint electrical crackle, the same sensation a human would feel brushing against a half-charged surface. It swallows the information, settles again, and the pattern on its back resumes its slow negotiation with the stone.

A second order of life moves even less. The bluish lichen plates that cling to a single lens are not fixed organisms but colonies in rehearsal. When the lens they occupy begins to flatten and sink into the rock, the lichen does not die; it simply detaches in microscopic flakes and drifts, suspended, until another lens condenses elsewhere. The flakes settle onto the new water, re-form their coin-sized plates, and resume whatever silent chemistry they conduct. Between settlements they exist as fine blue dust motes that the arthropods occasionally pass through without disturbing. The motes leave a temporary azure haze in the arthropods’ wake, a reminder that even the smallest tenants carry a trace of the previous arrangement.

Something still smaller and stranger occupies the thread of pale root hair dangling from the ceiling fold. The hair is not attached to any larger plant; it terminates in mid-air, its free end thinning until it becomes optical noise. Along its length, at irregular intervals, minute swellings appear and subside like breathing nodes. These nodes occasionally detach as single transparent cells that fall, impossibly slowly, toward the floor. Most dissolve before they land. One or two, however, reach a water lens and are immediately absorbed, after which the lens itself pulses once, as though metabolizing the cell into its next relocation. The root hair contracts fractionally each time this happens, then lengthens again, maintaining its exact length as though the space has unconsciously agreed to keep it at that distance from the floor.

No creature claims the half-formed impressions on the lower ridges. The shallow spirals and paired arcs are simply there, like half-remembered gestures the stone once considered making. Occasionally an arthropod will drift low enough that its phase-shift intersects one of these marks; when it does, the mark deepens by a hair’s breadth and then relaxes again, altered by the passage but not yet committed to permanence. The salamander, when it moves (which is rare), avoids the impressions entirely, stepping over them with exaggerated care, as though aware that any weight it places might be the one that finally decides what the stone will become.

All of them—the drifting arthropods, the pattern-shifting salamander, the migrating lichen, the breathing root hair—continue only because nothing has yet told them to stop. The light remains even, the sound remains absent, and the pocket keeps its single rule: what has not yet finished becoming is still permitted to exist a little longer.
