In that ribcage slit the builders are not visitors but extensions of the work itself. They move in the steam like suggestions given limbs—short, many-jointed forms whose pale segments seem half vegetable, half cooled clay. When light catches them they are nearly invisible; only the faint displacement of a leaf or the sudden beading of new moisture on a surface shows where one has passed. Their bodies are not solid so much as continuously poured: each segment is a sack of living sap wrapped in a translucent membrane that hardens and softens according to the task. One moment a limb is rigid enough to force a green sapling into an arch; the next it loosens into a dripping filament that can thread between reed strands and seal them with its own exudate.

They do not speak, yet the space is full of their attention. When a tuber splits its pot, several of them converge at once, their jointed torsos flexing like wet roots. They press the fracture closed with their own softening bellies, then remain there, fused for a while, until new clay is extruded from their seams and the wound is forgotten. Others work higher, clinging upside-down to the living ceiling. They gnaw the ends of vines with small, stone-colored mouthparts and dribble fresh pitch from glands along their flanks, encouraging the weave to tighten. Every few moments one will pause and press its flat, many-eyed face against a patch of still-wet daub, tasting whether the mixture has decided to set or prefers to stay pliant a little longer.

Their numbers are impossible to count because they never stop becoming. A single individual may divide along its length when it needs to be in two places, the two halves continuing the same motion without pause. When a larger task arises—shifting an entire mat of reed that has begun to rot—they flow together into temporary clusters whose combined shape is only roughly humanoid: four or five torsos braided into a thicker limb, eyes distributed like seeds across the surface. These clusters move with a slow, hydraulic patience, leaving thin trails of the same milky sap the vines weep. Once the mat is replaced they loosen again, each segment drifting apart to resume its separate errands, as if the cluster had only ever been a shared intention.

The translucent lizard is one of them, or was—sometimes a builder pauses mid-task, flattens itself against the clay, and for a moment its outline exactly matches the lizard’s quick shape before it lengthens again into something more suited to weaving. They do not mind sharing the pocket with the lizard; both are expressions of the same decision the crevice keeps making: to remain unfinished, to stay hot and crowded and insistently alive until the mountain and the sky finally agree on what this narrow throat is allowed to become.
