The basin is inhabited by a single, distributed organism whose body has no center and no boundary. It exists as a continuous weave of frond and filament, root and conduit, membrane and cable sheath. Each new surge of warmer water carries dissolved minerals and stray current from the deepening fractures; the organism drinks both at once, drawing silicates and copper ions into the same vascular channels. Where the channels thicken, they harden into pale metallic ribs that still conduct the faint, directionless charge; where they remain soft, they swell into translucent bladders that rise and fall with the methane they trap. These bladders are not separate creatures but temporary lungs of the larger mass, inflating until a current tears them loose and they drift upward like slow green lanterns before dissolving back into the weave.

The organism does not move so much as re-inscribe itself. When a surge leans an entire canopy sideways, the fronds do not resist; they extend new spiral tips along the direction of the push, threading fresh metallic threads through the apertures of any nearby machinery before the water settles. Inside collapsed stator housings the threads have already replaced the original windings, so that the turbines, though motionless, now pulse with the same dull orange light that leaks from torn stalks. The light is not waste heat but a shared signal; a fracture in one part of the canopy brightens a dozen others simultaneously, as though the organism is briefly reading its own map of stress and repair.

Scattered through this map are the last pockets of pre-flood geometry—an intact control room, a row of lockers, a signal tower—each of them already half-assimilated. The armored glass of the control room has bowed because the organism is pressing thin, root-like sheets against its inner surface, reading residual warmth from the electronics it has not yet reached. The lockers contain compacted mats that were once clothing and paper; the mats now extrude hair-fine conductors that reach toward the open doors like exploratory fingers, tasting the faint ozone that still leaks from the tower’s cycling lamps. The tower itself is no longer a structure but a spine: its rusted colors have become the organism’s own seasonal markers, shifting from dull red to corroded green whenever a current reverses and the canopy above it rearranges.

Nothing here reproduces in the old sense. Instead, the organism continually copies and revises the industrial patterns it has overgrown. Beneath the surface, in the vast lumen of the collapsed factory, new conveyors have begun to extrude segments of themselves made entirely of living tissue—segmented stalks whose joints articulate on the same timing as the original belt drives. Ventilators push columns of water that carry spores and dissolved metal upward; the spores settle on higher wreckage and immediately begin to repeat the factory’s floor plan at smaller scale, laying out girder-like ribs and rail-bed fronds as though the organism is still learning the surface that was lost and has decided to finish the work itself.

Everywhere the water remains brownish and tea-colored, the organism’s phosphorescence stays low, a private orange glow along fracture lines and conductor threads. Only when a stalk tears or a membrane bursts does the light flare outward, traveling along the metallic veins like a brief, shared memory of injury and continuation. In those moments the entire volume seems to inhale: fronds tilt, bladders deflate, currents momentarily clarify, and far below the factory’s empty conveyors turn once, carrying nothing but the organism’s own slow, faithful rewriting of the plans it inherited.
