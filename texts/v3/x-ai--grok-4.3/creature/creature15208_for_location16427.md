The plateau sustains a slow, distributed metabolism rather than discrete populations. The smallest beings, the translucent fist-sized forms, are not truly separate animals but mobile condensations of the salt-and-crystal film that already coats every fracture. Each is a temporary concentration: dozens of microscopic lattices knit together under the skin of moisture, then crawl until the local humidity drops or a heavier rivulet arrives. When they fragment they do not die so much as return to the surface tension; minutes later another concentration may form a few paces away from the same invisible reservoir. Their trails are not tracks but brief re-wettings, after which the glass reclaims the salt and the creatures begin again elsewhere.

Larger, ribbed shapes occupy the steaming basins. Their segmented carapaces are not chitin but successive sheets of the same translucent crystal that edges the rivulets, grown thick and faintly opaline. Between the plates they keep thin films of water that never quite boil; the mineral lattices on their undersides conduct heat downward into the rock while simultaneously drawing dissolved salts upward. Movement is rare and deliberate: a single segment flexes, shifts the whole mass a hand-span, then the creature settles again, allowing the next layer of crystal to accrete. Over years these individuals become almost indistinguishable from the basin rims they rest against, until a sudden increase in drip volume or a vent plume restarts their slow circuit.

Deeper still, inside the hairline fissures, networks of even finer life persist as living stains rather than bodies. These are not organisms with boundaries but sustained chemical gradients that move when water moves. Where the glass remains damp they precipitate new crystal faces; where vapor escapes a vent they briefly volatilize and re-seed the air. Occasionally a heavier drop carries a fragment of this gradient to the surface, where it may nucleate one of the scuttling concentrations or add another plate to a basin-dweller. The industrial remnants participate in this same cycle: rust streaks supply iron ions, pipe joints collect and re-release condensate, and the cast-stone blocks act as thermal reservoirs that moderate the daily swing between glare and the chill that follows each vapor plume. In that sense the ruins are not ruins at all but additional mineral surfaces the plateau has accepted into its equilibrium, their angles and cavities now serving the same functions as ledges and fractures.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual sense. Instead the entire surface maintains a single, patient crystallization that occasionally concentrates into motion, then diffuses again. The only measure of time is the accumulation of new salt fans and the slow thickening of crystal plates against the old iron.
