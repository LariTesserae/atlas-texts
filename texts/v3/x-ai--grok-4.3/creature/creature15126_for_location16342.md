The platforms are not empty. They host a thin, almost weightless ecology that moves at the same glacial pace as the stone itself.

The most visible residents are the mist-moths, but “moth” is only a borrowed name. Their bodies are lattices of condensed droplets held together by filaments finer than spider silk. When one alights on a fern, the frond does not bend; instead the insect merges briefly with the existing film of water, exchanging a few molecules before drifting onward. Their translucency is functional: they are mostly water arranged around a kernel of mineral dust gathered from the drips. Only in the steady, shadowless light do they become perceptible at all, appearing as brief prismatic glints that travel in loose, three-dimensional spirals between platforms. They never gather in numbers; two is already a crowd, and three will cause the group to dissolve into the haze within moments.

Far smaller and far stranger are the seam-dwellers that inhabit the ragged joints where new stone is still pushing upward. These are not insects but colonial films—layered sheets of microbes that stain the fresh pumice in faint, opalescent rings. Each ring marks a single cycle of extrusion and wetting. The films feed on the trace gases that arrive with the condensed droplets and, in return, secrete minute quantities of silica that help knit the stone’s surface into a slightly harder crust. When a thread of water detaches overhead, the impact sends a momentary ripple through the film; the ripple travels along the seam for the length of a finger before damping out, carrying information the colony uses to adjust its own growth rate.

Between the stone and the vines lives another order of organism: thread-like bryophytes whose individual strands are no thicker than a hair yet can span an entire gap between platforms. They do not grow toward light or toward moisture; they grow toward the precise interval where the next drip is statistically most likely to form. Their tips remain in constant, almost imperceptible motion, testing the air the way certain roots test soil. When a droplet finally strikes, the thread contracts in a single slow pulse, drawing the water along its length and into a hidden reservoir at the vine’s core. The contraction is so gradual that an observer might watch all day and see nothing, yet over weeks the reservoirs swell enough to push a new leaf node into existence.

The largest creatures—if they can be called creatures—are the rare, nearly sessile assemblies that occupy the undersides of the widest ledges. They resemble flattened anemones made of the same pale stone as the platforms, except that their “tentacles” are actually extrusions of living carbonate that can extend or retract by a millimeter per year. They harvest the constant cool mist by maintaining a slight electrostatic charge across their surface; the charge draws droplets out of the air faster than gravity alone would achieve. Once enough water has coalesced, a single tentacle detaches and falls, carrying the gathered moisture down into a crevice where moss waits. The anemone then slowly grows a replacement. These detachments are the only sudden events in the entire settlement, and they produce the deepest notes in the drip-choir.

Nothing here reproduces on a human timescale. New mist-moths condense fully formed from especially dense patches of haze. New seam-films appear only where a fracture opens wide enough to admit a different gas mixture. The thread bryophytes send out spores so fine they remain aloft for entire seasons, settling only when the wind drops to absolute stillness—an event that occurs perhaps once in a human lifetime. The stone anemones divide so rarely that any single platform may host only one or two individuals at any given moment.

Together they maintain the unfinished quality of the place. Every organism participates in the same closed loop of water, mineral, and sound, adding just enough persistence to keep the haze from dissipating and just little enough activity that the platforms never feel finished or inhabited in any ordinary sense. The settlement remains exactly as the description states: raw, cool, and still deciding what comes next.
