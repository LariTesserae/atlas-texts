The nodules are not individuals in the way an animal might be, but neither are they a single organism. Each one is a colony of mineral lattices that have precipitated around a seed-grain, layer by layer, until the whole mass reaches the size of a fist and begins to press against its neighbors. Where two colonies meet, they do not merge at once; they test one another through slow dissolution and regrowth, exchanging dissolved silica in threads too fine to see. A nodule that receives more nourishment from below will swell and push its neighbors aside by the width of a grain of sand each year. One that is shaded or underfed will slowly lose its facets and darken, then be overgrown.

Over centuries these pressures create the bridges and arches that cross the chamber. A bridge is not built; it is the place where two expanding fronts happened to meet and decided, through chemistry, to continue together rather than compete. Inside such a bridge the veins of liquid mineral run in both directions, carrying heat and dissolved matter from the pressure seams upward and returning waste silicates downward. When the seams brighten, the liquid quickens and the nodules nearest the light become briefly translucent, as if a thought had passed through them.

The older shapes—the pillar, the platform, the ruined arch—remain because the colonies have not found it profitable to dissolve them. Their surfaces are now covered in the same nodding, fist-sized masses, yet the underlying spirals and edges still impose a faint geometry. Occasionally a nodule that grows directly against one of these older planes will align its own facets to the remnant groove, as though copying a memory it cannot read. Over time the copy becomes part of the original again, and the distinction disappears.

Movement through the conduits is the slow negotiation of bodies that must rearrange themselves to let another pass. A colony will contract inward, faceted surface flattening until a gap forms, then swell again once the passage is made. The sound is only the dry click of lattice planes sliding against one another and the faint settling of loosened grains. Nothing hurries; nothing stops. The entire mass breathes heat together, expanding when the floor warms and contracting when it cools, so that every few hours the chamber shifts minutely, realigning every bridge and every conduit by the width of a molecule.

Nothing here dies in the ordinary sense. A nodule that can no longer metabolize is simply dissolved and its material reused. The older architectural forms are not ruins; they are earlier phases of the same process, still present, still being absorbed and recopied. The chamber is full because it has always been in the process of filling itself, and the life that sealed it from the world continues that work, one slow precipitation at a time.
