The plateau holds its own kind of quiet tenancy. Most of what moves across it does so at the scale of condensation itself—brief, opportunistic, and shaped by the same fleeting moisture that gathers and leaves. The smallest residents are the many-legged grazers, palm-sized and nearly invisible when still. Their translucent bodies are not so much flesh as lattices of fine, jointed struts that catch light like cut crystal. They drift in loose clusters, never quite touching, each individual pausing now and then to lower a cluster of sensory filaments onto the silvery plant-mineral threads. When they feed, the filaments brighten for an instant, then dim again; the grazers absorb only a measured fraction before moving on, leaving the thread intact for the next cycle of moisture. Their movement creates the plateau’s only reliable sound: a soft, dry clicking that travels a few meters and fades.

Larger and less frequent are the membrane-gliders. They are not flyers so much as slow distortions in the air, their folded vanes of translucent tissue catching whatever thermal lift the warming stone provides. A glider may hang motionless above a ridge for hours, vanes trembling at the edges, before drifting a dozen meters and settling again. Beneath each one, the rock cools by a few degrees; the sudden micro-condensation draws out a brief ring of filaments that would otherwise stay dormant. When two gliders pass close, their membranes chime once, a pure tone that vibrates the stone for several seconds. The sound is answered, if at all, by a faint answering pulse from the nearest curved shell, as though the ancient surfaces remember how to resonate but lack the strength to complete the reply.

The shells themselves are the oldest presence. They do not appear to be inhabited in any ordinary sense, yet around each one the air and the stone behave differently. Moisture is coaxed into longer-lived threads that run outward like invisible spokes; the grazers linger longer here, and the gliders descend lower, their vanes brushing the shell’s smooth curve before lifting again. Inside the largest shells—those half-buried and wide enough to walk across—the temperature holds steady even when the rest of the plateau bakes or cools. The faint internal light that rises every few hours does more than ease weight in passing bones; it also seems to retune the local chemistry of the filaments, encouraging them to grow in slightly straighter, more ordered lines for a short distance before the pattern loosens back into the usual wandering gaps.

Nothing here crowds. The grazers keep several body-lengths between themselves and any shell. The gliders rarely cross directly above one another. The filaments stop short of every curved surface, leaving a deliberate margin of bare stone. Over long stretches of the day the plateau looks almost empty, yet the emptiness is measured: enough space for moisture to form and vanish, enough distance for each form of life and each relic to register the presence of the others without collision. The entire arrangement feels held in a single, continuous exhalation—stone, filament, translucent bodies, and half-buried curves all suspended in the same slow exchange of heat, water, and faint, returning light.
