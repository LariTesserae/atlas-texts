In the high stone city, the people move as deliberately as their walls rise. They are a spare, long-boned folk whose features have grown angular under the same unmoving light that keeps the plateau raw. Their skin carries a faint mineral dust that never quite washes away, and their eyes—pale as condensation on rock—rarely widen beyond a measured glance. They speak little and finish one another’s short sentences without impatience, as if conversation itself were a form of masonry laid down over decades. Families do not gather in rooms so much as occupy successive generations of the same chamber; a child may sleep in the niche her great-grandmother first cut, while her own grandchildren will one day set the next course above her head. Work is measured in lifetimes: a man may spend thirty years dressing a single façade, then leave the finishing of its upper courses to his daughter. They keep their charcoal fires low and steady beneath clay domes, not for warmth alone but because the slow heat helps the living rock accept the next layer without cracking.

Among them move the smaller lives that have learned the city’s intervals. Lizards the color of fresh-split stone flatten themselves against paving stones the moment a footstep echoes, then resume their motionless basking when the sound fades. In the narrow drains beneath the terraces, quick-furred mammals no larger than a doubled fist thread through seams no wider than a chisel blade, emerging only in the long twilights between showers to lick the mineral film from wet stone. Higher up, tight flocks of dark-winged birds wheel between towers at the same measured pace as the water moving through the aqueducts; their nests are packed so densely into the ledges that from a distance the walls appear feathered. On the ridge crests above the city, goats with coats thick as felt browse the tufts of wiry grass, their hooves finding purchase where human feet would slip. Now and then a heavier shape—broad-shouldered, low-headed—crosses the skyline at the plateau’s margin, its call a single low vibration that travels through the stone and is felt more than heard inside the rooms below.

Nothing hurries. Even the presence of these lives registers as a quiet thickening rather than a commotion. When rain arrives, the goats move under the nearest overhang of unfinished wall; the birds tuck closer to their ledges; the people close the narrow doors cut into the living rock and listen to water find its slow path downward through the seams they themselves have left. When the sky clears again, the same figures emerge onto the same terraces, resuming the same tasks at the same unhurried tempo, as though the brief interruption had been only another course of stone settling into place.
