The plateau is inhabited by slow, composite organisms that have no single name among themselves. Each one begins as a cluster of broad, waxy leaves anchored around a buried pipe or housing; over decades the leaves thicken into woody plates while thin root threads penetrate the metal seams, tasting residual charge and guiding it outward into the surrounding moss. Where the metal is warmest the roots swell and split, forming pale, translucent nodules that pulse once every few days with the faint current they have collected. These nodules are both lung and memory: they release stored water as mist at night and retain, in their cellular lattices, the precise rhythm at which any given flywheel last turned.

The larger entities occupy entire ridges. Their “bodies” are the fused networks of stalk and conduit that run from one tier down to the next, trading moisture downward and static upward in an unbroken thread. When a new fracture opens in the rock, a filament of living tissue migrates along it within months, carrying with it a single stolon of pipe; the pipe cools the advancing cells while the cells seal the metal against further oxidation. The exchange is silent and without visible motion, yet every droplet that falls from an overhanging fin has already been tasted, measured, and directed by the organism that now claims that pipe as its own vein.

Smaller, more mobile forms exist inside the largest housings. They are little more than condensed films of water laced with motile cells; they travel the inner surfaces on cycles measured in years, cleaning corrosion and redistributing mineral salts left by evaporated condensate. Their trails glisten briefly beneath the dusk-glow of the hanging pods before vanishing into the root mat. When two such films meet they merge for several seasons, exchange genetic fragments and stored patterns of temperature change, then separate again, carrying away slightly altered instructions for the next stretch of pipe they will colonize.

Nothing here moves quickly enough to be perceived as an animal. The plateau’s residents register time as gradients of moisture and charge; a single rotation of a deep flywheel is to them what a heartbeat would be to a warmer creature. Their only urgency is the slow sealing of stone against light, the patient threading of root through every remaining bolt, until the distinction between bedrock, machinery, and living tissue finally dissolves into one continuous, cool, respiring surface.
