This is a chasm without a sky. In its place, leagues above, is a ceiling of bruised-purple and milky-white glow-rock that sheds a soft, even, and perpetual daylight. The light is cool, like that of a deeply overcast afternoon, and it seems to have no single source, radiating from the entire vast plate of stone that roofs the world. The air is still and chill, carrying the clean, sharp scent of wet minerals and something like the ozone after a thunderstorm. It is always damp here. Moisture weeps from the chasm walls in slow, glistening tears, tracing dark paths down stone faces. It condenses on every surface, beading on rock and wood, giving the air a tangible weight.

The floor of the chasm is not a floor at all. It is a dense, violent congress of geological becoming. This is a landscape where erosion has not yet had its say. It is the opposite of worn; it is emerging. Crystalline structures, half-born, shoulder their way out of the bedrock. Some are squat, thick-set quartz-like formations, their facets still cloudy and incomplete. Others are sharp, needle-thin spars of black, glassy obsidian that jut out at hostile angles. There are no smooth surfaces, no gentle slopes. The ground is a tightly packed, three-dimensional maze of nascent geology, a field of stone thorns. To walk on it directly would be impossible, a treacherous climb over razor edges and unstable, growing forms. The growth is imperceptibly slow to the casual eye, but over a week, a new fist-sized crystal might push its way through a fissure; over a year, a whole cluster might rise and alter the terrain.

And this is where they live, in a city built not *on* the landscape but *in tandem* with it. It is a settlement of immense density, layered and woven through the open void of the chasm. Because the ground is unusable, the city is vertical. It begins some twenty feet above the chaotic crystal floor, anchored to the more stable, monolithic spurs of rock that form the chasm's true foundations.

This is a city of scaffolds, bridges, and cantilevered platforms. The primary building material is a tough, pale wood, harvested from fungal forests in narrower, wetter chasms far away. The planks are lashed together with thick, fibrous rope. Walkways, hundreds of feet long, stretch between rock faces, swaying slightly in the chasm's slow, internal breezes. Platforms are wedged into geological clefts, held in place by massive, hand-hewn wooden pins. Houses are simple, functional structures of wood and stretched hide, clinging to the sides of stone pillars like barnacles.

The city is a web. There is no central plaza, no grand boulevards. There are only pathways, rising and falling, crisscrossing over and under one another. Looking out from a balcony, one sees not a single vista, but a saturation of presences: the walkway to the cisterns below, the rope-and-pulley lift hauling a basket of supplies to a higher habitation, the smoke-smudged doorway of a neighbor’s dwelling just across a narrow gap. Light from the glowing ceiling filters down through the countless gaps in the construction, creating a complex dapple of light and shadow that is constantly shifting as people move through the web.

Life here is governed by the geology. The constant, slow growth of the crystal floor is both a resource and a threat. Miners, secured by ropes, are lowered into the "growth fields" to carefully harvest desirable new crystals with bronze picks and stone hammers, a dangerous job. These crystals are used for tools, arrowheads, and ornamentation. But this same growth can threaten the city's foundations. A new crystalline mass can put pressure on a pylon, forcing it to crack. An entire platform might need to be dismantled and rebuilt a few yards away to accommodate a decade's worth of geological upheaval. The city is never finished; it is in a permanent, slow-motion state of construction, deconstruction, and repair. The dominant sound, when you can hear it, is not voices or music, but the distant, rhythmic *tock... tock... tock* of a hammer on stone and the scrape of a primitive saw.

Ecology is sparse. Thin, pale green moss grows in patches on the damp wood of the city, feeding small, iridescent beetles that are the most common form of fauna. In the deepest, wettest fissures of the chasm walls, where water pools, strange, stalk-like fungi grow in silent groves. The people cultivate these fungi in clay pots and wooden planters, their primary source of food besides the small, blind fish they catch in the subterranean pools far below.

The water itself is a quiet, active system. It drips constantly from the ceiling, a sound so ubiquitous it becomes a form of silence. This is the "Rain." Collection basins—huge, hollowed-out stone nodules—are strategically placed on stable outcrops to catch the water, which is then piped to different levels of the city through a network of hollowed wooden logs.

Life is quiet, ordered, and immensely pragmatic. One must be a carpenter, a mountaineer, and a geologist just to survive. Children learn to tie knots before they learn to write. The rule of the world is simple: the stone grows, the water falls, and the city adapts. To stand still is to be broken. Here, in the cool, perpetual daylight beneath the earth, humanity exists not as a master of its environment, but as a patient, tireless collaborator with a world that is still being born.
