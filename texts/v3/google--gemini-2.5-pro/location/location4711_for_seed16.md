The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not the sound of life, but of physics: a constant, multi-layered hum. It is the sound of a world-sized lung breathing in and out, a sound composed of a million smaller noises. It is the high, tortured whistle of hot wind being forced through countless narrow apertures. It is the low, resonant thrum of deep rock settling under immense, slow pressure. It is the sharp, infrequent *tink* of cooling stone and the dry, percussive skittering of silicate dust across flat surfaces. The air itself is a presence, thick with heat that presses on the skin not with the dampness of humidity, but with the dry, abrasive intensity of a kiln.

This place is a bowl, a great sunken kiva carved not by water but by the patient hand of thermal collapse over eons. It is a depression in a wider, desiccated plain, a pocket universe hidden from the larger emptiness. The scale is intimate. From one edge of the terraced rim, you can see the other, a hazy, shimmering crescent across the basin floor. The rock of the basin walls is ancient, smoothed and rounded by ages of thermal shock and wind-scouring. It displays its history in wide, gentle strata of baked ochre, dusty rose, and bands of charcoal-dark basalt.

There is no water. Not a drop. The concept is alien here. There are no riverbeds, no pooling basins, no stains of evaporation. The ground is a fine, glassy powder mixed with coarser sand, all of it warm to the touch, as if the sun set only moments ago, though there is no sun to be seen. The light is a dim, internal twilight, a sourceless glow that seems to emanate from the ground up.

And from this dry, warm, glassy soil grows a forest. It is a flora of profound contradiction, lush in form but utterly devoid of moisture. These are not plants. They are thermovites, crystalline lifeforms that feed on the world's deep heat. They rise from the basin floor in great, dense thickets, but each individual is a marvel of hollowness. They are stalks of spun, smoky quartz and obsidian filament, branching into forms that mimic ferns, horsetails, and great, feathery reeds. They are riddled with microscopic pores and tunnels, and it is the hot air being drawn up from geothermal vents below, whistling through these billion tiny flutes, that creates the landscape's dominant song.

Their "lushness" is in their number and their slow, constant activity. They pulse with a faint, internal light, a throbbing crimson and orange that waxes and wanes with the flow of geothermal energy from the vents they anchor to. A stand of them will brighten in unison, a silent coordinated breath, their combined glow casting long, dancing shadows across the basin floor. Then they will dim again, their song dropping a semitone. When a thermovite's life cycle ends, it does not rot. It cools, becomes brittle, and eventually shatters in a gust of wind, collapsing into a shower of black and crystal dust that replenishes the glassy soil. There is no decay, only disintegration.

Built into the gentle slope of the basin's walls is the city. It is old, layered, and dense. It seems less constructed and more excavated, an organized erosion of the cliffside. The architecture is of packed earth, sun-baked (or rather, heat-baked) adobe the color of the surrounding rock, reinforced with the hardened, dead stalks of the thermovites, which serve as a kind of primitive rebar. Shelves and terraces are stacked one atop the other, connected by steep, narrow stairways and rope-and-plank ladders. Doorways are low and ovular, windows mere slits designed to minimize the intrusive wind while capturing the dim, pulsing light from the forest below.

It is a city without inhabitants. There are no animals, no people, no ghosts. But it is not a ruin. It is maintained. The sound of the wind through the city is different from the sound of the wind through the crystal forest—it is a more organized music. Some structures are clearly designed as wind-catchers, with openings of varying sizes that turn the constant air currents into a complex, mournful chord. This is the city's role in the ecosystem: to add its voice to the landscape's choir. Plaster on the walls is new, ladders are steady, storage kivas are swept clean of dust. The work is done by some unseen, unknown process, a sense of active maintenance without a maintainer. The place is not abandoned; it is simply empty.

The emptiness is palpable. Despite the density of the crystal flora and the tiered city, the space between things is vast and silent. In the lulls between the wind's stronger gusts, you can feel the absence of small sounds: the scuttling of insects, the call of a bird, the rustle of flesh moving through undergrowth. All motion is geological or meteorological. Dust devils, miniature cyclones of fine-grain glass, waltz across the basin floor. The ground itself trembles with a low, continuous vibration from the heat engine deep below. The air shimmers, making the far side of the bowl waver as if seen through heated oil. This is a world of active, dynamic systems, but it is a world entirely without biology as we would know it, a loud, hot, breathing place waiting in a state of perfect, lonely equilibrium.
