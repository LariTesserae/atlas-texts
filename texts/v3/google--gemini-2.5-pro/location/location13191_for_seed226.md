The sound reaches you first, a constant, overwhelming presence. It is not the sound of nature, though it is made of natural things. It is a vast, percussive chorus, a million metallic clicks and resonant clangs that merge into a single, deafening, industrial chime. It is the sound of making, but on a geological scale. And beneath it, a deeper hum, a bass note so low it is felt in the bones more than heard, the vibration of a world under construction.

This place is a high-altitude basin, a caldera-like bowl perhaps a few kilometers across. You stand on the precipice of its inner rim, looking down. There are no distant vistas; the basin's far walls are visible, and beyond them, only a seamless, luminous white-grey sky. The air is cool and carries the clean, sharp scent of ozone, wet stone, and crushed green things. It is saturated with moisture, which condenses on every surface, beading on your skin and clothing. The light is bright but diffuse, as if filtered through an infinite layer of fine muslin. There are no sharp shadows, only soft gradients and a pearlescent glow that seems to emanate from the air itself.

The floor of the basin is not soil or old rock, but something new. It is a landscape in the process of becoming. Great, dark formations of a basalt-like stone are extruding from the ground, not violently, but with an immense, patient pressure. They rise like the crowns of unerupted teeth, in shapes that are not yet mountains or hills, but their raw conceptual forms: pillars, immense slabs, tilted planes, and vast, flat-topped mesas. The process is so new that the rock has no erosion. Its edges are sharp, its fractures crystalline and clean. In places, the dark, matte-black surfaces are split by geode-like interiors, glittering with coarse, smoky quartz crystals the size of a human fist.

And the stone weeps. Water slicks every surface, not from rain, but from the rock itself. It seeps from hairline fissures and drips from the tips of newly-formed overhangs. This water carves no canyons; the geology is too young. Instead, it pools in platters of obsidian-like glass on the mesa tops or cascades in thin, silent sheets down sheer black faces, feeding the riot of life that clings to it.

The vegetation is overwhelmingly lush, a vibrant green against the stark black of the emerging stone. But it is a specialist's garden. There are no trees. Instead, thick, springy carpets of moss, in shades from neon-lime to deep forest green, cover every horizontal surface not actively being worked. Liverworts, slick and translucent, plaster themselves to vertical walls. Plumes of maidenhair fern, dripping with condensation, erupt from every crack and fissure. The effect is of a colossal, unfinished Japanese rock garden, meticulously gardened yet fundamentally wild.

This is where the urbanity and the emptiness create their strange harmony. The formations are widely spaced. One could walk for a hundred meters across a perfectly flat, moss-covered platform before reaching the next rising pillar. The air between these features feels vast and hollow, amplifying the ever-present sound. Yet this is unmistakably a settlement.

Scattered across the landscape are figures—hundreds, maybe thousands of them. They move slowly, with a ritualistic deliberation. They are the source of the percussive sound. Dressed in thick, undyed woollen cloaks to ward off the damp chill, they are the artisans of this geology. Using simple tools—hammers of hard stone bound to wood handles, chisels of sharpened obsidian—they are guiding the formation of the land.

They are not quarrying it, but sculpting it as it grows. A team of a dozen artisans might spend a generation scoring a single, perfectly straight channel down the face of a rising slab, creating an aqueduct for the weeping water. Another group polishes a small section of a pillar, working for years to grind away the rough exterior and expose the glittering crystal heart within, creating a wall that sparkles with captured light. They tap and chip, not with violence, but with the patient rhythm of a heartbeat, their collective efforts creating the deafening chime that fills the basin. The structures are not buildings, but orchestrated geology: wide, tiered plazas; stairways carved into the side of a growing cliff; perfectly circular amphitheaters designed to collect water into a shimmering, shallow pool. There is infrastructure, but it is one of flow, form, and aesthetics rather than habitation.

There are no animals. The constant, overwhelming sound, the lack of edible soil-based plants, and the sheer weirdness of the place have kept them away. The only movement, aside from the slow progress of the artisans, is the constant drip and flow of water and the almost imperceptible growth of the stone beneath your feet. It is a city, but its citizens are not inhabitants; they are midwives to a mountain. The "houses" are perhaps small nooks and grottoes carved into already-stabilized rock, temporary shelters in a project that will outlast countless generations. This is a place of singular, monolithic purpose, a world hollowed out and filled with a single, resonant task: to listen to the slow song of the earth's creation and to add their own small, persistent chime to the chorus.
