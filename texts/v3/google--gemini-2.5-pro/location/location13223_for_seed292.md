This is not an ocean. An ocean suggests a surface, a division between air and water, a place where light from a sun can enter. This is The Churn. It is a world-ocean, a single, globe-encircling liquid body of incomprehensible depth, and you are far, far below any theoretical surface. Here, the water itself is the atmosphere, the sky, and the ground.

The first sensation is the pressure, a cool, constant, and immense weight on all sides. The water is not frigid, but it leeches warmth with a chilling efficiency. You are suspended in a medium that is never, ever still. The world moves in a state of perpetual, turbulent flow. There are no gentle tides or predictable currents; there are rivers of force, continent-sized eddies, and vortices large enough to swallow fleets. It is a storm that never began and will never end, a chaos of kinetic energy that has ground the very bones of the planet smooth.

Out of the deep gloom rise mountains. But they are not peaks. They are nubs, the worn and ancient teeth of a jawbone the size of a world, ground down by eons of hydrological violence. Their slopes are not sheer cliffs but vast, slumped shoulders of granite and basalt, so profoundly eroded that they look soft, like clay molded by a giant's thumb. Their surfaces are polished and slick in some places, scoured into vast, overlapping scoops in others. They are so old they have forgotten the fire of their birth. These are the Great Anchors, the only solid things in a fluid universe, rising for miles from an abyss too deep and dark to contemplate.

Yet the world is not dark. It is bathed in a perpetual, shifting twilight. The light is a living thing, generated by the impossibly lush ecology that clings to the ancient stone. Forests of colossal kelp, with trunks as thick as redwoods and fronds the size of sails, cover the mountainsides. They glow with a soft, internal, blue-green luminescence, swaying and whipping violently in the mega-currents. Vast meadows of phosphorescent algae carpet the flatter basins between the peaks, pulsing in slow, hypnotic waves of light, their patterns disrupted by the churning water. The light is never constant; it brightens and dims as swathes of glowing plankton, thick as snowstorms, drift and swirl through the water column.

The noise is immense and omnipresent. It is a layered, orchestral roar. At the bottom is the low, grinding groan of the Churn itself—the sound of trillions of tons of water in motion, a bass note that vibrates in your bones. Over this is the constant, high-pitched scream of metal under unimaginable stress, and the deep, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of colossal engines. This is the sound of industry.

Bolted and drilled deep into the flanks of the drowned mountains are the Harvesters. They are not cities, but scattered, brutally functional industrial stations. Each one is a tangle of conduits, pressure-domes, and scaffolding, all anchored around a single, titanic turbine. The blades of these turbines, each the size of a skyscraper, spin slowly and unstoppably in the most powerful of the deep-world rivers. They are harvesting the chaos, converting the planet's restlessness into energy. Harsh, focused beams of artificial white light cut through the soft bioluminescence, illuminating patches of machinery, work crews in armored exoskeletons moving like slow-motion crabs, and the endless dance of particulate matter in the water. The stations are battered, dented, constantly undergoing repair. For every active Harvester, you can see the ruins of two more: a crumpled dome, a snapped turbine blade half-buried in silt, a string of lights blinking on a derelict habitat module slowly being consumed by glowing moss. It is a marginal and dangerous existence.

Life is everywhere, dense and teeming. Immense schools of silver-sided fish, millions strong, move as a single fluid entity, a shimmering cloud that parts and reforms around the industrial structures. Great, armored grazers, the size of locomotives, methodically scrape glowing algae from the rock with grinding mouthparts, their thick carapaces deflecting the worst of the currents. In the open water between the peaks, leviathans swim—creatures of cartilage and blubber, larger than any whale, their calls a chorus of deep, resonant moans that occasionally cut through the industrial din. The density is staggering; the water is a thick soup of life, from microscopic organisms to the titans that hunt them. You are never more than a few feet from another living thing.

The rules of this world are simple. The Churn provides everything: the light, the food, the energy. The Churn is also the enemy, seeking always to tear down what is built, to scour the works of industry from the ancient mountainsides. To stand still is to be swept away. To be rigid is to be broken. Everything here, from the swaying kelp to the resilient fauna to the beleaguered engineering of the Harvesters, has learned to bend, to brace, and to endure within the heart of the endless, violent motion. Nothing lasts forever, but here, in the cold, loud, luminous deep, the struggle itself is a state of being.
