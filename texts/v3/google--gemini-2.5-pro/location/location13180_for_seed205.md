This world is a single living thing, a plateau of plant-flesh that stretches beyond any horizon. There is no soil, no rock, no true ground. You stand on the rind of the world-organism, a surface that is both substrate and life. It is called the Great Scythe-Plateau, and its scale is geologic.

The "ground" underfoot is a vast, rolling plain of spongy, calloused cellulose. It has the texture of ancient corkboard, cross-hatched with the fine, silvery lines of dormant vascular tissue. The colour is a muted ochre, bleached by a sun that beats down with a humid, oppressive heat. Though the air is thick with moisture, the surface itself is often dry to the touch, radiating warmth like a sun-baked stone. Distances are immense and deceptive. The topography is gentle, long swells and shallow basins, but the sheer size of it all creates an impression of profound emptiness. The world is full—in that it is entirely made of one plant—but the space upon its back is sparsely populated with its own grand features.

These features are the Scythes. Miles apart, they rise from the living plain like monoliths of a forgotten civilization, but they are no more built than a leaf is built. They are titanic, blade-like structures, leaves or sporophylls of a size that defies botany. Each Scythe is a sheer, upward curve of semi-translucent material, ribbed with darker, stronger veins of lignin-like fibre. They can be ten, twenty, thirty miles high. Their edges are impossibly thin, humming audibly as the high-altitude winds whip past them. They are not static. Over the course of a day, they pivot with excruciating slowness, tracking the sun in the sky. Their movement is the world's clock, a celestial motion brought down to earth, casting shadows that are themselves continental in their passage.

Water here is not a feature of geography but of biology. There are no permanent rivers or lakes. Instead, the world sweats. Condensation beads on the cool undersides of outcroppings and gathers in the deep grooves of the rind. When a passing Scythe-shadow falls across a super-heated plain, the temperature change can trigger a downpour as the atmosphere sheds its burden of humidity. The primary source of liquid, however, is the weeping of the Scythes themselves. From pores along their supportive veins, a thick, nutrient-rich sap constantly drips. This fluid carves temporary, meandering channels in the living ground, flowing for a few hours or days before being reabsorbed by the thirsty rind. These fleeting streams are oases, magnets for life, and their appearance is a major event.

And the life is overwhelming. The most defining characteristic of the Scythe-Plateau, after the plant-matter itself, is the sound. It is a physical presence, a deafening, unceasing wall of noise that batters the senses and makes conversation an act of shouting into a hurricane. It is the sound of the fauna. Trillions of creatures live on, in, and around the world-plant. The air, never silent, is thick with the buzzing of iridescent sky-jellies, gas-filled organisms that drift on the thermal currents. The rind is scraped and scoured by herds of six-legged grazers, their keratinous mouthparts creating a constant, grating static. Their calls are booming, resonant moans that carry for miles across the open plains. From within the rind itself comes a deep, intermittent thrumming, the sound of colossal burrowing larvae, each the size of a whale, slowly chewing their way through the endless bounty of cellulose. High on the Scythes, vast colonies of winged predators roost, their shrieks and clicks adding a piercing upper register to the cacophony. The sound is not a collection of noises; it is the single, unified voice of an ecosystem at full throttle.

In this overwhelming world, other intelligences have found a foothold. Not as conquerors, but as parasites or symbionts. Scattered across the plains, often miles from any other sign of habitation, are the faint marks of their existence. A series of deep grooves carved into the rind to channel the precious sap-drip. A shelter hollowed out from a tougher, burl-like nodule on the plant's surface, its entrance covered by a hide made from a grazer's flank. These places are not settlements so much as nests. Their tools are born of the world: sharpened femurs of predators, blades knapped from glassy, obsidian-like deposits found where the rind has been super-heated by lightning, ropes braided from the fibrous veins of shed Scythe-fragments.

The physics of this place are dictated by biology. The slow, grinding pivot of the Scythes is the primary dynamic, a force of unimaginable power that is utterly predictable. The ground itself is not stable; it breathes. A slow, twenty-four-hour pulse, a tensing and relaxing of the entire plateau, is the world's heartbeat. To live here is to understand these rhythms. To know when the sap will flow, to read the colour of the sky for a coming shadow-rain, to feel the vibrations in the rind that signal the approach of a burrowing leviathan. Danger is not a rogue storm, but a herd of grazers stampeding away from a hunting party of winged screechers. Survival is an act of deep listening, of making oneself a part of the deafening, living, breathing whole. To stand on the living plain is to be an ant on the back of a god, aware that every inch of the landscape is alive, and that the entire world may barely even register your presence upon its skin.
