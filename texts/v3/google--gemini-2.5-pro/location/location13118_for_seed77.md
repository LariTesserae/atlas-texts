The world is a floor. It extends in every direction, a pavement of biological make, meeting a hazy, white-hot sky at a horizon so distant it feels like a theorem. There are no mountains, only gentle, continent-sized undulations in the substrate itself. The elevation is universally low, a colossal basin curving with the planet.

This foundation, this living lithosphere, is not stone or soil. It is a continuous, secreted material, something between shell and ceramic, built by an organism so vast it constitutes the ground itself. Its color is a bone-white tinged with ochre and violet, like a bruise on ivory. The surface is warm to the touch, always, radiating a dry, steady heat that comes from the metabolic processes churning miles below. In the oldest, most stable regions, the surface is worn smooth, almost polished. But these areas are rare. Most of the landscape is new, raw, under construction.

Here, the "geology" is embryonic. Ridges, no taller than a person, are forming. They are not folded by tectonic pressure but are slow, linear swellings, the biocement accumulating in welts that might take a century to rise a few feet. These nascent ridges are rougher, their surfaces pocked with pores and hairline cracks where the material is still curing. They feel like un-sanded bone. Sometimes, a network of these cracks will meet and a plate, perhaps a mile across, will visibly shift, settling with a deep, sonorous groan that is felt more than heard, the only loud sound this world knows.

Heat is the engine of this place. The sun beats down from a sky that is rarely blue, but a shimmering, vapor-heavy white. The ground, in turn, bakes and breathes. The organism that forms the world transpires, not like a leaf, but like a feverish body. During the peak heat of the day, the air trembles with rising water vapor, a visible shimmer that distorts the already vast distances. The atmosphere becomes a planetary steam room.

This is where the water comes from. As the light begins to fail, or when a slight pressure change slides across the region, the air, saturated to its limit, can no longer hold the moisture. It doesn't rain from high clouds. Instead, a dense, ground-hugging fog precipitates directly from the air, and the world begins to weep. Dew, heavy as oil, slicks the ivory pavement. The air cools just enough for condensation to run in rivulets down the gentle slopes of the new ridges, collecting in shallow, temporary pools in the depressions. These pools are rarely more than a few inches deep, but they can be miles wide, mirroring the blank white sky. By mid-morning the next day, most will have steamed away, leaving behind a faint, salty residue.

Life exists here, but it is austere and scattered. There are no animals. Nothing scurries, slithers, or flies. The silence is profound, broken only by the whisper of condensing mist or the faint, high-frequency *tink* of the bioceramic contracting as it cools. Vegetation is sparse, and it does not seek soil because there is none. It grows directly from the living substrate, tapping into it.

Far apart, you might see the Sinter Spines. These are silica-based lifeforms, resembling massive, gnarled thorns of milky quartz growing in slow, spiraling clusters. They are cool to the touch, channeling the world's heat deep into their crystalline structures. They grow incrementally, adding a new translucent layer every cycle of condensation. Their roots are fine, glassy filaments that etch their way into the living pavement, drawing out minerals and the organism's metabolic water.

In the dampest hollows, where water lingers the longest, a different kind of life appears: the Ochre Mats. These are flat, leathery growths, biofilms of algae-like symbionts that spread across the substrate. They are the only vibrant color in the landscape, a deep, rust-red that turns almost black when wet. They pulse with a slow, barely perceptible rhythm, absorbing the intense light. When the pools they inhabit evaporate, the mats dry into a brittle, cracked sheet, waiting for the next condensation cycle.

The world is immensely active, but its pace is biological, not geological. To stand in one place for a week is to witness the landscape being born. A nascent fissure might widen by a finger's width. A new Sinter Spine, no bigger than a thumbnail, might emerge from the pavement. A depression might flood and dry out a dozen times. The weather is the exhalation of the ground. The terrain is the slow flex of a single, planetary-scale creature building its own body. There are rules here, but they are the rules of a metabolism. This is not a world of rock and water, but of flesh and breath, masquerading as a planet.
