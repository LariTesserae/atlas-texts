The world is a single, unbroken floor of fired clay.

It stretches across a basin so wide its edges are lost in a luminous haze, a valley floor constructed tile by tile. The ground is a mosaic of hexagons, octagons, and complex star-shapes, fitted together with impossible precision. This is the first rule here: nothing is natural. Every surface, every texture, from the ground beneath your feet to the pale, glowing ceiling that passes for a sky, is the work of a hand. A primitive hand, using simple tools, but a hand possessed by a dedication that borders on the absolute.

The air is hot and heavy, thick with water. It’s a greenhouse heat, pressing and constant, born from the ceaseless, shadowless light that radiates from the ceramic sky. There is no sun, no single point of origin, just a universal, brilliant glare that polishes the world to a high sheen. Sweat beads on your skin and refuses to evaporate, merging with the perpetual dampness of the atmosphere itself.

Water is everywhere, but it never moves. It is the second rule. Time, or at least the part of it that governs flow and change, is broken. The place is caught in a state of eternal, wet stillness. Some of the terracotta tiles are glazed and slick, capturing the overhead glare in bright, unblinking mirrors. On these, perfectly spherical droplets of water are frozen mid-slide, their potential energy locked away forever. Other tiles are porous and matte, dark with a moisture that has seeped into their very being. Along the bases of the larger structures, water seems to weep from the clay itself, a permanent sheen of condensation that never drips. You could get lost watching a single bead of water on the cusp of falling, a tiny, crystal-clear jewel of suspended physics.

Scattered across this immense, tiled plain are the “trees.” They are colossal sculptures of the same fired earth, fantastically complex and lush in their design, yet sparse in their placement. One grove consists of towering, blade-like structures that resemble cattails, each one fifty feet high, their surfaces ridged with a precision that suggests a comb was dragged through the wet clay just a moment ago. Miles away, a thicket of what looks like immense, coiled ferns dominates the horizon. Each frond is a marvel of detail, a fractal of smaller and smaller leaflets, every edge sharp and new, as if fired in the kiln only yesterday. Their colors are the natural pigments of the earth: sienna, ochre, umber, and a startling, chalky white. They are vegetation as architecture. This is the third rule: what looks like life is a monument.

The quiet is absolute. It is a dense, insulating silence, broken only by the sound of your own breathing and the soft, cushioned thud of your own heart. There is no wind to whisper, no water to babble, no leaves to rustle.

But the place is not empty of life. It is teeming with it. The fauna are abundant, but they are not made of flesh and bone. They are beings of warped light and condensed air, silent and translucent. They are the only things that move, and they move with a slow, liquid grace, unbound by the physical rules of the space. A herd of shimmering, deer-like creatures drifts through the tiled plain, their forms like heat haze on a summer road. As they reach one of the giant terracotta ferns, they do not walk around it; they pass through it, their forms momentarily distorted by the solid clay before reforming on the other side. They are like afterimages, ghosts from another reality layered atop this one. Flocks of luminous, bird-shaped apparitions wheel and glide high up near the ceramic ceiling, their silent motion a stark contrast to the utter stillness of the world they inhabit. They are everywhere and nowhere, their presence a constant, flickering reminder that the breakdown of reality here is profound and ongoing.

They are not the only fauna. If you look closely at the terracotta itself, you discover another kind of life, also frozen. The base of a pillar is a belt of exquisitely detailed insects, a fossilized swarm. A patch of wall is a bas-relief of lizards, their scales perfect, captured mid-scramble. A vast, whale-like form seems to be swimming through the very floor of the basin, the top of its barnacle-encrusted back a long, low island of sculpted clay in the middle of the plain. The entire world was made, and everything was made with it, captured in the final, glorious moment of its creation.

This is the final, and most perplexing, rule of the place: it is simultaneously brand new and infinitely old. The sharp edges of the sculptures, the lack of any erosion, the crispness of every detail—it all screams of recent birth. Yet its unchanging nature, the frozen water, the suspended physics, suggests it has been this way since the beginning of time and will remain so for the rest of it. You are an anomaly here. You are a dynamic presence in a static world, a fleeting noise in a universe of quiet. As you walk across the warm, damp tiles, you wonder if the makers of this place ever lived in it, or if its creation and its preservation as a perfect, silent, incandescent memory was the entire point.
