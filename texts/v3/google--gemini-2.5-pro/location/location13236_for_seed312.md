This is the city of Iskar, built in the throat of the world.

The terrain is a wound. A geologically recent upthrust has shattered the crust into a continent-sized splinter group of mountains, a wilderness of black and grey rock so young that the edges of every broken slab and toppled peak are still razor-sharp. There is no soil to soften these lines, only endless fields of scree and shattered flint that clatter underfoot. The scale is monumental; to stand on one of Iskar's high ledges is to see nothing but a sea of similar peaks, fading into the curve of the planet, their summits lost in the thin, cold haze of the upper atmosphere. This is a place that has its own gravity, its own weather.

The city itself is not built upon the mountains, but *of* them. Iskar is a dense, vertical metropolis of stacked and carved stone, clinging to the inner walls of a colossal, league-wide canyon. It is less a city of streets and more a three-dimensional warren of ramps, bone-railed ledges, and swaying bridges of hide and sinew. Construction is a slow, patient process, undertaken by generations. Using antler picks and basalt mauls, the people of Iskar flake away at the canyon walls, quarrying enormous slate slabs which are then levered into place to form the floors and ceilings of their cantilevered dwellings.

The city grows downwards, a cascade of stone and bone descending into the perpetual shadow of the canyon floor. From a distance, it looks like a colossal, petrified fungus growing on the rock. This verticality is amplified by its density; dwellings are packed near-touching, their outer walls forming a near-unbroken curtain of dark stone. The gaps between them are not alleyways but precipitous drops, navigated by rope or rickety ladder.

The dominant material besides stone is bone. The fauna here is abundant, primarily consisting of the Ghor, colossal, shaggy, six-limbed herbivores that graze on the high plateaus above the city. They are slow, placid mountains of muscle and hair, adapted to the thin air and extreme cold. The people of Iskar herd them, and no part of the Ghor is wasted. Their milk is drunk, their meat is the staple food, their thick, oily hides form walls, doors, and clothing. And their bones—massive, dense, and slow to decay in the cold—are the essential framework of the city's architecture. The ribcages of ancient Ghor form the arched roofs of great halls. Vertebrae are used as foundation stones. Femurs and tibias are driven into the rock face as structural supports for the precarious stone ledges, their bleached white forms a stark lattice against the black stone. A finished dwelling is a composite of mountain and animal.

The atmosphere is thin, cold, and damp. The temperature rarely rises above freezing. A perpetual mist, born from the breath of millions of people and their Ghor herds, clings to the lower levels of the city. Water does not flow in rivers, but seeps. The immense pressure and cold traps vast reservoirs of ice within the mountains themselves. The slight geothermal heat generated by the dense population causes the inner walls of the city to "sweat." Droplets of pure, cold water condense on the stone and bone, tracing dark paths down the walls. This condensation is the city's lifeblood. Every dwelling has a gutter system carved from horns or hollowed bones, channeling the precious drops into clay pots and hide cisterns. On the rare days when the sun is strong enough, the glaciers hanging precariously on the peaks far above will weep, sending trickles of meltwater down paths carved over centuries. This is a time of quiet celebration.

Vegetation is a treasure. On the sun-facing ledges, hardy, wire-stemmed mosses grow in cracks, their textures like stiff velvet. Ashen lichen coats the windward side of every stone. These are harvested and dried, used for insulation, tinder, and a bitter, vitamin-rich tea. Nothing more complex can survive the altitude and the cold.

Sounds are muted, swallowed by the sheer mass of stone and the damp air. The city is mostly quiet. There is the constant, low hum of the wind funnelling through the canyon, a sound so pervasive it is a form of silence. Close up, one can hear the gentle, rhythmic *chink-chink-chink* of a stone carver's hammer, the soft lowing of a Ghor in its stable-cavern, the scuff of a hide boot on a stone ramp, the drip of water into a cistern. There are no echoes here, only the deep, pervasive quiet of the high cold.

The rule of the world is slowness. The city expands at a glacial pace. A single family dwelling can take a decade to carve and secure. The cycles of life are tied to the slow gestation of the Ghor and the patient accumulation of water. The people themselves move with a deliberate economy of motion, conserving heat and energy in the thin air. They are wrapped in layers of felted Ghor-hair and dark hide, their faces often obscured by masks of carved bone to warm the air they breathe. Light is normal, but feels different here; it is a brittle, unfiltered light that casts knife-sharp shadows, making the sheer drops and dizzying verticality of the city all the more stark. This is a patient, enduring, and vertical world, a city built from the bones of its world and the patience of its people.
