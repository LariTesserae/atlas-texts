This is a plateau of impossible scale, a continent held aloft not on rock, but on the living architecture of the world-forest. The ground beneath your feet is not soil or stone, but a material like hardened, ancient wood, interwoven with living vascular systems. It feels solid, yet resilient. This is the skin of the Arch-Boles, colossal living structures that pierce the sky, their crowns lost in a high, dim haze, their bases lost in a cloud sea thousands of kilometers below. The space is defined by their presence and the immense emptiness between them. They are so vast and widely spaced that to journey from one to the next is a significant undertaking, a voyage across an open expanse of quiet air.

The air is warm and heavy with moisture, thick as a breath held too long. It is a perpetual twilight here, the light a dim, silver-grey filtering through a permanent, high-altitude vapor canopy. There is no rain in the conventional sense. Instead, the world weeps. The warm, saturated air condenses on the cooler, colossal surfaces of the Arch-Boles, and water beads on every surface. It gathers in rivulets that carve shallow, glistening paths down the living bark-wood, collecting in mirror-still pools in carved-out hollows before overflowing and tracing new, dark lines down the sheer vertical faces. This constant, gentle weeping is the world’s primary weather system, a process the inhabitants call the "Sweepless." The air smells of wet humus, ozone, and something like fermenting fruit and blooming fungus, all at once.

The sound is of silence. The vast emptiness between the boles swallows noise whole. There are no echoes. A shout dies a few meters from your lips, absorbed by the soft, damp air and the living substrate. The only persistent sounds are intimate and close: the soft, rhythmic *plink... plink...* of water dripping from a hanging leaf into a pool; the barely-audible chittering of unseen things moving within crevices of the bark-wood; the faint, sighing whisper of air currents navigating the titanic structures.

This is a world built, but not by machines or masons. It is a work of guided biology, of primitive tools and immense patience. The pathways are not paved but worn and carved into the living floor by generations of soft-soled feet. Handholds are scored into vertical faces, smoothed by a million touches. Bridges are not of wood and rope, but of living vines, coaxed over centuries to span the smaller chasms between outcroppings on a single Arch-Bole. Dwellings are hollows, natural or patiently scraped out, shielded by curtains of woven moss and tough, broad leaves. This is the pinnacle of a primitive technology that doesn't dominate nature, but whispers suggestions to it over geological time. The tools for this are shards of obsidian-like petrified sap, fibrous ropes, and the knowledge of which living tissues will grow, which will yield, and which will hold firm.

Fauna is abundant, but adapted to this vertical, spacious world. The dominant forms are gliders and climbers. Herds of six-limbed creatures, like flattened, leathery deer, browse on the phosphorescent fungi that grow in the shaded under-arches, their wide, splayed claws giving them purchase on the near-vertical surfaces. Great, silent predators, part-moth and part-mantis, with wings of membranous skin, drift on the air currents between the boles, their multifaceted eyes scanning the weeping walls for movement. Tiny, gem-colored amphibians live their entire lives in the water-filled cups of epiphytic flowers, their whole world a single, perfect bloom. The air is filled with motes, which on closer inspection are swarms of minuscule flying things, part-spore and part-insect, that glitter when they pass through a rare, weak shaft of light.

And here, reality itself feels as damp and pliable as the ground. The sheer scale and the muffling quietness work on the mind, but there is more. The geometry of the Arch-Boles is not always true. A path carved along a circumference might, inexplicably, lead you back to your own footsteps from an hour before, but approaching from the opposite direction. The emptiness between the boles is not just air. It is called the Quiet Fall. An object dropped over the edge does not plummet; it seems to slow, drifting with an unnatural languor, not accelerating, until it is consumed by the mists below, vanishing from sight long before it should. Some say things dropped do not land, but are un-made by the silence. Memory feels fluid here, prone to condensation and evaporation like the water in the air. You might find yourself holding a tool you don't remember picking up, or standing before a pool of water with no memory of the path you took to reach it. The world is stable, established over eons, but the perception of it is in constant, gentle flux. It is a place in a state of active, waking dream, where the landscape itself is the colossal, sleeping dreamer.
