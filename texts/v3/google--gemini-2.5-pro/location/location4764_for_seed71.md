The world is a plateau at the end of time. It is not flat, but it is smooth. Ancient mountains, once the size of continents, have been ground down by ages so deep they defy measure. They no longer have peaks, only swells. The landscape is a vast, frozen ocean of grey-and-black stone, its waves cresting miles high and leagues apart. An observer could stand on the curve of one such wave and see the next one rising on a horizon that is days away on foot. The scale is geological, but the texture is like bone, worn smooth by a final, cosmic polishing.

The air is thin, cold, and carries a clean, metallic taste. It is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is the high, lonely hiss of wind passing over the endless curves of stone. There are no birds to cry, no insects to buzz. The silence is a presence, broken only by the wind and by another, deeper sound, a hum that seems to come less through the air and more through the soles of the feet.

This is the hum of the vegetation. In the vast, shallow valleys between the great stone swells, life congregates. These are not forests as we know them, but groves of calciform flora. The plants are a deep, almost black-green, shot through with veins of viridian and cobalt that pulse with a slow, internal light. The leaves are thick, waxy, and cold to the touch, like jade. They grow in dense, tangled profusion, creating pockets of impossible lushness in the cold, high-altitude desert. Their strangeness is their survival. They do not draw their energy solely from the pale, distant sun. They tap into the world's fundamental instability. They resonate with it. This resonance is the hum, a vibration that generates a localized warmth. Within the groves, the air is still and several degrees warmer than on the open stone. Frost etches the edges of the leaves, but their cores remain metabolically active, humming with a borrowed energy that defies thermodynamics. This process sweats out a steady, slow moisture. The air inside a grove is heavy with condensation, and water drips constantly from the broad, waxy leaves onto a floor of rich, black soil and mosses that glow with a faint phosphorescence. These groves are the world's oases, its pockets of dampness and life.

From the crest of a stone wave, you can see one of the world's other anomalies. It appears first as a geometric discoloration on the curve of a neighboring swell, a place where the smooth grey is interrupted by pattern. This is the city, Amun-Aur.

It is a settlement built over millennia, a layered artifact of a culture attuned to stone and silence. It is not built *on* the swell, but *within* it. The outermost structures are megalithic, vast blocks of the native stone quarried and fitted together with a precision that suggests an immense investment of time. The architecture is low and angular, designed to shed the constant wind. There are no soaring towers; the city hugs the curve of the world. Deeper in, the city becomes a network of tunnels, galleries, and chambers carved directly from the living rock of the mountain-swell. It is an urbanism of subtraction as much as addition.

The technology is primitive, yet profoundly sophisticated. There are no machines, no metal alloys beyond simple, hammered copper used for fittings and decorative clasps. The tools are of obsidian and polished stone. Yet the inhabitants have achieved incredible feats. They have carved channels, miles long, that guide the condensation runoff from the resonant groves into vast, echoing cisterns deep within the city. The floors of the main galleries are polished to a perfect, glassy black, reflecting the dim light that filters through shafts carved to the surface. Everything is done by hand, and the evidence of work is everywhere: the slow, patient sanding of a wall, the gradual deepening of a channel. Time is their greatest tool.

The "weirdness" of this place is its engine. The breakdown of physical law is not a cataclysm; it is a resource. In certain zones, causality is soft. You might see a stonecutter's hammer strike a block, but the sharp *crack* arrives a full second before the impact. Motes of dust, caught in a shaft of light, will hang motionless for hours before suddenly spiraling upwards, defying gravity. The inhabitants understand this. They have learned to live within the inconsistencies. Their city is aligned along unseen lines of force, its passages twisting to avoid zones of temporal shear or gravitational anomaly. The hum of the deep flora is piped through the city in resonant chambers, not as music, but as a utility—a source of low, constant warmth.

There are no animals, but the city is not empty. The dynamic is slow, deliberate, and constant. From a distance, you might hear it: a rhythmic, patterned *chink... chink... chink...* from a quarry, a sound that might not repeat for a full minute. Or the deep, resonant *scrape* of a massive stone block being slid into place, a process that might take a generation to complete. The inhabitants are present in their works. Their pace of life is geological. They are a people as patient and as ancient as the eroded world they inhabit.

The atmosphere is one of profound, active stillness. It is a world in a late, stable state of decay, but a decay that has become a foundation for a different kind of existence. Standing on a high gallery of Amun-Aur, overlooking a valley of humming, glowing trees, the feeling is not of an ending, but of a deep, abiding, and utterly alien present. The wind hisses, the cold bites, and from the heart of the stone beneath your feet, you feel the faint, rhythmic pulse of a chisel striking rock, a sound that has echoed here for ten thousand years and will echo for ten thousand more. The world is active, but it is the activity of glaciers, of growing crystals, of a reality slowly, and purposefully, coming undone.
