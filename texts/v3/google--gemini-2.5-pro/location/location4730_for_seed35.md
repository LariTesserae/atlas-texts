This is the Chamber of Stasis.

The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not an event, but a property of the space, a texture of the medium you move through. It is a deep, foundational hum that vibrates in the bones of your jaw and the base of your skull. Layered over this drone is the ceaseless, complex clatter of a billion moving parts, the sharp, dry clicks of meshing gears, and the rhythmic, asthmatic sigh of immense pneumatic presses. It is the sound of a factory that has never known a shift change, running at full capacity for an age. The noise is so constant and complex that it resolves into a form of silence, a wall of energy that cancels out thought.

You are underground, in a single, vast geode. The scale is that of a great cathedral, but it feels claustrophobic, packed to the brim. There is no empty space. The terrain is a forest of crystalline structures, angular and lethally sharp. These are not the slow-grown, weathered crystals of a water-rich world; they are freshly-broken, born of pressure and fracture. They look like shattered panes of smoked quartz, obsidian, and dull, leaden salt, layered in impossible, self-intersecting planes. The air, if it can be called that, is thick and warm, a dry, palpable medium saturated with a fine, metallic dust that coats every surface in a uniform grey film. There is no water, none at all. Breathing here is an act of filtration.

Light is a scarce commodity. The cavern is dim, lit by the faint, sourceless luminescence of the crystals themselves—a phenomenon of stress and friction—and punctuated by weak, grimy yellow bulbs strung along the colossal machinery that dominates one hemisphere of the chamber. These lights do not illuminate so much as create pockets of slightly less-intense gloom, their glow smeared and diffused by the particulate haze.

The chamber is dense with life, an ecosystem thriving in utter defiance of biology as we know it. The vegetation is sparse, but present. They are not plants, but 'auraliths'—sessile, crystalline stalks that grow in patches where the ambient mechanical sound is most harmonically complex. They seem to feed on vibration, slowly extruding new, razor-edged fingers of silicon and metal that resonate with the Engine's drone. They are a dull, coppery color, and to brush against one is to feel an unnerving, high-frequency buzz pass through you.

Far more abundant is the fauna. The space teems with them. They are arthropodal things, the size of a human hand, with carapaces of the same mineral dullness as the landscape. Let us call them Geodes. Their legs are metallic, tipped with carbide points that produce a constant, high-pitched *skitter-scrape* as they move across the crystal surfaces. They are everywhere: clinging to the walls in dense, tessellated carpets; marching in single-file lines along crystalline ledges; swarming over the inert parts of the vast machine. They do not seem to eat the auraliths. Instead, they scrape at the crystal flats, consuming the new, raw material exposed by fresh fractures, their complex mouthparts grinding the mineral into sustenance. Their chitinous bodies click against each other, adding another layer to the wall of sound—a million tiny castanets in a metal drum.

This world is not natural. Scattered throughout the crystal jungle are the faint marks of industry. A rusted iron gangway leads from one crystal outcrop to another before terminating in empty space. A bank of gauges sits under a permanent shroud of grey dust, their needles frozen at readings that have been meaningless for millennia. On the side of a massive pillar of salt is a stenciled serial number, faded and half-obscured by the ceaseless traffic of the Geodes. These are the scattered remains and peripheral components of the chamber's heart: the Engine.

The Engine is not so much a machine as it is a geological process given mechanical form. It is an industrial behemoth of pistons, gears, crushers, and conveyors, all entangled with the native crystal of the geode as if it grew there. It is the source of the heat, the sound, and the dust. Its function is a perfect, pointless loop. A series of immense grinders at its base chew on the crystal floor, breaking it down. This activity creates fractures throughout the chamber, exposing fresh mineral for the Geodes. The pulverized rock is conveyed upwards, where it is heated and atomized into the metallic dust that fills the air. This dust then slowly settles, re-crystallizing over eons, forming new crystal structures for the Engine to eventually consume again. The auraliths feed on the Engine's song, the Geodes feed on its refuse, and the Engine feeds on the world it, in turn, creates.

And it never, ever changes. This is the great weirdness of the place. Despite the incredible noise and the swarm of life, the chamber is in a state of absolute equilibrium. The amount of dust in the air is constant. The temperature does not fluctuate by a thousandth of a degree. The population of the Geodes is fixed; they live, breed, and die at a rate that results in no net change. For every new auralith that sprouts, an old one becomes inert and is eventually buried by the slow crystal accretion. The Engine's rhythm is immutable. No part ever fails, no gear ever strips. It is a system that reached a perfect, steady state long ago and became locked in it.

The sound is not the sound of progress or production. It is the sound of stasis. The activity is not life, but the simulation of it. Time has no meaning here. An hour is the same as a thousand years. The chamber is a living fossil, a clockwork ecosystem sealed in a tomb, furiously and loudly going nowhere forever.
