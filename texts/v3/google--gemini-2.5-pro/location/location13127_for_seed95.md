This is a high place, a world worn down to its bones. The mountains are ghosts of their former selves, ancient beyond measure. Their peaks are not sharp but rounded, slumped like tired shoulders. Their slopes are not scree and cliff, but vast, flowing curves of stone that have been smoothed for eons, as if polished by a cosmic thumb. The material of the land isn't granite or slate; it's a compacted, impossibly old dust, something between stone and clay, in shades of ochre, plum, and a dusty, oxidized green. The erosion here has finished its work; this is the final, stable state of the world, and it has been this way for longer than the concept of a year has had meaning.

The air is warm and carries a significant weight, a humidity that doesn't come from rain. Rain is a rare, brief event here, a soft misting that barely wets the surface before the warmth draws it back into the sky. The primary source of moisture is condensation. As the day's soft, normal light gives way to the cool of the long, slow night, every surface grows a fine fleece of dew. The world weeps quietly to itself. In the endless shallow concavities of the land, this condensation gathers, forming ephemeral, tea-colored pools that will be gone by noon, leaving behind a faint, dark stain.

From a distance, the landscape seems barren, a sweeping vista of rolling, earth-toned hills under a wide-open sky. The scale is regional; you can see the curve of an entire valley system, the way a dozen soft ridges descend to a central basin miles across. But this openness is a lie. As you draw closer, the texture of the world resolves, and the initial impression of emptiness is revealed as an illusion of scale.

The ground is not bare stone. It is alive.

It is covered, nearly everywhere, by a single, continuous mat of life. A living carpet, inches deep, that is composed of billions upon billions of overlapping, chitinous bodies. They are creatures, something like trilobites or armoured woodlice, each the size of a thumbnail. They are packed so densely that they form the very substrate of the world, a slow-motion river of life that has become the land itself. Their colours match the ancient stone beneath them—ochre, plum, dusty green—and they are almost entirely still. This is the source of the world’s quiet. There are no calls, no chirps, no roars. The only sound is a faint, aggregate *hiss* if you press your ear to the ground, the sound of a billion infinitesimal shifts, of spiracles breathing, of digestive tracts working in near-unison. A dry, rustling whisper that is more a feeling in the bones than a sound in the ear.

This overwhelming faunal density paradoxically creates the sparseness of the vegetation. Few plants can find purchase in this living, shifting ground. Those that do are bizarre specialists. Pale, waxy stalks, like bleached asparagus, rise in scattered clusters. They have no leaves. They are hollow, and they grow not from soil, but from the husks of the dead carpet-creatures, drawing nutrients from their slow decay. They are a kind of fungal-plant hybrid, and they are the only green—a pale, sickly chartreuse—in this world of earth and shell.

Scattered across the landscape, visible from miles away, are what might be mistaken for buildings or rock formations. They are neither. They are structures, yes, but they are grown. They are the Still-Hives. Perhaps once every few square miles, the carpet-creatures have, over millennia, deviated from their horizontal instinct. They have piled upwards, fusing together, their secretions cementing them into a single, porous, coral-like mass. These structures stand twenty or thirty feet high, shaped like gourds or wasp nests writ large. They are hollow, their insides a complex lattice of chambers and tunnels. They are functionally dead on the outside, the creatures petrified and fused, but a slow, metabolic heat emanates from within. They are ancient, and no new ones seem to be forming. They are monuments from a more dynamic age, now serving only as wind-harps. When the slow breezes move across the plains, they pass through the porous walls of the Still-Hives, creating a single, low, resonant hum. It is a rare sound, a soft, mournful note that hangs in the warm, damp air for a minute before fading back into the profound, living silence.

The rules of this world are patience and density. Life has become so successful, so utterly dominant, that it has reached a state of near-geological stasis. There are no predators or prey in the conventional sense, only the slow, communal cycle of life, death, and nutrient reabsorption. Change is measured by the speed at which a shell weathers or a fungal stalk decays. The air is thick not just with water, but with spores, with the faint, ozonic scent of chitin, with the smell of damp dust and immense, quiet life. To be here is to feel the weight of time not as a force of destruction, but as a medium of preservation, a thick amber that has captured a world at the peak of its strangeness and held it, breathing slowly, for eternity.
