The air is a solid thing, hot and heavy as wet wool. It doesn't move, but it has weight, pressing down with the heat of a kiln just opened. The light is the colour of old tea, a dim, uniform amber that seeps from the low, featureless sky-ceiling, offering no sun, no shadows, only a pervasive, gloomy saturation. You are in a basin, a depression of continental scale, where the horizon is a mathematical concept rather than a visible line.

The ground underfoot is not rock or soil. It is a vast, uneven pavement of roughly-formed ceramic. The shapes are vaguely geometric—hexagons, lozenges, and warped squares of ochre, brick-red, and charcoal grey. They are not laid, but *formed* in place, their edges still soft-looking, fused together with glassy, dark seams. The entire world is made of this. It is a planet-kiln, a single, boundless act of pottery.

This is a world under construction, by a hand of impossible scale and primitive intent. Nothing here is eroded; it is all emerging. Across the sparse plains of tile, new forms bubble up from the substrate. A low wall, a yard high, will begin to extrude from the floor, not as a stack of bricks but as a continuous, groaning upwelling of material. It rises silently, its surface glistening wet, then sizzling as the internal heat bakes it dry from the inside out. Fine cracks appear on its surface with faint *ticking* sounds. It is a wall for no reason, enclosing nothing, beginning and ending arbitrarily on the plain. After a few hundred yards, the force behind it may falter, and the wall will sag back into the floor, leaving a scar of molten, re-absorbed ceramic.

Water is the lubricant of this creation. It does not rain. Instead, the thick atmosphere condenses on every surface, and the world weeps. Water runs in slick, warm rivulets down the sides of a nascent archway. It pools in the depressions of the imperfect tiles, not clear, but a viscous, mineral-rich slurry—the architectural slip, the raw material for whatever comes next. Stepping in it is like stepping in warm, liquid clay. In some places, these puddles are larger, simmering with a low geothermal heat. Bubbles of sour-smelling gas pop on their surface. Sometimes, the edge of such a pool will begin to glow, the slurry will vitrify, and a new tile will be born, adding its small acreage to the floor of the world.

There is no life here. No plants, no animals. The ecological niches are filled with raw geology and broken physics. In the hairline cracks of the cooling ceramics, where moisture gathers, a "vitreous moss" grows. It is not biological. It is a slow crystalline efflorescence, a frost of silica fibres that branch out in exquisite, fractal patterns, crunching like sugar glass if disturbed. It is the world's only gesture toward delicacy.

The weirdness is not an accent; it is the grammar of the place. You may see a flight of stairs rising from the tiles, each step perfectly formed, spiraling up into the soupy air before stopping abruptly, twenty feet from the ground, a path to nowhere. The logic is that of a blueprint being imperfectly remembered, of a dream of 'building' without an understanding of 'structure.' Gravity here is a strong suggestion, but not a law. A newly formed pillar might lean at an impossible angle for days before slowly, majestically, toppling over with a sound like a mountain of pottery shattering. But the sound doesn't travel far in the dense air; it's a dull, localized *crump*, quickly muffled, the shards already beginning to soften and melt at the edges, being reclaimed by the floor.

The instability is constant but quiet. This is not a world of explosions and earthquakes, but of turbulent, churning creation. It's the chaos of a pot on a wheel, spinning too fast, the clay slumping and rising in unpredictable waves. You have the sense that the entire landscape is a single, semi-liquid body beneath a thin, brittle crust. A low, resonant hum is the only constant sound, the vibration of the planetary engine of fabrication that drives all this. It is felt more than heard, a thrumming in the bones.

Everything is made, but it is all made by hand. Look closely at the side of a half-formed buttress, and you might see a fingerprint, a mile wide. You might see the drag-marks of a tool so vast its use is incomprehensible, a simple gouge scaled up to the size of a canyon. The tech is primitive—simple rules, simple forms. Arch. Wall. Floor. Pillar. Stair. But the application of these rules is total, obsessive, and deranged. It is a world built by a god who is a child, or a madman, armed only with clay and fire, and all the time in the universe. It is a place forever on the verge of becoming, but never arriving.
