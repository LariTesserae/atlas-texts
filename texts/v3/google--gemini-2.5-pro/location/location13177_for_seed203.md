This is the Undervault, a hollow space the size of a small continent, deep beneath a crust of stone and ice. There is no geology here, no rock or soil or scree. The world is made of a single, living thing.

You are not on the ground, for there is no ground. You are in the canopy of a tree that has grown to fill a world, a plant that is its own ecosystem. The "terrain" is a lattice of living and dead-but-not-decayed matter. You might stand on a bough as wide as a glacial valley, its bark a mosaic of gray-green plates, cool and slightly slick to the touch. From its edges, smaller branches fork and weave, some thick as ancient oaks, others as delicate as nerve-filaments. The structure is impossibly vast and yet profoundly empty. Between the major limbs are chasms of open air miles across. The world is a scaffold, not a solid.

Light is everywhere. It is a brilliant, cold, and silent illumination that comes from the plant itself. The youngest growths, the tender shoots and unfurling leaves the size of ships' sails, glow with a piercing blue-white intensity. Older, more established trunks and branches emanate a softer, steadier green. The very oldest, dormant parts of the structure are dark, but they are lit by the glow of the life around them. The light is not static. It is turbulent. Vast, slow pulses of luminescence travel through the network of branches, a visible representation of nutrient flow or some incomprehensible thought process. These are the weather systems of the Undervault: silent storms of light that race across the gulfs, casting colossal, shapeshifting shadows that take minutes to pass. One moment your perch is bathed in the stark blue of a new growth surge; the next, it is plunged into the soft, deep-sea green of a dormant phase, the abyssal void between branches now filled with a shimmering, ethereal aurora.

The air is thin, cold, and damp. There is no wind, only slow, massive currents of air that drift through the great voids as the plant's metabolism gently heats and cools different regions. Moisture, transpired by the quadrillions of leaves, hangs as a constant, fine mist. It condenses on every surface, forming a perpetual dew. Droplets gather, swell, and fall. The sound is one of the very few that exists here: a soft, intermittent dripping that is both everywhere and nowhere, a sound that only accentuates the profound quiet. Fall far enough, and the mist thickens into a fog that obscures the lower, older regions of the plant, a place from which no light emanates.

This world is young, but not in a geological sense. The plant, perhaps named Yggr by some forgotten tongue, is ancient, but its substance is in constant, violent flux. This is the source of its turbulence. A new primary branch may erupt from a trunk over the course of a week, a spear of pale, glowing material hundreds of miles long, sharp and clean-edged, a feature of fresh creation. It is a form of gentle violence; it does not shatter, it simply *grows through*. Elsewhere, a section of the plant may die. A vast, intricate network of boughs and catwalks, having exhausted its purpose, will darken, stiffen, and with a series of deep, groaning cracks that echo for an hour, detach. It does not plummet. It drifts, tumbling in ultra-slow motion down into the misty abyss, a falling continent of dead wood that will journey for centuries before reaching the unknowable bottom.

Fauna is exceedingly rare. For days, you might see nothing but the plant. Then, a flicker of motion. Small, six-winged creatures like moths made of frozen moonlight, called Glimmer-wings, flit between glowing leaves, feeding on the light or the pollens that drift in the air. They are silent, their wings making no sound. Larger things are the stuff of legend, but might exist: grazers the size of hills that slowly scrape sustenance from the bark, their movements so slow as to be imperceptible from a distance. The ecosystem is almost entirely self-contained within the plant.

And yet, there are others. Here and there, faint marks suggest passage. A series of notches carved into the side of a branch-path, a form of trail marker. In a sheltered confluence of smaller boughs, you might find a dwelling. It is not a building of stone or wood, but a pod, woven from the flexible, shed filaments of the Yggr's own structure. These nests are small, holding perhaps a dozen individuals. They are open to the glowing air, sheltered only from the constant, gentle drip of condensation. Inside, you might find a simple tool: a knife flaked from a piece of petrified, obsidian-like sap. Or a long, hooked pole for harvesting a particular type of glowing fungus that grows in the branch-axils. These artifacts are primitive, born of necessity and deep integration. The makers are gone, or perhaps they have simply moved on, following a new growth-surge across a chasm, using broad, stiff leaves as gliders to ride the slow thermal currents. They do not impose on the world; they are simply another, rarer form of life held within the arms of the great plant, utterly dependent on its quiet, luminous, and terrifyingly dynamic existence.
