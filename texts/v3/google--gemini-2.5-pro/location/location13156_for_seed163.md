The noise is the first thing, the only thing. It is not a collection of sounds but a single, solid object pressed against the world. It’s a high-frequency, pressurized roar, the sound of a universe-sized gas jet, and it is so constant and total that it has become the texture of silence. Beneath it, buried deep in the crushing weight of its main frequency, are other sounds: the resonant, bone-deep thrum of massive rotary pumps and the metallic scream of overburdened gears. But these are just harmonics of the great, singular roar. The sound is a physical presence that flattens thought and scours the air clean.

The light is the second thing. It is absolute. There are no shadows here. The sky is not blue, but a brilliant, searing, milk-white sheet, an infinite bank of fog lit from within by a sun you can never locate. This light diffuses through the saturated air, reflecting and re-reflecting until every surface is lit from every direction at once. It strips the world of depth, making distance impossible to judge. A hill a mile away might as well be a lump ten feet in front of you. The glare is immense, a cool, wet, white fire.

You stand on a plateau of pure life. There is no soil, no rock, no sand. The ground is a single, contiguous organism, a planetary-scale mat of what might be called plant matter. It is a living substrate, a geoflora. Underfoot, it is cool, spongy, and firm, like cork soaked in water. Its surface is a uniform, pale green-grey, patterned with the faint, repeating texture of its interwoven cellular structure. Water does not run over this landscape; it is inseparable from it. The ground is saturated, and with every step, a small pool of crystal-clear, unnaturally cold water wells up around your boot before being slowly reabsorbed. The air is so full of moisture that it condenses on every surface, running in slow, thick rivulets down pipes and sheathing the living ground in a perpetual, glistening slick.

This place is new. The landforms are not the product of erosion's patient artistry but of slow, tumescent growth. The terrain swells into low, rounded hills, their curves too perfect, too blunt. They look like bubbles rising in a thick liquid, frozen mid-motion. There are no sharp peaks, no jagged canyons, only these great, lumpy lobes of living matter, slowly expanding over centuries. This is a landscape still under construction, its geology being grown, not carved. The process is ponderously slow, a change measured in millimeters per year, yet the sheer scale makes it feel immense and inexorable.

And scattered across this vast, shadowless, empty expanse are the sources of the noise: the Regulators.

They are industrial towers, stark and utilitarian, spaced miles apart. Each one rises from the living substrate like a steel hypodermic needle. They are perhaps two hundred feet tall, skeletal frameworks of rust-red metal, slick with weeping condensation and filmed with a thin veneer of green algal growth. A complex of pipes, some as thick as a person, radiates from the base of each tower, plunging diagonally into the pale ground. These pipes thrum with a palpable, low-frequency vibration that you feel in your teeth and sternum.

Halfway up each tower is a blocky, windowless machine house, from which the deafening roar of escaping gas shrieks through colossal vents. Plumes of colorless, shimmering vapor—superheated steam or some other pressurized gas—jet sideways into the glaring sky, dissolving instantly into the ambient humidity. These are engines of creation. They are tapping something deep within the planet's mantle, or perhaps they are processing the very air, pumping a high-energy nutrient slurry into the geoflora’s vascular system. They are the terraforming machines, forcing the slow, cool metabolism of the landscape, driving its relentless, embryonic growth.

There is no other life here. No insects drone in the humid air, no birds wheel in the white sky, no creatures burrow in the living earth. The ecology is brutally simple: the substrate and the Regulators that feed it. The air smells of ozone, hot metal, and the clean, green scent of crushed vegetation, a sterile and industrial perfume.

Walking from one Regulator to the next might take a day. The journey is a sensory deprivation exercise, despite the overwhelming inputs. The constant sound and light erase the passage of time. The featureless, rolling ground offers no landmarks, save for the next Regulator, which appears as a faint, blurry smudge in the glare for hours before resolving into its hard, metallic form.

There are no paths. There is no need. The living ground heals any track within minutes, the water welling up and the fibers knitting back together. The world exists in a state of patient, powered creation. It is a factory floor the size of a country, manufacturing one single product: more of itself. The deafening sound is the song of its assembly line, a roar of birth at a geological tempo. And in the piercing white light, under the pressure of that terrible noise, the new world slowly, calmly, takes shape.
