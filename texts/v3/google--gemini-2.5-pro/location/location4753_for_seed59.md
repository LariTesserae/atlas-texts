This place is a basin, a hollow, a single cupped thought in a world of high peaks. Its formal name, if it ever had one, is forgotten. The inhabitants, and there are many, call it The Weft. The air itself is the first wall you meet—cool, heavy, and tasting of wet stone, crushed leaves, and the metallic zing of ozone from some unseen discharge. It is saturated, not so much raining as constantly condensing. Every surface is beaded with moisture, running with it, weeping it. To be dry here is to be dying.

The Weft is a city grown, not built. The architecture is vegetation, total and absolute. What might have once been a valley floor of soil and rock is now a substrate, a deep and ancient humus from which rise the foundational pillars of the city: colossal, woody growths that are not quite trees. Their bark is the colour of thunderclouds, gnarled and fused together over centuries intoload-bearing walls and buttresses. At this lower, foundational level, the growth is petrified in its purpose, ancient and unmoving. This is the "established" part of the world, bark as thick as a man's torso, ridged and furrowed by ages of slow, guided growth and the ceaseless flow of water. These are the bones of the place.

But upon these bones, life is a frantic, turbulent explosion. The upper levels are a riot of secondary and tertiary growth, a dense, near-impenetrable lattice of living matter. Vines as thick as pythons weave between the main pillars, forming suspended walkways slick with moss. Broad, waxy leaves, each the size of a small boat, overlap to create roofs and awnings, their surfaces perpetually collecting and channeling water into carefully coaxed grooves in the living wood below. These grooves are the city's arteries, streams of clear, cool water flowing down the walls, splashing into bowl-like fungi, and irrigating the countless smaller plants that cling to every available surface. The tech here is biological and manual: the careful pruning of a shoot to direct its growth, the weaving of pliant stems into a screen, the grafting of a fruit-bearing limb onto a structural branch. The tools are simple—obsidian knives for cutting, smoothed stones for grinding, and bone hooks for pulling and guiding.

The space is dense, layered, almost claustrophobic in its vitality. Light struggles to pierce the multiple canopies, arriving on the "ground" level as a diffuse, emerald-green twilight, constantly shifting as the foliage above sways and trembles. Higher up, you might find a clearing, a "plaza" where a canopy has been deliberately kept open to the sky, and here the light is brighter, catching the spray and mist in a billion tiny rainbows. But mostly, it is a world of green shadow and dappled, dancing light.

And it is overwhelmingly loud. The sound is a physical presence, a constant, multi-layered chorus of life. There is the endless sound of water: the drip from a million leaves, the gurgle of the channeled streams, the soft splash into pools. But this is just the baseline. Layered over it is the sound of fauna, a chaotic and deafening symphony. Huge, beetle-like creatures with iridescent carapaces scrape and click their way along the wooden walls, their mandibles strong enough to chew through softer growth. Flocks of what might be birds, or feathered reptiles, or something else entirely, swarm through the upper canopies in tight, chattering formations, their calls sharp and percussive. In the shadows and hollows reside slick-furred climbers, their calls a series of deep, resonant hoots that echo through the wooden chambers. The air is thick with buzzing, humming insects of every shape and size. There is no silence in The Weft, not even for a moment. It is the sound of an ecosystem at full boil.

This is the central tension of the place: the ancient, stable foundation and the hyperactive, chaotic present. The deep structure of The Weft has been established for millennia, a testament to slow, patient cultivation. Yet the surface is a maelstrom. Walkways of fresh, green vine might be grown in a week, only to be devoured by a swarm of the scraping beetles a month later, requiring the inhabitants to weave a new path. A fruiting fungus might erupt overnight, drawing a chaotic frenzy of feeding creatures, only to wither just as quickly. The "streets" and "rooms" are in constant flux. The city is a living organism, and it has the metabolic rate of a hummingbird. The builders, whoever they are, are not masters of this place, but frantic gardeners, constantly tending, repairing, and reacting to the explosive vitality that threatens to consume their creation at every moment. They live in a state of perpetual, managed crisis, a partnership with a force of nature so powerful it doubles as their home and their greatest adversary.
