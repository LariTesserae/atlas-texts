The first thing you register is not a sight but a sound, and not a sound but a pressure. It is a hum, a thrumming so deep and pervasive it feels like a property of the air itself, a texture of reality in this place. It is the sound of a billion tons of water being moved through capillaries the size of cathedrals, the sound of life operating at a geological scale. It is constant, unchanging, a single, unwavering chord that vibrates in your bones and behind your eyes. It is the Great Hum, and it is the only true voice of this world.

You stand on a wide, open expanse, a plaza or a plain that stretches for miles. The ground beneath your feet is not rock, nor soil, nor sand. It is a living substance, a kind of biological pavement. It feels cool and damp, slightly yielding like hard rubber or old cartilage, and its color is a pale, milky jade. From horizon to horizon, everything is made of this same material: the floor, the strange structures that rise from it, the very air that hangs thick and wet around you. This is a world grown, not formed. It is entirely, utterly built, but the architect was metabolism, the tool was life itself.

The landscape is open, the density of features sparse. Here and there, interrupting the vastness of the living floor, are growths. They are not trees or mountains, but something in between, structures that defy easy categorization. Vast, sweeping arches curve kilometers into the sky, their surfaces slick and weeping, shedding a constant, slow drizzle that is not rain but cellular exudate. Towers shaped like swelling calyxes, half-a-mile wide at their base, rise with a soft, upward momentum, their lips curled back as if perpetually on the verge of blooming. They look unfinished, embryonic. There are no sharp edges here, no facets carved by wind or water erosion. Every form is smooth, rounded, pillowy, as if sculpted from wet clay and then flash-frozen mid-gesture. This landscape is nascent, perpetually caught in the first moment of its own creation, a state of becoming that never resolves. This raw, un-eroded newness is its permanent condition.

The elevation is considerable. The air is thin in one sense, yet heavy and thick with moisture in another. The temperature is uniformly warm, like the inside of a greenhouse. The sky above is a normal, pale blue, with a single, familiar sun, but its light seems to struggle through the saturated atmosphere, arriving diffused and soft. It gives the jade-white surfaces of the bio-architecture a pearlescent, internal glow. There are no shadows, only gradients of luminosity.

Water is everywhere, but there are no rivers, no lakes. It is a world of seeping, weeping, and condensing. The air is so humid that your breath hangs visible not from cold, but from its own moisture failing to be absorbed by the saturated atmosphere. Water beads on every surface, forming shivering lenses that magnify the fibrous, cellular texture of the ground. The gigantic arches and towers constantly cry, their surfaces streaked with darker, translucent lines where the internal fluids weep out and run down their flanks. This water pools in shallow, perfectly circular depressions in the living floor, vast, still mirrors reflecting the soft-lit sky and the impossible growths. The pools are always full to the very brim, yet they never overflow. The weeping is constant, yet the water level never rises. It is a perfect, closed system in a state of frozen equilibrium.

This is a pure-plant ecosystem, so total that the word "plant" feels inadequate. There is no other kingdom of life present in any significant way. There are no insects droning, no birds calling, no animals rustling in any undergrowth—because there is no undergrowth. There is only the One Thing, the great Thallus, this planet-spanning organism that has built itself into a sparse city of flesh. You might, if you look closely at the edges of the weeping pools, see a shimmering patina of symbiotic cyanobacteria, but that is all. The silence of this ecological solitude is made all the more profound by the overwhelming, unchanging roar of the Great Hum. It is the sound of a body without a voice, a life without events.

The rules here are biological, not physical. Gravity exists, but the growths defy it with the patient insistence of life. Time passes, marked by the slow crawl of the sun, but it brings no change. The world is a photograph of a splash, a sculpture of a breath. To be here is to be an alien microbe on the skin of a single, world-sized being, a presence so insignificant that you are not even registered. You walk across its skin, you breathe its exhalations, you are deafened by its silent, internal processes, and you are utterly, completely alone.
