The world is a slow, warm, and total sea. There is no surface, no sky, only the dense, amniotic press of water from all directions. The pressure is a constant, a solid embrace that mutes all sensation, a presence as fundamental as the darkness. And it is overwhelmingly dark, a thick and final blackness that light has never known. But not an empty blackness.

Out of the gloom, structures resolve. They are not built, but grown. This is a city of biological origin, an urban sprawl secreted over millennia. Vast, porous spires of a material like pearl-black nacre rise from the silty abyssal plain. They are not solid but are riddled with a trillion holes, tunnels, and galleries, like colossal termite mounds sculpted by water. Time and the gentle, persistent currents have smoothed every edge, giving the entire metropolis the worn, settled feel of ancient river stones. These are the towers and tenements of a civilization with no tools.

The spires, some as wide as hills, are interconnected by sweeping, delicate-looking bridges of the same living substance, arching over dark plazas and silent avenues. The entire landscape, stretching for miles in every direction, is a single, contiguous architectural organism. Visibility is limited to a few hundred yards by the suspended motes in the water, so the city appears in sections, its further reaches fading into a deeper shade of black, a suggestion of infinite continuation.

The only light is life. A cold, biological fire pulses dimly through the city's framework. Veins of faint cyan luminescence thread through the nacrous walls, marking major arteries where the city’s builders, slow and immense, are still patiently extending the architecture. They are rarely seen, their work a geological process. Their presence is felt mainly in the faint, rhythmic thrum that resonates through the water, a vibration too low to be heard but felt in the bones, the slow heartbeat of the metropolis itself.

This faint, structural glow is augmented by the inhabitants. The density here is not of buildings, but of beings. The city is saturated with fauna. Schools of thumbnail-sized, silvery creatures, like living mercury, drift in coordinated clouds through the grand archways, their collective movements governed by invisible signals. They flash with synchronized sparks of green light—a warning, a signal, a word in a silent language—before vanishing into a porous wall.

Larger forms glide through the boulevards between the spires. Some are flat and broad, patterned with shifting violet chromatophores that mimic the faint light-play on the silt below, hunting for the grazers that scour the bacterial mats. These mats, a fine, rust-colored fuzz, are the base of the food chain, thriving on the mineral-rich warmth seeping up from the planet's core through the seafloor. The entire ecosystem is untethered from sunlight, powered by geothermal breath.

It is overwhelmingly quiet. The water swallows sound. There are no voices, no clangor of industry. The only noises are small and immediate: the dry *click* of a segmented leg on a chitinous surface; the soft, wet rasp of a grazer's mouthparts against a bacterial film; the barely-audible *whoosh* of displaced water as a predator, all sinew and shadow, lunges from its hiding place within a wall cavity. The air, which is water, carries smells more than sounds. Trails of copper-scented alarm and sweet, fatty lures of mating drift in the currents, a constant, silent conversation that paints a picture of action and intent in the darkness.

This is a world in dynamic balance. The Weavers, the architect-organisms, excrete their pearlescent cement, slowly raising the spires. The grazers and browsers keep the bacterial films in check. The hunters cull the browsers. And when any of them die, their bodies drift down to the silty floor, where decomposers—pale, eyeless things that crawl and burrow—break them down, returning their chemistry to the system. The city grows, erodes, and feeds itself in a closed, patient loop. It is less a place for creatures to live and more a single, complex creature in its own right, its citizens nothing more than cells in its body, its silent, glowing spires the bones of a god that is still growing.
