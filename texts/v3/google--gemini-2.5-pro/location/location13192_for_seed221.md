This is a sealed place. A room. The very concept of a horizon is an absurdity here, a piece of forgotten mythology. You are inside something.

The sound is the first and last law. It is not merely loud; it is a solid. A deafening, unchanging, physical presence that presses in from all sides, vibrating through the grated floor into the soles of your feet and up your spine. It is a chord, played by a titan's orchestra, and held for eternity. The bass note is a subterranean, tectonic hum, the drone of a colossal engine labouring against an impossible load somewhere below. Above this is the roar of a thousand waterfalls, a constant, high-pressure hiss of atomised liquids moving through unseen conduits. Layered over it all is a sound like a billion tiny bells being shaken in a metal box—a frantic, high-frequency chittering that is somehow both chaotic and utterly uniform. The sound never crests, never wanes. It is the steady-state scream of a world in a single, perpetual moment.

The air is cold enough to make your breath a thick, immediate fog. It is also completely saturated with moisture. Every surface weeps. Riveted iron plates, thick as a hand is wide, form the walls, and down their pitted, dark-grey faces, endless, slow rivulets of condensation trace paths. The water is impossibly clean but tastes of ozone and metal. It is not rain; it is the exhalation of the machinery. Droplets hang from every pipe, every bolt head, catching the dim light before falling with a soft *tink* onto the surfaces below, a sound immediately devoured by the greater noise. In the coldest corners, where pipes carrying cryogenic fluids are lagged in frosty asbestos-like material, the condensation freezes into delicate, intricate ferns of rime ice.

There is no ground. You stand on a floor of heavy-duty iron grating. Through the gaps, you see not earth, but a dizzying chasm of more pipes, conduits, and humming, monolithic components glowing with a faint, internal amber light. This entire chamber, perhaps ten meters to a side, is a node, a junction box, a single organ humming in the body of some incomprehensible machine. It is high up; a forgotten maintenance catwalk on a tower that pierces a sky you will never see.

From the grating of the floor, the world's only geology is being born. Hexagonal pylons of a chalky, porous, off-white ceramic material are being extruded from circular apertures. They rise at an impossibly slow rate—that is the theory. In truth, they are frozen. They are caught in the act of becoming. One is a mere nub, its top surface a raw, crystalline fracture. Another stands a meter high, its sides showing the faint, layered lines of its arrested extrusion. A third is malformed, split halfway up by a deep fissure from which a thick, viscous lubricating gel oozes and drips, freezing into a pale-yellow stalactite just above the floor. This is a nursery of stillborn architecture. The "emergence" is not a process, but a permanent state. The world is eternally "under construction," never to be completed.

And it is not empty. Every surface that is not weeping water or actively freezing is covered in life. Not plants—there is no soil, no sun. The fauna is overwhelming. They are the source of the chittering. Covering the pipes, the walls, the undersides of the grates, is a living carpet of what might be called Chime Mites. Each is the size of a thumbnail, with a body like a shard of smoked quartz and six, needle-thin metallic legs. They move in a constant, frenetic swarm, a shimmering, seething mass that flows like liquid over the machinery. They are not chaotic; their movements are a dance, a clockwork reaction to the machine's energy fields. They feed on the static electricity that bleeds from unshielded conduits, on the waste heat radiating from the engines, on the greasy lubricant that weeps from the joints of the frozen extruders. They are a perfectly efficient, self-regulating population in a closed system with an unchanging supply of nourishment. Their chitinous bodies, scraping against the metal of their world and each other, produce the million-fold chime.

There is no day or night here. The only light comes from the dim, sickly green glow of pressure gauges embedded in the machinery, and the faint, pulsing amber from the depths below the floor grate. Sometimes, a patch of the Chime Mites will glow with a soft blue bioluminescence as they digest a particularly potent surge of energy, a silent, slow ripple that moves through the swarm on the wall before fading back to shimmering darkness.

This space is dense, an exercise in claustrophobia. Pipes, some thick as a man's torso and others no wider than a finger, crisscross the chamber at every angle, leaving only narrow corridors to navigate. You are never more than an arm's length from a cold, vibrating, wet surface. The air is thick with the smell of cold iron, ozone, and a strange, cloyingly sweet scent that might be the mites' waste product.

The rule of this place is stasis. The deafening sound is unchanging. The temperature is unwavering. The rate of condensation is constant. The pylons are forever emerging. The population of the mites is at a fixed, roaring peak. It is a snapshot of a dynamic process that has been frozen in time, yet paradoxically, all the components of that process—the sound, the energy, the life—are still running at full tilt. It is a single frame of a film, projected for an eternity. It is a motion that goes nowhere. It is a scream, held for all time.
