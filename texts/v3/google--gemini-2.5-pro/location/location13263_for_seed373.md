The first thing you feel is the sound. It is not so much heard as it is worn, a pressure suit of continuous, structured noise. It’s a physical presence, a deafening, grinding roar that has the texture of a billion glass beads being poured into a turbine. It fills the ears, the chest, the bones, vibrating the very marrow until the distinction between self and sound begins to fray. It is the world’s one, constant weather.

This world is a high, cool plateau, a shelf of obsidian-dark rock no larger than a city square, cupped by jagged, silent peaks in the far distance. The ground underfoot is not soil but ancient, weathered basalt, worn smooth over eons into gentle, rolling swells. It is settled, placid, the work of a geology that has long since finished its arguments. There are no cracks, no scree, only the immense, solemn quiet of the stone itself.

But the stone is not bare. It is host to a forest, but a forest without water, without soil, without a single molecule of chlorophyll. This is a jungle of pure resonance. Growing directly from the polished rock are structures of crystalline lattice, a lush and intricate filigree of something like glass, or spun sugar, or frozen sound. This is the vegetation. It is not green, but opalescent, pearlescent, translucent. Some an inch tall, some ten feet. There are vast, web-like sheets of silica thread, stretched between pillars of hexagonal crystal that chime with a high, frantic energy, adding a layer of shattering treble to the bass roar of the atmosphere. There are fractal ferns that grow, visibly, in slow-motion explosions of complexity, their edges shimmering as the ambient sound waves feed them. The density is almost zero; you could walk through this "forest" and barely touch a thing, for it is 99% empty space, a scaffolding of impossible delicacy. Yet it is lush in its dizzying complexity, covering every inch of the plateau in its growth.

Through the vast, open spaces within this glass jungle, the fauna darts and swarms. They are not flesh. They are motes of kinetic energy, shimmering distortions in the air like heat haze given volition. Some are like origami insects folded from light, flitting with impossible speed, their passage marked by a brief, dissonant chord that cuts through the background roar. Others are larger, like manta rays made of polished chrome, that swim slowly through the air, their bodies warping the light that passes through them. They are abundant but insubstantial, a storm of animate phenomena that never collides, each occupying its own sliver of reality. They do not eat the crystal flora; they are born of the same turbulent energy, different expressions of the same sonic law.

The turbulence is total. This is not a static place. The deafening sound is a creative and destructive force. You watch as a towering spire of crystal, having reached some resonant limit, suddenly shatters into a million motes of dust-fine light. The sound of its end is a sharp, singing crack swallowed instantly by the background roar. But where the fragments land, new growth begins immediately—tiny fibrils of glass unspooling from the basalt, vibrating, catching the energy of the air and knitting themselves into new, intricate forms. The entire grove is in a state of constant, chaotic flux, a storm of creation and destruction measured in minutes, not seasons.

And scattered amongst it all, faint and alien, are the artifacts. They are not ruins, but single, perfect structures. Here, a trio of pylons made of a material so black it seems to swallow the light, emitting no sound but causing the air around them to hum with a palpable pressure. There, a single, floating ring, ten feet in diameter, hovering motionlessly thirty feet in the air. It is made of a seamless, matte-grey metal, and it rotates at an almost imperceptible speed. These things are clearly not of the glass jungle. They are older, more purposeful. Perhaps they are the source of the sound, the regulators of this impossible ecology. They are beyond modern understanding, silent anchors in the heart of the storm, their surfaces unweathered by the chaos they seem to orchestrate.

The light is normal. A single, sun-like star hangs in a thin, pale cyan sky. The air is cool and sharp, without scent of rain or rot; it smells only of ozone and the clean, hot smell of overwhelmed electronics. The normalcy of the light makes the scene all the more strange. It illuminates the impossible fragility of the glass-flora, catches the quicksilver glint of the energy-fauna, and is utterly defeated by the absolute black of the ancient pylons.

This place has one rule: existence is vibration. There is no water, no blood, no decay. There is only resonance and dissonance. Life here is not a struggle for resources, but a dance with entropy, a constant, shrieking, beautiful, terrifying process of being assembled and disassembled by a roar that will never, ever end.
