The first thing is the sound. It is not a feature of the landscape; it is the landscape. It is the substrate from which all other senses must be carved. It is a deafening, bass-heavy, and complex roar that presses in from all sides, vibrating up through the soles of the feet and into the bones of the skull. It is the sound of a billion tons of machinery grinding through a cycle whose purpose was lost before the concept of history was formed.

This is a place of low, open plains, but the plains are not made of earth. The ground underfoot is a continuous, near-flat expanse of interlocking plates. They might be metal, or a type of ceramic, or some hybrid material now known only as “substrate.” Eons of wear have ground them down, their seams blurred, their surfaces worn to a muddled, dark-grey sheen that drinks the faint light. There are no sharp edges left anywhere in this world. Every form has been submitted to a cosmic-scale tumbler, smoothed and softened into its final, most stable state. This is what erosion means in a world that was never rock.

The ground is not truly flat. It swells and dips in vast, gentle undulations. A rise that might be called a hill is the slumped housing of a piston-block the size of a municipal building, its form collapsed and rounded by ages of stress and molecular decay. A shallow valley is a former service channel, its edges now as soft and indistinct as the banks of a slow-moving river of tar. Everything here was once precise, angular, and functional. Now, it is just… landscape. An artifact of industry so ancient it has become geology.

Water is a constant presence, but it does not rain. The air is cool and heavy with moisture, a perpetual, condensing fog that clings to every surface. The machinery, deep within the planet-sized engine block of the world, generates immense heat in its cycles. This heat radiates up, meeting the cold, dead air of the upper strata, and the result is a constant, worldwide sweat. Every vertical surface weeps. The huge, rounded forms of the dead-but-active machines are slick with a film of this condensation, which gathers and flows in thick, syrupy runnels. The water tastes of ozone and dissolved metal, clean and sterile in a way that feels deeply wrong. It collects in the low-lying basins, forming shallow, motionless lakes of perfect, black clarity, their surfaces disturbed only by the thrumming vibration of the world itself.

There is no sun. The sky, if it can be called that, is the underside of another, higher level of the same construction, a ceiling so vast and high that it vanishes into a darkness that seems absolute. Light is scarce, generated by the world-machine itself. Far away, stretching in lines that curve with the world’s unseen horizon, are faint, pulsating glows. Some are a sullen cherry-red, the light of colossal heating elements. Others are a stark, anemic blue-white, the corona discharge from failing insulators kilometres long. These lights flicker and breathe on a geologic timescale, their rhythm a slow counterpoint to the unceasing, high-frequency roar. They do not illuminate so much as create silhouettes, rendering the open plains into a theater of immense, moving shadows.

In this dark, wet, and vibrating world, a strange form of life endures. It is not vegetation in any familiar sense. Growing in sparse, meandering patches are colonies of what might be a kind of galvanic fungus or a chemo-synthetic lichen. They favor the seams in the substrate where minute electrical currents still leak, or the shores of the condensation pools where specific metallic salts are most concentrated. They do not reach for the non-existent sun. Instead, they sprawl horizontally, forming intricate, circuit-board-like patterns on the ground. They are a dull, matte black, but when the low, bass frequencies of the machinery hit a certain resonant pitch—a cycle that occurs every few hours—they react. For a few moments, the filigreed patterns glow with a soft, internal, phosphorescent green light, tracing the flow of energy they are consuming. Then, as the vibration shifts, they fade back to black. They are the only living things here. There are no animals, no insects, no birds. Nothing scuttles or hunts. The only movement is the slow creep of the glowing mold and the juddering of the world-engine.

The world is active. It breathes. The deafening sound is not monolithic; it has seasons. For days at a time, a high-pitched, cutting whine will dominate the audio spectrum, a sound that shreds the nerves. This is followed by a period of low, gut-churning groans, as if continents of metal are shifting against each other. Tremors are the weather. Sometimes a distant actuator, kilometers long, will complete a billion-year cycle, and the ground will shudder with a single, colossal *CLANG* that briefly overrides the background roar. Pressure vents, releasing plumes of superheated, non-toxic gas that billow into the upper darkness, momentarily illuminated from below by the world’s own faint light before cooling and condensing back into the weeping damp.

This is a place built, in its entirety, for a purpose. But the purpose has been fulfilled, or forgotten, or has simply run its course for so long that it has become the new natural state. The system is now in its final, stable, self-perpetuating phase. It is not a ruin, for nothing has truly broken. It is an ecosystem whose geology is manufactured, whose water cycle is a function of thermodynamics, whose flora is powered by failing current, and whose soundscape is the continuing, deafening, and eternal voice of its own being. It doesn’t need a sun, or life, or silence. The process is enough.
