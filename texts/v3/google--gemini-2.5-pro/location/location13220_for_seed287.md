The sound is the first thing, and the last. It is not a collection of noises but a single, monolithic chord that has been playing for centuries. It is the sound of a world-engine, a deep and resonant thrumming that vibrates up through the soles of your feet and settles in your teeth. This bass note is the foundation. Layered over it is the ceaseless, high-pressure *hiss* of venting steam and the groan of immense, slowly turning components. The air itself is a physical presence: a wet, velvet weight, so hot it feels like a low-grade fever you can't sweat out. It smells of hot metal, of ozone, and of something else, something green and damp and peppery, like crushed jungle leaves and rich, dark soil.

This place is a cavern, but not a natural one. It is a cathedral built for a forgotten god of industry, sunk deep beneath the surface. The scale is confounding; the ceiling is lost in a swirling, heated fog, but you can feel its oppressive proximity. Great, arching trusses of dark iron, webbed with age but unyielding, crisscross the upper gloom. From them hang colossal pipes, some as wide as ancient trees, that snake down into the heart of the chamber. Their surfaces are not rusted, but coated in a dark, oily patina, slick with condensation that gathers and drips with a slow, maddeningly regular rhythm. *Plink. Plonk. Hiss.* Each droplet sizzles as it strikes a hotter surface below.

The light is a battle between two sources, and neither is winning. From the core of the machinery—great bronze spheres and titanic pistons moving with glacial slowness—comes a sullen, cherry-red glow. It is the light of contained, immense heat, painting the undersides of the pipes and tangled structures in shades of blood and rust. This angry orange is answered by a cool, silent rebellion. In the cooler pockets, on the lee side of pipes and clinging to the grooved iron walls, are vast colonies of fungi. They grow in impossible shelves and delicate, filigreed lattices, and they pulse with a soft, bioplasmic blue-green light. This living glow illuminates the second half of the landscape: the vegetation.

There is no soil. Life here has abandoned the earth and embraced the machine. A form of kudzu, thick as a human arm, wraps the pipes in a dense, muscular embrace. But its leaves are not green and soft; they are articulated plates of a coppery, metallic material that clatter faintly against each other in the thermal updrafts. They do not seek sunlight. They are heat-eaters, thermosynthesizers, siphoning the immense energy of the engine directly. Their "roots" are fine, filament-like tendrils that have worked their way into the seams and joints of the machinery, becoming one with it. Here and there, where condensation is heaviest, hang thick curtains of asbestos-white moss, dripping constantly, feeding the cycle. The air is thick with the spores of these strange plants, a faint, shimmering dust visible in the competing glows.

There are no animals. No insects drone, no creatures scuttle in the shadows. The absence is profound. The only movement, aside from the titanic, slow-breathing machine, is the drip of water and the imperceptible growth of the copper-leafed vines. This lack of fauna makes the sound of the place even more absolute. It is a purely mechanical and vegetal symphony.

Remains of the builders are scattered like fossils. Skeletal iron catwalks cling to the walls, their grates furred with glowing fungus. One leads to a control station, a bank of levers fused into a single lump of oxidized metal. A single, grimy indicator light blinks a steady, inexplicable amber, once every seven seconds. It has been blinking for an epoch. Below, half-submerged in a shallow, warm pool of condensed water, lies a jumble of what might have been a small maintenance vehicle, now so overgrown with vines it resembles a thorny burial mound.

The rules of this place are simple and unbreakable. The engine runs. It generates heat. The heat is food for the vines. The heat also turns water to steam, which rises, condenses on the cooler upper architecture, and drips back down, sustaining the mosses and fungi. The system is in a state of loud, hot, slow-moving equilibrium. It is a terrarium built around a star-heart, a self-perpetuating ruin. Nothing changes quickly here. A vine might fully encircle a new pipe over the course of a human lifetime. The great pistons complete a single cycle every ninety minutes, their groans marking a deep, slow time. The place is not dead, nor is it alive in a way that is recognizable. It is a functioning, eternal process, a monument singing a single, deafening note to itself in the dark.
