This is a place of absolute darkness, a warm, wet basin where the concept of light is a forgotten theory. The air is not merely air; it is a dense, saturated medium, thick with humidity and the weight of things unseen. To be here is to be suspended in a warm, black liquid. Your skin is always slick, beaded with condensation that has no sky to fall from. The temperature is a constant, muggy embrace, like the inside of a lung.

The ground is not solid. It is a vast, shallow floodplain of silt and raven-black mud, interrupted by islands of porous, weeping stone. These formations are geologically young, their edges still sharp and angular, as if they were extruded from the deep earth only yesterday. They are oddly crystalline in structure, and water perpetually seeps from their every facet, not in drips but in slow, silent sheets that slide down their sides and pool at their bases. The water is everywhere, lurking just below the surface of the mud, filling every depression with still, opaque mirrors that reflect nothing. To walk here is a slow, wet business, a constant negotiation with the ground, which sucks at your feet with a soft, lip-smacking sound. The world is mostly quiet, but it is never silent. The pervasive sound is the water: the sighing of the weeping stones, the thick, glutinous bubble of gas escaping a deep mire, the almost inaudible trickle of a thousand tiny runnels carving paths through the silt.

Over this low, wet expanse, a scattered and incomprehensible industry has been built. Immense pylons of a non-reflective, matte-black metal rise from the mud at intervals of hundreds of meters, or perhaps kilometers; distance is unreliable here. They are stark and new, their surfaces cool to the touch despite the warmth of the air, and they too are slick with a constant film of moisture. They are not lighting towers. They emit no glow. Instead, they hum. It is a low, foundational hum that is felt more in the bones than heard with the ears, a thrumming resonance that seems to be the source of the oppressive warmth. Heavy, sagging cables, thick as a human torso, stretch between some of them, disappearing into the dark. Others stand alone, terminating in complex arrays of fins and motionless rotors. These are not factories in any recognizable sense. They have no workers, no smokestacks, no visible outputs. They are regulators in a slow, patient process. Perhaps they are siphoning something from the air, or pumping something into the ground. The air around them tastes of ozone and hot metal, a sharp and sterile counterpoint to the sweet, organic rot of the marsh.

Vegetation is sparse, confined to tenacious colonies that have adapted to the totality of the dark. There are no leafy plants. Instead, wiry, rust-colored reeds grow in tight clusters around the bases of the weeping stones, feeding on whatever minerals the water carries. On flatter expanses of mud, vast, networked mats of pallid fungal mycelium spread just beneath the surface, their presence only betrayed when you put your foot through the thin crust and into the webby softness below. These fungi are the base of a bizarre food chain.

And the place is teeming with life. The density is unnerving. The air itself seems to wriggle. Swarms of tiny, soft-bodied insects, silent and wingless, drift on the imperceptible air currents, brushing against your exposed skin like motes of living dust. Larger things click and skitter through the mud. You hear the drag of a chitinous shell, the wet slap of a fleshy appendage being set down. The water is full of them. Step into a knee-deep pool and you will feel the immediate, probing touch of countless small, boneless creatures. They are not aggressive, merely curious and abundant. There are also larger inhabitants. Their presence is known by a sudden pressure change in the air, a deep thudding vibration through the ground, or the slow, massive displacement of water in a distant pool. They are slow, ancient-seeming things that navigate by senses that have no name—pressure, echolocation, a direct perception of the weak electrical fields generated by the pylons. They move with a geologic patience, their cycles of feeding and breeding measured in decades.

This is a place where physics has grown tired. The darkness is not an absence of light, but a fundamental property of the space, an active and hungry void. A torch beam would not pierce it; it would be swallowed, its energy converted instantly into a faint puff of heat and a whisper of ozone. Space itself is unstable. The pylon that seemed a ten-minute walk away might recede with every step you take towards it, while a sound of something moving to your left might originate from a creature physically located behind you. Causality is a suggestion, not a law. The ground beneath your feet might suddenly acquire the properties of a thick liquid for a few moments, before resolving back into mud. Memory becomes untrustworthy, as the very act of observing this place changes the observed. The slow, industrial process of the pylons is not manufacturing a product but maintaining this state of broken reality, steadily and quietly managing the decay of universal constants across a vast and open valley of black mud. It is a preserve of impossibility.
