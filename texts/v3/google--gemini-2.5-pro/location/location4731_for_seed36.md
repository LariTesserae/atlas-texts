The air does not move. It is a solid presence, thick with heat and the smell of hot, wet life—a scent not of decay, but of rampant, unstoppable generation. It is the atmosphere of a planetary greenhouse, held deep beneath a surface that is only a myth. There is no weather here, only temperature.

The light is the first and most defining thing. It is total, a sourceless, butter-yellow luminescence that saturates every volume of space. It does not cast sharp shadows, but rather creates soft, ambiguous pools of dimness on the undersides of things. The source is the world itself. The very matter of the place—the vegetation—glows from within, a biological incandescence that has no cycle of day or night. It is simply on. This light is bright enough to read by, were there anything to read.

You do not stand on rock or soil. The ground is a floor of living mass, a dense, interwoven mat of what might be roots, tubers, and hardened, fibrous flesh. It has the yielding firmness of cork, but it is warm to the touch. It is all one organism, or a colony of organisms so intertwined they have become a single substrate. This is the terrain: rolling, swollen hills and wide, shallow vales of plant-flesh. In the distance, rising into the luminous haze of the immense cavern, are mountains. They are not jagged peaks of stone, but colossal, smooth upwellings of the same living material, titans of cellulose and lignin that have swelled toward the unseen ceiling. Their surfaces are pristine, showing no sign of fracture or wear, as if they emerged from the ground-flesh a moment ago and then were frozen in place. They are the color of pale gourds and old ivory.

This place is in a state of arrested becoming. The shapes are those of emergence—the smooth, powerful curves of something under pressure, pushing its way into existence. It is a geology of growth. Yet nothing changes. The process is caught, held in a permanent, silent suspense. This is a world in its embryonic stage, forever.

Water does not flow here in rivers or streams. The humidity is so high that every surface is perpetually slick with condensation. The air itself feels liquid. Water oozes, it weeps. It gathers. Cut into the side of a plant-hill, and a clear, viscid sap, cool and tasteless, will pearl at the wound and refuse to drip. Throughout the landscape are vast, cupped leaves, some the size of small boats, that have collected millennia of this condensed moisture. They are basins of perfectly still, perfectly clear water, their surfaces like panes of glass. No dust motes dance on them, no insects break their tension. A slow, steady drip might fall from a hanging liana above, but the basin never seems to get any fuller. The equilibrium is absolute.

For a world made entirely of vegetation, it is shockingly uniform in its density. Space is not empty. The air is thick with floating spores, like a constant, silent snowfall that never lands. Great, fleshy vines, thick as a human torso, hang in dense curtains from the high, glowing ceiling, which is itself just a higher stratum of the same interwoven growth. To move through this place is to push aside living architecture. The plants are the walls, the floor, the sky. Everything is near-touching, layered, woven. Colors are muted: shades of cream, ochre, pale green, and the occasional startling magenta of a fruiting body the size of a house.

Here and there, the pattern is broken. Faintly, you can discern intention. There are lattices where the hanging vines have been braided together to form a screen. There are archways, not carved, but guided, as if some long-forgotten force pruned and trained the growth into shapes of passage. In a wide valley, a series of low, circular walls made of fused, bulbous roots sit half-swallowed by the floor-mass. They are not ruins in the traditional sense, as they are not decaying. They are living, breathing, and static, like the rest of the world. They are relics of purpose in a place that now has none. No tools are found here; whatever intelligence shaped these forms used only the biological material at hand, bending the world to a will that has since vanished.

The quiet is profound. It is not an empty silence, but a full one. There are no bird calls, no insectile chitters, no rustle of wind. There is only a single, pervasive, low-frequency hum. It is the sound of metabolism on a continental scale, the sound of the light being generated, the sound of life locked in a permanent, unmoving state of is. It is a sound you feel in your bones more than you hear with your ears.

Life, other than the all-encompassing botany, is almost entirely absent. For days of walking, you might see nothing. Then, you might glimpse it: a slow, silent drifting. High in the luminous air, between the columns of vine, float sac-like creatures of translucent membrane. They are simple beings, perhaps a foot across, propelled by the faintest pulsing of their gelatinous bodies. They are filter-feeders, subsisting on the eternally suspended spores, their internal organs glowing a faint, soft blue against the ambient yellow light. They move with an aimlessness that befits this static world, ancient and unchanging, their entire existence a quiet glide through a silent, living cavern, from nowhere to nowhere.
