This is a silent world, a world of grey and pressure.

The ground is a single, continental plate of basalt, buckled and folded in on itself over an immeasurable timescale. There are no sharp peaks, no jagged canyons. Every elevation is ancient, worn smooth by a process that is not wind or water, but something else entirely. These are mountains that have slumped, their spines broken eons ago, now appearing as vast, dark swells on the horizon, each one large enough to possess its own gravitational nuance. The scale is planetary; a single valley might take a lifetime to cross, and it would be a journey of numbing sameness.

The air is thin, cold, and still. It carries a dampness that never resolves into rain. It is a moisture born of condensation, clinging to every surface in a fine, greasy film. The light is a perpetual twilight, emanating not from a distinct sun but from the upper atmosphere itself, a diffuse, sourceless luminescence that robs the landscape of shadow and depth. Everything is rendered in an infinite gradient of grey on black. Sound does not exist here in any conventional sense. The density of the air is wrong for it, and there is nothing to generate it. The silence is not an absence but a presence, a physical weight on the ear.

The "tech" and the "fauna" are one and the same. This world is overwhelmingly populated, but not by flesh and blood. Across the endless, worn plains move the Harvesters.

They are colossal entities, geological in scale and composition. The smallest are the size of hills, the largest approach the mass of the worn-down mountains they mimic. They do not walk or crawl but glide, moving with an unnerving slowness, perhaps a few feet in a day. Their outer shells are composites of obsidian, fused quartz, and unidentifiable, non-reflective metals. They are featureless, save for vast, crystalline lenses that phase slowly from opaque to translucent, drinking the dim light. There is no sign of a mind, only of a process.

They are industrial machines, but they were never built. They are life, but they do not breed in any recognizable way. They are the planet’s metabolism.

A Harvester moves across the basalt plain, and its passage is the only dynamic force in this world. It does not eat the rock in a crude, grinding way. The leading edge of its immense, flattened underbody hums with a silent, contained energy that causes the basalt to disassociate. The rock simply delaminates, flaking away into fine, metallic dust without heat or sound. The creature absorbs this dust, its sheer mass a filter and a furnace. Internally, with the patience of a star, it sorts the elements.

This is the planet's industry. The sorting of rock into its constituent parts. Vast strata of processed, elementally pure dust are layered in the creature's wake. A wide band of iron oxide dust, kilometers across, gives way to a band of silicates, then to powdered nickel, each a different shade of dark grey. These bands are the only colours in the world.

The "dampness" of the atmosphere is their exhaust. From microscopic pores on their upper carapaces, they vent a steady, slow-pluming vapor of complex hydrocarbons and water. In the intense cold, this vapor immediately condenses, forming the low-lying fog that blankets the world and coats every surface. In the vast, shallow depressions of their tracks—the only topography that is not ancient—this moisture pools, creating shallow, still lakes of inert, chemical-smelling water.

There is vegetation, but it is not green. In the oldest, most settled dust-strata left behind by the Harvesters, a sparse, crystalline growth emerges. It is a lithotroph, a lifeform that feeds on the refined minerals. It grows in fractal patterns, like frost on a windowpane, forming brittle, knee-high lattices of metallic blue and dull brass. These formations are incredibly sparse; one might see a single cluster every hundred miles. They grow with geological slowness, and if a Harvester's path should intersect with them, they are silently un-made and re-absorbed into the cycle.

To stand on the surface is to feel the world breathing on a timescale that defies comprehension. The silence is broken only by the faintest *tink*, a sound that travels through the solid ground more than the air, as a super-cooled piece of the crystalline "vegetation" finally succumbs to thermal stress and sheds a single, perfect sliver. Far on the horizon, a shape that you had mistaken for a mountain has moved, its silhouette now obscuring a different part of the glowing grey sky. Its silent industry has been ongoing since the planet cooled, a slow, methodical deconstruction and re-ordering of itself. It is not dying. It is not living. It is simply processing.
