This place is called Am-Thenget, the Great Gutter. It does not lie within a valley or upon a peak, but rather occupies a vast, shallow depression atop a high, wind-scoured plateau. The scale is that of a small town nestled in a crater, but there is no town here, not in the way one might think of it. The settlement *is* the crater.

Over uncounted generations, the people here have paved the entirety of this bowl-shaped landscape. The floor and the gently sloping sides are a single, continuous work of masonry. Massive, irregular flagstones of grey, dun, and ochre rock are fitted together with impossible precision, their surfaces worn smooth by time and touch. There is no mortar; there is only weight and masterful geometry. The entire basin, perhaps a mile across, is one unified stone artifact.

This is a city built to harvest water from the air. The elevation is high and the air is cool and thin. During the day, under a clear and unforgiving sun, the stone warms slightly. At night, it radiates this heat away quickly, becoming intensely cold. As the damp air of the pre-dawn drifts across it, condensation beads on every surface. The genius of Am-Thenget is that the whole city is a watershed. Subtle, almost imperceptible grooves are carved into the paving stones, forming a filigree of channels that spiderweb across the entire basin. These grooves, no wider than a finger, coax the condensation downwards. They merge, growing into larger channels that run like silent, recessed rivers, until they all drain into deep, circular cisterns cut directly into the living rock at the basin's center.

The atmosphere is one of profound stillness. The world is stable, in a state of frozen equilibrium. Nothing happens quickly here. The paving was finished so long ago that the concept of "construction" has been forgotten, replaced by an eternal, ritualized "maintenance." The air is cool and clean, carrying the scent of wet stone and the faint, mineral tang of the cistern water. It is mostly quiet. The wind that howls over the surrounding plateau cannot gain purchase within the basin's lip, and so the air is often dead calm. The only consistent sounds are the impossibly slow *plink... plink...* of water drops echoing up from the cistern mouths, and the scrape of a caretaker's leather-soled shoes on stone.

Vegetation is scarce and deliberate. Most of the stone is kept meticulously clear, but in designated, rectilinear patches, the channels are allowed to clog with silt, creating shallow beds for a single type of plant: a tough, grey-green lichenous moss that is the people's primary food. It grows slowly, drawing nutrients from the mineral-rich water and dust. Small, hardy tufts of a wiry, rust-colored grass grow in the tightest seams between the great stones, but these are seen as flaws, to be weeded out during maintenance cycles.

The architecture is one with the pavement. Low, blocky, single-story structures rise seamlessly from the flagstones as if the stone itself had thickened and folded into a room. There are no doors, only shadowed openings. The roofs are also flat, heavy stone, angled just enough to shed their own condensation into the surrounding channel system. These buildings are scattered sparsely across the basin floor, with vast, empty plazas of bare stone between them. There are no streets, only open space and the faint lines of the water grooves to suggest direction.

The people of Am-Thenget are few. They move slowly, with a placid economy of motion. Their purpose is singular: to be the tenders of the stone. They patrol the basin in silent, meditative rounds, checking the channels for blockages, re-polishing surfaces that have grown dull, clearing any errant growth. Their tools are simple: hand-brooms of bound grasses, stone scrapers, and leather polishing cloths. Their society, like their world, is unchanging. Roles are inherited, tasks are cyclical, and the idea of radical change is not just foreign, but inconceivable.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. There are no birds, no mammals. The contained ecosystem is too sterile. The only visible life, besides the people and their moss-gardens, are tiny, silver-white insects that skitter in the perpetual twilight of the cisterns, and a particular type of small, flat snail that clings to the undersides of the stones, emerging at night to drink the condensation. The people do not interact with them. They are simply another feature of the settled world.

The rule of this world is Balance. The amount of water that condenses each night is, on average, the amount the people and their gardens require. The stone wears away at a rate imperceptible even over a human lifetime. The population does not grow or shrink. Everything is as it was, and as it will be. To exist in Am-Thenget is to be a part of a slow, geological process, a living component in an ancient machine that has long since found its perfect, quiet rhythm.
