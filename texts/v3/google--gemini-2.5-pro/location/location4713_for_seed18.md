The sound is not heard, but worn. It is a physical pressure, a garment of immense and complex vibration that settles over you the moment you enter. It is the first and last law of the Atrium. The sound is a bass note so profound it feels like the planet’s own resonant frequency, a hum that vibrates deep in the marrow of your bones. Layered over this is the roar of a million waterfalls, a constant, crashing, churning white noise. And threaded through that, a high, keening whine, the sound of stressed metal and energies barely contained. It is deafening, a solid wall of noise that extinguishes all other sound, making speech a fool's errand and thought a frantic, internal semaphore.

This is a room at the top of the world. Below is an impossible height, a fall through cloud and memory, but here, inside, the scale is contained. It is a single, vast chamber, perhaps the size of a grand cathedral nave, but it feels both larger and smaller. The walls are not straight but curve gently inward, engineered from a mosaic of immense, interlocking ceramic plates. They are the colour of bone, but are stained with millennia of weeping moisture, tracked with green and black streaks of resilient algae. The erosion here is not one of decay, but of seasoning. The structure has settled into its final form, the edges rounded by time and condensation, the surfaces holding a patina that speaks of an ancient, unceasing function. This place is not dying; it is in its prime, a prime that has lasted for ages.

Everything is built. There is no soil, no rock, no earth. The floor is a grille of a dark, bronze-like alloy, warm to the touch. Through its tight mesh, you can see down into a secondary space of churning machinery and pulsing, soft lights, the source of some of the heat. The air is tropical, thick with a humidity that sheens every surface and feels heavy in the lungs. This constant dampness is not rain; it is the exhalation of the chamber’s heart.

At the center of the Atrium stands the Engine. It is a colossal, tiered column of the same bone-coloured ceramic and dark metal, rising from the grated floor to a ceiling lost in misty glare. This is the source of the sound, the heat, the life of the room. It is not manufacturing anything. It is a regulator, a lung, a heart. Water weeps from hairline cracks in its ceramic shell, not as a fault, but by design. These droplets are heavy, slow, coalescing and dripping with a patient rhythm onto the flora below. Pumps, hidden deep within, cycle this water endlessly. Great vents, arrayed like gills along its circumference, breathe out waves of shimmering, humid air.

And on every surface, there is life. This is the overwhelming paradox: the chamber is an industrial powerhouse, yet it is a jungle. The vegetation is extravagantly lush. Cable-thick lianas, slick with condensation, spiral around the central Engine and snake across the curving walls, their roots burrowed deep into the seams between the ceramic plates. Great, dripping fans of emerald moss, soft as velvet, cushion the joints of the structure. Ferns with fronds like filigreed jade burst from alcoves, and strange, waxy, bell-shaped flowers of an impossible blue hang from dangling vines, their petals beaded with moisture. They grow not in soil, but on the made-world itself, sustained by the warm, wet air and the light.

The light is a steady, even, day-bright luminescence that filters down from the unseen ceiling. It seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse wash that eliminates most shadows and gives the rampant greenery a hyper-real glow. It feels like the clean, unfiltered sun of a place above the clouds.

Abundant fauna has adapted to this monolithic, roaring ecosystem. There are no birds that sing; there would be no point. Instead, flocks of silent, jewel-toned avians with iridescent wings flash and glitter as they flit from vine to wall, communicating in shifting patterns of light. Geckos the size of a man’s forearm, their skin patterned to mimic the stained ceramic, hunt for metallic-shelled beetles that crawl along the warm pipes. Moths with wings like powdered rust and copper drift on the thermal updrafts from the Engine, their antennae twitching to navigational cues you cannot perceive. They live and die in a world without quiet, their entire biology rewritten by the absolute rule of the noise.

Yet, for all this life, the place feels utterly empty. The density is zero. The surfaces—walls, floor, Engine, plants—are crowded, teeming, a riot of detail. But the air between them, the vast central volume of the Atrium, is a void. The machine that produces the heat and humidity is also a perfect filter. There is no dust, no floating spores, no haze other than pure water vapour. Looking from one wall to another is like looking through a vacuum. The clarity is unnerving. You can see a single drop of water forming on a leaf a hundred metres away with perfect, crystalline precision. The space itself is sterile, hollow, a vast emptiness defined by the noisy, living skin that contains it.

The world here operates on a slow, geological timescale. The Engine has not changed its rhythm in centuries. The water drips, the plants grow, the silent birds flash their signals in cycles that are gradual and deeply entrenched. It is a perpetual motion machine of atmosphere and life, a sealed terrarium built for a purpose that is perhaps forgotten, continuing its function with an industrial single-mindedness. To be here is to be an anomaly, a fleeting presence in a place of deafening permanence, a breathing thing in a room that is already breathing its own eternal, mechanical breath.
