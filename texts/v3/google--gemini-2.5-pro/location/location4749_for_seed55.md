The world is water. Not in the sense of a sea on a world, but that the world *is* the sea. There is no surface, no shore, no sky. There is only the deep. The pressure is immense, a constant and total embrace that has crushed all but the most resilient forms into their final state. The water is cold, a degree or two above freezing, its temperature unchanging across millennia. It is a cold that leaches heat not with aggression, but with infinite patience.

This is a place of absolute darkness, yet it is not entirely dark. A faint, internal light pervades. It comes from the life that fills this ocean: the vegetation. Vast, slow-growing forests of kelp-like structures rise for miles from the abyssal floor. Their broad, leaf-like fronds, the size of city blocks, are traced with networks of capillaries that pulse with a cold, blue-green bioluminescence. This is the only light, a ghostly and perpetual twilight that reveals a world of impossible scale and construction.

For there is no ground. There is no mud, no rock, no sand. The substrate of this entire world is artificial. It is a single, contiguous, planet-sized structure, a floor paved in precisely-fitted basalt polygons, each one miles across. The surface is not flat. It swells into low, continent-spanning hills and dips into shallow basins wide enough to hold entire terrestrial oceans. From this floor rise the true marvels: buildings, monuments, and structures of a purpose long forgotten.

These are not spires or towers that claw at a phantom sky, but massive, heavy, low-lying forms. Everything is built to withstand pressure and time. Great terraced pyramids, whose steps are a hundred feet high, descend into darkness. Labyrinths of yard-thick walls enclose courtyards the size of kingdoms. Lines of monolithic pillars, featureless and impossibly wide, march in formation over the horizon, their ranks disappearing into the gloom. The tech used to build this was primitive; one can see the marks of colossal hand-tools on the basalt, now softened and blurred by eons of slow erosion. Every edge has been rounded. Every corner has been worn smooth. It is a city that has been sandpapered by geological time.

The erosion is not from wind or rain, but from the slow, inexorable chemistry of the cold water and the near-imperceptible currents that drift through the world. A fine dust, the ground-down remains of the architecture itself, drifts in a constant, lazy fall, a kind of eternal marine snow. It settles in the corners of the great structures, filling the glyphs carved into their sides—each glyph a sprawling geometric pattern acres in size.

And into this settled, final world, life has crept. The vegetation is not rooted in soil but directly to the stone itself. Sheets of luminous moss, which glow with the soft intensity of starlight, carpet the immense plazas. The great kelp-forms anchor themselves to the roofs of submerged stadiums and the tops of the silent pillars. Their fronds sway in unison, not to a rhythm of minutes or hours, but to the turning of a vast, slow current that takes a century to complete its circuit. The water is dense with the spores and shed filaments of this life, a thick soup that shortens one's view to a few miles at most, cloaking the world in a perpetual, luminous haze.

There are no animals. Nothing swims, nothing crawls, nothing hunts. The absence of movement is profound. The only motion is the glacial sway of the plant life and the patient downward drift of silt. It is utterly silent. There is no birdsong, no chittering, no splash. If you were to listen for a thousand years, you might hear the deep, resonant groan of a cantilevered slab of stone settling a fraction of an inch under its own unthinkable weight. You might hear the faint, collective sigh of a trillion kelp-fronds releasing their spores into the current. But mostly, you would hear a quiet so deep it feels like a pressure in your ears, the sound of a world holding its breath for eternity.

This place has its own weather. Not of cloud and storm, but of nutrient flows and shifting thermoclines. A slow upwelling, taking millennia to rise from the deepest basins, can cause the forests of glimmer-kelp to brighten over the course of a generation, a slow-motion bloom of cold light. After an age, the light will dim again as the flow subsides. These are the seasons. Change is measured not in years, but in the lifespan of the stone and the slow colonization of a new surface by the patient, glowing moss. It is a world built to be a monument, that has become a garden, that will someday, in a time beyond comprehension, become dust.
