This is a world of sound and stone.

The first thing you notice is not a sight, but a feeling that is also a sound. It is a drone, a deafening, all-encompassing hum that seems to emanate not from a single source, but from the very atoms of the place. It is not a roar or a shriek, but a chord of impossible complexity, a million bass strings plucked at once. The sound is so pervasive it's no longer just heard in the ears; it is felt in the bones, a constant vibration that shivers up through the soles of your feet and settles deep in your chest. At first, it is maddening. After a time, it becomes the silence against which all other, smaller sounds are measured.

The landscape is a vast, open basin, worn down by an erosion so profound it feels like the final exhalation of a dying planet. There are no mountains, only the low, rounded ghosts of what might once have been hills, worn smooth like river stones the size of continents. The ground is not soil but a single, contiguous expanse of bedrock, polished to a dull, dark sheen and slick with perpetual weeping. Water is everywhere. It does not rain from the sky, which is a high, permanent canopy of dim, grey-silver cloud that filters the light of a distant, indifferent sun into a permanent state of twilight. Instead, the water condenses directly out of the cool, saturated air, beading on every surface. It seeps from microscopic pores in the ancient stone, gathering in endless, perfectly clear, ankle-deep pools that mirror the luminous gloom above. The world is a floor of dark mirror and weeping rock.

There is no green. Not a single blade of grass, not a patch of moss, not a speck of lichen in the way we know it. The ecology here is founded on something else. Covering vast patches of the polished bedrock is a thin, shimmering film, a living patina that looks like oil-on-water. In some areas, it is the color of verdigris; in others, it is a deep, ferrous rust; in still others, it flashes with a faint, coppery iridescence. This is the primary producer: a chemotrophic, mineral-eating skin that draws its energy directly from the crystalline structure of the ancient rock, metabolizing stone into a thin layer of life.

And this life supports an astonishing abundance of fauna. Great, slow-moving things, the size of boulders, crawl across the flats. They are the grazers. Their bodies are low-slung and plated with thick, chitinous armor that echoes the mineral hues of the rock they live on. They are like colossal trilobites or woodlice, and from beneath their armored carapaces extends a wide, rasping foot, a muscular tongue of silicate teeth that scrapes the living film from the stone. Their passing is the one sound that can momentarily cut through the planetary hum: a rhythmic, deafening *sccrrraaaaape... sccrrrraaaaape...* that grinds on the ears.

They are hunted. Sleek, amphibious predators, black and glossy as obsidian, move with shocking speed through the shallow pools. They are built like greyhounds crossed with salamanders, long-limbed and sinuous, their heads narrow and arrow-like. They hunt not by sound, which is useless here, but by sensing the pressure-waves their prey creates in the water and the vibrations they send through the rock. They will lie in wait, perfectly still in a clear-water pan, and then erupt in a motion too fast to follow, their claws clicking sharply on the stone as they tackle a grazer, trying to flip it and get at the soft, rasping underbelly. Their battles are silent but for the percussive clatter of armor on rock and the splash of displaced water.

Everywhere else, in the spaces between, skitter smaller creatures. Things like metallic beetles, six-legged harvesters that pick at the leftover film, their movements jerky and precise. The air, thick as it is, carries winged things, but they are not birds or insects. They look like flapping sheets of mica, translucent and rigid, navigating the dense air with short, buzzing glides, catching airborne spores or preying on the smaller skitterers.

Amidst this active, alien ecosystem are faint traces of intention. Scattered sparsely across the plains are arrangements of stone. They are not dwellings. They are simple, man-high cairns or carefully balanced tripods of elongated, water-smoothed rocks. They have no mortar, relying on gravity and an intuitive understanding of balance. These structures are primitive but precise. They seem to interact with the world's constant drone. Some are built in places where the hum gains a specific harmonic overtone, and the rocks of the cairn are chosen and placed to magnify it, to make the air around them tremble with a focused, nodal purity of sound. Others seem to do the opposite, their geometry creating small pockets of relative sonic quiet, a few square feet where the drone is dampened to a bearable thrum. They are the work of a people, past or present, who did not seek to conquer the landscape but to tune it, to find a way to live within the crushing, deafening music of their world.

To be here is to be constantly damp, cool, and vibrated. The air smells of ozone, wet stone, and a faint, metallic tang that is the scent of the living film. Communication must be by touch, by gesture, by feeling the tremors another being sends through the stone floor. It is a world stripped to its essentials: stone, water, a strange and minimal life, and the all-consuming sound. It is a dynamic place, not of growth and decay, but of scraping and consumption, of silent, violent motion against a backdrop of ancient, resonant stillness. It feels less like a planet and more like the inside of a single, colossal, living instrument, nearing the end of its song.
