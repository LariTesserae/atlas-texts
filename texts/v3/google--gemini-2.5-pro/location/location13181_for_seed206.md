The noise is the first thing, the only thing, for a time. It is a dense, layered clamor that suggests a factory floor, but one designed by a mad composer. A deep, resonant thrumming provides the foundation, the feeling of a colossal engine turning over at a slow, implacable rhythm. It's a vibration felt in the teeth and bones more than heard. Over this is the constant, wet hiss of a thousand pressure releases, and the slick, percussive sound of thick liquids being pumped through resilient tubes. And punctuating it all, a sharp, irregular clatter, like ceramic rods knocking against each other in a chaotic but endlessly repeating pattern.

The place is a room, but it was never built. You stand at the edge of a shallow, sunken declivity, perhaps no more than fifty feet across. It is a pit, a basin, a vat. The walls and floor are not stone or earth, but a single, continuous, living substance. It feels like plant matter, but a kind that has petrified into a state of living functionality. The surface is a latticework of pale, bone-white roots and woody growths, forming a complex and porous scaffolding. It's not dense; there are wide gaps in the structure, revealing a hollow darkness within the walls themselves. This is a place of total vegetation that is also strangely sparse, an architecture of emptiness framed by life. The material is hard and cool to the touch, like unglazed pottery, but it is slick with a constant film of moisture.

The heat is immediate and oppressive, the air thick enough to chew. It smells of ozone, of hot metal (though there is no metal), and the sweet, cloying scent of complex fermentation. This is a greenhouse running a fever. Water is everywhere, but there is no rain. The air itself is so saturated that it condenses on every surface, forming heavy droplets that crawl down the ligno-ceramic fretwork before falling with a soft *plip* into clouded pools below. These pools are not clear water; they are milky white, or rust-colored, or a shimmering, oily black, residues of the place's ceaseless industry.

For this place is an engine. Embedded within the living walls are its organs. Great, translucent sacs of taut cellulose pulp and retract with the bass thrum, their interiors swirling with luminescent fluids. Gnarled, piston-like growths of hardened wood, polished smooth by eons of movement, grind against each other in grooved channels, generating the sharp clatter. They crush mineral-rich nodules harvested from within the substrate by unseen inner processes. A vast network of pale, vein-like tubes, some as thick as an arm, wrap and weave through the entire structure, their surfaces pulsing visibly as they pump the system's various slurries. This is a factory made of a single plant, a biomechanical process in a state of perfect, unending equilibrium. It builds nothing, exports nothing. Its only product is its own continued existence.

The light is normal daylight, filtering down from an unseen opening above, but it is fractured and complicated by the latticework, casting a perpetually shifting web of shadows on the floor. Within these moving patterns, the fauna of the Vat thrives. They are not visitors; they are components. Armored, beetle-like creatures with iridescent carapaces scuttle along the machinery, using specialized mandibles to scrape away crystalized chemical deposits, their chewing adding a faint, sandy grinding to the soundscape. In the pools of effluent, gelatinous, slug-like amphibians, eyeless and pale, drift and feed, filtering the liquids and excreting a purified water vapor that rises to rejoin the humid atmosphere. Swarms of tiny, winged insects, like motes of dust caught in a sunbeam, hover around junctions where the plant-pipes spark with faint blue flickers of bio-electricity. They seem to feed on the static charge, their wings beating at a frequency that creates a high, almost unbearable whine.

There is no change here. The thrum has not altered its tempo in a thousand years. The pistons have not worn down, their self-repairing cells replacing material as quickly as it is abraded. The rate of fluid production is perfectly matched by the rate of its reabsorption by feeder roots that delve back into the core of the machine. The fauna breeds and dies at a rate that maintains a stable population, their corpses and waste immediately broken down in the chemical pools. It is a closed system in its final, established form. To stand at its edge is to witness a loud, violent, and intricate process that is also as static and eternal as a stone. It is a pocket of industry hidden from the world, a self-devouring, self-perpetuating engine, furiously busy doing nothing at all.
