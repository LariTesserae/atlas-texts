The world is grey and wet. It is not the grey of shadow, but the grey of diffusion. Light from an unseen, ordinary sun is scattered into a near-uniform luminescence by an atmosphere thick with water. This is the Great Plateau, a continent-sized tableland so high that its peaks are lost not in the thinness of space, but in the thickness of its own perpetual cloud. The air is cool and carries the clean, metallic scent of ozone and stone.

This place is known, to the few who could ever name it, as Bas-en-Haut: the Low-in-the-High. It is a city, but it was never built. It grew.

Over unimaginable spans of time, a form of life—neither animal nor plant—has drawn minerals from the super-saturated air and the weeping stone, precipitating it into architecture. This is a metropolis of biocement, a single, contiguous structure sprawling over a territory larger than a nation. It is dense, a morass of form. There are no streets in a human sense, only channels and canyons carved by the ceaseless flow of water. There are no plazas, only broad, shallow basins where mist pools like dry ice.

The structures themselves are not blocks and beams, but organic extrusions. Towers rise like hollow, petrified femurs, their surfaces pocked with countless spiraling pores from which water perpetually condenses and drips. They are packed so tightly that they form a near-solid mass, a forest of bone-colored stone. Arches and buttresses curve between them, not as planned supports but as the incidental connections between two adjacent growths, like the fused roots of ancient trees. The city is layered upon itself—the foundations of the upper levels are the rooftops of eons-old structures below, delving down into a gloom that the soft, high light cannot pierce.

The ground is slick, uneven. In some places it is smooth and polished by the flow of water; in others, it is a sharp, crystalline crust where evaporation has left behind layers of mineral salts. There is no soil, and thus, almost no vegetation. What little exists is tenacious and simple. A kind of verdigris velvet moss clings to the shaded undersides of arches, feeding on the mineral-rich drip. On the highest, most wind-scoured surfaces, glass-paper lichens grow in flat, grey rosettes, so thin and hard they feel like part of the stone itself.

There is a profound, active silence here. The dominant sound is water, but it is not the roar of a river or the crash of waves. It is a vast and layered symphony of quiet noises. The constant, soft *hiss* of mist condensing on a million acres of stone. The slow, rhythmic *tick... tick... tick* of a single heavy drop falling a hundred feet into a resonant chamber below. The subterranean *gurgle* of water finding a new path through the city's porous heart. When the wind moves, which it often does, it doesn’t whistle; it creates a low, bass hum as it presses through the countless pores and channels of the megastructure, a sound like a world breathing in its sleep.

The entire city is in a state of slow, constant flux. This is not the dynamism of traffic and commerce, but of geology and biology intertwined. In one quadrant, a cluster of towers might be in a growth phase. Here, the air is slightly warmer, and a faint, almost imperceptible clicking can be heard—the sound of mineralization, of the city’s builders at work. These builders, the Architects, are rarely seen. They are immense, slow-moving organisms, closer to mollusks or fungi than anything else. They live deep within the stone, their soft, amorphous bodies lining the internal cavities. They are the city's own flesh. Over centuries, they extend fleshy, syrupy pseudopods into the open air, which then slowly calcify into new bone-white forms.

Elsewhere, a different process is underway. An ancient section of the city, its Architects long dormant, is succumbing to the patient power of water. A channel deepens, undercutting a buttress. With a groan that echoes for miles, a tower fractures, not crumbling into dust but splitting along its growth lines, falling in huge, curved sections into a chasm below. For a few moments, the silence is broken by the deep crash and grind of stone on stone. Then, the quiet returns, leaving only a new canyon in the cityscape, a new path for the water to follow.

Fauna is exceptionally rare. The mineral-based ecology supports very little. Tiny, silverfish-like arthropods skim the surface of pooling water, feeding on algae. The only flying creature is the Glass-wing Moth, a hand-sized insect whose wings are utterly transparent, membranous sheets, visible only by the way they refract the grey light. They are silent in flight and drift on the air currents like scraps of cellophane. They do not feed on plants, but land on freshly exposed biocement, unspooling a delicate proboscis to sip at the mineral-rich ooze left behind by the Architects.

To be here is to feel an overwhelming sense of scale, not just in size but in time. The city exists in its own state of equilibrium. It grows and it decays, but it does so on a geological timescale. It is a monument not to a king or a god, but to a process. It is a living, breathing, weeping mountain range, a city made of rain and stone, inhabited only by silence and its own slow, ponderous life.
