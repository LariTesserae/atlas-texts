The air is a hot, wet towel over the face. It is thick enough to see, a shimmering haze that blurs the horizon into a wavering line of bruised pewter. This is a basin, a vast, shallow depression in the land, and everything trends downwards into it. The ground underfoot is not soil or rock in its natural state, but a uniform, dark, and semi-porous slag, smoothed by ages of slow-moving water. It's laid out in immense, flat pans, separated by low, broad dikes that look less built and more like the accumulated sediment of some long-concluded titanic industry.

Water is universal, but it does not flow in rivers. It seeps. It weeps. It condenses. The oppressive heat, generated by the structures that dot the landscape, keeps the atmosphere permanently saturated. Puddles of scaldingly hot water spread in wide, mirror-like sheets across the slag pans, some only a centimeter deep, others ankle-deep and treacherous. These geothermal pools are crystalline clear, their bottoms stained with mineral deposits of rust-red, ochre, and a virulent turquoise. The primary source of this water appears to be the city itself. Vast, dark structures bleed streams of clean condensate, which runs down their pitted flanks in weeping curtains to feed the pools below.

There is no plant life. Not a single blade of grass, not a smear of algae. The world is one of metal, ceramic, and mineral precipitate. It is a constructed landscape, but one so ancient it has become its own geology. The built environment is of a scale that feels more suited to giants than humans. Immense, black cubes the size of city blocks stand miles apart from each other, their surfaces featureless except for the patina of age and the stains of weeping water. Between them, monolithic cooling towers, shaped like squared-off ziggurats, puff leisurely columns of clean steam into the already thick air. A web of elevated infrastructure—hulking, rust-proof pipelines and enclosed transitways on thick pylons—connects these structures, forming a sparse, functional grid fifty feet above the wet ground. The effect is one of profound emptiness; it is a city with all the mass of a metropolis but none of the density, a collection of silent monuments set in a steaming mire.

The technology is industrial, but it is a settled, mature industry. There is no clatter, no percussive banging of hammers. The only sound from the city is a deep, omnipresent hum, a sub-bass thrum that you feel in your bones more than hear with your ears. It is the sound of continent-sized engines turning over in their sleep, of automated refineries performing their billionth cycle. The light is a perpetual, dim twilight, filtered through the thick haze and steam. At night, this dimness is punctuated by weak, downward-facing amber lights affixed to the undersides of the pipelines and transitways, casting long, feeble glows on the water below.

And in that water, and on the dark slag, is the life. It is overwhelming. With no plants, the entire food chain is built on the waste and heat of the city. The ground is never still. A chitinous tide of armored, arthropodal life, ranging from the size of a fingernail to the size of a dinner plate, scuttles and burrows everywhere. They are the colour of cooling iron and wet rust. They are silent, making only a collective, dry, rustling sound, like a billion grains of sand being poured onto glass. They swarm around the base of pipes that drip nutrient-rich fluid and cluster in the warm shallows of the geothermal pools, filtering minerals.

These are the prey. Larger things hunt them. Long, multi-limbed predators, low-slung and flat like armored salamanders, move with a deliberate patience through the scuttling masses. They are ambush hunters, staying motionless for hours in the murky heat, their forms barely distinguishable from the slag, before a sudden, violent lunge. There are no sounds of struggle, only the ceaseless, quiet rustle of the herd momentarily disturbed before it swarms back into place.

This is a world of cycles. Water condenses on high, cool surfaces, drips to the ground, forms pools, is heated by geothermal vents in the slag, evaporates into steam, and rises again. The factories run on their own endless loop, drawing some unseen resource and expelling heat, water, and chemical nutrients as waste. The creatures follow this, their teeming migrations dictated by the output of a specific thermal vent or the purge cycle of a processing unit.

To exist here is to understand the two levels. The upper level—the transitways, the interiors of the silent, humming cubes—is a place of quiet, sterile, mechanical order. The ground level is a hot, wet, teeming wilderness of chitin and steam. There is no interaction between the two, save for the city feeding the swarm. It is a place both achingly empty and unbearably full, a quiet machine whose purpose is lost to time, now serving only as the heart of a vast, crawling, and utterly alien ecosystem.
