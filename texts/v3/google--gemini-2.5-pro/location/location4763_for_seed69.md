There is no sun here. There is no sky. To speak of day or night is to use a dead language for a forgotten anatomy of time. There is only the deep, unbroken darkness and the thrumming.

The thrumming is the baseline of existence, the first and last sensation. It is not merely sound but a physical presence, a constant, low-frequency vibration that works its way into bone and marrow. It rises from beneath, from the very substance of this high plateau, a continental shelf of stone floating in an ocean of permanent cloud. The noise is immense, a multi-layered symphony of industry. It is the sound of a billion tons of machinery grinding against geology, the roar of colossal furnaces, the oceanic hiss of steam vented into the perpetual dark, and the high, whining song of transformers stepping down impossible voltages. This is the world’s heartbeat, rhythmic and unceasing.

The ground is soft and treacherous. What should be bare rock is swathed in a meters-deep blanket of life. It is not soil, but a saturated, living mat of mosses, fungi, and interwoven root systems, all slick with a constant, warm condensation. Every step sinks, releasing a puff of spores and the scent of ozone, wet earth, and hot metal. Water is everywhere. It does not fall as rain but manifests directly from the super-saturated atmosphere, beading on every surface, gathering in pools of black, still water, and weeping from the faces of the ancient, rounded escarpments. These rock formations are monoliths of erosion, their rough edges smoothed away by millennia of this gentle, persistent dampness, leaving them looking like the bones of sleeping giants.

And in the darkness, life has found its own light. The dominant vegetation is a form of parasitic moss that carpets everything, and it glows. It feeds not on sunlight but on the immense electrical fields and thermal gradients pouring from the planet's industrial core. Close to a thermal vent or a humming conduit, it flares a brilliant, hot blue-white. Further away, it settles into a cool, phosphorescent green, painting the landscape in ghostly, shifting patterns of light. Whole valleys are filled with this cold fire, outlining the contours of the land, the shapes of the trees that grow here.

The trees are not trees as we know them. They do not reach for a sky they cannot see. They grow low and broad, their branches spreading horizontally, forming dense, multi-layered canopies that trap heat and moisture. Their bark is often laced with conductive filaments, drawing ambient energy from the air, and their leaves are wide, waxy plates, designed to funnel the condensing water down to their roots. This canopy is a world unto itself, dense and near-impenetrable, a labyrinth of glowing fungi, dripping epiphytes, and thick, coiled lianas that look more like insulated power cables than vines.

This is a loud world, and not just from the machines. The fauna is abundant, and has adapted to navigate by sound, vibration, and the dim, shifting bioluminescence. Flocks of creatures, part-bat, part-insect, with resonating chambers in their chests, swarm through the under-canopy, their clicks and calls creating zones of echolocating chaos. They hunt the smaller, scurrying things that feast on the glowing moss. Larger animals, the size of bears, move through the lower world. They are blind, six-legged grazers with massive, scoop-like jaws for scraping moss from the rocks, their footsteps muffled by the spongy ground. Their only sound is a low, continuous moan, a call to a herd they can only sense through vibration. And above it all, in the highest, densest parts of the canopy, live the predators—things of elegant, biomechanical horror, with limbs that end in carbon-fiber claws and senses that can detect the faint electrical twitch of a hiding creature's muscles. Their hunting cries are shrieks of tearing metal, a sound that cuts through the industrial drone and stops every other living noise for a moment.

The hand of the builder is faint, but it is everywhere. Scattered across this vast plateau are the outposts of the system itself. They are not cities or even towns, but single, colossal structures—a vent stack wide as a lake and tall as a mountain, a cooling tower whose steam-plume generates a permanent local weather system, a network of humming, moss-covered pipelines that run for a thousand miles, disappearing over the horizon of a glowing valley. There are skeletal catwalks clinging to cliff faces, their metal latticework almost entirely consumed by the volt-moss, leading from nowhere to nowhere. These are not ruins; they are active components of a machine that is as much a part of the geology as the rock it is built from. The tech is ancient, established, and self-repairing. Drones, dark and silent, sometimes flit between these structures on paths of pure logic, their forms barely distinguishable from the hunting creatures of the canopy.

To be here is to be inside a machine that has become an ecosystem, or an ecosystem that has become a machine. The line is gone. The distinction is meaningless. The weather is a byproduct of the engine coolant cycle. The primary producers feed on waste energy. The animals have incorporated the sounds of industry into their language. The rules are simple: energy is life, and the source of energy is the constant, grinding, world-spanning engine that thrums in the dark. It is a place of profound strangeness, a world that took a different path, where the dawn was not a star, but a furnace, and life learned not to reach for the light, but to make its own.
