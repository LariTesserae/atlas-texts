The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a sound that comes from any one direction, but a sound that *is* the direction, a constant and pervasive component of the air itself. It is the sound of a great weight being dragged over stone, of a thousand grinding mechanisms turning in imperfect synchrony, of a low and resonant hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet. It is a Chord, and it never changes. It has never been different, and the thought that it might ever fall silent is an absurdity.

The air is warm and dense with moisture. It clings to your skin, smelling of hot metal, wet clay, and something else—an ozonic, electric tang, the scent of a vast engine running at capacity. The light is flat and sourceless, a diffuse white glow that filters through a permanent, high ceiling of gauzy cloud and steam. There are no distinct shadows, only a soft darkening on the lee side of forms.

You stand on a vast, shallow basin floor. It is not soil or sand, but a seamless, fused plain of ochre-colored ceramic, cracked in wide, tectonic plates. The surface is worn smooth, almost polished in places, as if by millennia of slow, abrasive flooding. Scattered across this plain are shallow, tea-colored puddles, their water still and warm to the touch. Nothing grows in them. Indeed, nothing grows anywhere. The world is an exercise in mineralogy. There is no green, no brown of decay, only the rust-reds, slate-greys, and basalt-blacks of the geology.

This geology is active. Sparsely, across the open expanse, are the sources of the noise: the Pistons. They are colossal cylinders of polished, dark-grey rock, scores of meters in diameter, rising vertically from circular chasms in the ceramic floor. They are not built; they appear to have grown, crystallized into their perfect, mechanical forms. Their surfaces are scarred with the fine lines of geological time, yet gleam with a film of condensed, oily water.

Each Piston operates on a slow, eternal cycle. You watch one, half a kilometer away. It rises with an agonizing slowness, a deep groan resonating through the basin as strata complain. It ascends for what feels like an hour, revealing a throat of obsidian-slick, steaming rock below. It pauses at its apex, a titan of stone hanging silent for a moment against the white sky, dripping water that sizzles on the hot rim of its silo. Then, with the clang of a colossal locking mechanism disengaging somewhere deep in the planet's crust, it begins its descent.

It drops, not with the speed of a fall, but with the inexorable, crushing force of a hydraulic press. As it sinks back into its chasm, it forces the steam and heat roiling within out through a ring of vents at its base. This is the loudest part of the cycle: a prolonged, high-pressure shriek of escaping vapor that billows out in a horizontal blast, scalding the ceramic plain and obscuring the Piston's base in a temporary cloud. The water from this steam is what collects in the shallow pools.

And that is it. That is all that happens. The Pistons rise, they pause, they fall, they vent. Some are rising while others are falling. There is no coordination, yet their collective, overlapping cycles create the single, unchanging industrial Chord. The process accomplishes nothing. It does not build, it does not destroy. The material it processes is a closed loop of steam and heat. It is a machine the size of a nation, running in perfect, pointless equilibrium. Its components are weathered and ancient, its function settled beyond question.

Life is an afterthought, an anomaly. There are no plants, no fungi. But sometimes, in the shimmering heat-haze near the base of a Piston, you might see a flicker of movement. The Glimmer-skitters. They are palm-sized creatures, low to the ground, with carapaces of iridescent, coppery metal. They do not seem to eat or drink, but scuttle with impossible speed from one vent to another, thriving in the moments of intense heat and pressure, perhaps feeding on the static charge or some mineral precipitate left by the steam. They are as much a part of the engine as the Pistons themselves, rare and silent cogs in the great, meaningless machine.

To be here is to be an observer of a solved equation. The landscape is not hostile; it is merely occupied. It is a factory without an architect, an engine without a purpose, a world whose single, loud, unceasing task is to continue being itself, forever. The air trembles. The ground hums. The steam shrieks. And the great stone Pistons rise and fall, as they always have.
