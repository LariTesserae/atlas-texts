The world is a roof of bruised purple and charcoal grey, a permanent twilight held aloft by mountains so ancient their peaks have been worn down to vast, rolling plateaus. You are at an impossible height, where the air is thin and sharp, carrying a cold that is not the aggressive bite of wind and ice, but the deep, cellular chill of a place that has not been properly warm in millennia. The scale is immense; what looks like a nearby hill is a day’s journey away, and the valleys between these worn-down giants are chasms of shadow that swallow light whole.

The ground itself is not rock, not soil, but a continuous, living mat. It is a form of vegetation so successful it has become the landscape’s skin, a deep, resilient pile of what might be moss, lichen, and countless other things woven together. It is saturated, but not with rain. It is damp with its own internal condensation and the fine, almost imperceptible drizzle that occasionally precipitates from the low, heavy sky. The mat is dark—shades of bottle-green, deep umber, and black—but it is not lifeless. Press a hand to its surface and it yields like dense velvet. It muffles every footstep, rendering movement utterly silent. This universal carpet is the reason for the profound quiet. There is no rustle of leaves, no snap of twigs, only the drip of moisture from a saturated lobe of fungus onto a lower one, a sound lost absorption two inches down.

This world is empty, yet it is not. The emptiness is volumetric. The space between the gentle, miles-wide curve of one plateau and the next is a vast, hollow gulf filled with nothing but cold air and mist. But the place is not without its titans.

Miles apart from one another, rooted deep in the living carpet, are the Stills.

They are not built. They have grown. They are entities that defy simple classification, occupying a space between mineral, plant, and machine. From a distance, a Still resembles a cluster of colossal, verdigris-coated copper pipes and boilers, fused together and half-sunk into the earth. They rise hundreds of feet high, their forms a jumble of tangled tubes, spherical chambers, and broad, finned surfaces that radiate a faint, internal heat. A closer look reveals their surfaces are not metal, but a bio-mineral secretion, like the chitin of an insect scaled up to the size of a factory. Faint, geometric patterns, like the memory of a circuit board, are visible beneath the patina of age and damp.

These are the planet’s engines. Slowly, inexorably, they perform a kind of geological digestion. Great, sphincter-like orifices at their base pulse almost imperceptibly, drawing in cubic tons of the vegetative mat. Deep within their bodies, a process of extreme pressure and cold chemosynthesis breaks down the organic matter, extracting minerals and energy. The only external sign of this immense, silent industry is a faint, resonant hum that is felt more in the bones than heard with the ears, and the slow, rhythmic venting of steam from high on their structures. This steam, thick with complex organic compounds, rises, cools in the thin, dark air, and becomes the source of the world's perpetual dampness, a closed and patient cycle. A faint, low light emanates from them, a dull blue and orange glow that shines through translucent panels in their bodies, illuminating the churning processes within. It is the only significant light source in this landscape.

While the space between the Stills is empty, the world within the mat is not. It is teeming with a silent, abundant fauna. Life here has adapted to pressure, darkness, and quiet. There are vast, slow-moving grazers—creatures like enormous, iridescent slugs the size of seals, their bodies shimmering with oily blues and purples. They navigate the dark, feeding on specific strata of the bio-mat. Hunting them are things that move with unnerving speed through the spongy terrain, blind predators that sense vibration and heat, their bodies flattened and wide to distribute their weight. Dig into the mat and you find a microcosm of branching fungal networks, colonies of phosphorescent mites, and worms made of a substance like cloudy glass. Life is everywhere, just hidden, playing out its dramas of predator and prey in a silent, cushioned arena.

The only real sound, besides the near-subsonic hum of the Stills, is the fizzing release of gasses from the digestive pools that sometimes form where the mat has been over-eaten or a creature has died and is being reclaimed. The air smells of cold ozone, wet stone, and the faint, metallic tang of the Stills' exhaust. There is a sense of immense, active process, but it operates on a timescale that is just beyond human. You can feel the thrum of the planet's metabolism, see the slow billowing of the clouds from the nearest Still, and know that beneath your feet, millions of creatures are living and dying. But it all happens with the ponderous, silent grace of an ancient machine that has long forgotten its creators, or never had any to begin with. It is a world in a state of dynamic, weird, and lonely equilibrium.
