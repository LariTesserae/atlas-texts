The most present thing is the sound. It is not the crash of a waterfall or the rush of wind, but a deep and resonant hum that seems to emerge from the ground itself. It is a loud, unwavering chord, a bass note held for a thousand years. The sound has texture; you feel it in the soles of your feet as a fine, high-frequency vibration, and in the hollows of your chest as a profound, sonorous thrum. This is a place that sings with water.

You are in a shallow, mist-filled basin on a high plateau. The scale is intimate, like a single, vast chamber in a cathedral carved from living stone and air. The ground is not soil but a continuous, spongy mat of emerald and jade mosses, saturated and yielding underfoot. Every step releases a small puff of cool, mineral-scented vapor and the faint squelch of trapped water being displaced. The air is warm, thick with moisture that beads on every surface, condensing on skin and hair, tasting of ozone and wet rock. Light filters down through the omnipresent mist, a soft, shadowless, pearlescent glow that illuminates everything evenly.

The source of the sound and the water are the stones. They are not mountains or cliffs, but enormous, smooth-worn monoliths that rise from the mossy floor. They look like immense, dark river-pebbles, some the size of a hut, others as large as a whale, scattered sparsely across the basin. Their surfaces are a polished, near-black igneous rock, but a closer look reveals them to be impossibly porous, riddled with microscopic, capillary-like channels. These are the weeping stones. Deep groundwater is forced upward by some immense geological pressure, seeping through the porous lattice of the stones. As the water moves through these millions of tiny, resonant channels, the stones themselves vibrate, producing their constant, chordal hum. The water emerges as a perpetual, silent dew on their surfaces, gathering into heavy, glycerine-like droplets that crawl down their curved flanks before dripping onto the moss below with a soft, thick *ploc* that is entirely lost in the greater hum.

This place is lush, but also empty. The sparseness is in the arrangement. Between the great, humming stones are expanses of open, mossy ground, giving the basin a feeling of profound and intentional emptiness. The lushness comes from the vegetation that has adapted to this unique environment. Growing in deliberate, isolated clusters are colossal ferns, their fronds the size of longships. Their central stalks are thick and black, almost chitinous, but the leaves are a marvel—they are not green, but a form of semi-translucent, crystalline cellulose. They unfurl in slow, geometric spirals, their vast surfaces catching the diffuse light and refracting it into faint, shifting rainbows. These glass-fronds are silent organisms, but they participate in the landscape’s water cycle. The heavy, condensing mist gathers in the upturned cups of their leaflets, and when a sufficient weight has collected, the leaflet slowly tips, pouring a clear stream of water back onto the mosses below in a slow, deliberate rhythm. The entire basin is a slow, patient, cyclical machine of water, stone, and plant.

There are no buildings here, no sign of any intelligent hand having shaped the place, and yet there is a form of technology. Where the weeping stones are oldest, their surfaces sometimes flake. The shards that fall away are night-black, vitreous, and possess an edge of impossible, molecular sharpness. They lie scattered on the moss like cast-off surgical tools. They are natural scalpels, geological artifacts that could be used for carving, cutting, or scraping.

Fauna is exceptionally rare. For days, you might see nothing but the slow unfurling of the glass-fronds and the patient weeping of the stones. But the ecosystem is not entirely without animals. In the shadow of a great monolith, something moves with geological slowness. It is a creature like a giant, six-legged woodlouse, its carapace the exact colour and texture of the damp moss it grazes upon. Its movements are so gradual, it appears static unless watched for minutes at a time. This is what lives here: things that have learned to accommodate the constant hum, to mistake it for silence. And in its mandibles, it holds a glistening shard of the weeping stone, using it to scrape away the toughest, mineral-rich lichens growing at the base of the singing monoliths. It is a tool-user in a world with no craft.

The basin is bounded. To walk towards its edge is to walk into a crescendo. The humming of the perimeter stones is louder, the vibration more intense, until it becomes a physical pressure against the body. The mist thickens here into an impenetrable white wall. There is no seeing beyond it, and to step into it is to be deafened by the convergent harmonies of the weeping stones. The world is just this one room, a resonant chamber humming softly to itself, forever warm, forever wet, and forever waiting.
