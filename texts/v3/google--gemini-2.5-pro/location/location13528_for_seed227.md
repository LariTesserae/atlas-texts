The first thing is the sound. It is not a thing you hear, but a thing you are inside. It is a physical pressure, a constant and unyielding shove against the bones of your skull, a vibration that works its way through the soles of your feet and lives in your marrow. It has no highs or lows, no rhythm or cadence; it is a single, eternal, granulated roar. It is the sound of the world’s largest river falling endlessly into a hole that has no bottom. Though the source is unseen, its presence is absolute. The sound is the architect of this place.

The air is cold enough to make your breath a thick, fleeting ghost. Each inhalation is a sip of chilled stone and wet earth. There is no wind, only this deep, cellar-cold stillness that is somehow made more still by the violence of the sound. The air is heavy with moisture, not rain, but a perpetual, atomized mist churned up by the unseen cataract. Everything is slick and beaded with condensation. To touch any surface is to come away with fingers numbed by cold and soaked with pure, ancient water.

This place is a hole in the world, a vast subterranean chamber or a sinkhole of impossible scale, hidden beneath a sky it never sees. The scale is intimate and crushing at once. The space is a hollow, a near-void of air and sound and mist, but the surfaces that contain it are intensely alive. The true ground is lost beneath a carpet of life that has grown here for ten thousand years. It is a deep, unbroken felt of mosses. They are not green, but a spectrum of pallid greys, bruised purples, and luminous, death-pale blues, starved of true light. Walking on it is like walking on sodden velvet laid over packed earth; your boots sink an inch, the sound of your passage utterly devoured by the moss and the greater roar.

The place is lush, but it is also empty. There are no forests, no thickets. Instead, individual, monumental life forms stand in stark isolation, hundreds of yards apart in the echoing dark. These are not trees. They are fungal spires, some as thick as a grain silo and reaching up into the oppressive black, their caps spreading out like pale, petrified thunderheads. Their surfaces are smooth and cool, like porcelain, and weep a slow, syrupy black liquid that the mosses drink. In other areas, colossal ferns grow, their fronds sixty feet long and unfolding with a geological slowness, each leaf a lacework of impossible intricacy. They don’t reach for light, but seem to sag, pulled down by gravity and the weight of the water beading on their surfaces. The lushness is not in density, but in the sheer, mad vitality of these few, ancient, solitary kings.

Scattered across the moss-floor and sometimes built into the curving stone walls are the remains of habitation. They are simple, low-domed huts, made of stacked, unmortared river stones. The stones are black, worn perfectly smooth by eons of water, and each structure sags, looking less like a building and more like a natural formation of boulders that has grown tired. They are all empty. Inside, the roar is slightly muted, becoming a deep, gut-rumbling hum. The floors are padded with thick, woven blankets of dried moss, still musty but cold to the touch. Stone tools lie where they were left: hand-axes with chipped, milky quartz heads, bowl-shaped grinding stones, and long, sharp slivers of obsidian that must have been carried here from another world. There is no metal. There is no fire. Any spark would be instantly extinguished by the damp.

Life, other than the plants, is profoundly rare. In the shallow pools that form in depressions in the stone, away from the direct mist of the falls, you might see a blind, white salamander, perfectly still for hours. Armored, pill-bug-like creatures the size of a human hand slowly, deliberately, chew their way through the decaying fronds at the base of a great fern. There are no birds, no buzzing insects, no mammals to speak of. Anything that relies on hearing to hunt or mate or survive would be driven mad.

The rule of this world is patience. The ferns uncurl over decades. The fungal spires may add an inch of height in a century. The river grinds the unseen chasm deeper by a millimeter a year. The people who built the stone huts must have lived by that same rule. They could not have spoken. Communication would have been by touch, by a slow and deliberate sign language, their thoughts paced to the ponderous growth of the world around them.

There is no light, save for the faint, ghostly bioluminescence of certain moss patches that pulse with a cold, blue-green fire. It is not enough to see by, only enough to outline the edges of the absolute dark, to give you a sense that the void has a shape.

To be here is to be gradually erased. The sound scours your thoughts, leaving only the dull, vibrating hum. The cold leaches your warmth. The damp seeps into your core. The darkness swallows your sight. You become another still object in the vast, roaring, empty room at the bottom of the world, waiting for the moss to grow over you.
