The ground here has no memory of being dry. This is a low-lying, basin-like expanse, a valley floor that stretches for miles, cupped by hills so ancient they have been worn into gentle, sleeping arcs against a permanently overcast sky. The air is cool, carrying the scent of wet stone, ozone, and something like the inside of a new machine. It is thick with moisture, not quite raining but always on the verge, condensing on every surface. Water weeps from the black, smooth rock of the hillsides and wells up from the ground itself, which is a sodden, yielding mattress of peat, hyper-vivid green moss, and a strange, resilient flora that is not quite either.

The sound is the first and most dominant sensation. It is not the sound of wind or water, though those are present. It is a deep, architectural hum that is more a pressure in the sinuses than a sound to be heard. This is the bass note of the landscape, a constant, foundational vibration. Layered over it are other, more chaotic noises: the sharp, crystalline chime of something shattering in the middle distance, followed by a profound silence from that specific point. The pop and hiss of static electricity arcing between the fronds of giant, glass-like ferns. And, most disorienting, a constant, high-frequency fizz, like a billion microscopic bubbles bursting in the air itself. This is the sound of reality losing its integrity.

This place is not natural, though it is devoid of any structure or artifact. It is the result of a long-running, catastrophic success of some form of technology. The physics of this valley are a managed and failing system. The land itself is a substrate, a computational medium saturated with processes that have run for so long they have become geological forces. The "erosion" here is not from wind or rain, but from eons of algorithmic abrasion, rounding the hills by repeatedly calculating their simplest form and nudging them towards it.

Vegetation is lush and unsettlingly perfect. Towering, black-stemmed ferns unfurl in a slow motion that is somehow too quick for the eye to comfortably follow. Their fronds are not cellulose but a kind of bio-silica, catching the dim, diffuse light from the grey sky and refracting it into slivers of algorithmically pure, impossible color. Patches of moss glow with a soft, internal light, their radiance pulsing in time with the deep hum of the valley. Flowers bloom from the sides of weeping rocks, their petals unfolding with geometric precision, revealing not stamens and pistils but complex, fractal emptiness. These plants are not parasites on the system; they are its peripherals, its external hardware, converting informational noise into physical form.

The water that saturates everything is heavy, slick, almost oily to the touch. It pools in shallow, tea-colored tarns and flows in streams that do not quite follow the path of least resistance. A rivulet might suddenly veer uphill for a few feet, shimmering with a strange tension before collapsing back into its gravity-fed course. To look into a still pool is not to see a reflection of the sky, but a slow-motion swirl of ghostly, after-image geometries and lattices of faint light—the visual representation of the data being processed and corrupted in the water-as-coolant.

Life is an anomaly. There are no birds, no insects, no mammals. The informational density and the constant acoustic efflorescence make complex nervous systems inviable. The only fauna are the Phase Grubs. They are slug-like creatures, a foot long, with bodies of opalescent, semi-translucent crystal. They have no visible sensory organs and move with an unnerving smoothness across the moss and rock. They do not eat; they are scavengers of reality-glitches. Where a pocket of local non-causality appears—a place where a stone might hang in the air for an hour, or light bends around nothing—a Phase Grub will eventually arrive. It will slowly absorb the anomaly, its crystalline body pulsing with soft light as it metabolizes the errant physics. They are the landscaper's janitors, or perhaps its tumors.

To stand here is to feel the world itself thinking, and in the process, going mad. The air shimmers in patches, not from heat, but from computational load. Sometimes, a tree-sized pillar of what looks like television static will flicker into existence for a few seconds and then vanish with a sound like tearing fabric. The density is not just of matter, but of presences; the past, future, and alternate presents of this location all feel near-touching, layered over one another like transparencies. The deep hum is the sound of the engine that holds them barely apart.

This is a settled place, an ecosystem in a state of long, slow decay—not biological decay, but ontological decay. It is active, constantly churning, but the processes are alien. The weather cycles are not meteorological but logical; a "storm" might be a cascade of corrupted data that manifests as a rain of non-newtonian fluid, or a period of intense gravitational fluctuation. This is what remains when a technology powerful enough to define reality forgets its purpose and is left to simply *run*. It is not a place to visit; it is a process to be caught in. To stay too long is to feel your own physical and mental states begin to waver, to feel the hum of the valley trying to find a place for you in its broken, endless calculations.
