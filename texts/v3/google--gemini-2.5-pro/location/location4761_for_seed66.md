The first thing you notice is the air. It’s warm, thick, and tastes of wet stone and ozone. It’s too heavy to be called mist, too thin to be called rain. It clings, condensing on any cool surface, which is to say, none. Everything here is warm, from the ground underfoot to the metal housings of distant engines. Water is a constant presence, not in pools or rivers, but as a universal dampness. It weeps from the rock, beads on humming conduits, and runs in slow, deliberate rivulets down channels carved into the very floor of the world.

This is a high place, a plateau of continental breadth, but there are no peaks or sharp ridges. Eons of slow, wet erosion have softened every edge, leaving a landscape of immense, undulating forms. The world is a series of vast, shallow basins and wide, rounded domes, like a sea of gargantuan, solidified bubbles. The material of this world is a dark, porous stone, ranging in color from charcoal grey to a deep, bruised purple. It’s slick with a perpetual film of moisture and, on closer inspection, a shimmering, organic glaze.

There is no soil, no grass, no tree. The category of "vegetation" is an empty one. But the world is not sterile. Far from it. The organic glaze on the rock is the base of it all: a planet-spanning super-organism of chemoautotrophic film, a thrumming, barely-living carpet that feeds on dissolved minerals from the weeping stone and atmospheric particulates. It is the world’s quiet, patient pasture.

And on this pasture grazes an apocalypse of fauna.

Life is not diverse here, but it is overwhelming in its density and biomass. It is a world of carapaced things, of arthropods and molluscoids, a kingdom of chitin and slime. Every surface is a tapestry of motion. Armies of fist-sized, iridescent beetles crawl in unison over the domes, their collective clattering a part of the permanent soundscape. They scrape the organic film with serrated mandibles. In the dells and basins where the water is thickest, slug-like creatures the size of sea lions wallow, their rasping radulae scoring patterns into the rock as they feed. Flocks of winged, insectile scavengers, more like metallic dragonflies than birds, patrol the air, their buzzing a high, anxious counterpoint to the drone of the world. Millipedes as long as trains, with hundreds of synchronized, percussive legs, migrate in slow, unstoppable rivers of segmented armor, flowing through canyons they themselves have worn over millennia. They are not a feature *in* the landscape; they *are* the landscape.

And in the midst of this, inseparable from it, is the city.

It is not a city built *on* the plateau. It is a city that has grown *from* it, and for it. Vast structures rise from the stone because they are of the same stone, carved and terraced and hollowed out. There is no clear line where geology ends and architecture begins. Buildings are simply more organized domes, more geometric basins. Walkways are smoothed-over ridges, highways are widened migratory paths.

This is a city of industry, but not for its own sake. The overwhelming fauna is not a pest to be endured, but a resource to be managed. This city is a ranch, a slaughterhouse, and a processing plant on a planetary scale.

Enormous pipes, slick with condensation and furred with the same living film, follow the contours of the land. They do not pump oil or water, but a slurry of harvested beetle larvae and crushed shells. Great, open-topped factories, more like arenas, hum not with the sound of manufacturing, but with the wet, organic sounds of sorting and rendering. Their engines are slow, patient, and immensely powerful, their thrumming a bass note that vibrates up through the soles of your feet. Steam and strange-smelling vapors rise from their vents, mingling with the natural dampness of the air to create a low, pearlescent ceiling that diffuses the "normal" light of the system's sun into a perpetual, shadowless glare.

The sound is constant and layered. At the bottom is the low, infrasonic thrum of the deep engines. Above that, the gurgle and rush of water in the endless channels. Then comes the life: the million-fold click and skitter of the beetle legions, the wet, rasping sighs of the great slugs, the percussive march of the deep-canyon millipedes. Woven through it all is the high whine of atmospheric processors and the hiss of steam from the city’s metallic arteries. It is loud, but it is not chaotic. It is the steady, established hum of a system in equilibrium.

Change here is slow, generational, geological. A new channel might be carved for a developing slug colony over the course of a century. A processing plant is not built, but grown and adapted over a thousand years, its walls thickening, its pipes rerouted to follow the slow, inexorable drift of a beetle swarm's nesting ground. The dynamic is not one of frantic action, but of a vast, unceasing metabolic process. The entire plateau—city, rock, fauna, and all—functions as a single, slow-breathing organism. Its lifeblood is the water weeping from its stone heart, its flesh is the chittering, crawling mass of its fauna, and its mind is the humming, industrial network that manages the eternal cycle of consumption and processing. To be here is to stand inside a creature of impossible scale, to feel its warmth, to hear its heartbeat, and to breathe its wet, living breath.
