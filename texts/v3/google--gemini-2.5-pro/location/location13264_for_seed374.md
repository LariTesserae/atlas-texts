The world is never quiet.

It is a place defined by its sound, a deafening, omnipresent roar that is the aggregate noise of its own creation. It is not the crash of thunder or the violence of an earthquake, but a constant, high-pressure hiss layered with the wet, percussive crackle of a billion cooling embers. It is the sound of a world perpetually being born, but never aging a single day. This roar is the bedrock of perception, the canvas upon which all other senses are painted. You feel it in your bones, a vibration that works its way up your spine and makes the fluid in your ears tremble.

The ground underfoot is warm and yielding, a vast, low-lying basin of soft, ochre-colored material. It is not soil or sand, but a mineral precipitate, a sinter that continuously and invisibly crystallizes out of the super-saturated air and the sluggish, pooling water. The process is one of geological birthing, raw and undeveloped. The landscape is a plain of embryonic stone. Walk ten paces and look back, and your footprints are already beginning to soften, to fill in as new micro-crystals form, knitting the wound you made in the surface. This material, when first formed, has the consistency of dense, wet chalk. Left to the air for a few hours, it hardens into a workable, porous rock the color of dried blood and rust.

Water is everywhere, but there are no rivers, no seas. The atmosphere itself is a heavy, dripping medium, so thick with moisture that distant forms are lost in a dim, ruddy haze. The light from the pale, smeared disk of the sun is weak, a perpetual twilight that filters through the humid shroud. Droplets condense on every surface, tracing slow paths down vertical faces before dripping into shallow, alkaline pools that shimmer with an oily, mineral sheen. These pools are not sources, but sinks, places where the water gathers before seeping back into the ever-growing sinter floor or evaporating back into the dense air in a cycle without end. The air smells of hot metal, wet clay, and ozone.

There is no green. Not a single leaf, blade of grass, or strand of moss. The base of the ecology is the rock itself. A shimmering, biofilm of chemoautotrophic organisms—neither plant nor fungus—spreads across the newest, wettest formations of sinter. This film is the pastureland of this world. Upon it graze a staggering abundance of creatures. Vast herds of trilobite-like grazers, their bodies the size of dinner plates, move in slow, clicking tides. Their undersides are fitted with rows of flint-hard mandibles that audibly scrape the mineral-rich film from the rock, the sound of a million tiny chisels adding to the world's overwhelming hum. They are prey for sleek, six-legged stalkers that move with an unnerving silence, their padded feet making no sound on the soft ground. Their hunting tactic is ambush, a sudden burst of speed to shatter a grazer's carapace with armored forelimbs. The air, too, is populated, not by birds but by leathery, dragonfly-like creatures that swarm in dense clouds, their wings a dry, frantic rustling. They feed on microbes suspended in the thick air itself.

And in the midst of this static roar and mineral birth, there is the city.

It is not built so much as it is excavated from the world's own flesh. It is a dense, sprawling, urban lattice that covers the valley floor from one hazy wall to the other. There is no plan, no grid, only an organic accretion of structures that have grown over millennia. The builders, a stooped and silent people, work with simple tools of bone and hardened sinter. They do not quarry rock and transport it; they carve their city directly out of the newly-hardened ground. They work in teams, their movements rhythmic and unceasing. A new floor of sinter forms, and they carve it into dwellings, passages, and plazas. As the ground level perpetually rises, they carve new levels on top of the old, burying the city of their ancestors beneath their feet. The city is layered downwards for an unknown depth, a vertical timeline of habitation, each stratum a generation's work.

The architecture is one of subtraction. Doorways are simple arches cut into walls. Stairs spiral down into the layered gloom of the under-city. Great, sweeping terraces are carved to channel the constant atmospheric weeping into cisterns. The density is staggering. Walls are shared, rooms flow into one another, and public spaces are often just wider points in a continuous, carved warren. The population of builders lives in near-touching proximity, their lives a constant, shared labor. They are as much a part of the world's balanced system as the grazers. Their purpose is not to conquer or change, but to participate in the one, single process of the world: its constant, unchanging creation.

This is the great paradox. The ground is always emerging, the city is always being built, the creatures are always feeding, but the landscape never changes. It is a system in a state of furious equilibrium. The roar you hear is the sound of this stasis. The scraping of the grazers is answered by the growth of the film. The carving of the builders is matched by the deposition of new stone. It is a place caught in a single, deafening moment of becoming, a moment that has lasted for eternity and will never, ever end.
