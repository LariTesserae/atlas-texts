This world is a sound before it is a place. It is a bone-shaking, tooth-loosening hum that is not a sound but a state of being. It presses in from all sides, a physical force that vibrates through the very medium you exist in. There is no air here. The space is filled with a heavy, super-pressurized liquid, warm and saline, thick as oil but clear enough to perceive the dimness. It is not water, not exactly, but a kind of industrial coolant, endlessly circulating. To be here is to be submerged inside the deepest, loudest part of an engine.

The world is a room. A vast, spherical chamber hidden kilometers below the surface of a larger, solid world. You are inside a geode. The walls are not rock but colossal, amethyst-dark crystal facets, some the size of city blocks, others a mess of fractal splinters. They don't reflect light so much as swallow it, their depths holding a bruised, purple darkness. The chamber is perhaps a mile in diameter, a perfect bubble of impossible geology buried in the planet's mantle. This smallness is deceptive; the emptiness within makes it feel infinite.

Suspended in the dead center of this crystalline sphere, tethered to the walls by a thousand taut, groaning cables, is the city. It is a work of industrial filigree, a sphere of scaffolding, pipes, pressure-domes, and slender towers that points no particular direction. It is a city built by engineers who had forgotten about gravity, or for whom it was only one of many forces to be managed. The structures are a tarnished, greenish bronze, slick with the thick liquid of the geode. There are no streets, only a web of enclosed catwalks and pneumatic transit tubes connecting humming substations and silent habitation blocks. It is undeniably urban in its complexity, its layered infrastructure, its sheer density of construction.

And it is almost entirely empty.

The deafening hum originates from the city's core. At its heart is not a star or a reactor in the traditional sense, but a vast, spherical impeller, a vortex engine of incomprehensible power. It is a machine that churns the very liquid of the geode, inducing a constant, chaotic turbulence. This is the source of the world’s two dominant states: warmth and turmoil. The liquid is never still. It boils without boiling, forming massive, slow-motion whirlpools and unpredictable shear currents that make the entire city sway and shudder on its anchor cables. The groan of stressed metal is a constant bass note beneath the overwhelming roar of the central engine.

Light is a rare commodity. It does not come from above or below, but bleeds from the city itself. Dim, corrosive orange light leaks from grimy portholes. Stuttering blue arcs of electricity flash intermittently between capacitor towers. Navigation lights on automated maintenance drones—slow, beetle-like machines that crawl along the city's hull—blink a lonely, methodical red. The overall effect is of a city perpetually caught in a dim, stormy twilight, the crystal walls of the geode occasionally catching a stray spark and glinting with a deep, hungry purple.

There is no native biology here. The world is mineral and metal. No soil, no plants, no fungus. The city's surfaces, where they are not polished by the turbulent flow, grow a kind of metallic rime, a slow crystallization of the minerals suspended in the coolant liquid. It forms brittle, feather-like structures, the only thing here that resembles growth.

Life is an anomaly, an afterthought. The only fauna are the Tarnish Eels. They are not fish, but sinuous, meter-long creatures of flexible, segmented metal, like living strands of coaxial cable. They are rarely seen, preferring the deepest maze of conduits and heat exchangers. They do not eat, but draw energy directly from electrical leakage and galvanic corrosion, their smooth, bronze-colored bodies sometimes glowing with a faint blue aura of stolen power. They are drawn to decay, to the places where the ancient city is finally beginning to fail.

This place has existed for a very long time. It is established in its own chaos. The ceaseless churning of the liquid has weathered the city's outer surfaces to a smooth, organic finish. The sharp corners of its initial construction have been rounded over millennia. It is a system in a strange, violent equilibrium: the engine churns the fluid, which erodes the city, which is repaired by the automated drones, which are powered by the engine. It is a closed loop of creation and destruction, a storm in a bottle. Its purpose is lost, or perhaps it never had one beyond its own deafening, turbulent existence. To be here is to be a single, silent thought in the mind of a screaming god-machine, a ghost in a factory that builds nothing but its own noise.
