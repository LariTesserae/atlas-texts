The air has no memory of the sun. It is cool, heavy, and thick with the smell of wet metal, ozone, and something like crushed rock mixed with damp cellar earth. This place is a basin, a hollow carved or collapsed long ago, and its edges are lost in the perpetual dimness. The scale is intimate, like a cathedral forgotten underground. You could walk from one unseen boundary to another in an hour, if you could find a clear path.

But there is no clear path. The floor is a treacherous, dense tapestry. Smoothed, time-worn plates of rusted iron lie half-submerged in a dark, gritty slurry. Thick, rubber-coated cables, now calcified with mineral deposits, snake between them like the roots of dead, geometric trees. Nothing here is sharp. Eons of slow dripping and steady, low-energy friction have rounded every edge, softened every break, so that a fallen gantry and a natural stone outcrop have the same weathered, settled texture. They are becoming each other.

Water is the blood of this place. It does not rain from an open sky. Instead, the atmosphere itself seems to be endlessly condensing. Water weeps from the walls of the basin, which are a layered fusion of bedrock and ancient, riveted metal plates. It drips constantly from a sky of tangled pipes and conduits overhead, each drop carrying a trace of rust or verdigris to add to the slurry below. The sound is not of a downpour, but of a thousand tiny, uncoordinated clocks, a persistent, gentle *tick-tick-plock* that is the dominant, yet somehow quiet, music of the world. In the lowest points of the basin, the water pools into shallow, mirror-still ponds of absolute black, their surfaces disturbed only by the slow march of a falling drip or the brief, concentric rings of something moving beneath.

There are no plants. Not a single leaf, blade of grass, or strand of moss. The primary layer of life is something else, a kingdom that bridges the gap between geology and biology. Vast, undulating veils of litho-fungal mats cover everything that holds still for long enough. They are pearlescent, grey, and ochre, shimmering with a faint internal light in some patches, and utterly light-devouring in others. They are slick to the touch and feed directly on the mineral-rich water and the electrical leakage from the failing, ancient technology. In some places, these mats thicken into solid-looking structures, resembling shelves of polished onyx or clusters of pale, fleshy nodules that pulse with a slow, deep-blue glow. This is the pastureland of this world.

And where there is pasture, there are grazers. The fauna is abundant, but silent. Creatures like oversized, flattened woodlice, their carapaces an iridescent bronze, move in slow, deliberate trails across the fungal mats, their multiple J-shaped legs making no sound on the slick surfaces. Their mouthparts scrape continuously, leaving faint, phosphorescent trails that fade within minutes. In the pools, things like metallic eels, jointed like spinal columns, swim in lazy figures-of-eight, sipping at the charged water. Skittering sounds betray the presence of smaller, faster life: multi-limbed scavengers, no bigger than a hand, that dart from the shadow of one metal plate to another, their bodies a perfect camouflage of rust and mineral-stain. They are hunting for the weak, or for the rare bounty of a fallen scraper.

All of this life operates in a dim, constant twilight. The only illumination comes from the ground-up: the bioluminescence of the fungal fields, the glowing trails of the grazers, and the steady, unwavering glow of countless small, recessed indicator lights. Red, amber, and green lights blink and shine from panels set into the metal walls, from the hearts of decaying machinery, and from deep within the water pools where some submerged device still reports its status to a long-dead master. They are as ancient and natural as the stars would be, if there were a sky to see them.

The entire place hums. It is a sound more felt than heard, a low B-flat vibration that seems to emanate from the very rock and metal. This is the one remaining clue to the world's origin. It is the sound of a vast,
subterranean engine or generator, still turning, still producing the faint electrical charge that powers the indicator lights and nourishes the fungal mats. It is the heart of the ecosystem.

Occasionally, and with no discernible pattern, a section of the machinery overhead will cycle. A great, groaning *clank* will echo through the basin, followed by the hiss of a pneumatic release and the grinding of gears. For a moment, a shower of fine, rust-colored particles will drift down, or a fresh gush of lukewarm water will pour from a newly opened valve. These events are the equivalent of seasons. The fauna reacts instantly. The skittering scavengers swarm the new deposits, and the eel-like creatures congregate under the fresh flow. It is a system in a strange, mechanical-biological balance, an engine that has become a world, an automated factory that has become a self-sustaining terrarium, ticking along in the deep, quiet dark. The only rule is the hum. As long as the heart beats, the strange life it has fostered continues its silent, lightless dance.
