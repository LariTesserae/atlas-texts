This is an interior place, a chamber carved from the highlands not by water or wind, but by a principle of growth. You enter it not through a door, but by finding a seam in the world, a place where the universal weave of root and stem loosens just enough to admit a body. The air that spills out is shockingly cold, carrying the scent of stone wet with snowmelt and a deeper, greener perfume of crushed fir needles and black soil.

The space is a cathedral, but a living one. Its scale is contained, perhaps the size of a great market hall or a small town square, but the density is absolute. There are no clearings. There is no discreet ground. The floor, walls, and ceiling are one continuous, interwoven substance: a form of vegetation that serves as its own substrate. This is the Frost-Garden, a city built by a single, silent architect.

The material of this place is a kind of cryo-lignin, a dark, almost black cellular mass that is slick to the touch, like wet slate. It is packed into immense, organic structures that resemble the logic of a settlement. Great, arching buttresses of living wood curve overhead, not to support a stone roof, but to form one, their smaller offshoots and broad, frost-rimmed leaves layered so tightly that only a dim, bruised-purple light filters through. These are the eaves of the living city. Pillars, thick as ancient redwoods, are not monoliths but colossal, fused bundles of vertical trunks, their surfaces grooved with channels through which a viscous, antifreeze sap slowly pulses. You can just perceive its thrumming, a vibration felt more in the bones than heard with the ears.

This is a wet place, but not with rain. The atmosphere is saturated, a perpetual mist that clings to every surface. The cold—a sharp, biting cold that hovers just above freezing—causes this moisture to condense constantly. Water beads on the black surfaces of the "buildings," tracing slow paths down their flanks before pooling in the spongy, moss-like vegetation that constitutes the "ground." This ground is not soil but a dense carpet of living tissue, which gives underfoot with a wet sigh. Here and there, water weeps directly from the plant-architecture itself, a biological exudate that freezes into delicate, crystalline filigrees of ice along seams and stress-lines.

The landscape is young. Sharp, new growths, the color of pale green jade, pierce through the older, blacker structures like splinters. A new arch might be growing, its leading edge a sharp, almost crystalline point of cellular division, pushing aside millennia of settled growth with imperceptible force. The edges of the broad, canopy-leaves are serrated and sharp. The whole place has the feeling of recent, rapid formation, a geological process enacted at the speed of biology.

This is an urban space without inhabitants, an infrastructure without purpose—or at least, without a purpose an outsider can comprehend. "Streets" are not paved avenues but deep, winding channels in the living floor, their sides slick with algae and their bottoms filled with a slush of shed organic matter and freezing water. These passages twist between the tower-like clusters of fused trunks. These "towers" are not hollow; they are solid plant matter, their highest reaches crowned not with crenellations but with massive, sealed pods that look like gargantuan, unripened pinecones. Perhaps they are reproductive organs, waiting for a signal a thousand years hence to crack open and release their spores into the high-altitude winds.

It is overwhelmingly quiet. The thick mass of vegetation dampens all but the most intimate sounds. There is the slow, steady drip-drip-drip of water falling from a leaf-eave to a waiting pool. There is the impossibly deep, resonant groan of a major buttress shifting its weight. And there is the sharp, sudden *crackle* of a new sheet of ice fracturing as the plant structure beneath it minutely expands. Occasionally, a breeze finds its way through some high, grown-over aperture, creating a low, fluting whistle that echoes through the dim halls.

Fauna is nearly nonexistent. The ecosystem is almost entirely closed, the plant-city feeding on its own decay, drawing minerals from the rock it has enveloped, and harvesting light through its dense canopy. But if you press your face to the slick black "walls," you might see them: Rime-Mites. Tiny, almost microscopic creatures with carapaces of what looks like spun glass, they move with geologic slowness, scraping the thin films of algae and bacteria that grow on the perpetually condensing surfaces. They are less animals and more a part of the city's self-maintenance system, living sandblasters polishing the ever-growing walls.

The world here operates on a single, implacable rule: everything is part of the Garden. There is no distinction between organism and environment, between architecture and occupant. The city is growing, breathing, and repairing itself on a timescale that is both immediate—the freezing of a water droplet—and impossibly ancient. It is a metropolis of one. To be here is to be a foreign body, a temporary flaw in its perfect, cold, living solitude. You are tolerated only as long as you are irrelevant to its slow, immense, and silent processes.
