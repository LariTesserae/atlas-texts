The first thing you understand is the sound. It is not a collection of noises, but a single, solid object that fills all space. It is a deafening, bass-heavy, all-encompassing Hum. It is the sound of a trillion tiny frictions, a billion chittering mandibles, a million scraping legs, all resonating in the immense, enclosed air of the under-world. The Hum is so constant and so total that it becomes the definition of silence. True silence, the absence of the Hum, would be a cataclysm. It presses on you not like a wave, but like a great, lithic weight. It is the sound of the world’s own metabolism, made audible and inescapable.

You are standing on a plain of impossible flatness, a floor laid down by the patient settling of dust over millions of years. The ground is a fine, cool powder, shades of grey and ochre, soft as flour underfoot. There is no soil, no loam, no hint of moisture. Poke a finger into it and it leaves a perfect, dry impression. The air itself is arid, carrying no memory of rain. It is cool and still, heavy with the scent of ozone, crushed minerals, and a vast, organic musk, the exhalation of a billion bodies.

This world is below. Far, far below any surface that might know a sun. The ceiling is a sublime rumour, a darkness so profound it might as well be infinity. Here and there, across distances that feel like miles, are monolithic pillars, columns so incomprehensibly thick that they are not geological features so much as they are fundamental architecture. They are the bones of the world, worn smooth by an erosion so ancient it has forgotten what a sharp edge ever was. They are the result of stalactites and stalagmites that met before the first soft-bodied things crawled on the planet above, and have spent every eon since just getting wider, merging, becoming one with the rock.

From the featureless dust of the plain, things grow. This is a lush place, but it is not green. Scattered in sparse, elegant copses and isolated, sculptural forms are growths of what can only be called vitreous flora. They are forests of pure, raw mineral, drawn up from the deep rock by some lightless, waterless alchemy. Some are like colossal fans of mica, translucent and shimmering with a faint, internal light. Others are thickets of gypsum crystal, branching like white coral and catching the ambient glow. Great, bell-shaped geodes, some as large as huts, lie half-buried in the dust, their interiors a glittering violet that pulses with a slow, biological rhythm. This vegetation does not drink; it metabolizes the stone itself. Growth is a process measured in centuries. To see a new crystalline leaf unfurl is the work of a lifetime.

And among these mineral groves, life swarms. This world is overwhelmingly faunal. The source of the Great Hum reveals itself. The ground is not still. In braids and rivers, in sheets and tides, are seething masses of insectoid creatures, each no larger than a thumb. They are the world’s blood cells, flowing in endless, purposeful circuits. Their chitinous bodies are the colour of rust and polished jet. They are the baseline of existence here, the primary movers.

Their work, their instinct, has built a city. In the middle distance, rising from the plain to brush the unseen ceiling, is not a mountain, but a Terminus. It is a biological metropolis, a hive-structure of continental scale. It is constructed from cemented dust, metabolic waste, and the bodies of its own dead, all fused into a porous, organic cement. It has no windows, no doors, no streets in any recognizable sense. It is a single, unified organism pretending to be architecture. Its surface is a history of its own growth, with the lower, most ancient levels worn as smooth as river stones, and the higher, newer reaches showing a rougher, more complex texture of tunnels and pores. From every one of these countless apertures, the architect-insects spill out or pour in, their collective movement a visible, shimmering haze that cloaks the lower reaches of their world-sized home.

Larger things move through the groves of crystal. They are the grazers. Creatures the size of cattle, with six thick, columnar legs, move with immense slowness. Their bodies are low-slung, covered in thick, matte-black plating that seems to drink the dim light. They have no visible eyes. They navigate the sparse landscape with an unerring sense of purpose, lowering their great, wedge-shaped heads to scrape at the crystalline flora. The sound they make is a percussive, high-pitched grating, a nail on a blackboard the size of a mountain. This sound is a thread of treble woven into the deep bass of the Hum. They are not eating the crystal so much as abrading it, consuming the fine powder their rasping mouthparts create. Their passage leaves shimmering trails of mineral dust that hang in the still, cool air for days.

The light is a constant, sourceless twilight. It is the sum of a million tiny biological glows. The fungal-like growths on the cavern walls pulse with a soft blue. The mica-fans of the flora give off a gentle green. The insects themselves carry a faint, orange luminescence in their abdomens, and their collective flow turns the plains into a slow-motion river of embers. Shadows are deep, soft, and everywhere.

There are other lives. In the upper gloom, things with vast, membranous wings glide on the imperceptible thermal differences between the living city and the cool stone, hunting the smaller fliers that flicker like motes of dust in the twilight. The powdery ground itself is home to burrowers, and the cast-off carapaces of the grazers are consumed by pale, soft-bodied decomposers. It is an ecosystem utterly complete, functioning on principles alien to a world of sun and water. It is not dynamic; it is stable. It is a system that found its equilibrium in a primordial age and has seen no reason to change. The city grows, but so slowly it is a geological process. An individual creature lives and dies, but the Hum is eternal. To stand here is to feel like a fleeting thought in a mind made of stone, sound, and time.
