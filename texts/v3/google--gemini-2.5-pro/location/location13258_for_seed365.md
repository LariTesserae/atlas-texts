The silence is not an absence, but a presence. It is a thick, heavy blanket woven from mist and cold. The only sound that has ever truly existed here is the gentle, metronomic *tok* of a water droplet letting go of a high ledge to fall an impossible distance, striking a flat, black stone below. It is the place's heartbeat, a rhythm so slow and spare it only serves to deepen the quiet.

This place is a basin, a caldera, perhaps a cathedral carved by a god who valued intimacy over grandeur. The scale is contained, graspable. One could walk from edge to edge in half a day, if the terrain were not so treacherous. The walls rise up sharply, jagged and new. They are made of a black, vitreous rock, like obsidian or cooled magma, fractured into enormous, sharp-edged shards that lean against one another. There is no softness of erosion here; the mountain is a fresh wound in the world, its edges still keen. The air is thin and carries the bitter, clean scent of ozone and wet stone. It is perpetually cool, the kind of chill that sinks into your bones not through wind, but through simple, persistent contact with the damp air.

Mist is the local form of water. It boils up from unseen vents in the caldera floor and pours over the high rims, a constant, slow-motion waterfall of cloud. It catches on every surface, condensing into a slick, clammy film. Water beads on stone, on leaf, on chitin. It is this condensation that feeds the basin's impossible lushness.

The vegetation is not a carpet, but an arrangement. There is much open space, fields of the sharp, black gravel and polished stone. But where things grow, they grow with theatrical vigor. Succulent monoliths, the size of small huts, stand in isolation, their thick, pale-green leaves swollen with water and curled into the shapes of cupped hands. Vast, waxy ferns unfurl in slow motion, each frond the size of a longship's sail. And everywhere, clinging to the vertical rock faces and spreading in filigreed patterns across the ground, is a phosphorescent moss. It is the only significant source of light, casting a dim, blue-green wash over everything. The world exists in a perpetual, submarine twilight, where shadows are soft and deep, and distances are deceptive.

This dim light reveals the place's most unsettling truth: it is overwhelmingly, suffocatingly alive. But it is a silent life.

The air is thick with not just mist, but with fliers. They are not birds or bats. They are moth-like creatures the size of a human hand, with heavy bodies and wings of black velvet. They fly without a sound, their wings making no disturbance in the air, moving in dense, hypnotic swarms that drift like smoke. On the ground, the open spaces are not empty. A closer look reveals that the black gravel itself is in motion. Millions of beetle-like creatures, each no bigger than a thumbnail, form a living carpet. They are the decomposers, the grazers of the glowing moss, their chitinous bodies a glossy, iridescent black that blends perfectly with the stone. Their movement is a constant, silent, liquid shimmer across the floor of the caldera. Larger things move among them: herds of six-legged grazers that look like armored woodlice scaled up to the size of sheep. They move with a weightless, deliberate grace, their jointed legs making only the faintest *schhh* of shell scraping against rock as they browse the giant succulents. There are predators, too—things that uncoil from the shadows like living ink, all sinew and polished carapace, hunting by sight and scent alone in this soundless world.

And there are people. Or there were. This is a city, of a kind. Built not upon the land, but *within* it. The inhabitants carved their settlement directly into the sheer, crystalline walls of the caldera. There are no buildings, only hollows. Ledges were widened, caves were smoothed into chambers, and great, sweeping staircases were hewn from the living rock. The finish is extraordinary; the black stone of their dwellings is polished to a mirror sheen, reflecting the dim, mossy glow. The "city" is a sparse network of these black mirrors embedded in the cliffs, connected by swaying bridges woven from the pale, tough fibers of the giant ferns. The technology is primitive—stone tools, woven fibers, patience. The immense labor implies a society organised and focused, a true polis.

You can see the logic of their existence. In a world defined by a crushing, silent biomass, they sought refuge in the vertical plane. They lived their lives on the cliff faces, suspended between the shimmering floor and the swirling mist above. They were a people adapted to silence. Perhaps sound was a taboo, a danger that attracted the darkest things from the deepest shadows. Or perhaps their bodies were simply not made for it. They must have communicated through gesture, through the intricate patterns they carved into their obsidian walls, through a language of touch and sight.

The city is active, but not with its builders. The ecosystem is in full, dynamic swing. The silent moths pollinate the waxen flora. The herds of grazers trim the succulents, their passage clearing paths for the glowing moss to spread. The predators stalk the herds in a slow, patient ballet of survival. Water condenses, pools, drips, and is absorbed. The mist ebbs and flows, sometimes revealing the entire, breathtaking scope of the basin—the scattered, luminous plants, the web of silent bridges, the shimmering plains of insect life—and sometimes closing in until the world is nothing but the single ledge you stand on. It is a bell jar, self-contained and teeming, operating by a set of rules that have no parallel in the loud world outside. All of it unfolding in a quiet so profound that the single, patient *tok* of a falling water drop feels like a shout.
