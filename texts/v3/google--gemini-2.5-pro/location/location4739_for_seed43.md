This place is called the Kiln, though no fire has touched it for ten thousand years.

The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a roar, but a constant, high-density clatter, a million tiny sounds of scraping, clicking, and chittering layered so thick they become a single, solid texture in the air. The sound is wet, echoing in the close, warm humidity that clings to your skin like a second layer of clothing. The air smells of damp terracotta, animal musk, and something else, something faintly sweet and fungal, like overripe fruit left in a cellar.

You are standing at the bottom of a wide, circular pit, perhaps a hundred paces across. It is not a natural feature. The world here is entirely constructed, a vessel made of fired, reddish-brown clay. From the floor, concentric tiers rise up towards a distant circle of open sky. There are perhaps thirty of these tiers, each as tall as a person and wide enough to walk along, forming a colossal, inverted ziggurat sunk into the earth.

Everything is worn. The sharp corners of the bricks and blocks, painstakingly stacked by some long-forgotten hand, have been rubbed smooth. The faces of the tiers were once covered in intricate, low-relief carvings—spirals, lattices, processions of stylized figures—but time, condensation, and the ceaseless activity of the fauna have softened them into a bumpy, suggestive topography. They are like memories eroded into the very substance of the place.

Water is not abundant, but it is constant. The Kiln is a giant rain-catcher. Built into the lip of each tier are shallow, winding gutters. When the infrequent rains fall, they are channeled along these paths, spiraling down the structure to feed a series of small, moss-rimmed cisterns and pools carved into the wider tiers. Even when it is not raining, the warm air, meeting the cooler mass of the subterranean clay, weeps condensation onto every surface. Droplets gather and crawl down the walls, tracing the ancient gutters and keeping the pools topped up. The clay is perpetually dark with moisture in these channels.

Vegetation is a tertiary presence. There is no soil. Instead, growing directly on the damp clay, and especially thick around the animal droppings that pepper every horizontal surface, is a sparse fuzz of pearlescent, grey-green mold. It’s not quite plant, not quite fungus. In the diffuse light from the sky above, it has a low, oily sheen. This is the entirety of the Kiln’s flora: an opportunistic film that lives on the waste of its fauna.

And the fauna is everywhere. The source of the endless noise is a creature known as a Tile-skimmer. They are the size of a man’s hand, a six-legged fusion of gecko and starling. Their bodies are flat and wide, the colour of the wet clay, with iridescent blue-black heads. They do not fly so much as they flutter and glide, launching themselves from a high tier to a lower one with a papery rustle of their short, membranous wings. Their primary mode of movement is climbing, their six splayed feet tipped with hooked claws that make that incessant scraping, clicking sound as they swarm over every vertical surface. They move in a constant, frenetic flurry, a liquid sheet of life pouring over the architecture. Tens of thousands of them.

Their ecosystem is simple and furiously active. The stagnant pools of captured rainwater are breeding grounds for swarms of tiny, gnat-like insects. The Tile-skimmers feast on these insects, their quick, sticky tongues lashing out to snatch them from the air. The skimmers themselves are the primary food source for a slower, more deliberate predator: the Tier-Stalker. These are long, sinuous reptiles, the colour of dry brick, and almost perfectly camouflaged when pressed flat against a tier wall. They are rarely seen, but their presence dictates the flow of the skimmers, creating sudden voids in the chittering curtain of bodies as they hunt.

The whole Kiln is a single, self-regulating machine. The architecture funnels the water. The water breeds the insects. The insects feed the skimmers. The skimmers feed the stalkers. The waste of all this life feeds the scavenger molds, which slowly break it all down, preventing the place from being buried in its own effluence. The structure is ancient, settled, and in a state of near-perfect equilibrium. The life within it, however, is a chaotic, loud, and dynamic storm, a churning engine of consumption and procreation contained within its static, earthenware shell. It is a cathedral built not for worship, but for a very specific, and very loud, form of life.
