There is no surface. The concept is a myth, a flawed extrapolation. There is only the medium.

It is a low-temperature liquid, denser than water, holding in suspension an impossible quantity of fine, dark sediment. It is a colloidal sea, an abyss of grit held in a state between solution and silt. The pressure is immense, a constant, monolithic weight that has squeezed all frivolity from existence. It is cold, not the sharp, biting cold of ice, but a deep, energy-leeching gelid state that slows chemistry and thought. This is the Underside.

The floor of this world is ancient beyond measure. Over eons, the turbulent medium has acted as a universal polishing cloth. There are no sharp edges. Every feature of the bedrock has been worn into a soft, undulating smoothness. Great, whale-backed domes of basalt-like rock rise from the abyssal plain, their forms so gentle you could mistake their slopes for flat ground. They are the Drones, hills that have slumbered under the scouring flow for geological ages. Between them lie vast, concave basins, filled with a deeper, looser sediment—a black, silty powder that behaves like a fluid in its own right.

The defining paradox of this place is the union of primordial stability and ceaseless chaos. The bedrock is the past, settled and eternal. The medium is the present, a state of perpetual, violent change. There is no wind or weather as a surface-dweller would know it, but there are currents. These are not gentle streams; they are colossal, invisible rivers of differing density and temperature that tear through the ambient slurry. A region can be still for a century, the fine silt settling into a fragile, temporary crust, only to be obliterated in an instant by an inexorable, slurring wall of motion.

These deep currents are the world's only engine. They are silent but for a sound you feel more than hear: a low-frequency, pervasive hum. It is the sound of a trillion, trillion microscopic particles grinding against each other and against the ancient stone of the Drones. It is the sound of planetary-scale erosion happening in real-time, a sound so constant it becomes the definition of silence. In the rare moments between the passing of major currents, a deeper quiet falls, a pressure-hush where the only sound is the impossibly slow settling of individual mineral flakes.

The darkness is nearly absolute. Light cannot penetrate the saturated medium for more than a few feet. What little illumination exists is organic and fleeting. On the sheltered, downstream flanks of the great rock domes, where the currents are weakest, sparse life clings to existence. These are the Glass-Knots. They are not plants, but chemosynthetic colonies, drawing sustenance from mineral seeps in the bedrock. They grow in brittle, interlocking crystalline structures, like formations of frost. They filter elements from the water and, as a metabolic byproduct, shed a cold, momentary light. A single Glass-Knot might pulse with a faint blue-white luminescence once an hour, or once a day. A field of them, seen from a distance, is a constellation of intermittent stars, their flashes revealing for a heartbreaking second the sheer, empty vastness between them.

Life is an event. The ecological pyramid is so flat it might as well be a line. On the basin floors, living within the top layer of shifting silt, are the Crawlers. You do not see them so much as the evidence of them: broad, shallow furrows plowed through the sediment, sometimes miles long. They are heavy, armored creatures, their carapaces geological in their thickness, designed to withstand the grinding currents and the immense pressure. They are scavengers and grazers, consuming the fallen particles of Glass-Knots or, more often, simply ingesting the mineral-rich silt itself. They move with ponderous patience, anchoring themselves with hooked limbs to ride out the passage of a current. A Crawler might use a loose, heavier stone to brace itself—not a made tool, but a found object used with instinctual purpose, then discarded when the turbulence passes.

Far rarer are the Phantoms. They exist in the mid-body of the medium, creatures of the currents themselves. They are gelatinous, almost entirely transparent, their forms constantly mutable. They are filter feeders, distending vast, near-invisible membranes to trap the few motes of organic matter drifting in the slurry. Their encounters with the bottom are accidental and often fatal. Their only real defense or communication is a sudden, shocking discharge of brilliant green bioluminescence, a flash that illuminates the swirling chaos of the water for a split second before a predator strikes or a mate is found. This flash, to a Crawler on the floor below, is a meaningless flare in the upper dark, a sky it can never reach.

The rule of the world is this: solid ground is a lie. The only permanence is the ancient, scoured bedrock, but to rest on it is to be exposed to the full, grinding force of the currents. The only safety is in the loose, shifting silt of the basins, but this ground is inherently unstable, a bed of quicksand that could be swept away at any moment. Life exists in the negotiation between these two impossibilities. It is a world packed to bursting with moving particles, yet it is profoundly empty. It is a place of immense, silent violence, where the land has been worn to a state of near-peace, and the only dynamism left belongs to the turbulent, all-encompassing sea of silt that will, in another billion years, finally grind it all to nothing.
