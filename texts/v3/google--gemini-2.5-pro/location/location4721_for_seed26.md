This place knows no sun. It is a basin carved from function, a valley of industry sunk deep beneath the surface. The air is cold, heavy, and wet, tasting of ozone, chilled iron, and something else, something like the smell of a stone dug from deep, damp earth.

The ground underfoot is not rock or soil. It is a continent of riveted iron plates, vast and dark, slick with a perpetual, weeping moisture. Gutters wide as roads are cut into this iron floor, sluicing sluggish, faintly iridescent water towards unseen drains. The plates are not uniform; some are diamond-plate for traction, others are smooth and treacherous, others still are grates of immense thickness, through which a dimmer, more heat-hazed light can be seen, and from which a constant, deep humming emanates, a vibration felt more in the bones than heard.

The scale is breathtaking and disorienting. This is not a room, but a region. The walls of the basin curve away and up, so far and at such a shallow angle that the ceiling is a concept, not a feature one can easily see. It is a manufactured sky of scaffold-struts and cabling, a colossal, dimly lit web that fades into a perpetual twilight far above. Spotlights, spaced miles apart it seems, cast long, hazy cones of weak, yellow-white light through the condensing atmosphere, illuminating motes of dust and tiny droplets of water that fall like a ceaseless, microscopic rain. Away from these cones of light, the darkness is profound but not total, filled with the residual glow of countless status lights, indicator panels, and the dull cherry-red of superheated parts.

This is a young place, or a place constantly being remade. There is no soft patina of age, no gentle rust. Instead, decay here is sharp and crystalline. Along the seams of the iron plates and at the base of structural pillars, a strange efflorescence grows. Not mold, but brittle, needle-like shards of an oxide-salt, sometimes white, sometimes a virulent turquoise. They crackle underfoot, sharp as glass. This is the terrain's equivalent of scree, the first stage of its own unique, aggressive erosion.

And it is loud. The sound is a physical presence. It is a symphony of inhuman industry, layered and complex. At its base is the profound, sub-bass thrum of the floor-grates, the vibration of planetary-scale machinery somewhere below. Layered over this is the rhythmic, cavernous *ka-chunk… ka-chunk* of immense pistons, so far apart that you can count a dozen heartbeats between each stroke. Then there is the hiss of steam from a thousand pressure-relief valves, the high, tearing shriek of metal being machined on a distant automated lathe, and the percussive, ever-present drip and drizzle of condensation—the Weep. The sound echoes, but the space is so vast and the air so thick that the echoes are blunted, returning as a muddy, continuous roar rather than a sharp report.

Life here is an afterthought, an intrusion. There is no green. The "vegetation" is sparse and alien. In the deepest, wettest corners, where runoff pools before entering the great gutters, there are patches of a strange bioluminescent lichen. It casts a ghostly blue-green glow, feeding on the metallic soup of the water. It is cold to the touch and smells of burnt wiring. These glowing patches are rare, navigational hazards or landmarks in the gloom.

Movement is constant, but not biological. This place is dynamic. High above, suspended from the ceiling-web, colossal gantry cranes slide along unseen tracks. Their movements are slow, ponderous, accompanied by the groan of stressed metal and the wail of warning sirens that cut through the background noise. They carry objects of incomprehensible size and purpose, their forms indistinct in the high darkness. On the floor itself, broad, flat-topped crawler-drones, the size of houses, navigate the iron plains on their own inscrutable errands. They have no visible driver or operator, only a single, searching red light on their front that sweeps back and forth across the slick plates. They are the true inhabitants, the predators of this ecosystem.

And the fauna? Almost non-existent. In the deepest, fastest-flowing sluiceways, a flick of silver might be seen, a flash of movement too quick to register. Stories are told by the maintenance crews of 'conduit-eels' or 'wire-worms', creatures that have somehow adapted to the electrified, chemical-rich waters, but a sighting is a rare and often-doubted event. The true "animals" are the smaller machines: cleaning drones the size of dogs that scuttle along the edges of the gutters, their bristling appendages scrubbing at the crystalline growths; repair bots that clamber like insects up the sides of the great support pillars.

A rule of this world is that everything has a purpose, but that purpose is not for you. This entire basin is a machine, a single component of something larger. Perhaps it is the sump and cooling-system for a city that blankets the surface world above. The cold is generated, the water is a coolant, the machines are all part of a process that keeps the world overhead functioning. The air is processed and vented, the waters are filtered and recirculated. It is a lung, a kidney, a liver for a body that knows nothing of its existence. To be here is to be a microbe in the guts of a leviathan, an environment active, loud, and utterly indifferent to your survival.
