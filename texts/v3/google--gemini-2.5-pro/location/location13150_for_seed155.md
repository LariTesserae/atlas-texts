The first thing you notice is the sound. It's not a single noise, but a chord of industry, a vast and dissonant hum that has soaked into the very substance of the world. It’s a B-flat of immense, grinding weight, shot through with the higher-pitched whine of turbines and the arrhythmic, percussive clang of multi-ton hammers striking somewhere unseen. The sound is so pervasive it's become a form of silence; you only register its components when they shift. It is the sound of the city, and the city is the world.

You stand on a wide, open plateau. The ground isn't earth or rock, but a resilient, living material, a kind of planetary callus. It's the color of old cork and bruised mushrooms, and it gives slightly underfoot, like petrified flesh. It's damp to the touch, cool and slick with a constant, thin film of condensation, as if the whole world is perpetually breathing on a cold windowpane. The air itself is cool, thin, and heavy with the smell of ozone, wet cellulose, and hot metal.

This is a high place. The concept of a horizon is complicated here. Across the expanse, which feels miles wide, rise the true structures of this landscape. They are not mountains, but growths. Colossal, woody, burl-walled towers, their bases miles in diameter, ascend into the foggy upper atmosphere. They are spaced far apart, creating a profound sense of sparse grandeur. These vegetable pillars are the high-rises. This is the city.

The built environment is excavated, not constructed. The faces of the pillars are pocked with openings—some are raw, gaping wounds leaking a slow, viscous sap, others are framed with intricate latticework carved from the living wood itself. Balconies jut out, their railings twisted into baroque forms by the plant’s own slow growth around some long-rusted metal armature. These are the homes, the workshops, the public squares. Between the impossibly distant pillars, thick cables are strung, sagging under their own weight. Upon them, skeletal, box-like gondolas crawl, their electric motors adding a faint, shrieking descant to the industrial thrum.

The source of the noise, the heart of the city's activity, is grafted directly onto the living architecture. Massive, rust-colored machines are bolted to the sides of the pillars. Great geared wheels, hundreds of feet across, turn with geologic slowness. Pistons the size of locomotives drive into the plant-flesh, pumping rhythmically. This is vascular mining. The city is tapping the sluggish, nutrient-rich lifeblood of the world-plant. Vents and stacks billow plumes of steam that are not smoke, but superheated water vapor driven from the processed sap. This is the source of the dampness, the artificial weather system of condensation and drizzle that keeps the surface slick. The runoff, a lukewarm, tea-colored liquid called the Weep, carves shallow, temporary rivers into the ground-flesh before being reabsorbed.

For all this activity, the place feels profoundly empty. There are no birds. No insects drone in the damp air. No lizards skitter across the living ground. The ecosystem is a stark triumvirate: the single, immeasurable plant organism, the city carved into it, and the machines that feed on it. It is a biological cycle replaced by a mechanical one. The only movement, apart from the machines, is the impossibly slow torsion of the pillars themselves as they grow, and the occasional, shuddering groan that travels through the ground-flesh—a sound of protest, or perhaps just digestion.

And then there is the weirdness. The reality here is soft at the edges, worn thin by the ancientness of the substrate and the brute force of the industry. The constant, droning frequency of the Churn, as the processing is known, has a strange effect on perception. Distances, for instance, are not fixed. The pillar that seems five miles away might suddenly feel reachable, its carved windows sharpening into focus, only to recede again into an indistinct haze. The open space between the towers is a canvas for auditory ghosts; you can hear machinery that isn't running, the echo of a hammer blow from a crew that packed up yesterday.

The light is normal, a familiar sun-white that filters through the perpetual high-altitude haze, but it falls on a non-Euclidean geometry. The shadows cast by the gondola cables sometimes seem to bend at impossible angles on the ground below. Looking down a long, carved avenue inside a pillar, the vanishing point has a tendency to drift. The rules are not broken, merely… negotiable. The world is an ancient, dreaming entity, and the industrial clamor is giving it a strange, fitful sleep. To live here is to develop an intuition for the instability, to know when to trust your eyes and when to trust the trembling in the soles of your feet, to navigate a world that is not a fixed map, but a constant, dynamic, and deeply loud negotiation between the living and the built.
