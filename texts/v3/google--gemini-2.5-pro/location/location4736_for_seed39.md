The air is thin, tasting of ozone and wet metal. You stand on the floor of a great upland basin, a valley cradled between peaks so high they seem to scratch the belly of a sky that is permanently bruised. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight filtered through a high, unbroken ceiling of cloud. It’s a cold that is less about ice and more about a profound absence of energy; a deep, cellular chill that seeps through layers, drawn out by the dampness that clings to every surface.

This is a new place. The mountains are not the old, time-worn, dignified ranges of other worlds. They are a worksite. Their slopes are not smoothed by erosion but are chaotic, jagged, and raw. They look unfinished, like skeletal scaffolding. Great, sheer faces of black basalt and gabbro are interrupted by unruly gashes and violent upthrusts of rock that haven't yet settled. It is a geology that is still in the process of becoming, an embryonic landscape whose bones are still knitting. The process of erosion has barely begun; every edge is sharp.

And yet, it is wet. The water doesn't come from rain, which seems incapable of forming in the frigid, stable air. Instead, the rock itself weeps. This is the engine of the world. Across the dark faces of the new mountains, thick, milky-white veins of some crystalline mineral pulse with a faint, internal light. These are not passive geological features; they are active. They are the planet’s factories. From hairline fissures along these geode-veins, a steady, viscous, mercury-like fluid beads and drips, tracing shimmering paths down the rock. This fluid is warmer than the surrounding air, and where it meets the cold stone, a cloud of vapor instantly condenses. The entire basin is filled with this ground-hugging mist, a perpetual exhalation from the living rock. The fluid collects in pools that are not clear but iridescent, swirling with dissolved metals, their surfaces shifting with oily rainbows.

There is life, but not as it is commonly understood. Vegetation is almost non-existent. There is no soil, no grass, no trees. But clinging to the rock, especially near the weeping fissures, are thin, microbial films. In the dim light, they look like patches of black velvet, but they are chemo-synthetic, drawing their entire existence from the strange chemistry of the mountain’s effluent. This is the entirety of the basin’s flora: a sparse, greasy smear at the bottom of a vast food web.

The quiet is deep, but not absolute. It is a canvas against which small, specific sounds are painted with unnerving clarity. There is the constant, almost subliminal *tsssk* of water condensing on cold surfaces. There's the slow, rhythmic *plink... plink...* of the viscous fluid dripping into the iridescent pools. And beneath it all, if you stand perfectly still and hold your breath, you can feel more than hear it: a low, resonant hum vibrating up through the soles of your feet. It is the thrum of the geode-veins, the deep, planetary machinery at work.

This emptiness is punctuated by an abundance of fauna. This is the central paradox of the basin. The creatures here do not eat plants; they consume the mountain itself. Small, beetle-like organisms with carapaces of polished chrome-rock swarm over the weeping veins, their hardened mandibles scraping away the mineral crust. They are the size of a fist, and their skittering sounds like fine gravel being poured onto a sheet of metal.

Grazing on a larger scale are the Clatterherds. These are six-legged, barrel-bodied creatures, covered in a shaggy, insulating coat of what looks like spun fiberglass. Their heads are low-slung, protected by a thick, bony plate, and their mouths are wide, lipless slots filled with grinding surfaces of incredible hardness. They move in slow, deliberate lines across the rock faces, licking the mineral-rich fluid and using their mouths to shear off and pulverize chunks of the softer, nutrient-laden stone around the veins. The sound of a herd eating is a discordant, metallic grinding, a slow-motion rockslide that echoes miles across the basin.

The herds are in constant, slow motion, a nomadic cycle dictated by the pulsing activity of the veins. And where there are grazers, there are predators. Solitary hunters, called Ridge-Stalkers, are things of lean muscle and impossible agility. They are long-bodied, like weasels scaled up to the size of a wolf, with soot-black fur that renders them nearly invisible against the dark rock. Their claws are not for slashing, but for hooking, allowing them to move with terrifying speed and silence across vertical and inverted surfaces. They are ambush predators, launching themselves from the jagged heights onto the backs of the Clatterherds below. The only giveaway of their presence is a sudden, panicked bellow from a herd, a brief, violent sound of crushing rock and tearing flesh, quickly muffled by the damp, heavy air.

The world is active. The fog ebbs and flows as the veins pulse with greater or lesser intensity. A tremor will run through the ground, and a new fissure will split a rock face a dozen miles away, its accompanying groan echoing for minutes before a new stream of life-giving fluid begins its slow descent. The herds follow these new sources, and the stalkers follow the herds. It is a complete, functioning ecosystem, built not on sunlight and soil, but on geothermal pressure and raw chemistry. It is a world under construction, and life has not waited for it to be finished. It has simply adapted to the noise and dust of the worksite, finding its niche amidst the raw materials, on the factory floor of a planet being born.
