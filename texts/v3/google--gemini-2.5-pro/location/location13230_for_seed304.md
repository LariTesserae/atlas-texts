This is the Puddle-City of Umbril. It occupies a shallow, dish-like depression, perhaps a mile across, carved into a plateau of dark, glistening stone.

The ground is never truly dry. It is a mosaic of shallow, interconnected pools and slick, black flagstones. These are not separate materials; the flagstones are simply the higher ground of a single, monolithic substance that forms the entire basin. This stone—known to its inhabitants as Sobbing Slate—is the city's foundation, its building material, and its water source. The slate is a deep, bruised purple-black, shot through with milky white veins that resemble frozen lightning. It has a unique property: it perpetually weeps. Water, cool and tasting faintly of ozone and salt, seeps from every surface, collecting in the low spots and running in shallow, intricate rivulets down every vertical face. The air is heavy with condensation, and at eye-level, a thin, perpetual mist hangs, blurring the edges of things more than a hundred feet away.

The city itself is an exercise in absolute density. There are no plazas, no open squares. It is a single, contiguous structure. Buildings are carved directly from the living slate of the basin floor, or built from quarried blocks of it, fitted together without mortar. They press against one another, their walls shared, their roofs overlapping like the scales of a fish. The "streets" are narrow, shoulder-width canyons, their floors channeled to guide the constant flow of water. To cross from one district to another, one often has to travel through the lower levels of several buildings, a dim and dripping internal transit. The architecture is sharp and geometric. Because the Sobbing Slate is relatively easy to carve but hardens upon prolonged exposure to air, the lines of the buildings are clean and angular, a stark contrast to the organic flow of the water that slicks them. These are young forms; the city feels freshly hewn, its edges still holding the bite of the tools that made them.

There is almost no soil. Vegetation is consequently rare and specialized. Clinging to the shaded, perpetually damp walls are patches of Glimmer-Moss. This is the city's primary flora, a biomechanical wonder that draws minerals directly from the weeping stone. During the day, it is a velvety, dark green, but as the normal daylight fades, it begins to phosphoresce with a soft, blue-green light, its intensity varying with the mineral content of the water it’s consuming. The glowing moss provides a secondary, ethereal illumination to the city's nights, its cool light reflecting in the thousand puddles and streams.

Fauna is scarcer still. The dominant lifeform, besides the builders, is a creature known as a Chitin-Slug. These creatures, the size of a human hand, are perfectly adapted to this environment. Their shells are made from the same minerals as the Sobbing Slate, making them almost impossible to distinguish from a wet rock when they are still. They move with an unnerving slowness, their single muscular foot leaving no trail on the wet stone. They graze on the Glimmer-Moss, their slow, rasping feeding the only natural sound besides the water. Their presence is a quiet, geological process, another slow part of the city's machinery.

The world is governed by the rhythm of the weeping. The flow is not constant. It follows a slow, tidal pulse that seems unrelated to the moon or sun. For several hours, the seepage will increase, the water level in the channels rising, the sound of trickling becoming a more insistent murmur. This is the "High Weep," or the "Sob." During this time, lower passages may flood, and the city's quiet is filled with the sound of a thousand gurgles. Then, the flow recedes to a gentle dripping for a period of "Low Weep," or the "Sigh." The inhabitants' lives are structured around this cycle—transport, maintenance, and quarrying are all timed to the geological breath of their home.

The tech is primitive and born of necessity. Tools are made of chipped flint, sharpened bone, and a specific type of petrified wood, dense and water-resistant, that is occasionally found in deep fissures. There is no metal, as any ore would be impossible to smelt in the damp, and any forged metal would quickly corrode into nothing. They work the Sobbing Slate with stone saws and bone chisels, a laborious process that requires immense patience. They are masters of stonework and water management, carving intricate spillways and cisterns to survive the Sob.

The atmosphere is one of profound, meditative quiet. The dense stone construction and the constant dampness absorb sound. There are no birds to sing, no wind to howl through trees. The soundscape is purely aquatic and architectural: the *plink-plonk* of individual drips in a cistern, the soft *hiss* of water sliding down a wall, the low *gurgle* from a drain, the distant, echoing *clack* of a stone tool on a stone block. Voices do not carry; they are intimate, close things. It is a city that murmurs.

Umbril is not a place of grand events, but of constant, subtle activity. The moss glows and fades. The chitin-slugs creep and graze. The water rises and falls. It is a city built inside a single, living geological process, a settlement packed so tightly that it has become its own small, self-contained world, cool, damp, and forever weeping.
