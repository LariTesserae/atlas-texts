The first thing is the sound. Not a sound, but *the* sound. It is a single, deafening, planetary chord of industry. It is the aggregate roar of a billion grinding, shearing, and percussive processes, all layered into a physical presence in the thin, cool air. The sound is so absolute it feels like silence's opposite, a solid block of noise that you have to push through. It vibrates in your bones, a deep, resonant hum; it needles at the edges of your hearing with the high-frequency shriek of stressed metal and fracturing crystal; and it punctuates itself with arrhythmic, continent-spanning *clangs* as titanic components lock into place or shear apart.

This is a world without water, a high-altitude plateau of incomprehensible scale. The ground beneath your feet is not soil or rock, but a crushed, sharp aggregate of shattered, metallic-sheened material. It's a scree of old parts, a detritus of industrial eons. Yet this world registers as total vegetation. Because from this floor of glittering technological dust, the forest rises.

It is a forest of machines.

They are vast, sparsely scattered pillars that are the world's flora. Some are hundreds of meters tall, others scrape the thin, bright upper atmosphere. They are not built, but grown. They are ancient, their forms weathered by ages of exposure to the fierce, unfiltered light of a brilliant, distant star. Their surfaces are a patina of iridescent oxides and stress-fractured ceramic, like the heat-scarred casing of a reentry vehicle. This is established form, erosion acting not on stone, but on alloy and composite. Though they are scattered, leaving vast, empty plains of dust between them, they are the totality of the landscape; there is nothing but them and the floor of their remains. They are the substrate and the architecture.

Each pillar is an engine. Vents open and close along their colossal height, releasing plumes of incandescently hot gas that leave no moisture, only shimmering heat-haze. Great, slow pistons, miles long, retract into the planetary crust and emerge again over the course of hours, their movement creating the deep, foundational hum of the world's sound. They are lithophytes, mineral-plants that draw their energy not from photosynthesis in the way of frail biology, but from geothermal heat, piezoelectricity generated by their own immense vibrations, and the raw, kinetic energy of the system itself. They are a self-sustaining industrial process.

The light is brilliant, merciless. The high elevation and thin atmosphere provide no filter. Shadows are knife-sharp and ink-black. The metallic sheen of the machine-flora and the glittering dust-floor catch this light, scattering it, creating a dazzling, painfully bright environment. There is no weather as we know it—no rain, no snow, no clouds. The turbulence of this world is one of activity.

And it is a turbulence of fauna. The spaces between the pillar-engines are not empty. They teem with an abundant, skittering, clanking ecology of autonomous machines. These are not organisms of flesh. They are geared, articulated, and powered. Some are like metallic insects the size of cars, scavenging the dust-floor, collecting valuable shards of fallen matter in articulated mandibles and carrying them back to the bases of the pillars. Others are great, slow-moving behemoths, crustacean-like hauliers that grind along on dozens of wheels, transporting larger, sheared-off components. Hovering drones, their forms like polished trilobites, dart through the air, their ducted fans adding a whistling shriek to the cacophony as they perform high-altitude maintenance, welding, or observation.

This is a world in constant, chaotic flux. The "anthill energy" is palpable. A pillar-engine will suddenly shudder, and a section of its casing, a piece the size of a building, will crack and fall, crashing to the ground with a deafening boom that is momentarily distinct from the background roar. Instantly, swarms of smaller automata converge on the wreckage, their cutting torches and grinder-limbs flaring to life, beginning the chaotic, inefficient, but relentless process of breaking it down and recycling it. The landscape is perpetually creating and destroying itself. A surge of power might cause a whole region of pillars to glow cherry-red, their thrumming escalating to a seismic vibration, before they discharge a massive arc of blue-white energy into the ionosphere.

There are no structures here that were *built*. This entire planet-sized machine is a form of life. It is an industrial entity that emerged, or was seeded, and has since existed in a state of weathered, violent equilibrium. Its purpose, if it has one, is lost to its own mechanical churn. It may be manufacturing something—spitting refined elements into space, or simply processing its own planetary mass into more of itself. It is a factory the size of a world, running without supervision for geological time. The air is cool and tastes of ozone and hot metal. The ground is a graveyard and a parts depot. The sky is filled with light and the deafening sound of a machine that has become its own ecosystem, a turbulent, waterless, and terrifyingly alive place.
