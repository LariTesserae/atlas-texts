This is the Singing Plateau, a place defined not by sight, but by sound.

The sound is the first and last thing. It is not a wind, for the air is unnervingly still. It is not the crash of water, for there are no great torrents. It is a hum, a drone, a chord of impossible scale that has been playing for millennia. It is a sound so deep and pervasive that it is felt more than heard, a vibration that works its way into the marrow of your bones, making your teeth ache and your vision swim at the edges. It is a deafening, absolute presence, the baseline reality of this world, a single, unchanging note held since the beginning of time. Silence is not an absence of this sound; it is a concept that cannot exist here.

You stand at the roof of the world, on an immense, gently undulating tableland of stone that stretches to every horizon. The elevation is extreme, placing you above the haze and the weather of a lower world. The sky is a deep, piercing violet-blue, and the light from the unblinking, white sun is so bright it seems to have physical weight. It bleaches the colour from everything, rendering the landscape in stark contrasts of white glare and knife-edge shadow. The air is thin, cold, and carries a clean, metallic scent, like ozone after a lightning strike. There is a sense of profound emptiness, of a space so vast and uncluttered it feels hollowed out.

The terrain itself is ancient, weathered into a state of finality. The plateau is made of a strange, semi-translucent stone, something between quartz and obsidian, polished to a low sheen by eons of a process that is no longer active. Great, smooth-shouldered monoliths rise from the plain, not jagged and sharp, but shaped like massive, worn-down teeth or the bells of titanic instruments. These are the Resonators. They are the source of the sound. Some internal, geological process—a static friction, a piezoelectric effect of unimaginable scale—causes these crystalline mountains to vibrate, and they have vibrated in this precise, unvarying manner for longer than any living thing can measure.

The fierce, unfiltered light and the constant, bone-shaking drone make for a hostile environment. Vegetation is almost non-existent. There are no trees, no shrubs, no grasses. But look closer at the shaded lees of the Resonators. Here, where the glare is softened and condensation gathers, the ground is damp. In these perpetually moist pockets, thin films of lichen—some a startling cobalt blue, others a powdery rust-orange—cling to the stone. They are the only fixed life, patterns of colour in a monochrome world, subsisting on trace minerals and the thin, vibrating air.

And yet, the place is not empty. It is teeming with life.

The fauna is abundant, bizarre, and perfectly attuned to this sonic world. They are the Choir. They are arthropodal creatures, ranging from the size of a thumbnail to the size of a human hand. Their bodies are low-slung, with many spindly legs that allow them to scuttle across the vibrating stone. Their most striking feature is their carapaces. They are not uniform, but are crafted from interlocking plates of the same crystalline material as the Resonators, polished and faceted.

These creatures do not eat the lichen. They do not hunt one another. They feed on the sound.

This is the fundamental rule of the plateau: sound is energy. The constant, overwhelming hum is a perpetual source of power. The Choir's crystalline shells are sonic collectors. They vibrate in sympathy with the landscape, and the creatures metabolize this resonance directly. They spend their lives crawling in slow, deliberate pilgrimages from one harmonic zone to another, their bodies shimmering as they absorb the acoustic onslaught. They glitter in the harsh sun, vast, silent swarms of them flowing like liquid mercury across the plains of stone, their movements the only dynamic element in a world locked in equilibrium.

There is a form of primitive technology here, but it is not human. The larger members of the Choir carry tools. Aforelimbs that have evolved into delicate manipulators are used to scrape and polish their own shells, tuning them to specific frequencies. They carry slivers of fallen stone, using them to flake away imperfections or to strike their own bodies, producing a faint, percussive *tink, tink, tink*—a tiny, personal counterpoint to the world's eternal drone. They use these chimes to communicate, to signal location and status in a way that can be heard above the roar. They are not building a world; they are maintaining their place within it.

To be here is to be an anomaly, a thing of flesh and blood in a world of stone and vibration. The light is blinding. The sound is an assault. The ground itself is a living instrument, and the air is filled with the silent, glittering dance of creatures made of crystal and sound. Nothing ever changes. The sun does not dim, the note does not waver, the Choir does not cease its slow, timeless pilgrimage. It is a place of perfect, deafening stasis, an ecosystem built not on a food chain, but on a single, unending chord.
