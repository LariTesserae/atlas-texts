The air is thick, and warm, and still.

You are on a high plateau, a tableland of immense scale, recently thrust up from some deep geologic slumber. The horizon is a serrated edge of newer, even-higher peaks, so sharp they look like shards of torn slate. There are no foothills, no gentle slopes that have been softened by millennia of rain and wind. This is a landscape of abrupt angles and fresh fractures. The stone underfoot and all around is a pale, milky grey-green, like jadeite mixed with cream. It is porous and strangely light, a form of travertine precipitated from the world’s omnipresent water, laid down in layers that are now broken and exposed.

This plateau is a city. Not a city of commerce or habitation as one might understand it, but one of form and structure. Immense, wide terraces descend from the central spine of the plateau, each level a vast, empty plaza hundreds of meters across. The ground is not paved with separate stones but appears to have been smoothed and polished as a single surface, slick with a constant, paper-thin film of moisture. The geometry is stark, monumental. The risers between these terraces are not walls but sheer, weeping cliff faces, ten to twenty meters high, perpetually dark with running water.

Water is the landscape's blood, but it has no pulse. It does not flow in rivers or gather in lakes. It emerges. It seeps from every crack in the rock, it oozes from the ground if you press down, it condenses on every surface. The weeping walls are curtains of silent, slow-moving liquid, catching the light in a steady, un-shimmering sheen. This water is perfectly clear and carries no sediment. It does not carve or erode; it merely anoints. The sound of this world is the infinitely layered sound of dripping—a soft, percussive ‘tock’ in a nearby crevice, a continuous wet hiss from a broad stone face, a deep, resonant ‘plock’ as a heavy drop falls from a high ledge into a perfectly still, street-wide puddle below. The sound is a form of silence.

Clinging to the edges of the terraces, rooted in deep, square-cut pits in the stone, and clustered in the damp crevices of the weeping walls is the vegetation. It is lush in its color and form—broad, waxy leaves of emerald and deep forest green; thick, sculptural succulents with geometric precision; feathery ferns the size of small trees. But there is no sign of growth. No new shoots, no withered leaves. Each plant is a perfect, static specimen, as if cast in wax. Not a single leaf has a hole eaten through it. Not a single frond is yellowed or torn. They are utterly, unnervingly pristine. They exist in defined, separate clusters, leaving the vast stone plazas completely bare. The arrangement feels deliberate, like a rock garden the size of a valley.

The structures of the city are one with its geology. There are no houses with roofs or doors. Instead, there are monoliths—vast, simple shapes of the same travertine stone, cut and placed with impossible precision for a pre-metal culture. Cubes, obelisks, and long, low rectangular prisms are scattered sparsely across the plazas. They have no apparent function. They are not adorned. Their only texture is the raw layering of their precipitated stone. They cast long, stark shadows that never move.

The light is the light of a perpetual high noon. There is no visible sun, only a uniform, pearlescent glow from the thick, hazy sky above. The world is brightly lit but without a direct source, eliminating glare and providing a soft, even illumination that reveals every detail of texture on the wet stone and motionless leaves. The shadows cast by the monoliths are permanent fixtures, regions of cooler, deeper grey against the pale green ground.

There is no life here that moves. No insects buzz in the humid air. No lizards skitter across the stone. No birds sing from the perfect trees. No fish trouble the surface of the mirror-still pools. The absence is not just a lack of sound; it is a lack of narrative, of cause and effect. Nothing chases, nothing flees, nothing forages, nothing builds a nest. The ecology of this place is a simple, two-part equation: weeping stone and waxen plant.

This world is not changing. It has never changed. It will never change. The water weeps but the level never drops. The plants are vibrant but they do not photosynthesize or draw nutrients. The city was not *built*; it simply *is*. It exists in a state of frozen, finished equilibrium. It is a monument to a purpose you cannot fathom, created by hands that worked with the patience of geology itself. To stand on one of the empty plazas is to feel like an unacceptable variable in an immaculate equation, the only thing in a million square kilometers that is subject to the passage of time. The air is warm and easy to breathe, but you feel a chill rise within you. The silence deepens, and you become acutely aware of the sound of your own heartbeat, a frantic, chaotic rhythm in a world of perfect, eternal stillness.
