The first sensation is not of sight, but of heat and pressure. The air is a solid thing, heavy and hot and wet, pressing in from all sides. It is so saturated with moisture that breathing feels like drinking. It carries a faint, metallic, and slightly sweet smell, the scent of warm minerals and decay on a scale so vast it is no longer offensive, merely geologic. There is no wind. The air is a stagnant, thermal blanket laid over a world that has not known a breeze for millennia.

Then, the sound. It is almost silent, but the quiet is not empty. It is filled by a single, endlessly repeating noise: a slow, soft, viscous *drip... plock*. Water, heavy with dissolved matter, gathering on some unseen surface high above and falling a long way to strike the shallow sea below. The interval between these drops is the only clock in this place.

When the eyes adjust, there is no dawn. The darkness is a fundamental property of the world, a black soup through which you must wade. But it is not total. Faint, ghostly traceries of light glimmer in the middle distance. They are not cast from a source, but seem to be internal to the things they illuminate—a dull, phosphoric green-white, like the afterimage of a flashbulb, but one that never fades.

These lights reveal a city.

It is a city in the same way a coral reef is a city. Skeletal towers of a dark, chitinous material rise from the placid, ankle-deep water. They are arrayed with an alien but undeniable intentionality, not in a grid, but in sweeping, fractal curves that echo the branching of neurons or the veins of a leaf. The structures are impossibly tall yet seem frail, riddled with pores and archways and hollows. They look less built and more grown, extruded over eons by a biological process that knew nothing of tools, plumb lines, or masons. The surfaces, slick with perpetual condensation, are smooth to the touch where they haven't been furred with a thin, slimy, black film—the only thing here that resembles vegetation. This film grows in patient, circular colonies, feeding on the mineral-rich water and the faint, ambient energy of the place.

The ground is not soil or rock, but a uniform, yielding mud, the silt of ages settled into a perfectly flat basin. The water covering it is warm, almost hot to the touch, and unnervingly clear despite its thickness. It appears oleaginous, clinging to the skin. To disturb it is to cause slow, syrupy ripples that travel for an unnervingly long time before dampening into stillness. There are no fish, no insects, no amphibians, not even microbes visible to the naked eye. The water is a sterile chemical bath, a medium, not a habitat.

This is a city without citizens. There is no fauna. No scurrying thing in the alleys, no bird in the lightless sky. The lack of movement makes the place feel both dead and intensely alive, as if you have found your way inside the architecture of a single, impossibly vast organism. The towers are its bones, the glowing filaments are its nerves, the black film is its skin or perhaps a symbiotic fungus. The city is one being, and it is either sleeping, dying, or living on a timescale so slow that its life is indistinguishable from geology.

The weirdness bleeds in from the edges of perception. Sometimes, a low, resonant thrumming can be felt more than heard, a vibration that works its way up through the soles of the feet and into the bones. It is the city's pulse, a slow, circulatory beat that shifts the great masses of fluid through its porous infrastructure. Following this thrum, new lights might kindle in a distant spire, a synaptic firing that takes hours to complete its arc across the skyline.

There are rules here, but they are not the rules of physics as we know them. Gravity feels… conditional. The immense height of the spires should not be possible given their perforated structure. They seem to hold themselves up by a kind of internal tension, a biological will. Sometimes, the water will inexplicably recede from one area of the basin and deepen in another, a tidal shift with no moon to command it. This is the being's slow inhalation.

The world is active, but its dynamism is that of a body at rest. A sudden warmth that washes through a sector of the city is not a weather front, but a fever. A slight tremor that shakes loose a film of slime is not a seismic event, but a metabolic spasm. Nothing happens quickly. A new archway might form between two towers, a slow secretion of material that bridges the gap over a century, a thought taking physical form. The city is thinking itself into existence, and it is a process that has been going on in the hot, wet dark for longer than the mountains have existed in the world outside. The silence is the space between its thoughts. The dripping water is the only sound it makes as it waits.
