This is a world of wood. There is no stone here. No soil, no gravel, no sand. The bedrock of this place is a compressed, petrified, and unimaginably ancient mass of what was once living cellulose. It is a substrate of lignin and fossilized fibre, kilometers deep, layered like sedimentary rock but telling a story not of geology but of botany. This is the **Great Tableland**, a plateau so vast it has its own Coriolis effect, so high its edges are lost in a different climate zone. But here, in its heart, the world has settled into its final form.

The ground underfoot is not hard, not soft. It is yielding, but with a profound, unhurried resistance, like pressing your thumb into the heartwood of an ancient tree. It is black-brown, slick with perpetual moisture, and crisscrossed with shallow channels where water, thick with tannins and spores, moves with the lethargy of cooling glass. This "ground" is the first and oldest layer of the forest, a floor made of its own ancestors.

From this foundation, life rises not in competition, but in a slow, architectural collaboration. What you might mistake for mountains on the horizon are, in fact, the trunks of the Arch-Boles. These are organisms of a scale that defies biology as a separate discipline; they are geobiology. Their bases are dozens of kilometers wide, their bark a craggy, furrowed landscape of its own, with cliffs and ravines scaled for smaller beings. They do not grow upwards so much as they have already *arrived*, ancient beings whose growth cycle is measured in glacial time. Their lowest regions are perpetually in shadow, cool and damp, draped in thick, velvety mosses that glow with a faint, phosphoric green.

The atmosphere is thick, warm, and heavy, like the inside of a lung. It is saturated with water. Rain does not fall from the sky; the sky is too close for that. A canopy, a true and final ceiling, forms the firmament of this world. Far, far above, the interlocked crowns of the Arch-Boles and a hundred other species of titanic flora have created a continuous layer of living matter. This Upper Canopy catches what little, distant sunlight filters through the planet's high, hazy atmosphere. Almost none of it reaches the lower world. The light here is a deep, eternal twilight, a diffuse green-gold glow that seems to emanate from the air itself.

Water, therefore, is a process of condensation and dripping. Every surface weeps. The undersides of the great leaves, the crags of bark, the dangling curtains of air-roots—all are jeweled with droplets. The water gathers, combines, and falls not as rain, but as a continuous, sound-dampening percolation. The loudest sounds are the resonant *plock* of a heavy drop falling a great distance into a pool of still, dark water, or the slow, groaning creak of a colossal branch shifting its weight, a process that can take a decade to complete.

Despite the dimness, secondary and tertiary layers of life thrive. The space between the forest floor and the Upper Canopy is not empty, but filled with a dense, layered lattice of limbs, vines, and epiphytic gardens. Some plants are like enormous, translucent bellows, slowly inhaling and exhaling the misty air. Others grow in spirals, creating staircases of broad, waxy leaves. Fungal forms abound, not as mere decomposers but as active structural elements, their mycelial networks binding entire groves together, pulsing with faint, rhythmic light that serves as a beacon or a warning.

Fauna is abundant, but almost silent. You do not see it at first. Then, you realize the bark on a nearby vine is slowly undulating—a colony of flat, chit-shelled grazers. The shimmer in that pool of sap is a swarm of minute, iridescent swimmers. Something that looked like a dangling, dead leaf detaches itself from its stem and glides on whispered air currents to another branch, a creature of membrane and patient hunger. The animals here are not intruders in the green world; they are extensions of it, their forms dictated by the absolute dominance of plant life. They are climbers, gliders, crawlers, and swimmers who live their entire lives within the wooden architecture. There are no herd animals that thunder across plains; there are no plains. There are only the endless, vertical and horizontal corridors of the wood.

And there are remnants. In certain clearings, where a truly ancient Arch-Bole finally fell eons ago, creating a momentary gap in the canopy, one finds the "structures." They are not built. They appear to be the fossilized, crystalline remains of something else. Imagine a beehive, but the size of a hill, and made of a translucent, amber-like substance. Its interior is a maze of geometric chambers, smooth and cool to the touch. They emit a low, sub-audible hum. They are not tombs or homes; they feel more like resonating chambers, artifacts of a biological process that has long since ceased, leaving behind its strange, perfect shell. In other places, one finds lone, monolithic slabs of what seems to be petrified pollen, their surfaces covered in patterns too complex to be accidental, too organic to be language. These are the faint marks left by a form of life that existed within the rules of this world—no tools, no fire, only biology as its medium.

To be here is to be inside a single, slow-breathing organism. The world is a process, not a place. The slow drift of spores is the weather. The gradual brightening of a bioluminescent fungal field is the dawn. Change is measured by the pace of a root wrapping around a fallen branch, by the centuries it takes for a water channel to carve a new path in the living wood. It is a world without hurry, a world that has absorbed time itself into its own fibre. The silence is not empty; it is full. It is the sound of life so pervasive and so ancient that it no longer needs to make a noise.
