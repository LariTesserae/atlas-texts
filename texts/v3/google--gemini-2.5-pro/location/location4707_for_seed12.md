There is no air here. Your entire existence is suspended in a medium, a liquid that is preternaturally warm and clear, thick as a thin, warm syrup. It presses on you not with the crushing hostility of a deep ocean, but with a constant, firm, and universal embrace. This is the first rule of the world: you do not breathe, you filter. You do not walk, you drift.

You are high up. Below is an abyss of impossible scale, a void of absolute black from which no light or information returns. But you are not at a peak so much as on a plateau, a vast, miles-wide mesa of rock that has shouldered its way up from the unseen depths. This rock is young, impossibly so. It is not the worn, ancient stone of a mountain range but the raw, glassy material of a world still being born. It is obsidian, basalt, and something else, a semi-translucent stone that seems to have cooled only yesterday. In many places, it is still warm to the touch, and faint, hair-thin cracks in its surface leak a lazy heat and a soft, internal luminescence. This entire mountain is a new tooth, growing slowly from the jaw of a sleeping god.

This nascent, glowing geology is the foundation for everything. Rising from the warm fissures and spreading across the plateau is a forest of impossible lushness. It is not a forest of wood and leaves, but of sessile, living structures that defy categorization. Great, broad fronds of a rubbery, amber-colored material grow in dense groves. They are not photosynthetic; they are chemosynthetic, drawing energy directly from the mineral-rich water seeping from the mountain's core. They sway in the almost imperceptible currents, their movements ponderous and slow, taking minutes to complete a single bow and return. Between these are stalks of what looks like spun glass, each one tipped with a soft, pulsing node of rosy light. The collective glow of this living landscape is the only illumination, a dim, perpetual twilight that is both comforting and deeply strange.

Woven into this dense, glowing forest is a city. Kaimer. It is not built *on* the plateau so much as it is carved *from* it and braided *with* its ecology. Great, sweeping terraces descend along the mesa's edge, their faces hollowed out into a honeycomb of dark chambers and open-fronted galleries. There are no doors, only flowing curtains of the living amber kelp, which have been patiently woven into a screen that still lives, still sways. The "streets" are not paved roads but wide, cleared channels in the geological floor, their paths defined by low, carved walls or lines of the pulsing, rose-lit stalks.

The technology here is one of patience and intimacy with the environment. The obsidian rock, though hard, fractures predictably. The inhabitants have used simple, heavy tools—perhaps crafted from the bones of some long-extinct leviathan, or a denser form of the local stone—to chip and flake away the rock, creating their spaces over generations. Everything has the soft, worn-in feel of manual craft. Intricate lattices are carved into the walls of the chambers, not for decoration, but to encourage the growth of smaller, moss-like bioluminescent organisms that provide a gentle interior light. Walkways that span the wider channels are not bridges of stone but living slings of the amber kelp, woven together while the fronds were young and then allowed to grow into a strong, flexible, living suspension bridge. The density is immense; every surface is either carved, inhabited, or covered in a living sheath of light-producing growth.

The soundscape is one of profound quiet. The thick water muffles everything. There is no birdsong, no wind, no clatter of feet. There is only a single, constant, bowel-deep hum that seems to emanate from the rock itself—the sound of the mountain's slow, geological growth. Occasionally, this hum is punctuated by a soft, wet chime as the broad kelp leaves brush against each other in a shift of the current. The sound of life is the sound of a slow pulse, a quiet filter, a shadowy form drifting with deliberate slowness through a glowing channel.

Animal life is exceptionally rare. Tiny, opalescent shrimp-like creatures swarm in clouds around the geothermal vents, and are the primary food source, but anything larger is a legend. The builders of this city, the Kaimeri, are the dominant fauna. They are slow, graceful, silent beings, perfectly adapted to this dim, warm, high-pressure world. To see one is to see a shadow detaching itself from other shadows, a streamlined form moving with the same patient rhythm as the swaying flora.

From the edge of the highest terrace, you can look out. The open scale of the world is breathtaking and terrifying. You are on an island of dim light and warmth in an ocean of utter cold and darkness. Far in the distance, separated by miles of abyssal void, you can see other, fainter points of light. Another mountaintop city like this one? A solitary, giant creature, glowing with its own strange purpose? A different kind of geological process? There is no way to know. The distance is too vast to cross. All there is, is this city, this patient, living, glowing singularity, slowly building itself, slowly growing, a single warm thought in a cold and silent mind.
