There is a stone, an ovoid boulder the size of a trade-wagon, half-buried in the wind-scoured dust of a high, ancient plateau. Its surface is basalt-dark and pocked, smoothed by ages of dry, thin gales to a matte finish. It is unremarkable among the other stones of this flattened, dead-calm summit. But it is not whole. A fracture runs its circumference, a hairline crack from some long-ago trauma, and from this crack seeps a low, constant hum and a palpable warmth.

To press one’s face to the crack is to be met not with a breeze, but with a pressure of air, thick and moving. It smells of hot stone, of ozone, and something else—a complex, musky sweetness like powdered, sun-baked honeycomb. The sound is not a single note, but a million tiny sounds layered into a single, overwhelming chord: a chittering, a scraping, a rustling, a constant, dry, percussive din.

Inside, there is no empty space. This is not a geode of stark, clean crystals. This is a world, a city, a forest, crammed into a chamber no larger than a small room. It is a Hush-Hive, sealed and self-contained.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight cast by the "vegetation." These are not plants of cellulose and water. They are slow-growing crystalline forms, vast, interlocking lattices of a mineral that faintly glows—a pale, phosphorescent green-gold. They rise from the "floor" of the chamber in thick, trunk-like spires, branching into delicate, razor-thin fans and fractal webs that nearly touch the curved "sky" of the boulder's inner shell. This is the Glass-wood. It grows through a process of piezoelectric accretion, drawing energy from the immense pressure of the surrounding rock and converting ambient grit into its own glowing structure. It takes a thousand years for a spire to grow an inch. The air is warm because of this constant, slow-burn energy release. There is no moisture here. Life is not based on liquid, but on solid-state exchange.

And this solid-state world is overwhelmingly, terrifyingly alive. The source of the deafening sound is the fauna. Every surface of the Glass-wood, every inch of the dusty floor, is swarming with creatures. They are arthropodal, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the length of a human hand. Their carapaces are not chitin, but spun and hardened silicate, shimmering with mineral flecks like living jewelry. The most numerous are the "Scrapers," beetle-like creatures with faceted eyes and mandibles of incredible hardness. They move in tireless rivers up and down the crystal spires, their mouthparts grinding away microscopic layers of the glowing mineral. This is how they feed, consuming the stored energy of the Glass-wood. Their scraping is the dominant note in the hive's roar.

Their waste is a fine, glittery dust that perpetually rains down from the upper reaches, coating everything. This dust is the soil. It is the foundation of the ecosystem. Smaller, mite-like creatures filter this dust for residual nutrients, and in turn are hunted by faster, centipede-like predators that dart through the undergrowth of fallen crystal shards. The air is thick with this dust, a dry fog that catches the dim light in shifting, shimmering curtains.

Interspersed with the wild crystal growth is the city. The largest, most stable spires of the Glass-wood have been hollowed out. The builders are a more specialized caste of the Scrapers—larger, slower, with broad, spade-like forelimbs. Using simple, hardened tools of shed exoskeleton, they have carved the forest's heart into a dense, vertical metropolis. Spiraling ramps, too steep for human feet, encircle the glowing pillars. Honeycomb chambers serve as nurseries for their pearlescent eggs. Bridges of spun mineral-fiber, sticky with a self-secreted resin that hardens on contact with the air, connect one tower to the next. It is a primitive city, but it is organized, dense, and ancient. Its architecture follows the logic of the forest, a settlement grown into its pantry.

There is a constant, frantic activity. The Scrapers mine the walls of their own homes, which are simultaneously, imperceptibly, growing back. Below, on the floor, chain-gangs of builders dredge the nutrient-rich dust into piles for new construction or to reinforce older structures. Larger, armored creatures, like fist-sized, six-legged armadillos, act as guardians, their heavy carapaces deflecting the careless slides of rock and crystal from the crowded spires above. Predator and prey dart through the carved alleyways. A creature will die, and its silicate shell will be immediately swarmed by decomposers who break it down, returning its mineral wealth to the dust-floor in a matter of hours. Life and death are a rapid, grinding, particulate cycle.

The entire world runs on this dry, silicate metabolism. Warmth is not from a sun, but from the slow decay of the rock itself. "Rain" is a fall of dust. Plants are glowing crystals. Animals are living stones. And it all exists here, in this single, loud, warm, hidden pocket, a universe of overwhelming life inside a dead rock on a silent mountaintop.
