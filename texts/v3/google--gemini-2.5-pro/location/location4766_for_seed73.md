The air is the first and final thing you notice. It is thin, betraying the impossible altitude, yet it is also heavy, thick with a heat so profound it feels like a liquid medium. It clings to the skin, beaded with moisture, and each breath is a conscious effort, a sip from a hot, wet towel. The atmosphere is a paradox: the oxygen-starved emptiness of a summit married to the suffocating humidity of a sealed greenhouse.

You stand on a vast, undulating plateau that stretches for miles in every direction. There is no soil here. The ground is a single, continuous formation of black, glassy stone, weathered over eons into soft, rolling hills and shallow basins. It’s not basalt or obsidian; it has a faint, internal lattice that catches the light in ways that shift with your viewing angle. The entire surface is slick, weeping a constant, clear fluid. It isn't rain. The sky above is a pale, hazy blue, mostly cloudless. The water emerges directly from the rock itself, condensing from the super-saturated air or drawn up from some unimaginable depth. It forms shivering films on the convex surfaces and gathers in wide, placid pools in the hollows. The water is warm to the touch, and tastes of ozone and dissolved minerals.

This is a world drowning in its own sweat.

From this wet stone, life erupts in a frenzy of green. It is a jungle, but one without dirt. Massive, banyan-like trees with slick, muscular roots anchor themselves directly into the weeping rock, spreading like grasping hands over acres of surface. Their broad, waxy leaves are the size of dinner tables, each one cupping a small puddle of condensed water. Beneath them, a chaotic understory thrives: ferns with fronds of an iridescent, coppery material; thick mats of moss that feel like plush, sodden velvet; and phosphorescent fungi that pulse with a slow, soft light in the deeper shade. The vegetation is so dense that in many places it forms a continuous, multi-layered canopy that the normal sunlight struggles to pierce, creating a world of dappled green twilight.

The soundscape is a constant, overwhelming drone. The loudest element is the ceaseless chorus of insects—a high-frequency, multi-tonal hum that vibrates in your teeth. It’s the sound of a billion tiny, jewel-toned beetles and cicada-like creatures with membranous wings. Layered beneath this is the wet percussion of the landscape: the slow, syrupy *ploc, ploc, ploc* of heavy drips falling from the immense leaves, the gentle gurgle of water overflowing from one basin to another, and the faint, almost subliminal hiss of condensation forming on a million surfaces at once.

And beneath all of that, there is another sound. A hum. It is a perfect, unwavering bass note, so low and so steady it seems to be a property of the rock itself. This is the only clue to the scattered, barely-present built environment. Here and there, interrupting the rolling black stone, are the Conduits. They are not buildings, but extrusions of a different material—a matte, bone-white ceramic that is utterly immune to the pervasive wetness. They are featureless and seamless, some rising as simple, thirty-foot pillars, others lying flush with the ground as perfect circles hundreds of feet across. They are ancient beyond measure, the black stone weathered smoothly around their unyielding forms. These are not ruins; they are operational. They radiate the immense, silent heat that turns the high-altitude air into a sauna. They are the engines of this place, the source of the geological hum, the regulators of a climate that has no right to exist. Their function is a slow one, a patient cycle measured in millennia.

Life here is abundant and strange, adapted to the vertical, wet world. Great, slug-like amphibians, the size of sheepdogs, crawl slowly over the weeping rocks, their skins shimmering with kaleidoscopic patterns. They graze on the thick mosses, their rasping mouths leaving clean black trails behind them. In the canopy, flocks of creatures that are somewhere between a reptile and a plant drift on the thick air. They have leathery, leaf-shaped gliding membranes and dangle tendril-like limbs to snatch at the oversized insects. There are no mammals to be seen. Everything is slick, amphibious, or chitinous.

From a high ridge, the scale becomes apparent. This plateau is a world unto itself, a tableland dozens of miles across. In the far distance, the weeping jungle thins, and the black stone rises to form a smooth, rounded rim. Beyond that rim is nothing but empty sky and, thousands of feet below, a global ocean of white clouds that stretches to the curve of the Earth. The light is bright but diffused, the whole sky a hazy, luminous ceiling.

This place operates by its own unbreakable laws. The heat does not come from the sun, but from the Conduits. The water does not come from the sky, but from the stone. Life does not root in soil, but clings to the wet, energized surfaces. Change is a gradual, almost imperceptible process—the slow spread of a moss colony, the patient crawl of an amphibian, the unhurried pulse of the light within a fungus. It’s a terrarium built on a mountain peak by forces beyond comprehension, an ecosystem sustained by a geology that is also a technology. And it has been running, uninterrupted and unsupervised, for a very, very long time.
