The world is built on a high, sprawling plateau of interlocking basins. The ground isn't soil or rock in the familiar sense, but a pavement of creamy, terraced sinter. It’s a landscape precipitated out of water. For millennia, superheated, mineral-rich water has gushed from deep within the earth, cooling as it spreads, leaving behind layer upon layer of siliceous deposits. The effect is of a world sculpted from bone-white and pastel-colored porcelain.

The air is a visible, tangible thing. It is crushingly hot and thick with water vapor that hangs in a perpetual, shimmering haze. The light is brilliant, a single, unfiltered glare from a white-hot sun that turns every wet surface into a blinding mirror. Visibility across the open basins is theoretically vast, but the heat-shimmer and steam conspire to dissolve distant forms into wavering, uncertain mirages. There is no sky, only a ceiling of luminous white haze.

This is a place of profound, almost absolute quiet. The muffling effect of the dense, wet air swallows sound. There is no wind, only the slow, convective roil of the atmosphere itself. What little sound exists is low and resonant: a deep, subsonic hum that seems to emanate from the ground itself, the quiet, rhythmic sigh of geothermal vents, and the viscous, slick-and-plop of water oozing over the slick terraces. It is a silence not of emptiness, but of suppression.

From this mineral landscape rises a city, or what passes for one. It is not built *on* the sinter, but *with* it and through it. Vast, arching structures of dark, oxidized metal rise from the centers of the basins. They are not towers, but immense, skeletal frameworks of pipes, radiators, and condensation fins, latticed and layered over centuries of accretion. These are the Exchangers. Their function is a slow, silent industry. They draw the superheated geothermal fluid up through titanic arteries, channeling it through miles of quiet, humming conduits. The intense heat differential between the fluid and the metal fins causes the thick atmosphere to condense on their surfaces. Pure, distilled water weeps constantly from every edge, running down dark metal channels to collect in shimmering, crystal-clear cisterns built into the sinter floor. The city's purpose is not habitation, but harvesting the sky.

The architecture is weathered, established. Rust-red and verdigris-green patinas crawl over the dark iron, stark against the pale ground. In many places, the slow deposition of sinter has begun to reclaim the machines, sheathing their lower levels in smooth, flowing stone, blurring the line between the built and the geological. It is a city in equilibrium with its own decay and renewal.

This world is devoid of plants, but it teems with life. The basis of the entire food web is the rich soup of chemicals and minerals in the geothermal water. The hot, flowing channels are lined with thick, gelatinous mats of chemosynthetic bacteria, ranging in color from sulphurous yellow to iron-oxide red and deep, manganese purple. These microbial mats are the pastures of this world.

Grazing upon them are the most common fauna: the Sinter-Scrapers. These are broad, flattened creatures, the size of large flagstones, with low-slung, multi-jointed legs that move with a deliberate, trilobite-slow gait. Their carapaces are iridescent, seeming to be made of the same opalescent silica as the ground they walk upon, providing perfect camouflage. They move in silent droves across the terraces, their undersides equipped with rows of rasping radulae that scrape the bacterial jelly from the stone. They leave behind clean, white trails on the colored mats. Their only sound is a faint, wet grating, easily lost in the ambient hum.

Higher up the food chain are the Heat-Stalkers. They are slender, almost skeletal creatures that blend in with the pipes and struts of the Exchangers. Their bodies are a dull, non-reflective black, like cooled iron, and their limbs are impossibly long and spindly. They move with an unnerving fluidity, clinging to the industrial structures, their forms lost in the complex shadows. They are patient predators. A Heat-Stalker will wait for hours, motionless, its head cocked, watching a drove of Scrapers. Its eyes are clusters of heat-sensitive pits. When it strikes, it is a silent drop, a swift and precise puncture of the Scraper’s shell with a needle-like proboscis, followed by an equally silent retreat to the high architecture to let its injected enzymes do their work.

There are other things, too. In the clear, hot water of the cisterns, translucent, shrimp-like organisms paddle with glassy limbs. Hovering over the steam vents are creatures that look like nothing more than drifting, gelatinous sacs, sustained by some unknown process, their bodies rippling with faint, internal pulses of bioluminescence—a silent form of communication.

The world operates on a slow, cyclical dynamic. A geyser might erupt, changing the flow of water and minerals, creating a new sinter terrace. A bacterial bloom will follow, shifting the color of an entire basin. The Scrapers will migrate, and the Stalkers will follow. An old section of an Exchanger, weakened by heat and corrosion, might finally sag and collapse with a muffled, watery crunch, the only loud noise for a decade. Within a century, the sinter will have entombed it completely, and the process will continue, a quiet, industrial ecology running on the planet’s own internal fire.
