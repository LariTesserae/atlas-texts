The first thing is the sound. The second is the space.

The sound is a solid thing, a pressure on the ears, a vibration felt deep in the bones of the jaw and sternum. It is the overlapping, compounding, ceaselessly generated drone of a billion trillion wings, carapaces, and resonating membranes. It is a physical wall of noise, so constant and so total that it becomes its own form of silence. Discrete noises—the scrape of a foot, a spoken word—are immediately swallowed by it, lost like single raindrops in a hurricane. To be here is to be immersed in a deafening, unchanging chord that has likely been sounding for millennia.

The space is a profound emptiness between titans. You stand on a surface that is not rock or soil, but something like petrified, living wood. This is the Chordate Rim, a vast, open plateau that is in fact the upper, outer flesh of a single, world-spanning organism. Or a colony of them, grown together so long ago that the distinction has been lost. These are the Great Boles, colossal living pillars that rise from an unseen base thousands of feet below, so wide their curvature is the horizon. You are at the summit, on the weathered, ancient epidermis of a being whose lifespan is geological.

The "ground" underfoot is a landscape of its own. It is a material like immensely compressed cork, furrowed and grooved by eons of wind and trickling water. It has the texture of ancient bark, but the scale of a canyon land. Old water channels, now dry and smooth, meander like fossilized rivers. In places, the surface is worn down to a fibrous, almost muscular layer beneath. The colors are muted, a symphony of ochres, greys, and deep, mossy greens where moisture persists. This is a landscape at the end of its life, or rather, at the end of its erosion. It has been worn into its final, most stable form. There are no sharp edges. Every cliff is a soft, rounded shoulder; every valley is a gentle, sweeping basin.

Despite the extreme elevation, the air is warm and heavy with moisture. There are no rivers or lakes in the conventional sense. Water exists as a product of the Boles' slow, steady respiration and the atmosphere they generate. Mists form in the vast chasms between them, condensing on the furrowed bark-ground. This condensation is the world's water supply. It gathers in the eroded basins, forming mirror-still pools of clean, cool water that can be hundreds of feet across but only a few inches deep. The air smells of wet wood, ozone, and a faint, sweetish odor of decay that is not unpleasant, like the floor of a primordial forest. Sunlight filters through the high, thin haze, casting a bright, even light with soft-edged shadows. It feels like a perpetually overcast summer afternoon.

The emptiness between the Great Boles is not empty at all. It is the primary domain of the fauna. This is where the overwhelming sound originates. Filling the air, thick as dust motes in a sunbeam, are glimmer-wings, resonator-moths, and chitin-gliders. They range from the size of a thumbnail to the size of an eagle. They are a living, shimmering, roaring cloud that populates the immense voids. They swarm in currents of air, their wings beating at frequencies that create the world's drone. They are the ecosystem. They consume the microscopic spores the Great Boles release into the air; they prey on each other in frenetic, short-lived dogfights; and when they die, their bodies rain down on the surfaces of the Boles, where they are broken down by mats of fungal felt, returning their nutrients to the colossal host. The sound is their collective life, a measure of the system's incredible biomass and energy.

Life here is lived on the edge of the sky. Scattered across the plateaus are the faint signs of habitation. Not cities, not even villages, but solitary dwellings or small clusters of two or three. They are carved directly from the living substrate of the Boles. A hollow scraped from a thick, woody burl becomes a room. A lean-to is made from plates of shed bark, lashed together with fiber woven from the sinewy roots that line the water channels. There are simple, elegant bridges spanning narrower fissures, also woven from plant-fiber, strong enough to hold one or two people at a time.

The tools of these people are born of the world. Knives and scrapers are fashioned from the sharpened femurs of the larger gliders. Drills are made from the serrated ovipositors of burrowing insects. Sap from certain secretory pores on the Boles can be harvested and hardened by exposure to the air, creating a translucent, amber-like substance as hard as obsidian, which can be knapped into keen-edged blades. Their technology is one of intimate knowledge and gentle adaptation, of taking only what is shed or what has already died.

The world is immensely active, but its pace is slow and cyclical. The mists roll in and out of the great chasms. The deafening choir of the fauna ebbs and flows with the light, quieting by a few decibels in the deepest part of the night, only to swell again to its full, overwhelming power at dawn. Occasionally, a section of the Bole's ancient bark will peel away and fall into the abyss, a slow-motion avalanche of living matter. The inhabitants of the Chordate Rim are watchers. They are few, and they are quiet, their presence a near-irrelevance to the scale of the life around them. To survive is to understand the currents in the insect-thick air, to know which pools the mist will fill, and to treat the deafening, ever-present sound not as an intrusion, but as the steady, constant heartbeat of the world itself.
