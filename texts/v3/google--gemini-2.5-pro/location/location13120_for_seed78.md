This is a place of sound and structure, a world built from a single principle: growth. There is no ground, no sky, only the Arbor.

You stand on a bough wider than a river, its surface a mosaic of dark, fibrous bark and frozen, crystalline sap that crunches underfoot like shattered glass. The air is thin and achingly cold, smelling of ozone and something ancient and resinous, a smell that seems to have its own weight. It is dark, a profound and living darkness punctuated not by stars, but by the faint, milky luminescence of distant, continent-sized leaves, or perhaps the collective bioluminescence of a trillion tiny organisms. The world is a lattice, a filigree of unimaginable scale. Above you, the same structure repeats—colossal branches crisscrossing into the gloom. Below you is the terror of the void: a drop that continues for miles, through stratum after stratum of the same twilight forest, a dizzying matrix of wood and leaf and space. This is the great paradox of the Arbor: it is a world of total vegetation, yet it is almost entirely empty. The space between its limbs is vast enough to contain mountain ranges.

The dominant sound is a constant, deep groan, the chord of a world-sized string instrument being tuned. It is the sound of the Arbor itself, the noise of a trillion tons of living matter straining under its own incredible mass, the slow, tectonic shifting of boughs growing and settling. This bass note is scored by the high, fluting whistle of wind tearing through the lattice, a sound that changes pitch as you move between sheltered platforms and exposed spars. Louder, more immediate, are the sudden, violent cracks, like cannon fire, of ice-laden branches giving way somewhere in the structure. The sound echoes strangely in the immense voids, making distance impossible to judge. A fracture a mile away might sound as if it’s at your elbow; a fall into the depths is a drawn-out, percussive symphony of impacts, fading over minutes.

This is a young world, or a perpetually renewing one. Every surface has a sharp, unfinished quality. Where a branch has sheared off, the exposed wood is a raw, pale wound in the darkness, weeping sluggish, black sap that quickly freezes into daggers and filigrees of obsidian-like material. New growth is everywhere: tight, coiled fists of buds covered in icy down, and pale green shoots, tough as iron, that visibly lengthen over the course of a day, questing out into the void.

Moisture is not in pools or rivers, but in the air and on every surface. The cold condenses what little humidity there is into a slick, greasy film on the bark. In sheltered hollows, where the wind cannot reach, thick clouds of mist hang motionless, and the constant drip-drip-drip of condensation is the only sound apart from the Arbor’s song. Sometimes, a great weather system of spores, like a golden-brown fog bank, will drift for weeks through the upper strata, blanketing everything in a fine, allergenic dust.

Life is abundant, and it is loud. The emptiness is not silent. Chittering, clicking flocks of six-limbed grazers, the size of large dogs, move along the underside of the great boughs, their hooked claws scraping audibly on the bark as they feed on parasitic ice-fungi. Their calls are sharp, staccato whistles, used for echolocation in the dark. In the vast open spaces between branches, things that are more akin to manta rays than birds glide on thermal and kinetic currents, their leathery wings making soft, percussive *thumps* as they adjust their flight. They are filter-feeders, living on the clouds of spores and smaller aerial plankton. Their predators are rarely seen, but their hunting cries are heard—long, ululating screeches that drop in pitch, the sound of a falling hunter diving upon its prey in the dark.

And there are marks of intention here. Not cities, not even villages, but scattered signs of a people who live within the Arbor's embrace. They are nests, woven not from dead twigs but from living shoots, coaxed and braided over generations into spherical, wind-proof shelters clung to the junctions of major boughs. Ladders of knotted fiber, stiff with ice, descend into the gloom. You might find a single, abandoned tool: a knife flaked from a shard of frozen sap, its edge still keen; a hand-axe made from a weighted, sharpened piece of heartwood, its handle wrapped in the cured hide of a glider. Most haunting are the chimes. Hanging from a spar, a delicate mobile of carved bark plates and hollowed seed pods clinks and sings in the constant wind, a tiny, personal melody almost lost in the overwhelming groan of the world-tree.

The world is in constant, dynamic motion. The primary law is not of seasons, but of collapse and regrowth. An entire "glade"—a region of the lattice the size of a country—can become overburdened, its branches cracking under the weight of ice and its own mass. It will begin to fail, its song rising in pitch to a scream of tearing wood and shattering sap. Over weeks, it will cascade into the depths, a slow-motion avalanche that scours the lower levels. The inhabitants, both fauna and people, are nomadic by necessity, masters of reading the Arbor's stress-songs, forever moving away from the ripening collapses and towards the promise of fresh, stable growth in the newly opened space. To live here is to understand that the ground beneath your feet is not a right, but a temporary privilege, a living, breathing, and ultimately mortal thing.
