This is a place of organized growth. You are inside it, though the distinction between inside and outside is not entirely clear. The ground beneath your feet is not soil or stone, but a firm, resilient mat of living tissue. It feels like hard leather or compacted cork, yielding only slightly under your weight. Its color is a deep, tea-brown, and it is woven from countless strands of what might be root, or muscle, or something in between. The surface is mostly smooth, weathered into shallow undulations over centuries, but here and there, fine, silvery filaments grow like a low-lying mist, catching and holding beads of moisture.

The air is cool and still, carrying the scent of wet earth, ozone, and fermenting fruit. It is heavy with humidity, a constant dampness that clings to your skin and clothes. Droplets of condensation form on the vertical surfaces around you and occasionally fall with a soft, distinct *pat* onto the living floor. This is the only rain. The water is cycled internally.

This place is a single, vast, interconnected structure of vegetation. It is not a forest, but an architecture. Great, pale boles, thick as pillars in a cathedral, rise from the floor. They are not separate trees, but articulated limbs of the same underlying organism. They branch and fuse high above, creating a vaulted ceiling of interwoven wood-like material, forming groined arches and complex trusses. The spaces between these pillars are immense, giving the impression of a grand, empty hall. This is the source of the sparseness: the plant-life has grown into a form that values void and volume. It has built rooms, not a thicket.

Through the latticework of the ceiling, a diffuse, normal daylight filters down, dappled and shifting. The light is not green, as one might expect from a canopy. The foliage is high above this main level, a true canopy exposed to the open sky. Down here, in the "city" level, the plant-matter is mostly structural—lignified, pearlescent, and non-photosynthetic. The light that reaches you is simply the light of the sky, softened and shaped by the organic architecture it passes through.

This is where the structure becomes a city, a factory. Running along the pillars and across the vaulted ceilings are conduits, grown directly from the plant's flesh. These are not pipes laid on top of the wood; they are integrated vascular systems, some as thick as your arm, others as fine as wire. Some are opaque and dark, pulsing with a slow, peristaltic rhythm. Others are translucent, revealing the slow drift of luminescent fluids within—a cool, blue-green light that gives off no heat. This is the industrial process. This is the technology.

The sound is a deep, pervasive hum, so low it is felt more than heard. It's the thrum of countless biological processes scaled up to the level of infrastructure. It is punctuated by soft, mechanical clicks as sphincter-like valves open and close along the conduits. There is the gentle sigh of pressure being equalized in biological accumulators, and the quiet gurgle of fluids being pumped from one chamber to another. It is the sound of a living machine, maintaining its own metabolism. There are no engines, no clanging metal, only the quiet, efficient work of a biomechanical system that has had eons to perfect itself.

This city-organism is constantly in motion, but at a biological pace. Over the course of an hour, you might notice a series of vents in a far wall unfurl like giant morning glories to release a cloud of vapor, regulating the hall's humidity. The pattern of light on the floor shifts as the high canopy leaves angle themselves toward the sun. Nutrients, processed in organ-like nodes that bulge from the pillars, are shunted through the glowing conduits towards sections undertaking new growth or repair. It is a closed-loop system, an automated factory whose only product is itself.

Fauna is nearly non-existent. The ecosystem has no need for pollinators or large herbivores. The only creatures are small, specialized, and symbiotic. You might see a crustacean-like creature, no bigger than your thumb, meticulously scraping algae from the inside of a transparent vessel before retreating back into a crevice. Or perhaps a colony of tiny, moth-like things with silver wings, which cluster around pressure-vents, feeding on the released esters. They are less animals and more living components, custodian-symbiotes that are as much a part of the machine as the conduits themselves.

The rules of this world are simple. There is no distinction between the built and the grown, the natural and the technological. Everything is part of a single, homeostatic system. Its form is established, its processes refined. It is not expanding, but perpetually *sustaining*. There is no one to run the city; the city runs itself. You are a visitor in the nave of a cathedral that is also a power plant, built by a god that is also the stone, the mortar, and the light. It is a place of profound, quiet, and unending activity.
