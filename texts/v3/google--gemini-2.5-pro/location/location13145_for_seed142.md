The world is a held breath.

To arrive here is to step into a warmth that has no source and a darkness that is not empty. The air, thin and high, is utterly still and carries a dry, baked-mineral scent, like the inside of a kiln that has been cooling for a thousand years. It is static against the skin, unmoving, its warmth a constant, uniform presence that does not radiate from a single point but simply *is*. It is the ambient temperature of the universe here.

The ground underfoot is not soil or rock in a familiar sense. It is a landscape of immense, rolling swells, the final, slumping shoulders of mountains that died epochs ago. The process of erosion has been so absolute, so patient, that every sharp edge has been ground into a soft, undulating curve. The material itself is a dark, matte-black substance, something between volcanic glass and sintered iron, which absorbs all but the faintest fraction of light. It is swallowing the darkness. Walking here is like traversing a landscape of frozen, solid smoke. The slopes are gentle, vast, and a single hill might take hours to crest, its curve so shallow as to be almost imperceptible.

Scattered across these black, ancient highlands is the city. It is not dense. Great voids of the silent, rolling landscape separate its districts, making it feel less like a cohesive metropolis and more like an archipelago of industry in a sea of nothing. The structures are colossal, built to a scale that dwarfs the individual. They are pylons, ducts, boiler-housings the size of cathedrals, and girder-work lattices that climb into the black sky and vanish. The architecture is brutally functional, a language of pure industry—rivets, plates, vents, and conduits—all rendered in the same non-reflective, charcoal-grey metal. There is no adornment, no concession to aesthetics.

This is an industrial place, but it is silent. The silence is the second most profound quality of the place after the dark. The great engines are not running, but they are not cold. Stand a hand's breadth from the wall of a massive cylindrical tower and you will feel its profound, steady heat pressing against your palm. The air shimmers just above the vents, but no steam or gas emerges. The technology here is not off; it is paused. It is held in a state of permanent, potent equilibrium, a single moment of immense industrial activity frozen at the quantum level. A factory caught in the instant before the whistle blows, but the instant has been stretched into eternity. The power is there, the heat is a byproduct of its containment, but the action is never resolved. There is no vibration in the ground, no hum in the air. Only the deep, abiding quiet of a process that is neither living nor dead, but perpetually on the cusp.

Light is a rumor. There is no sun, no moon. The starless sky is an oppressive, seamless black. The only illumination is a faint, almost subliminal ghost-light, a weak phosphorescence that seems to emanate not from any source, but from the air itself, as if the inert gas molecules could be coaxed into glowing by sheer proximity to the city's contained energy. It lends no visibility. It only serves to make the darkness feel tangible, to give it a texture and a deep, abyssal violet hue that your eyes can never quite resolve. The world is seen in shades of black-on-black, a study in silhouettes. Navigation is a matter of memory, of counting steps, of feeling the radiating warmth of the silent machinery wax and wane as you pass.

There is life here, of a kind. It is exceedingly rare. Days, or what pass for days in this timeless place, can go by without a sign. But then, on the hull of a great furnace, a patch of the dark metal will seem to detach itself. It is a creature of pure surface, a thing of geometry and slow, patient hunger. It might be described as a crystalline shard, or a complex, articulated sheet of the same material as the ground, moving with a tectonic slowness. It has no discernible head, no limbs, no organs. It slides across the warm metal of the city, its passage so slow as to be invisible in the moment, only noticeable by its changing position between long, patient glances.

These are the Glisteners. They are not biological. They do not eat or breathe. They seem to subsist on the state of things, on the strange physics of the arrested moment. Perhaps they feed on entropy, or on the subtle energy gradients between the warm metal and the slightly-less-warm air. They are part of the world's equilibrium. Upon their passing, the metal they have crawled over seems infinitesimally duller, its heat infinitesimally less. In this place of perfect stasis, they are the only agents of change, and their work is so slow it is functionally a part of the stillness itself. To see one is to witness a geological process unfold on a living thing. They make no sound.

The one rule of this world is the stillness. The city's function is its own preservation in this state. It is a machine designed to do nothing but maintain its own paralysis. Perhaps it was a weapon, designed to stop time in a target area, that deployed upon itself. Perhaps it is a library, preserving a single, vital instant for a future that will never come. Or perhaps it is a tomb, holding something so terrible in stasis that heat and silence are the only locks that can hold it. Whatever its purpose, it is fulfilled. Nothing happens. Nothing changes. There is only the dark, the warmth, the silence, and the great, patient, waiting machinery of the city on the hill at the end of time.
