You are afloat in a cool, resonant pressure. The water is not saltwater; it is fresh, ancient, and impossibly clear, with a density that makes movement feel like a deliberate, slow dance. This is the Lenticle, the great sky-ocean, a continental lens of water held impossibly high in the atmosphere, and you are near its bottom, where the peak of a mountain has pushed up into its liquid embrace.

Above, the distant surface of this sky-ocean is a silvery ceiling, and through it, the sun’s light penetrates as a diffuse, holy luminescence. There are no sharp shadows, only gradients of pearlescent blue and soft-edged darkness. The light descends in broad, shifting columns, like the pillars of a cathedral consecrated to the sky itself.

This place is a city, a settlement known as the Caldera-Ward. But there is no stone, no metal, no dead timber. The entire construction is alive. It is a single, colossal organism of cultivated hydrophyte, a plant bred over millennia to serve as architecture. Great, living pylons, thicker than ancient redwoods, rise from the mountaintop. Their substance is not woody fibre but a smooth, cool, semi-translucent material like living alabaster, whorled with an inner light. These pylons branch and connect, forming a vast, open-air framework of archways, bridges, and cantilevered platforms. The city is dense with structure, yet sparse in its substance. Walls are not solid plates but intricate lattices of calcified vine, allowing water, light, and life to pass through effortlessly. Floors are woven mats of a tougher, darker green plant, springy underfoot and perpetually clean, grazed by unseen organisms. The whole settlement breathes, hums, and grows, though its growth is too slow for a single lifetime to measure.

The sound is immense, a constant chorus that vibrates through the water and into your bones. It is not a cacophony but a layered symphony. The base note is a low, powerful hum—the sound of the city-plant itself, its metabolic processes turning light and minerals into structure. Layered over this is the ceaseless, high-frequency clicking and chittering of fauna. Swarms of tiny, jewel-backed crustaceans, like living dust motes, fill the open spaces, their collective movement a form of shimmering static. Vast shoals of silver, leaf-shaped fish, thousands strong, move as one organism, their synchronized turns catching the light in silent, blinding flashes.

Larger creatures drift slowly through the latticed avenues. These are the Grazers, behemoths like placid, six-limbed manatees, with beaks of polished obsidian. They are the city's gardeners and janitors, their slow, methodical scraping of algae from the living architecture a percussive rhythm in the soundscape. Their passage is unhurried, their ancient eyes patient. They are fauna, but they are also infrastructure, their function deeply woven into the city’s established balance.

The builders, the shapers of this place, are somewhere within it. You see the evidence of their work: surfaces of the living architecture are scored with fine, repeating patterns, scraped into being with hand-tools made from Grazer-beak fragments or sharpened shells. Inhabited dwellings are distinguished by delicate, shimmering curtains woven from the golden cilia of a particular anemone, which pulse with a soft, internal light. These screens provide a semblance of privacy in a city built of holes. Their technology is one of cooperation with biology, of patient guidance rather than forceful construction. A new chamber is not built, but grown, its form coaxed over decades by careful scoring and pruning of the living substrate.

Change is glacial. A current that shifts its course does so over a century. The chimes you hear are not bells, but the resonant fronds of specific plants, cultivated to a certain length and thickness, which ring out a single, pure note when the slow, predictable tide of the Lenticle ebbs or flows. The sound marks the passage of a day, but it feels like it could be marking the passage of an age. This is a place that has found its equilibrium. It is old, cool, loud, and alive, a city that is a single organism, a mountain peak that is a cathedral floor, breathing slowly in its ocean in the sky.
