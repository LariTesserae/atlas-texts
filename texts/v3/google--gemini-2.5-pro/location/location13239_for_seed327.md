This place is called the Bellweather.

The first thing you notice is not a sight, but a sound. It is a constant, complex, and overwhelming carillon. It is not the single note of a bell tower, but a thousand different chimes, gongs, and resonating tones, rising and falling with an organic, untimed cadence. The sound is so pervasive it seems to be the texture of the air itself, a presence you feel in your bones as much as you hear with your ears. It is loud, but not painful; it is intricate, but not chaotic. It is the voice of the place.

You are standing on a perfectly flat, roughly circular disc of stone, perhaps a quarter-mile across, floating in an ocean of sky. This is the peak. Not a jagged spire, but a table-top of ancient, dark basalt, weathered by eons into a plateau as smooth as a worn grinding stone. It is an island of impossible height, so old that its ferocity has been gentled into a serene flatness. The world below—of other mountains, valleys, and clouds—is an abstract map seen from a terrifying altitude. This small platform is the only solid thing in a universe of air. This is the source of its profound emptiness: the sheer, crushing void that surrounds it on all sides. Though the surface teems with life, the volume it occupies is ninety-nine percent nothing.

The air is cool and heavy with moisture. The Bellweather spends most of its time swimming through the cloud layer, and the atmosphere is in a continuous state of condensation. Fine, silver mist drifts in sheets, obscuring the view and then parting in dramatic, silent reveals. Water weeps constantly from the porous black rock, not in streams, but as a universal, glistening sweat. Every surface is slick with it. Droplets hang from the tips of leaves and the edges of stone, trembling with the deep resonance of the air before falling with a soft *plink* into the moss below.

And the moss. The entire plateau, where not bare rock, is carpeted in a deep, pillowy layer of it. It is a dozen shades of green, from near-black to electric lime, and it muffles your footsteps, turning each tread into a damp squelch. This living carpet is the foundation of the ecosystem. Growing from it are thickets of strange, low-lying flora: ferns with fronds like iridescent blue feathers, woody shrubs that creep along the ground instead of reaching for the sky, their leaves thick and waxy to hold the moisture. There are no trees; the wind that scours this height would not allow them. The vegetation is lush but low, a dense tapestry woven directly onto the stone.

The authors of the ceaseless sound are everywhere. They are the primary fauna, the Chimes. At a glance, they might be mistaken for large, flightless birds or stout, mammalian grazers, about the size of a sheepdog. They move on four sturdy legs, covered in dense, oily fur the color of wet stone. But their strangeness lies in their backs, which are ridged with a series of hollow, crystalline plates. These plates, grown from their own skeleton, are their voices. By shifting their posture, tensing muscles, and forcing air through internal chambers, they can strike these plates against each other or cause them to vibrate, producing tones of astonishing purity and depth. A single Chime can create a chord. A herd of them, scattered across the plateau, creates a symphony. They are in constant communication: warnings, location calls, familial greetings, a sonic map of the entire community.

Their calls are amplified by the only built structures on the Bellweather. Scattered across the mossy plain are dozens of menhirs—standing stones of a paler, granitic rock not native to the basalt peak. They have been shaped. Each one is forked at the top, like an immense tuning fork, or carved with deep, hollow bowls. They are primitive, hand-hewn, and unimaginably ancient. The Chimes have a symbiotic relationship with these stones. They stand within the forks to project a call for miles, they rub their resonating plates against the flat sides to create a deep, humming drone that shudders through the ground, and they sleep curled within the bowls, which seem to perfectly amplify their soft, sleeping vibrations into a protective sonic shield. These stones are not ruins; they are active instruments, used daily.

Life here is furiously dynamic. The mist is a constant actor, moving and changing the landscape from an intimate, closed room to an exposed precipice in seconds. The loud, vibrant society of the Chimes is the main drama, but they are not alone. Small, furry creatures the size of hares, almost perfectly spherical, roll through the moss, consuming it. These are the Moss-moles. And hunting from the limitless sky are Skydarts, sleek, winged predators somewhere between a hawk and a shark. They ride the updrafts for days, circling high above, their keen eyes watching for a moment of inattention. A sudden shriek from a Chime, a percussive *thwomp* of alarm, will send the whole herd bolting for the safety of the resonating stones, their panicked calls a discordant, deafening clatter. Then, silence, as a Skydart ascends with its prey, and the symphony slowly, cautiously, begins to rebuild itself.

The rule of this world is sound. To be silent is to be lost or dead. The constant chiming is a declaration of existence against the overwhelming emptiness. It is a map, a language, and a defense. The ever-present water gives life, the ancient stone gives a foundation, and the empty air gives it a cathedral. It is a small place of intense, loud, and vibrant life, all balanced on a pinhead in the palm of the sky.
