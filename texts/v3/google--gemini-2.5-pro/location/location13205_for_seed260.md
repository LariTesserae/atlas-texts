The first thing is the sound. It is not a noise that arrives at the ears, but a condition of the air itself. It is a pressure, a physical presence that pushes against the skin and vibrates deep in the bones. It is a DEAFENING, multi-layered, grinding drone, the sound of a world being born and torn apart in the same instant. It has the bass shudder of continental plates colliding, the high, clear ringing of a billion stressed crystals, and a pervasive, static hiss like the universe's pilot light. There is no silence here. Silence is a theoretical concept, a myth from a world with different physics. To be here is to be saturated in this sound, to have it replace the beat of your own heart.

The ground you stand on is a high, knife-edge ridge, one of many that serrate the landscape. These are not old, weathered mountains. They are nascent, raw, and still growing. Their peaks are impossibly sharp, like shards of flint the size of skyscrapers. The rock is a dark, vitreous obsidian laced with veins of something that pulses with a faint, internal light, a piezoelectric response to the immense strain of their own formation. There is no dust, no soil, no scree. The geology is too new for that. No erosion has had time to soften these edges. Gravity seems to be pulling the world upwards, extruding these mountain ranges from a planetary mantle in a process that is patient but unstoppable.

Stretching out from the ridge is a vast, open valley, a basin of tectonic agony. And in this valley, things grow. This is the paradox of the place: in a realm utterly devoid of water, there is a form of life, and it is lush. It is not fauna; there is nothing that crawls or walks or flies. There is only the "vegetation." Scattered in sparse, majestic groves across the valley floor are forests of what can only be called chime-fronds.

They are not biological. They are crystalline, growing in slow, fractal patterns from the vibrating ground. A single "tree" might be a hundred feet tall, a central stalk of milky quartz branching into a canopy of delicate, blade-like leaves made of a resonant, ruby-colored glass. They are sonotrophic. They feed on the sound. The constant, deafening roar of the world is their sunlight, their water, their soil. The fronds tremble constantly, not in a wind, but in harmonic sympathy with the planetary drone. Their "lushness" is not in soft tissue, but in their complexity, the sheer density of their crystalline foliage, the way they catch the dim light and refract it into a thousand momentary splinters of color. They hum, adding their own choir of overtones to the world's grinding bassline.

The light is dim, a permanent state of deep twilight. There is no visible sun, no moon. The sky is a thick, uniform ceiling of bruised violet, and the light seems to emanate from the air itself, sourceless and cold. The only other illumination comes from the mountains, which flash with random, silent jolts of inner lightning, and the chime-fronds themselves, which glow with a soft, stored luminescence, a visible echo of the sound they consume. The temperature is cool, the air still and heavy with the pressure of the sound.

Here and there, faint marks of intention disturb the raw geology. They are not settlements, but strange, primitive constructions. A single, perfectly smooth monolith of black basalt stands in the center of a grove of chime-fronds, its surface absorbing all light. Elsewhere, a low, curving wall of stacked stone seems to cup a small depression, like an acoustic mirror meant to focus the sound. On a low mesa, a series of trenches have been carved into the rock, running in parallel lines for half a mile. These are the works of a hand-tool culture, of beings who understood the fundamental rule of this reality: sound is the primary creative and destructive force. These scattered artifacts are not homes. They are tuners, baffles, resonators. They are attempts to garden the sound, to cultivate specific frequencies, perhaps to encourage the growth of purer crystals or to dampen a particularly dissonant overtone in the world's roar.

This is a place where reality itself feels thin. The sheer acoustic energy causes the air to shimmer, creating visual distortions like heat-haze over a desert. Distant peaks seem to wobble and drift. The very fabric of spacetime feels frayed at the edges, stretched taut by the vibration. A thrown rock might not arc downwards in a smooth parabola, but might instead stutter, its trajectory subtly altered by a passing wave of infrasound. The ground beneath your feet feels not entirely solid, more like a substance held in a state of extreme tension, on the verge of changing its phase.

It is a world of slow, immense dynamics. The mountains grow by inches per century. The chime-fronds might take a thousand years to reach maturity. Yet, it is a world of unimaginable violence, the violence of creation, expressed as a constant, overwhelming song. It is a deafening, empty, and yet fertile expanse, a geological womb where resonance takes the place of chemistry, and where the only life is a beautiful, crystalline response to a scream that will never, ever end.
