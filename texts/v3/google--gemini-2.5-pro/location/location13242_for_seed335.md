The sound comes first, a noise that does not belong in nature. It is a deep, arrhythmic, and profoundly industrial thrumming. It is the sound of a vast and poorly-maintained engine, the shuddering resonance of a colossal piston near its breaking point, the groan of metal under intolerable strain. The sound rises and falls not in a predictable rhythm but in a series of violent, percussive events: a deep, hollow *BOOM* that vibrates through your feet, followed by a long, high-pressure hiss that slowly fades, then a series of sharp, fracturing cracks like a glacier calving. This is the heartbeat of the place.

You are standing at the edge of a chamber, a pocket universe carved from the planet's flesh. The scale is intimate, no larger than a grand cathedral, but it feels impossibly vacant. The opening you came through is a recent fracture, a wound in the rock overhead that lets in a perpetual, grudging twilight and a cascade of cold, damp air. This cold mixes with the chamber's own internal heat, creating a slurry of thick, swirling mist that writhes in the shifting gloom. The air tastes of ozone, wet stone, and something metallic, like hot copper.

The floor is a vast, mostly unbroken mirror of polished black obsidian. It is a perfect void, reflecting the dim light from above and the faint, internal glow of the chamber's heart. This floor is the source of the emptiness. It is sterile, glassy, and utterly devoid of life. Across its great, dark expanse, nothing grows, nothing clings.

Life, however, is here. It is overwhelmingly lush, but it is not on the floor. It is on the walls. The sheer, vertical cliffs of the chamber are caked in a thick, dripping tapestry of life. From the obsidian floor, great mats of vegetation rise hundreds of feet, clinging to the rock in shelves and terraces. They are comprised of things that are part-fungus, part-moss, and part-vine. Their structure is rubbery and robust, swollen with moisture. Their color is a deep, bruised purple and an electric, phosphorescent green. In the dimness, they pulse with a soft, biological light, tracing the paths of water and nutrients through their own tissues. Water, generated by the chamber's engine, weeps constantly from the rock behind them, trickles down their fronds, and drips from their heavy, swollen lobes to spatter silently on the glass floor below, where it forms shallow, spreading pools of mineral-heavy, inert water.

At the absolute center of the obsidian plain is the source of all this activity: the engine. It is not built, but grown. A colossal, chaotic cluster of crystalline structures thrusts up from the floor. They are not quartz, not salt, but something in between, translucent and filled with networks of glowing filaments. They are sharp, new, their facets gleaming. This is a geological process made manifest, a geothermal vent that, instead of merely releasing pressure, has entered a state of frantic, accelerated growth and decay.

This is the engine. The deep *BOOM* is the sound of immense pressure building within one of the larger crystalline shafts until it finds a microscopic fracture, creating a sympathetic resonance through the entire structure. The sharp *CRACKS* are the sounds of the crystals themselves growing, their molecular bonds snapping into place so rapidly it is an audible event. And the long, deafening *HISS* is the primary function: a percussive venting of superheated, mineral-rich steam from a nozzle-like formation at the engine's apex. The steam jet punches a hole in the fog, blasting towards the distant ceiling where it condenses, completing the water cycle that feeds the walls. The engine is a factory for this single, contained ecosystem.

The place is teeming with fauna, all of it adapted to this bizarre, percussive world. Skittering across the empty black floor are beetle-like creatures the size of a human hand. Their carapaces are iridescent shells, and they move in swift, gliding dashes, feeding on the mineral dust that settles after a steam-venting. They are the scavengers of the void.

Within the vertical jungles on the walls, life is a frantic ballet. Six-legged climbers—part-mammal, part-insect—with padded, gecko-like feet and prehensile tails, dart through the glowing foliage. They are the primary herbivores, their sharp teeth scraping the bioluminescent flesh from the fungal vines. They live in a world of constant dripping and precarious holds, their chirps and clicks lost in the overwhelming noise of the engine.

The active, dynamic nature of the place is driven by its primary predator. It is a creature that has learned to mimic the engine. It stalks the boundary between the living walls and the empty floor. Most of the time it is silent, a shadow among shadows, but its hunt is an acoustic assault. It can tense muscles and snap ligaments in its own body to produce a perfect replica of the *CRACK* of a growing crystal, startling the wall-climbers into stillness. Then, from its throat, it unleashes a focused, deafening hiss, a short-range blast of sound that panics its prey, causing them to lose their grip and fall from the safety of the foliage. They land, stunned, on the obsidian floor, where the creature scuttles from the shadows to claim them.

This place has one rule: the rhythm of the engine dictates all. The periodic blast of steam is the signal for the floor-skitterers to brace or hide. The constant cracking and groaning is the backdrop to every moment, the static of existence. The intervals of relative quiet are moments of unique and terrible tension. Life and death unfold in the cycling of a machine that does not know it is a machine, in a garden that does not know it is a garden. It is a sealed, perfect, and monstrously loud example of an industrial revolution that never required a mind to begin.
