You descend into the heat.

The air does not move, but it has weight, pressing against your skin like a hot, wet towel. It smells of ozone, damp rock, and something else—a clean, coppery scent like freshly-struck flint mixed with the green perfume of crushed stems. The heat is not the dry, baking heat of a desert, but the close, suffocating warmth of a sealed greenhouse. Every surface slicks with a film of condensation.

This place is a cavern, but it was not formed by water's patient work over millennia. The geology is young, violent. The floor is a pavement of fractured basalt, sharp-edged and treacherous, the breaks so fresh they still show a glassy, conchoidal pattern. In places, the floor has buckled upwards into jagged pressure ridges, their knife-like crests glistening with moisture. The walls are not smooth but sheer, riven with vertical cracks, as if a titanic wedge was driven into the earth and then removed. The scale is intimate, contained; you could walk from one end to the other in minutes. It feels less like a natural cave and more like the inside of a burst geode, a single, magnificent chamber whose ceiling is lost in absolute darkness high above.

There is no sun here. The only light comes from the vegetation. It is not a carpet, but a scattering of deliberate, isolated gardens. Tall, stalk-like growths, reminiscent of colossal horsetails, rise from the broken stone. Their segmented, chitinous-looking stems are a deep, matte black, but from the joints between each segment, a soft, internal, blue-white light pulses with the slowness of a sleeping breath. These are the Lumen-flora. Their glow is cool and steady, casting long, stark shadows that move with an almost imperceptible rhythm. Around the base of these luminous stalks, broad, waxy leaves unfurl, their surfaces covered in intricate, vein-like patterns that glow with a dimmer, greener light. The leaves are heavy with water, and every few minutes a fat, condensed droplet gathers at a tip, swells, and falls with a soft *plock* onto the stone below, the only consistent sound in the chamber. There are no other life forms. No insects are drawn to the light, no mosses soften the sharp edges of the rock, no fungi claim the fallen leaves, which seem not to decay but to slowly turn brittle and translucent, like panes of sugar glass.

The silence is profound, broken only by those falling drops of water. It is a sterile silence, an abiotic emptiness.

And yet, you are not alone. Scattered through the open spaces between the glowing flora are the remains of industry. This was a place of work. A massive, central structure of dark iron and verdigris-caked bronze squats in the heart of the cavern. It might have been a pump, or a generator, or a master control station—its purpose is now obscured by neglect. Thick, armored cables, insulated with a perishing rubber-like substance, snake out from it, running in shallow, crudely-cut channels in the floor before terminating in corroded junction boxes or disappearing into the rock walls. Near the cavern's edge sits a skeletal watchman's hut, its metal panels rusted through in lace-like patterns, a single swivel chair still aimed at a bank of gauges whose glass faces are cracked and milky with condensation. There is no sign of who built this, or why. There are no bodies, no personal effects. Only the machinery, a testament to a forgotten purpose.

Most of it is dead. But not all.

If you stand perfectly still and hold your breath, you can feel it more than hear it: a low, resonant hum, just at the edge of audibility. It is the sound of immense, dormant power. It emanates from the central iron machine—the Regulator. The hum is not the energetic thrum of a working factory but the patient, enduring frequency of a system on standby, waiting for a command that will never again arrive. This low thrum is the source of the heat, a slow, geothermal bleed managed and focused by the machine, warming the chamber and feeding the engineered life that illuminates it.

The world here operates on a glacial timescale. The Lumen-flora unfurl a new leaf over the course of a year. A flake of rust takes a decade to fall from the Regulator's hull. The water that drips from the leaves is the only cycle of any discernible speed, a constant, gentle metronome counting out the immense, silent moments. This is a terrarium at the end of time, an automated garden whose gardeners have been gone for centuries, its slow, hot, quiet processes running down in a darkness lit only by the strange, living lamps it was designed to sustain.
