This is a city at the bottom of the sky.

The liquid medium is not water, not quite. It is heavier, clearer, and carries a profound, silencing pressure. It is called the Still. Here, miles deep in the warm, dense fluid that constitutes this world’s atmosphere and ocean in one, stands a lone mountain. We are on its summit, a peak that rises to a height where a different world might have broken into sunlight and thin air. Here, it only means the pressure is fractionally less immense.

There is no natural ground. The entire peak has been capped, paved, and built upon, transformed into a single, cohesive structure. The material is a kind of vitrified, thermoset earth—a primordial clay or loam that was shaped and then fired into a seamless, dark ceramic landscape. The technology was primitive but monumental. One imagines generations of builders moulding the wet, mineral-rich mud with their hands and simple tools, then channeling geothermal heat from the mountain’s core to fire the entire summit into a single, city-sized artifact.

The result is a dense hamlet of black, semi-lustrous architecture. Buildings are low, domed, and thick-walled, huddling close as if for comfort against the crushing uniformity of the Still. They flow into one another, their walls becoming archways, their roofs becoming courtyards. There are no sharp corners. Every edge, every threshold and lintel, has been worn into a soft, yielding curve by uncountable ages of slow, inexorable currents. The erosion is so complete it feels less like decay and more like a final state of being—a settlement that has settled. The texture of the ground and walls is like polished river stone, cool to the touch despite the ambient warmth, and impossibly smooth. It is a city made of touch.

There is no sun. There is no sky. The only light comes from life.

The world is without vegetation, but it is not without life. The fauna are abundant, slow, and the source of all illumination. Crawling with infinite patience upon the black ceramic surfaces are the Polishers. They are great, heavy creatures, shaped like flattened bells or gigantic, streamlined trilobites. Their underbellies are soft, muscular, and pulse with a gentle, sourceless luminescence, a cold chemical fire of softest gold and pale blue. This light washes over the surfaces they traverse, revealing the utter smoothness of the ancient pavement. They move with a slow, peristaltic grace, grazing on a thin, almost invisible film of chemosynthetic bacteria that grows upon the city, nourished by the mountain’s inner heat. Their passage is the only form of change and maintenance, their aurorean bellies buffing the city to its perpetual gleam. The sound they make, and it is the loudest sound here, is a faint, wet scraping, a textural noise that is felt in the bones more than heard.

Drifting in the liquid alleyways between the domes are the second kind of life: Clouds of tiny, spindle-shaped beings known as Embers. Each is no larger than a finger, translucent and filled with a single, slowly pulsing dot of orange light. They swarm in the thousands, a dense, ever-shifting constellation of warm points that drift on the imperceptible currents. They are not fast, and their movements are not governed by panic or predation, but by a kind of communal, slow-motion ballet. They are the city’s lanterns, its dust motes, its weather. Their collective presence fills the small, cathedral-like volumes with a deep, saturated, and mobile twilight.

The silence is the rule. The pressure of the Still dampens all sound to a dull, immediate thud. A dislodged pebble—if one could be found—would not 'clink', but 'thump' once and be still. The soundscape is one of near-total quiet: the soft, rhythmic scrape of a Polisher’s shell against ceramic, the barest hum of a million Embers displacing the liquid, the deep, sub-audible groan of the mountain settling deeper into its own ancient heart.

It is a closed, patient ecosystem. The mountain provides warmth. The warmth feeds the bacteria. The Polishers eat the bacteria and keep the city clean. The Embers drift and glow, perhaps a different stage in the Polishers’ life cycle, or perhaps a wholly different phylum that feeds on the waste of the grazers. Everything is recycled. Nothing is wasted. The city was built, but it has become a reef. The architecture is the skeleton, the substrate upon which this slow, quiet, and luminous biology plays out its eternal, unchanging drama. To be here is to feel the weight of a finished thing, a place that has reached its final form and asks for nothing more than to be.
