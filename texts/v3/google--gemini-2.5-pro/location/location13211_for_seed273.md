This is a world of manufactured peaks. There are no mountains of stone, only titanic, load-bearing pylons and cantilevered continents of ceramic and iron. You stand on a plateau, a vast, flat expanse of interlocking hexagonal plates, each the size of a town square. The material is a pale, bone-white ceramic, warm to the touch. Looking out, you see not a horizon of earth and sky, but a vista of more such structures, a geography of pure architecture. Valleys are the open-air gaps between these colossal supports, chasms thousands of feet deep, their bottoms lost in a hazy gloom. The scale is breathtaking; a neighboring plateau, miles away, is connected to yours by a web of suspension bridges, their cables thick as ancient tree trunks.

The air is bright. The sun at this altitude has a fierce, unfiltered intensity, making the white ceramic plates almost blinding. Glare bounces between surfaces, filling every space with a brilliant, shadowless light. Yet the atmosphere is thick, heavy, and wet. It feels like a greenhouse. This is a world of managed water. There are no rivers, but condensation is a constant process. The warm, moist air, a byproduct of the infrastructure's deep geothermal taps, beads on every cool surface. Water weeps from the undersides of overhanging gantries and trickles down the grooved channels carved into the pylons. Thin pipes, green with verdigris, act as arteries, collecting this atmospheric dew and channeling it into vast, open-topped cisterns. The air is never dry, and the constant, gentle *drip-drip-drip* of water is a high-frequency counterpoint to the world’s bass hum.

Despite the complete absence of natural soil, life is riotously lush. The builders of this place provided for it. Everywhere, the ceramic plains are punctuated by immense, recessed planters, troughs, and basins. Within them is a manufactured loam—a substrate of porous pellets, spun mineral fibers, and recycled organic mulch. From this engineered earth, vegetation grows with an almost aggressive density. Thick-leafed, waxy plants press against each other, their foliage forming a solid canopy of green a few feet above the ceramic decking. Enormous ferns, with fronds as long as buses, uncurl in the shade of structural overhangs. Flowering vines, heavy with blossoms of impossible blues and oranges, crawl up the iron lattices that fence the edges of the plateaus, their scent thick and sweet in the warm air. Mosses and algae paint every surface that stays damp for long, creating soft green carpets on pipe junctions and velvety streaks down the faces of the pylons.

This world is never silent. The noise is a constant, industrial thrum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet. It is the sound of a patient, continent-sized engine. Deep within the pylons, ponderous machinery turns. You can hear the slow, rhythmic *clank… clank… clank* of gears the size of houses, the continuous hydraulic hiss of colossal pistons managing atmospheric pressure, the deep resonant hum of electrical current flowing through superconducting cables. This is not the frantic noise of production, but the sound of maintenance. It is the sound of a world-machine in a state of equilibrium, a system so old and established that its processes unfold over geological time. Change is imperceptible moment to moment, but the evidence of its slow work is everywhere: the polished tracks where maintenance drones have run for millennia, the weathered patina on bronze fixtures, the settled sag of a million-year-old bridge.

Fauna has adapted to this vertical, metallic ecology. Flocks of birds with feathers like polished steel swoop through the open straths, their cries sharp and echoing. They build their nests from stripped cable insulation and plant fibers inside the hollows of decommissioned vents. Gecko-like lizards, with iridescent scales and sucker-padded feet, scuttle up the vertical ceramic walls, hunting for the metallic-shelled beetles that tick and click in the planters. Larger creatures exist, too. The "Plack-beasts" are slow, heavy quadrupeds, like a cross between a hippo and a tortoise, with broad, rasping mouths. They move ponderously across the plateaus, methodically scraping and consuming the mineral deposits and hardy algae that accumulate on the ceramic surfaces, their scraping sounds adding another layer to the world's deep hum. They are living maintenance crews, an integrated part of the ecosystem.

There is no "natural" here. Every rock is a formatted concrete block, every patch of dirt an engineered substrate, every gust of wind part of a colossal ventilation cycle. The rules are born of this artifice. Gravity is a constant, but the primary axis of life is vertical. To fall is not to hit the ground, but to be lost in the layered superstructure below, a darkness of humming machinery and dripping water that extends for miles downward. All life here is either descended from what the builders introduced or has colonized the structure from some unknown outside. The purpose of this immense place is lost to time. Perhaps it was a refuge, an ark, or a monument. Now, it simply *is*. A self-regulating, high-altitude ecosystem of iron and chlorophyll, running on a slow, patient, and deafeningly loud clockwork heart.
