This is a high, hollow place. You stand in the centre of a great basin, a caldera perhaps, though its volcanic origins are lost to an unfathomable depth of time. The mountains that once ringed it have slumped, eroded by ages of wind and frost into a set of immense, rounded shoulders that form the horizon. They are not peaks but presences, ancient beyond comprehension, their rock worn to a soft, grey-green smoothness, like river stones the size of continents. The floor of this basin is not flat, but undulates in vast, gentle swells. The scale is intimate, contained; you can walk from one edge to the other in an afternoon, yet the feeling is one of profound emptiness.

The dominant feature is not visual, but audible. The air is never silent. It is filled with a sound so loud and complex it is a physical pressure against your skin. It is a chord, a chorus of thousands of distinct, resonant notes—deep, vibrating hums, piercing crystalline whistles, and a thousand tones in between. The sound is constant but not static; it modulates and shifts, swelling and receding with a logic of its own. It is the sound of a colossal pipe organ playing a symphony without beginning or end.

The source of the music is the city. But there are no builders, no masons, no technology. The "buildings" are biological formations, immense structures that rise from the damp earth like bone-white coral. Let us call them Sonophores. They are not scattered randomly, but arranged in clusters, forming what feel like organized districts and plazas of empty space between them. Each Sonophore is a unique architecture of hollow tubes, porous galleries, and delicate, fluted spires. They are made of a substance like unglazed porcelain, matte and cool to the touch, latticed with millions of precisely-sized openings. The cold wind of this high altitude, funneling down from the eroded rim of the basin, pours through these structures. The entire city is an instrument, and the wind is its breath. When the wind gusts, a new swell of harmonies washes through the basin, so loud it can make your teeth ache. When the wind is gentle, the music softens to a complex, billion-voiced whisper.

The ground is not bare rock but is carpeted in a deep, lush layer of life. It’s a tapestry of cold-loving vegetation. Thick, velvety mosses in shades of malachite and rust cover the ground and climb the bases of the Sonophores. In the damp hollows between the swells of land, forests of dark, glossy ferns unfurl, their fronds heavy with condensation. Fleshy, succulent plants, like clusters of pale green fingers, store water scavenged from the perpetual mist that clings to the basin floor. There are no trees, no flowers in the conventional sense. The lushness is one of texture and deep, saturated colour against the stark white of the living architecture.

Water here is a constant, subtle presence. It does not flow in rivers but seeps. It drips from the ferns, condenses on the cool porcelain of the Sonophores and runs in slow, glistening tracks down their sides, gathering in the mosses below. In the early morning, under the plain, honest light of a normal sun, the whole basin glitters as if threaded with diamonds. The air is always damp, tasting of cold stone and wet earth. Weather is a cycle of sound: a front moving in is a gradual crescendo. Rain is not a patter but a percussive accompaniment, its drumming on the mossy ground a soft rhythm beneath the city’s unceasing song.

Life, aside from the plants and the city itself, is exceptionally rare. For hours, you might see nothing move. The emptiness feels absolute. But then, a flash of motion catches your eye. On a vast carpet of moss, a creature like a living trilobite, the size of a dinner plate, slowly grazes. Its shell is a dark, iridescent chitin, and it moves with an unhurried deliberation that speaks of a world with no predators. These grazers are the primary fauna, trimming the moss and lichen, their slow-motion lives playing out against the deafening, eternal music. Perhaps smaller things live within the hollows of the Sonophores, symbiotes that feed on vibrations or airborne nutrients, but you will never see them. They are part of the city’s internal machinery.

This place is active. It is a living system. The Sonophores grow, though at a rate imperceptible to the casual observer. New, smaller spires might rise over centuries, adding their young, high-pitched voices to the chorus. Older sections, their internal structures clogged by mineral deposits from the seeping water, fall silent and begin to erode, crumbling into a fine, white sand that mixes with the dark soil. The wind is the engine, the water is the lubricant, and the sound is the primary output of an ecosystem built not on a food web, but on a principle of resonance.

This is not a ruin; it was never built to be inhabited. It is a city that performs itself. Its purpose is its existence. To stand within it is to feel like a mote of dust inside a singing bell, to be dissolved into the sheer, overwhelming fact of its music. The strangeness is not in any one element, but in their perfect, bizarre synthesis: the urban form without intelligence, the lush life in the cold, the overwhelming sound in the vast emptiness. It is a world unto itself, governed by the physics of harmony and the biology of stone.
