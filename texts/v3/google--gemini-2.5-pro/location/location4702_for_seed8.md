This is a world of mud and sky.

The ground is not truly solid but a vast, saturated floodplain, a basin the size of a small country that has been scoured and flattened over geological ages. There are no mountains on any horizon, no true hills, only immense, shallow swells in the earth like the muscles on the back of a sleeping leviathan. These low rises, barely a dozen meters high, are the oldest things here. They are domes of primordial clay and silt, worn so smooth by eons of rain and sheet-flow that they have the gentle, final curvature of objects settled at the bottom of the sea. The world has been ground down to this—its last, most stable form.

Water is not in the landscape; it *is* the landscape. The air is thick and warm, heavy with moisture that beads on every surface. It is a perpetual state of post-rain humidity, where the sky is a bright, hazy silver-white and the sun is a diffuse, gelatinous orb. The ground is a patchwork of shallow, tea-colored pools and glistening mudflats that stretch for miles. Even on the highest hummocks, the earth is dark and damp. Press a hand to it, and water instantly wells up around your fingers, cool and smelling of wet stone and ancient minerals. There are no rivers, for there is no gradient to drive them. When it rains—a daily, late-afternoon affair of warm, heavy drops—the water doesn't run off so much as it slowly, imperceptibly, sloshes across the entire plain in a single sheet.

For a place so wet, it is unnervingly empty of plants. There are no trees, no shrubs, no grasses or moss. The sparse vegetation that exists is more mineral than biological. Scattered across the mudflats, sometimes alone, sometimes in clusters of two or three, are what might be called reed-thorns. They are thin, glassy stalks, ranging from a foot to six feet tall, made of a translucent, silica-like substance. They are a pale amber color, internally ribbed, and utterly rigid. They do not sway in the infrequent breeze but stand like forgotten geological survey markers. They do not provide food or shelter. They are simply fixtures of the land, as much a part of the geology as the clay itself.

The quiet emptiness of the terrain is a lie, for the world is deafeningly loud.

The sound is the first and last thing you perceive. It is a constant, overwhelming chorus from the abundant fauna. This noise is not the chirping of birds or the humming of insects, but a thick, layered wall of organic, mechanical sound. It is a million clicks, a thousand wet pops, a hundred deep, resonant thrums that you feel in your chest cavity. The air vibrates with it.

The source of this cacophony is everywhere. The shallow waters and soft mud are teeming with life. The most numerous are the "clickers," crab-like organisms the size of a human hand. Their carapaces are the color of oxidized copper and iridescent oil slicks. They move with a slow, deliberate purpose, scuttling through the muck, their multiple legs making ceaseless, sharp *ticking* sounds against one another. They are not predators, but filter-feeders, combing the mineral-rich water and mud with feathery mouthparts. Their sheer number creates a background static of a billion tiny machines.

Above this static are the bellowers. These are hulking, amphibious creatures, low-slung and heavy, the size of large sea lions. They spend most of their time half-submerged in the deeper pools, only their broad, blunt heads and dorsal ridges breaking the surface. Their skin is smooth and mottled grey-brown, like river stones. They possess huge, inflatable throat sacs. Every few minutes, one will draw a deep breath that is a wet, guttural rasp, and then exhale in a powerful, low-frequency boom that rolls across the flats for miles. This is not an aggressive call, but a territorial broadcast, a slow, patient declaration of presence. In any direction you look, you can see the water trembling faintly around one of these creatures as it adds its note to the chorus.

The ecosystem here is simple and ancient. The reed-thorns draw minerals directly from the water table. The clickers and other small creatures filter microorganisms from the water, which in turn feed on the dissolved minerals leached from the eons-old sediment. The great bellowers are also filter-feeders, sieving vast quantities of water for the same microscopic sustenance. There are predators, to be sure—long, finned hunters that move through the deeper pools like shadows—but the system is overwhelmingly based on drawing energy directly from the water and the earth. It is a food web without a green base.

To be here is to feel a profound sense of temporal vertigo. The land itself feels finished, its long story of erosion and change all but over. The cycles are glacially slow: the patient seeping of water, the gradual silting of a pool over a century, the slow march of a bellowing beast from one feeding ground to another over the course of a week. Life is slow, patient, and deeply entrenched. And yet, this stillness is overlaid with a riot of sound, a frantic, unceasing broadcast of existence. It is the sound of a billion lives being lived in a world that has almost stopped changing altogether. The sun shines brightly, the air is warm, and the only thing that moves with any speed is the noise itself.
