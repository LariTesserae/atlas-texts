The first thing to know is the sound. It is not an assortment of noises; it is a single, monolithic presence. It is a physical weight that presses on the ears and vibrates through the soles of the feet. It is the sound of a waterfall made not of water but of a trillion chitinous bodies rubbing together, the roar of a billion tiny engines whirring in unison, the oceanic hiss of a million wings beating the thick, warm air. It is a deafening, unrelenting drone that has existed for millennia and will exist for millennia more. It is the baseline of the world, the static against which no other signal can be heard.

There is no soil underfoot. The ground is a vast, constructed thing, an architecture of biological origin. It feels like porous, unpolished ivory or ancient, smoothed bone. Great, sweeping terraces and balconies form the landscape, their surfaces worn into soft, shallow bowls and flowing channels by ages of slow seepage and the passage of countless living things. This substance, this world-material, is a pale, creamy white, but it is rarely seen. It is almost entirely carpeted in life.

Blankets of what might be moss, thick and velvety, cover everything. But this moss is not green. In the profound darkness, it glows with a faint, internal phosphorescence, a sickly yellow-green that provides the world’s only light. It casts no distinct shadows, only a pervasive, dim wash that allows you to perceive form without detail. This cool, biological light reveals that the moss grows in dense, overlapping mats, sometimes a foot thick, creating a soft, sound-dampening layer over the hard substrate.

From this glowing carpet, other things grow. There are no trees with bark and leaves. Instead, there are great, fleshy fungi, shaped like anemones or broad, tiered shelves, that climb the risers between the vast terraces. Their flesh is a bruised purple and a pale, veined grey. They pulse slowly, almost imperceptibly, perhaps circulating fluids. Interspersed are vines, not of wood, but of a tough, cartilaginous material, as thick as a wrist. They snake across the ground and hang in dense curtains from the edges of the tiers above, their surfaces slick with a constant, condensing humidity.

The air is warm, thick, and wet. It feels heavy in the lungs, saturated with the smells of damp decay, of ozone, and of the chalky, mineral scent of the substrate itself. Droplets of water pearl on the surfaces of the fungal growths and the hanging vines, gathering until they are too heavy and fall with a soft *pat* that is utterly lost in the cacophony. Water does not flow here in rivers; it seeps and condenses. The porous, bone-like ground is saturated, and in the shallow basins worn into its surface, still pools of black water have collected, their surfaces quivering with the universal vibration.

This place is high, a plateau-structure of unimaginable size. Looking out from the edge of a terrace, you see not a fall to a valley floor, but a vast, open expanse of gloom. In the distance, perhaps miles away, you can make out the faint, greenish glow of another, similar tier, its form indistinct, a ghostly echo of the one you stand on. Above, there is no sky, only more darkness, punctuated by the faint outlines of yet higher levels of the great construction, ascending into an unseen, unknown peak.

And everywhere, there is fauna. It is the source of the sound and the engine of the world. The mossy ground is a living carpet, and if you look closely, you see that it is crawling with life. Millipede-like creatures, some no bigger than a finger, others the length of a human arm, flow in slow rivers through the glowing vegetation, their countless legs clicking on the hard ground beneath. Beetles with iridescent, pitted carapaces scuttle in dense, seething masses, their forms looking like shifting black stones in the gloom. The air, thick as it is, is clotted with moths. Their wings are not dusty but membranous and wet, and they flutter in clouds so dense they resemble smoke, their bodies constantly colliding. There are no predators and prey in a way that is easily understood. There is simply a cycle. The creatures feed on the moss, the fungi, each other’s leavings, and their own dead. They are born from the substrate, they live upon it, and when they die, their bodies are quickly broken down by smaller organisms, their matter reabsorbed into the glowing carpet.

There are no tools here, no signs of intellect. This entire world, a city-sized structure built with total intention, was not *made* by tools. It was grown. It is a single, colossal organism’s skeleton, or the hive of a species long since vanished, or perhaps it is still growing, adding a millimeter of new bone-scaffolding to its highest reaches every century.

To exist here is to be subsumed. The constant, thunderous noise scrubs thought clean. The humid, close warmth saps strength. The dim, featureless light offers no direction. There is only the slow, inexorable, deafeningly loud pulse of a world built of life, for life, a system in perfect, horrifying, and utterly normal balance with itself.
