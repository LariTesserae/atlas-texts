There is no ground. The world underfoot is a floor of interlocking, rust-pocked iron plates, their surfaces worn smooth by ages of water and friction. Every plate is slick with a perpetual film of condensation, running in rivulets toward drainage grates that sigh with a constant, gentle current. The air is cool and heavy, saturated with a fine mist that never coalesces into rain but never dissipates, clinging to everything in a silver sheen. It smells of wet stone, of deep, cold loam, and faintly, of ozone and hot metal.

This is a city built in a chasm, or perhaps it is a city that has become a chasm. It is a single, contiguous structure of staggering scale, a landscape of architecture. There are no individual buildings, only districts and sectors of an all-encompassing machine-edifice. The topography consists of stacked and cantilevered terraces, vast as plains, connected by a labyrinth of sky-bridges, external stairways, and skeletal crane gantries. Looking out from any ledge, you see not a horizon but a dizzying vertigo of more city, receding into the dim, grey light. Far below, through layers of fog and superstructure, you might glimpse the faint, mechanical twinkling of lower levels, a fallen sky of industry. Above, the same thing: the underside of a higher terrace hangs like a permanent storm cloud, its metal belly weeping steadily, feeding the world below.

And the world below is green. Impossibly, aggressively green. The city is choked with life. This is not the hopeful green of a ruin being reclaimed; it is the deliberate, engineered green of a hydroponic system gone feral, or perhaps, working exactly as intended. Thick, rubbery vines, heavy with water, crawl over every surface, their sucker-pads clamped to concrete and steel. They obscure massive ventilation ducts and swallow entire pylons. Enormous ferns, with fronds as broad as blankets, erupt from integrated, city-block-sized planters. Mosses, thick and plush as velvet carpets, grow in a hundred shades of emerald and jade, muffling the world. They pillow the sharp corners of machinery and line every channel, absorbing water and sound. The vegetation is not fighting the city; it is symbiotically fused with it. Pipelines, some glass, some corroded iron, snake alongside the vines, pulsing with a slow, viscous slurry of nutrients that occasionally leaks, creating patches of startlingly rich, dark muck where fungi bloom in pale, ghostly clusters.

For a place so dense and industrial, it is profoundly quiet. The constant drip and trickle of water is the dominant sound, a liquid percussion on every surface. There is no birdsong, no insectile drone. The fauna is limited to the nearly unseen: pale, blind isopods that feast on pipe-sludge, and long, translucent worms that churn the soil in the massive planters. The sounds of technology are muffled and distant, felt more than heard. A low, subsonic hum vibrates up through the soles of your feet, the breathing of a machine so vast its individual movements are silent. Occasionally, a section of wall will emit a soft pneumatic hiss, or a distant bridge will grind into a new position with the groaning complaint of ancient, unoiled gears. These are the only reminders that this is not a geological formation, but a mechanism.

And it is a mechanism in constant, chaotic flux. This is the great paradox of the place: it is ancient, weathered, and established in its form, yet it is violently, ceaselessly dynamic. The city is rebuilding itself, always. Without warning, a section of walkway a hundred yards long might retract into a wall, leaving a sheer drop where moments before there was a path. A great cascade of water, falling for a century from one terrace to another, might be suddenly rerouted, its roar ceasing abruptly to be replaced by the slow trickle of what remains. Massive panels shift, walls slide, entire blocks rise and fall on colossal pistons, all part of a city-wide metabolism that is incomprehensible. It is not construction and it is not demolition; it is a constant, purposeless-seeming reconfiguration. "The Shuffling," the few who live here call it.

To exist here is to be a spectator to a process that does not acknowledge you. You learn the rhythms not of the sun and moon—for there are none—but of the machine. You learn to recognize the subtle tremor that precedes a major structural shift, to stay away from plates that have begun to vibrate at a new frequency. You navigate by the unchanging shapes of the largest support pillars and by the unique species of moss that favor the runoff from certain copper-alloy conduits. Life is a state of perpetual, low-grade alertness, a quiet tension beneath the damp calm.

The light is a permanent twilight, filtering down from unseen, distant lumens high up in the ceiling of the world, or perhaps just from the accumulated glow of millions of individual work-lamps and status indicators, all blurred by the miles of mist into a uniform, shadowless dimness. Everything is starkly present but half-seen. You can see for miles, but all you see is more of the same oppressive, beautiful, terrible, and indifferent pattern: the dark grey of the metal, the dark green of the foliage, and the silver glint of the endless, weeping water. It is a city that drowned in the sky and has been breathing water ever since.
