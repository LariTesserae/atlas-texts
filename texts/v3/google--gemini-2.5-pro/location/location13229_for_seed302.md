The first thing to know is the sound.

It is not a sound that arrives at the ears; it is a sound that the skull is already full of. It is a pressure, a solid object of vibration that occupies all space. It is a single, immense, unwavering chord played on an organ the size of a mountain range. It has no rhythm, no melody, no variation in pitch or volume. It simply *is*, a deafening, crystalline hum that vibrates up through the soles of the feet, through the bones of the legs and spine, to meet the same vibration coming down from the air. To speak is impossible, as the sound would swallow the words before they were formed. To think is to have one’s thoughts flattened and stretched thin against the unyielding wall of noise.

The second thing is the light. It is absolute. There is no sun, no singular point of origin, but the sky itself is a flawless, bone-white sheet of brilliance. It is a light without warmth, a clinical, high-altitude glare that erases all shadow. Every surface is lit with equal, merciless intensity. Standing in it feels like being an object on a scanner bed, every flaw and feature illuminated from all directions at once. The air itself seems to glow, thick with a mist that catches and refracts the light into a blinding, pearlescent fog that never dissipates.

To lower your gaze from the white-out sky is to find the source of both the sound and the light's terrible diffusion. This high, open plateau is not made of rock, or soil, or sand. It is a landscape paved in beads.

Countless trillions of them, each the size of a small pearl, are packed together in a perfectly settled, impossibly dense mosaic that stretches to every horizon. They are not scattered; they are fitted, one against the other, with no space in between, forming a contiguous surface. They are old, weathered by eons of this strange stasis, each one worn perfectly smooth. They are made of a milky, opalescent crystal, and under the crushing light, each one glows from within.

This is the great organ. Every single bead resonates. Fueled by the unending light, each sphere vibrates at a precise, unchanging frequency. The hum of one is imperceptible, but the unified chorus of trillions upon trillions creates the deafening, monolithic sound. They are the singers, and the song is forever.

The world is wet. Not with rain, for nothing ever falls. Not with rivers, for nothing ever flows. Every single crystalline sphere has a perfect, glistening film of moisture. The cool air, saturated past its breaking point, constantly condenses onto the infinite surface area provided by the beads. Each bead wears a liquid skin, a tiny lens that captures the white light and splinters it, atomizing it into a billion microscopic rainbows that are immediately lost in the overwhelming glare. The water trembles with the vibration of the beads, held in a state of shimmering tension, but it never drips. The system is in a perfect, frozen equilibrium. The light forces the sound, the sound vibrates the beads, the vibration holds the water in stasis. Nothing changes. Ever.

The terrain rolls in vast, gentle swells, like a petrified ocean of milk-glass pearls. One can walk for miles across this resonant pavement, the crunch of your own weight a dissonant, temporary intrusion that is immediately annihilated by the permanent sound. The vista is immense, a regional-scale expanse of these luminous, humming hills under a sky of painful white. The air is thin and cool, sharp in the lungs, a stark contrast to the thick, visible moisture that clings to everything.

Life is almost entirely absent. There is nothing to eat, nowhere to hide from the light and sound. Yet, very rarely, something moves. It is a creature so adapted to this place that it is barely distinguishable from it. In a basin between two swells, something might be seen slowly, glacially, tracing a path across the beads. It is a form like a great, flattened woodlouse, perhaps twenty feet long. Its carapace is a mosaic of the same opalescent spheres as the ground, scavenged and cemented to its own body, making it a living piece of the landscape. It has no visible eyes, no mouth. It seems to navigate by feeling the minute variations in the universal hum, a creature of pure resonance. It moves by inches, filtering minerals directly from the condensed moisture on the spheres it passes over, its slow metabolism timed not to days or seasons, but to geologic ages. It is less an animal and more a mobile, self-perpetuating mineral formation. To see one is to see the landscape itself decide to briefly ambulate, a rare and unsettling glitch in the eternal stasis.

This place has no history and no future. It is a single, sustained moment. It is a rule set in stone and crystal and light: the world will be bright, and so it will scream, and in screaming, it will be still. It offers no quarter to biology, no silence for thought, no shadow for rest. It is a monument of sensory saturation, deafening, blinding, and utterly, perfectly, at peace.
