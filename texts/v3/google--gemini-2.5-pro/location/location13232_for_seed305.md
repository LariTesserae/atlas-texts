The noise is the first thing, the first wall you hit. It is not the crash of a wave or the roar of a waterfall, but a steady, complex, and deeply resonant industrial thrum. It is the sound of a trillion moving parts, a planetary-scale engine running at full capacity, never idling, never shutting down. It has a bass note that vibrates in your bones, a grinding midrange of geologic friction, and a high, keening whine of stressed metal, except there is no metal here.

The light is the second wall. The sky is not blue, or black, or grey; it is a solid, seamless dome of searing white, a sheet of incandescent magnesium. There is no sun, no moon, no point source to cast a shadow. The light is everywhere, coming from every direction at once, flattening perspective and bleaching all color to a stark monochrome of black detail on white glare. It is a cold light, containing no warmth, only an aggressive, clinical brightness that makes the eyes ache.

You are standing on a knife-edge ridge in a mountain range of impossible scale. The peaks are not weathered and ancient but sharp, serrated, and new, as if they were thrust up from the planet's mantle only yesterday. They are black, crystalline rock, fractured and angular. These ranges stretch beyond the horizon in every direction, a continent-spanning upheaval of fresh geology. The air is thin and carries the brutal chill of high altitude, frosting the edges of things with a persistent rime.

But nothing is bare. Every surface—every sheer cliff face, every needle-like spire, every boulder-strewn slope—is covered. This is not soil, but a living mat, a dense, purple-black felt of what must be called vegetation. It is thick, resilient, and rubbery to the touch. It is not photosynthetic; in this shadowless, cold glare, it has evolved a different metabolism. It seems to feed on the air itself, on the metallic dust that hangs in it, and on the perpetual dampness.

The dampness is not from rain. It is a condensation, a clammy sheen that coats every surface. It is the exhaust of the world's industry. Look closer at the slopes. They are not still. They are crawling.

This is the source of the noise, the heart of the world's engine. The fauna. It is overwhelming, a living tide that saturates the landscape, leaving no empty space. There are no individuals, only functions, castes in a global hive.

The largest are the **Geophages**, the Rock-Eaters. They are the size of freight cars, vast, trilobite-like creatures with dozens of articulated, piston-like legs. Their shells are the same black, crystalline material as the mountains they consume. Their undersides are a nightmare of grinding plates, diamond-hard mandibles that bite into the rock, pulverizing it. They move with an inexorable, slow-motion force, chewing the fresh mountains down, their passage a trail of shattered stone and scree. Their ceaseless grinding is the bass note of the world's song.

The scree they produce is immediately set upon by the second caste: the **Crucibles**. These are immense, semi-stationary mounds of flesh, like gargantuan, armored slugs. They have no visible sensory organs, only a vast, sphincter-like maw that sucks in the pulverized rock left by the Geophages. Within their bodies, biological furnaces operate at incredible temperatures. Plumes of hot, mineral-rich steam vent from rows of dorsal chimneys along their backs—this is the source of the planet's dampness, the hot breath that condenses on the cold rock and nourishes the purple felt-flora. The Crucibles process the stone, extracting specific metallic and silicate compounds. Their waste is extruded in the form of glowing, viscous cables of slag that cool into brittle, glassy threads.

Swarms of the third caste, the **Weavers**, descend upon these threads. They are smaller, insectile, a frantic, clattering tide of chrome-and-chitin bodies. They are a constant, high-speed motion, a blur of legs and manipulators. They snip the glassy threads into precise lengths and weave them into complex, latticed structures, anchoring them into the living mat. They are reinforcing the mountain, plating it in its own processed remains, building something that is neither natural nor artificial but both at once. They are cabling the living world together. Their frenetic, clicking clatter is the high, whining treble of the endless noise.

There is no room for anything else. The density is absolute. Geophages crawl over and past each other. Crucibles are packed flank-to-flank in the high valleys, their steam plumes merging into a low-hanging fog. The Weavers are a single, shimmering carpet of motion over everything. Life here is not a struggle for survival against the elements; it is a single, unified, geologic-scale process of production. The world is a factory, and the organisms are its cogs.

The entire system is a slow, patient cycle. The mountains are thrust up by immense tectonic forces, and they are immediately consumed, processed, and re-integrated by the biome. This is what passes for erosion here: not wind and water, but a biological industry that eats the land alive. Change is measured in the slow retreat of a mountain range over millennia, the gradual shifting of a Crucible colony over a geological epoch. The process is loud, violent, and overwhelming at the local level, but the system as a whole is in a state of near-permanent, grinding equilibrium. It has been running this way for longer than the mountains have existed, and it will continue long after they are ground to dust and replaced by a new range, born from the planet's molten heart to feed the machine.
