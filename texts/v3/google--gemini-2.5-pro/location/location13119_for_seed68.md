The first thing you register is the sound. It is not an event, but a condition of the air, a presence as fundamental as gravity. It is a vast, resonant hum, a B-flat so low and pervasive it ceases to be a note and becomes a physical pressure against the ears, the teeth, the bones of the skull. It is the sound of a world-sized engine idling just beneath the floor. This hum is the bedrock of the landscape; all other noises are merely texture upon it.

You are standing in one of the open spaces, a plaza of sorts, paved with large, interlocking hexagonal plates of a dark, basalt-like concrete. They are new, their edges still razor-sharp, without a single chip or crack. No weeds grow in the hairline seams. The ground is not level, but slopes gently toward a central channel covered by a grate of non-ferrous, bronze-colored alloy. The entire area feels recently assembled, as if the components were lowered into place last week and switched on.

This is a ward of a constructed city, a district perhaps the size of a small town, confined by towering, windowless walls of the same black concrete. There are no streets in a conventional sense, only these wide plazas and narrower, canyon-like corridors between immense, cubical buildings. The scale is strange; the buildings are massive, but the spaces are intimate, creating the feeling of being in a single, complex room with a very high ceiling. That ceiling is the sky, a permanent, pearlescent white, a soft-box that provides even, shadowless light from horizon to horizon.

The coolness is immediate. The air is damp and still, carrying a clean, metallic tang like ozone after a lightning strike, mixed with the scent of wet stone and rich loam. Water is not abundant, but it is everywhere. It does not rain. Instead, the air itself seems to weep. Great vertical fins of the same bronze alloy jut from the sides of the buildings, their surfaces beaded with a constant, heavy condensation. Fat, slow drops of perfectly clear water gather and fall with a ponderous rhythm, striking the concrete below with a percussive *plock* that is almost, but not quite, lost in the omnipresent hum. These drips trace dark paths on the ground, converging in the grated channels where the water vanishes with a quiet gurgle.

On this industrial skeleton, life has found a way to be lush. It is not a chaotic overgrowth, but something more deliberate, more integrated. The sheer faces of the concrete buildings are draped in thick, velvety curtains of moss, so dark green they are almost black. Growing out of this moss are ropes of fleshy, translucent vines. Their leaves are not leaves at all, but fluid-filled bladders, some small as a teardrop, others swollen to the size of a human fist. They tremble with the deep vibrations of the hum, their skins glinting in the flat light. They seem to drink moisture directly from the air. Where pipes and conduits snake along the base of the walls, they are encrusted with growths of fungus that look like cascades of melted, chalky-white wax, or tiered shelves of a substance resembling soft, blue coral.

The sparseness is in the arrangement. A colossal building, clad in its living shroud. A wide, empty plaza of wet concrete. Another building. There is no clutter, no detritus. Each element—structure, plaza, vegetation—is a discrete, monumental presence with clean air between it and the next.

Fauna is abundant, but quiet, its own sounds subsumed by the drone. The most visible are the snails. They are enormous, their spiraled shells whorled with shifting, iridescent colours, like oil on water. These shells are not calcium carbonate; they have the hardness and subtle gleam of a metallic alloy. They move with glacial slowness across the moss-cloaked walls and fungi-encrusted pipes, their rasping mouths methodically scraping nutrients from the strange flora. Their slime trails leave shimmering, quicksilver traces that dry into a fine, sparkling dust.

In the higher reaches, near the dripping cooling fins, small, swift-like birds with obsidian-dark feathers dart in and out of ventilation shafts. They build their nests from the fibrous husks of the bladder-vines and the metallic dust left by the snails. Their calls are sharp and high, but the drone swallows them, rendering their frantic activity into a silent ballet against the white sky. Other things stir in the shadows of the machinery, and in the deep, water-filled basins beneath the grates: segmented, armored creatures that scuttle in the dark, and pale, blind amphibians that pulse slowly in the filtered water, their life cycle tied to the patient mechanics of the place.

The rules of this world are becoming clear. This place was not abandoned. It was not built for humans or any organic life in the conventional sense. It is a machine. A vast, self-sustaining apparatus for… something. Perhaps it processes the atmosphere, or manufactures a substance in its silent, subterranean factories. Its operations are slow, measured in cycles of decades. The constant hum is its metabolism. The dripping condensation is its perspiration. The life that thrives here is not an infestation but a symbiotic element, perhaps even an intentional one. The mosses and fungi may help regulate temperature or filter effluents. The snails, with their metallic shells, might be part of the machine's own maintenance crew, bio-mechanical janitors processing waste and returning minerals to the system. Change happens here, but it is the slow, deliberate change of a glacier, not a river. A new layer of moss takes a year to gain a millimeter. A snail takes a decade to crawl the face of a single building. The sound never changes. The light never fails. The water never stops dripping. It is a city ticking like a clock with a sweep-second hand the size of a planet.
