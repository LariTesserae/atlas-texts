The first thing you notice is the silence. It is not an empty silence, not the void left by a departed sound, but a dense, foundational quiet that seems to be a property of the air itself. There is no wind. The air, cool and heavy with a moisture that never coalesces into rain, is perfectly still. It carries the scent of wet stone and something like crushed green stems.

This is a land of peaks, a young mountain range arrested in the moment of its birth. The rock is a dark, near-black basalt, fractured into sharp, aggressive angles. No water or ice has ever had the chance to smooth these edges; they remain as keen as knapped flint. From a distance, the mountains look like shattered glass stood on end. The scale is vast, a valley floor miles across cradled by these jagged tors. Yet between the major features—the spires of rock, the great, sheer cliffs—there are expanses of unnerving flatness, as if the land between the peaks were planed smooth by a giant’s hand.

Water does not flow here. A wide riverbed cuts through the valley, but its contents are a solid, unmoving ribbon of glassy, dark water, a petrified cascade frozen mid-tumble. The dampness of the landscape comes from the air itself. Every surface glistens with a permanent dew. Water beads on the faces of the rock, hangs in perfect, un-falling droplets from the tips of leaves, and coats exposed skin with a clammy film, yet nothing ever drips. It is a world in a state of suspended condensation.

This perpetual moisture feeds a shockingly lush ecology. But the green here is not a uniform blanket. It grows in dense, discrete islands. Thick cushions of emerald and lime-green moss, some as large as houses, cling to the lower slopes of the rock spires. They are so saturated with water they have a deep, velvety weight, swallowing any sound that might touch them. In the flatter areas, leathery, cabbage-like succulents grow in scattered, geometric patterns, their broad grey-green leaves arranged in perfect spirals. Strange, wiry trees with bark like polished bone find purchase in the deep crevices of the basalt, their branches holding stiff, unmoving canopies of dark, needle-like leaves. There are no flowers in the conventional sense, only crystalline growths that sprout from the moss beds, their petals like slivers of quartz that refract the clean, unwavering light into cold rainbows.

And there is a city here. It is undeniably a city, yet it defies the form of one. There is no grid, no central hub, no sprawling suburbs. Instead, massive, isolated structures rise from the valley floor, separated by a mile or more of moss and bare, damp stone. They are built from the same black basalt as the mountains, hewn into colossal, precise blocks. The architecture is severe and monumental. Solitary towers, so tall their tops vanish into the low, motionless cloud ceiling, stand like silent sentinels. Long, impossibly slender bridges, built of stone and thick, petrified timber, span the chasms between peaks, their undersides fuzzy with green moss. Great, open plazas are paved with hexagonal stones, their surfaces slick with the constant dew.

The technology is primitive. You see no metal beyond the occasional glint of bronze on a chisel or the head of a mallet. The immense scale of the construction speaks not of advanced machinery, but of unimaginable patience and time. Ropes, thick as a human torso and woven from some pale, fibrous plant, are coiled near half-finished works. Scaffolding is made of massive, interlocking timbers, lashed together with the same rope. This is an urbanism of manual craft, scaled up to a geological level.

Fauna is abundant, but it shares the world’s quietude. Great, shaggy beasts with six legs and backs like mossy flagstones graze placidly on the succulent patches, their movements slow and deliberate. They make no sound as they walk, their padded feet leaving dark prints on the damp stone. Birds that look like overlarge swifts, with iridescent black feathers, glide on the still air between the towers, never flapping, never calling out. Their flight paths are not random; they seem to be following invisible, unchanging circuits through the sky. Small, amphibious creatures with luminous, pale skin rest in the dampest crevices, their wide, dark eyes unblinking.

The rule of this world, the source of its deep and pervasive weirdness, is its permanence. Nothing changes. The mountain range is young, but it is not aging. The erosion is zero not because it is slow, but because it is arrested. The dynamic of the world is set to null. The clouds do not move. The light of the sun, when it appears, is a clean, steady, unwavering illumination, casting shadows that never shorten or lengthen. Time passes—the inhabitants live and die—but the world itself is a static set piece.

The only sources of change, the only sounds that truly break the foundational silence, are the inhabitants. They are tall, gaunt people, clad in simple tunics of woven fiber. Their actions are the sole dynamism in this equilibrium. The sharp *clink* of a bronze chisel on basalt, a sound that seems impossibly loud, rings out from a half-carved wall and then is utterly gone, swallowed by the damp air and moss. A shouted command from a construction foreman on a high bridge echoes once, sharply, and then vanishes. These people are the world's only engine of entropy. They slowly, deliberately carve their existence into a landscape that will never weather their marks, building a city that will never crumble in a world that is forever the same, day after unchanging day. Their work is the only story being told.
