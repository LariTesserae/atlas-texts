The first thing is the sound. Not a sound, but the sound. It is a physical presence, a pressure on the bones that precedes hearing. It is the grinding roar of a tectonic plate being milled into dust, the shriek of a billion tons of steam forced through a pinhole, the bass-heavy, all-encompassing thrum of a power that has no off-switch. Here, sound is the primary medium of existence, more fundamental than the fluid that carries it.

You are submerged. Not in water, not precisely. It is a warm, viscous broth, thick with suspended mineral glitter and glowing organic matter. It presses in from all sides, a constant, soupy embrace heated from below. The pressure is immense, but the fluid itself buoys you, holding you in a state of perpetual, slow-motion freefall. Currents move not like water, but like muscle, in unpredictable, turbulent spasms that can tumble you end over end without warning. One moment, the medium is as clear as warm gin; the next, it thickens into an opaque, swirling gel, then thins again.

Visibility is a luxury. There is no sun here. The world is a basin, a vast caldera sunk low into the crust, and the only light comes from within. Dim, arterial reds pulse from the abyssal floor, the glow of industrial heat painting the underside of the gloom. This infernal up-lighting is met by a competing, colder light from the flora.

And the flora is everywhere. This is a jungle of impossible botany, thriving in the crushing depths. Great, kelp-like trees, with trunks of braided, rubbery fiber, rise for hundreds of feet, their canopies forming a dense, tangled roof far above. Their broad, translucent leaves are not green but a deep, bruised purple, and they ripple constantly in the chaotic currents. Every surface—the rock, the stalks of the canopy-trees, the scattered detritus on the seafloor—is carpeted in a lush shag of moss that glows with a soft, cyan light. Pale, ghostly fronds, as delicate as spun glass, unfurl from crevices, pulsing with bioluminescence in slow, hypnotic rhythm. It is a dense, high-saturation world, layers of growth upon layers of growth, a sterile lushness. There are no animals. No fish dart between the stalks, no crustaceans scour the glowing moss. The overwhelming energy of this place has produced life, but not a food chain. It is an ecosystem of one kingdom, thriving on geothermal heat and chemical absurdity.

The terrain itself is young and violent. Jagged spires of black, glassy rock, obsidian or basalt, thrust up from the floor, their edges still razor-sharp, uneroded. They look like they cooled yesterday. These spires are the bones of the landscape, freshly extruded from a tormented geology. They are a stark, geometric counterpoint to the soft, swaying chaos of the vegetation.

Between these spires, scattered across the valley floor, are the artifacts. They are the source of the heat, the sound, the turbulence. They are machines. Not ruins, but active, functioning industrial components, divorced from any recognizable factory or purpose. A colossal iron piston, the size of a silo, cycles endlessly up and down, plunging into the silty bed and retracting with a hydraulic scream that shakes the fluid for miles. Nearby, a gear-train of interlocking cogs, each twenty feet across, turns with immense, groaning force, meshed with nothing, grinding against the open fluid. Elsewhere, a furnace door swings open on a massive hinge, vomiting a silent billow of pure heat—a visible warp in the liquid—before slamming shut again, the concussive force a solid blow you feel in your teeth.

These are not part of a coherent system. They are the dissected organs of a vast, industrial body, scattered and left to run in a state of perpetual, nonsensical motion. They are the architects of this place, their waste heat feeding the glowing flora, their ceaseless churning stirring the medium into a frenzy.

The reality of the place is fungible, especially near the machines. The sound they make seems to actively destabilize causality. A stalk of kelp might appear to be in three places at once, its image stuttering in the churning medium. The light from the mosses will occasionally bend at impossible angles, creating fleeting, distorted lenses that show you a patch of the seafloor from a dozen perspectives simultaneously. Time itself feels elastic, stretching and snapping in rhythm with the cycling of a titanic piston.

The rule of this world is motion without motive. The First Law is the Sound, the unceasing, deafening pronouncement of industry without purpose. The Second Law is the Churn, the constant, violent kneading of the world by forces that build and destroy with equal disinterest. A new rock spire may jut from the floor, cracking the foundation of a great gear, while a current, spun into a vortex by a steam-vent, rips a thousand-year-old kelp tree from its mooring. There is no stasis, only a high-energy, turbulent equilibrium of creation and chaos. It is the frantic activity of a demolished anthill, but the ants are mindless engines and the ground itself is coming apart and reforming with every passing second. It is a world caught in the infinite, roaring scream of its own birth.
