The first thing you notice is the sound. It's not a discrete noise, but the very texture of the air—a deep, resonant, unwavering hum. It’s a B-flat tone that vibrates in your teeth and sinuses, the sound of immense and patient power. It is so constant that your mind begins to treat it as silence, a new baseline against which smaller sounds struggle to be heard.

You are standing on a platform, perhaps a hundred feet across, at the very top of the world. Below you is not ground, but a roiling, endless sea of white clouds, their tops gilded by a sun that feels thin and close. The air is cool on your face but the environment is thick with a greenhouse warmth, a pocket of manufactured climate clinging to this peak. The world exists on this single, isolated stage.

The platform is not natural. The ground is a foundation of jagged, black basalt, so fresh and sharp it looks like it was shattered yesterday. Its edges are razors, its faces are sheer, unweathered by wind or time. But bolted and grouted into this primordial rock is the source of the sound: the Machine.

It is a great, industrial heart. A complex of enormous metal spheres, maybe thirty feet in diameter, are connected by an arthritic network of thick-walled pipes and transfer bridges. Everything is painted a faded, institutional green, the kind of color chosen for its resilience, not its beauty. But the paint is old, and rust bleeds in weeping orange streaks from every rivet and seam. The surface of the machine sweats constantly. Not with the dew of a cool morning, but with a heavy, oily condensation. The warm air, saturated with moisture, hits the chilled surfaces of the spheres and pipes, and the water is wrung from the atmosphere.

This is a place of water. It drips from every surface, a thousand tiny, asynchronous clocks. It runs in rivulets down the curved green plating of the spheres. It pools in the dips of sagging catwalks and overflows, tracing green-black paths of algae down support struts. The deep hum of the machine is accompanied by this constant, percussive symphony of drips, dribbles, and gentle streams. The sound is loud, yet has the quality of a quiet room with a leaking faucet.

And where there is water, there is life. The vegetation is not just lush, it is suffocating. It has swallowed whole sections of the machine. Thick, verdant carpets of moss, spongy and engorged, cover the horizontal surfaces, muffling footsteps and turning flat planes into soft, rolling hillocks. Great, wiry ferns erupt from the joints of pipes, their fronds unfurling in the perpetual damp. Creeping vines, thick as a wrist and covered in waxy, dinner-plate-sized leaves, have snaked their way up the machinery, their tendrils probing for purchase in the seams of metal plates. The line between the built and the grown is blurred, a slow-motion battle where the patient green tide is clearly winning.

The air smells of ozone from the electrical components of the machine, of hot metal and lubricating oil, and beneath it all, the rich, loamy scent of wet earth and decaying plant matter. The light is bright but featureless, the sun's glare diffused by the ever-present haze of water vapor that steams gently off the warmer parts of the engine.

There are almost no animals. No birds wheel in the thin sky; no rodents scurry along the pipes. The noise and the isolation have made this place an ecological island of a very specific sort. But look closer. On the sweating, metallic skin of the spheres, slow-moving beetles, the size of a thumbnail, make their patient way. Their carapaces are not chitin, but a dull, iridescent metal, like heat-blued steel. They don't seem to eat the vegetation. Instead, they press their mouths to the rivulets of pure, condensed water, drinking directly from the machine's tears. They are the only fauna, perfectly adapted to this strange, manufactured ecosystem.

The purpose of this place becomes clear when you follow the largest of the pipes. They all converge, sloping downward, toward the center of the platform. Here, carved out of the raw basalt, is a simple, circular basin, perfectly round and lined with a thin layer of what looks like grey, waterproof cement. All the weeping of the machine, all the captured atmospheric water, is channeled here. The pool is startlingly clear, the black rock at its bottom visible through twenty feet of still, pure water. Ripples from the constant influx spread slowly across its surface. There is no outlet. It is a cistern at the top of the sky.

This place does not change quickly. The machine has hummed its B-flat note for decades, maybe centuries. The water has dripped, the moss has grown, the level in the basin has risen by millimeters a year. It is a slow factory, a patient process, harvesting the sky inaudibly slowly. The only evidence of its builders is a small, rusted-out control shack on the edge of the platform, its doorway open to the elements, its single metal chair long since swallowed by a cushion of moss. This is a place built for a purpose, and then left to fulfill it, a loud, wet, lonely garden of metal and leaf, humming its single note to the empty sky, waiting for a collection that may never come.
