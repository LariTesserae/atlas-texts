The air doesn't move. It is a thick, solid presence, so saturated with heat and water that breathing feels like drinking. You are standing on a tableland at the roof of the world, a vast plateau of rust-colored stone and impossibly deep green life. The sky is not blue but a milky, luminous white, a cauldron lid trapping the steam that rises endlessly from the ground below. From the edge of this high place, you can see for a hundred miles, but you see no ground. What you see is the top of a swirling, sea-like cloud deck, and rising from it, like archipelago islands, are the flat-topped silhouettes of other, similar plateaus.

The ground underfoot is not simple rock. It’s a spongy, resilient carpet of thick mosses and liverworts, metres deep in places, that squelches with every step. Water is not a feature *in* the landscape; it *is* the landscape. It weeps from black rock faces in slow, syrupy curtains. It condenses on the underside of every broad leaf, gathering into a heavy drop that falls with a soft *plock* onto the moss below. The air itself is a reservoir, and the slightest drop in temperature makes it release its burden, causing shimmering sheets of rain to drift down even when the sky is clear of storm clouds.

This is a world of immense, quiet pressure. It's in the heat, the humidity, and it’s also in the rock itself. From the ground comes a low, pervasive hum, a vibration felt more in the bones and teeth than heard with the ears. This is the source of the heat. The plateau is not dead stone; it is a geological engine. Dotted across the landscape are immense pillars of weathered, grey-black basalt, some fifty metres high, shot through with veins of dull, coppery metal that bleed green-blue oxidized trails down their sides. These pillars are warm to the touch, and they act as immense heat sinks and radiators. Water that seeps deep into the fractured rock is superheated by geothermal chaos, and the whole plateau sighs and breathes with its release.

Great fissures, some narrow, some wide enough to be called canyons, cut across the tableland. From their depths, plumes of clean, white steam rise in slow, rhythmic pulses. There is no roar, only a steady, powerful *whoosh* like the exhalation of a colossal lung. The rhythm is not constant; it is the slow, deliberate work of a machine that has been operating for eons. A fault line, over geological time, has become a release valve. A magma pocket, a boiler. The system is ancient, its parts weathered into forms that look almost natural. There are no right angles, no logos, no tailings piles. The erosion is established; the factory has settled into its form, becoming part of the geology it exploits. The metallic veins in the pillars aren't wires, but they channel telluric currents that cause the air around them to crackle with the faint smell of ozone.

Life has not just adapted to this; it is integrated into the machinery. The vegetation is a dense, layered riot. Towering tree-ferns, their fronds the size of small boats, create a high canopy. Their trunks are slick with algae and clotted with epiphytic orchids whose flowers are the colour of hot steel. Below them, a thick understory of broad-leafed plants—some with a waxy, water-repelling sheen, others with a felt-like texture to capture condensing moisture—jostle for any available light. Growing directly on the hot rock near steam vents are "boiler blooms," strange, silicon-based flora that look like coral fans made of cooling slag. When the pressure cycle hits its peak and a gust of hot steam erupts, their seed pods burst with a quiet *hiss*, casting spores into the moving air.

Fauna is abundant but strangely silent. The constant dripping and the low hum of the earth are the dominant sounds. Swarms of iridescent beetles, their carapaces like polished hematite, crawl over the metal-veined pillars, scraping minerals with hardened mandibles. Their clicking is a soft, percussive rain beneath the other sounds. Large, six-legged grazers, somewhere between a tapir and an armadillo, move slowly through the undergrowth. Their bodies are covered in thick, keratinous plates that are perpetually slick with condensing water, helping to regulate their temperature. They are essentially silent, communicating through scents released from glands on their flanks.

The ecosystem is active, in constant, slow motion. Hidden in the canopy or camouflaged against the dark rock are the predators. The most formidable is the Condenser Stalker, a creature that has evolved to hunt using the landscape's own rhythms. It has a long, sinuous body and wide, membranous limbs that allow it to glide on the thermal updrafts from the steam fissures. Its skin is a shifting pattern of mottled greens and greys, and its eyes see in the infrared, perceiving the world as a map of heat signatures. It will wait for hours above a game trail, motionless, letting condensation gather on its cool skin until it is dripping, indistinguishable from the weeping foliage. It waits for the geothermal pulse, and in the moment the steam roars and visibility drops to zero, it descends.

There is no sense of malice or chaos. The world operates on a slow, powerful, industrial logic. The light of the sun, filtered through the perpetual haze, is even and diffuse, casting few sharp shadows. As "dusk" approaches—more a gradual thickening of the white sky to grey—the metallic veins in the pillars begin to glow with a faint, cherry-red light, discharging the energy they’ve accumulated. The geode beetles, which have absorbed this energy all day, begin to luminesce a cool blue, drifting through the ferns like slow-moving sparks. The landscape is a factory with no builders, a machine with no purpose but to be. It is settled, powerful, and utterly indifferent, its processes churning on in the hot, wet silence at the top of the world.
