The world is a tableland of rust and violet, kiln-fired and ancient. It is a high, open plateau stretching into a haze of shimmering heat. The horizon is a serrated edge of distant mesas, their forms softened by eons of abrasion, looking less like stone and more like the worn teeth of a colossal jawbone. There is no soil, only compacted dust and dunes of fine, metallic particles that whisper and shift in the thermal currents. The ground underfoot is warm to the touch, humming with a deep, resonant heat that has soaked through to its core.

The air itself is thin and caustic, smelling of hot metal, ozone, and something else, something like burnt sugar or singed nerves. There is no water. Not a drop. No rivers, no clouds, no humidity. The concept feels alien here, a forgotten chemical myth. The physics of this place seem to have outlawed the molecule, choosing instead a dry, volatile chemistry that expresses itself in static discharges that crackle from rock to rock, and in the very substance of the world.

There are no plants. No lichen, no moss, no blade of grass. The landscape is a testament to inorganic chemistry. But it is not dead.

From the shimmering distance, the noise asserts itself first. It is not the sound of wind or geology, but the sound of industry. A vast, distributed, and unending cacophony: the percussive clang of a thousand hammers, the high-tension scream of turbines, the grinding of colossal gears, and the rhythmic, percussive hiss of pneumatic vents. The sound is loud, yet the emptiness of the space swallows its echoes, creating a disorienting sense of things being both near and miles away simultaneously. The sound is the true atmosphere of this place.

And then you see them. The fauna. They are overwhelming.

They are not biological in any recognizable sense. They are geomechanical. Swarms, herds, and solitary titans of articulated metal and ceramic crawl, scuttle, and roll across the plains. A herd of tripod walkers, each fifty feet tall, stride with a slow, deliberate gait. Their bodies are bulbous, boiler-like chambers of patinated copper, their legs spindly and bird-like. From vents along their carapaces, they exhale plumes not of steam, but of incandescent gas that briefly illuminates their own, intricate workings before being whipped away by the hot wind. They seem to be grazing, lowering their heads to dredge the metallic sands with magnetic probosces. They are not eating. They are filtering. Sorting.

Elsewhere, a different species—a low-slung, centipede-like entity half a mile long—carves a perfectly straight trench in the violet bedrock. Each of its hundreds of legs is a hydraulic piston tipped with a diamond-hard chisel. It moves with the slow, inexorable patience of a glacier, yet the noise of its work is a deafening shriek of tortured stone. It is not building anything. The trench it leaves behind is slowly filled by a different, smaller type of creature, a swarm of beetle-like automatons that secrete a molten, glassy substance that hardens into seamless obsidian. Then, other machines will come, perhaps in a century, perhaps in a millennium, to grind that obsidian back into dust.

This is the ecology. A closed loop of pointless, magnificent industry. These entities are not built; they seem to be born. In heavily-weathered depressions that might be called nesting grounds, smaller versions of the titans can be seen, still and silent, as if being assembled by invisible hands or growing from the land itself. They run on a thermodynamic heresy, drawing energy from the temperature differential between their scorching inner workings and the merely furnace-hot air. They are perpetual motion machines in a universe that should forbid them.

The weirdness is profound, a constant, low-level breakdown of causality. The light of the single, white sun casts sharp shadows, but the shadows sometimes lag behind the object casting them. The metallic dunes will spontaneously organize themselves into complex geometric patterns, hold the shape for an hour, then collapse. The industrial creatures produce things—byproducts of their endless processing. They might excrete a perfect, polished sphere of tungsten, which sits on the plains for a decade before sublimating into nothing. They extrude filaments of solid sound. They weave lattices of captured light. These artifacts are immaculate, esoteric, and utterly useless. They are the thoughts of a mad, mechanical god, given form and then abandoned.

The landscape is the result of this eternal process. The established, eroded forms are not the work of wind and water, but the long-term, cumulative effect of this industrial ballet. The mesas are not mesas; they are slag heaps from a forgotten eon of production. The canyons are the abandoned quarries. The shimmering particles of the desert are the metal filings of ten million years of grinding.

Despite the clamor and the overwhelming presence of the fauna when they are encountered, the world is defined by its emptiness. The herds are vast but few, and the space between them is immense. You can stand for hours in the humming silence between the waves of noise, seeing nothing but the heat-distorted horizon. This place is not changing, not in a human-scaled way. The cycle is slow, patient, and eternal. The machines process the world, the world becomes the machines, and the noise goes on forever. It is an industrial engine the size of a planet, running in neutral, forever.
