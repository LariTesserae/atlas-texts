This place is a basin, a dish in the highlands, cupped by sheer, weeping cliffs of dark, slick rock. The scale is that of a grand cathedral nave, enclosed and intimate. There is no sky, only an overcast ceiling of thick, warm vapour that perpetually condenses and drips. The light is uniform, shadowless, and pearlescent, as if filtered through an infinite layer of pearl-lustre cloud.

The air is heavy, warm, and utterly still. It is so saturated with moisture that to breathe it feels like drinking. Every surface is slick with a film of condensation. The dominant sound is the quiet, irregular percussion of water dripping. A drop from a high ledge into a pool of moss, a trickle tracing a path down a stone face, a soft *pat-pat-pat* onto a broad, waxy leaf. It is a silence filled with the sound of water returning to water.

The ground is not soil but a deep, saturated mattress of mosses and liverworts in impossible shades of green, from near-yellow to a deep, resonant emerald that is almost black. It swallows sound and cushions every non-existent footstep. Walking here would be a slow, sinking effort. The basin floor is not flat, but undulates in soft, ancient hillocks, the forms of a landscape long past its dramatic youth, now settled into a deep, waterlogged equilibrium. The edges of the basin are defined by the weeping cliffs, where water seeps constantly from hairline fissures in the stone, nurturing hanging gardens of fern and algae.

The vegetation is lush, yet starkly arranged. There is no chaotic undergrowth. Instead, rising from the carpet of moss at deliberate, harmonious intervals, are the primary inhabitants of this place. They are not trees in any recognisable sense. They are immense, vase-like organisms, perhaps a form of colossal, silicified lichen or fungus. Their trunks, if they can be called that, are thick, greyish-white, and have the texture of unpolished marble, ridged with the slow ripples of their growth over millennia. Each one stands alone, a solitary presence in a field of green, spaced far from its neighbours.

Each of these "Vessels," some as tall as a person, some as wide as a ceremonial drum, opens at its top into a broad, shallow basin of its own. Here, the condensing atmosphere and the gentle, perpetual drip from above collect. The water within these living chalices is placid, clear at the surface but growing murkier with depth, thick with a suspension of golden spores that circulate in languid, hypnotic currents, stirred by no wind, but by some slow, internal chemistry.

There are no animals. No insects drone in the humid air, no frogs call from the mossy pools, no birdsong echoes from the cliffs. The ecosystem is ruthlessly simple: rock, water, moss, and the great Vessels. The entire dynamic of the place is turned inward, upon a single, bizarre process.

For this place has a form of technology, a primitive craft without a craftsman. Anything that falls into the water of the Vessels—a stray leaf from a cliff-fern, a sloughed-off piece of moss, a larger flake of the Vessel's own inner lining—does not simply rot. The spore-rich, mineral-heavy water is a medium of slow petrification. Over immense periods of time, organic matter is replaced, molecule by molecule, by a dense, flint-like substance. The gentle, ceaseless gyre of the water acts as a tumbler, smoothing and shaping the object as it is transformed.

The cycle is glacially slow. A Vessel might spend a thousand years on a single piece. When the slow alchemy is complete, a subtle shift in the organism's internal pressure will cause it to gently tip, decanting its water and depositing its creation onto the soft moss below.

And so, scattered across the green floor, half-sunk in the yielding carpet, are these artifacts. They are smooth, dense, and cool to the touch. They have the familiar, weighty shapes of primitive hand-tools: teardrop-shaped axes, rounded rubbing stones, palm-sized scrapers with worked-looking edges. But they are not flint or obsidian. Cross-sections would reveal the faint, ghostly outlines of leaf veins, the cellular structure of moss, the fibrous grain of ancient plant matter. They are fossils, not tools. They are the strange, lithic fruit of a silent, living world. They are a mimicry of intention, born from a place with no mind. They are made, but not used. They simply lie where they have been cast, slowly being reclaimed by the encroaching moss, waiting for a purpose that will never arrive. The only change here is the gradual accumulation of these beautiful, useless objects.
