The air is the first thing you notice, because it is two opposite things at once: full and empty. It is empty of warmth, of scent, of humidity. It is a blade-sharp cold that feels scoured and pure, the kind of cold that exists only at extreme altitudes where the sky is a dark, bruised violet even at noon. But it is also full. It is full of light.

The light is not a gentle wash. It is an active, architectural presence. Unfiltered by atmosphere, it pours into this small basin, a natural amphitheater no larger than a cathedral's nave, cupped between jagged, monumental peaks that are beyond the scope of this particular place. The light strikes a dense forest of crystalline spires and is shattered into a billion pieces. The glare is absolute, a physical weight on the eyes. Without a deep hood or slitted goggles, you would be blind in minutes.

This is a world without vegetation. The ground and everything that grows from it is mineral. The floor of the basin is a dark, porous basalt, pocked and scarred like ancient bone. From this dark foundation rises a thicket of what could be mistaken for ice, but isn't. These are the spires. They range from the thickness of a thumb to the girth of an old tree, soaring up to twenty or thirty feet. They are packed together, a dense and nearly impassable forest of blades, pillars, and fins of a translucent, milky-white crystal. The material—call it acholite—is fibrous in its structure, and where a spire has broken, it has snapped cleanly, leaving a surface that glitters with its own internal light.

The place is old, its forms settled. The spires are weathered not by crumbling but by a slow, sculptural polishing. Wind, laden with fine ice crystals, has smoothed their edges over millennia, giving them the soft, matte finish of sea-glass. They feel cool and impossibly smooth to the touch. The basalt floor is similarly worn, its hollows filled with a fine, dark grit that is the dust of ages.

This is a damp world, but not a wet one. The water cycle is subtle and constant. During the blinding brightness of the day, the intense light provides just enough energy to melt the thinnest layer of rime that coats the shaded sides of the spires and the dark basalt. This meltwater doesn't form pools; it is instantly wicked away by the porous rock, drawn into its fine capillaries. As the sun sinks and the temperature plummets, this trapped moisture freezes. The expansion is infinitesimally small, but over eons, it is the engine of this place. It is this slow, internal frost-heave that pushes the acholite spires upwards from their basalt seedbed, a geological growth measured in millimeters per century. It is also this process that causes the constant, subtle sounds.

The landscape is mostly quiet. There is no birdsong, no insectile hum. The dominant sound is absence. But it is not silence. When the wind picks up, it doesn't whistle; it moves through the dense, blade-like fins of the spires and creates a complex, shifting resonance. It is not one note, but a chord of dozens, even hundreds of overtones, a low, glassy humming that you feel in your teeth and sternum. This is the landscape's voice. As the temperature drops at twilight, a different sound emerges: a series of sharp, delicate *pings* and *ticks* as the cooling acholite contracts. It sounds like a vast, untuned wind chime being stirred by a gentle hand.

There are signs that others have been here. Not to build a city, but to listen. Scattered between the spires are a few low, circular structures, crude stone rings of stacked basalt, barely waist-high. They are not shelters from the cold, but from the wind. Inside one of these rings, the roaring hum of the gusts subsides, and the subtler resonances of the individual spires become clear. In the center of the largest ring is a single, flat-topped stone, its surface hollowed into a shallow seat, polished by long use.

Attached to the outer walls of these listening posts are the only artifacts. They are simple chimes, crafted from shards of fallen acholite. Each shard has been painstakingly knapped and ground—primitive work done with patient hands and other stones—to produce a specific pitch when struck. They hang from twisted cords of some fibrous sinew, not of animal origin but perhaps peeled from the base of the less stable spires. They do not ring in the wind. They are hung to be struck by hand, perhaps to find a sympathetic resonance with the great spires themselves, to join the chorus.

This place has a rhythm. It is the slow, active cycle of light and dark, melt and freeze, sound and silence. The rules are simple: the light will blind you, the cold will kill you, and the ground beneath your feet is imperceptibly, inexorably, alive. The only fauna are the ghosts of those who sat in the stone rings, their purpose lost to time, their patient marks the only testament to a people who came to a world of brilliant, singing glass and chose not to conquer it, but simply to listen.
