This is the Great Bellows. The name is a feeling before it is a word, a vibration that works its way up through the soles of your feet and into your bones. The sound is the first and last thing you will know of this place. It is a roar composed of a billion smaller sounds, a wall of living noise so absolute it feels like a physical pressure against the skin. It is the sound of growth, of friction, of life devouring itself to make more life.

You are underground, in a cavern of impossible scale—a valley carved not from stone, but from life itself. The ground beneath you is not rock or soil but a springy, warm substrate of interwoven, petrified, and still-growing root-systems. It is a floor of rust-colored cellulose, hard as ironwood in some places, spongy and yielding in others. Step unwisely and you might sink ankle-deep into a pocket of raw, nascent growth, a pulpy mass of plant matter still deciding what it will become. The world is embryonic, perpetually under construction. Great, smooth-barked pillars of fungus-flesh, miles thick, rise to a ceiling you cannot see. They are the load-bearing columns of this world, but they are not static. You can feel their titanic, hydraulic pressure; you can hear the deep, groaning bass note as they infinitesimally but ceaselessly expand, pushing the unseen ceiling ever higher. Cracks appear in the ground-mat not from tectonic stress, but from the uncontainable force of a new trunk-spire shouldering its way into existence. Landslides here are not of rock, but of sloughed-off bark scales the size of rafts, and gushing torrents of dry, fibrous pulp.

There is no water. The concept is alien here. The air is warm, verging on hot, but it is a dry, desiccating heat, like the inside of a vast kiln. It is thick, not with moisture, but with a blizzard of other things: shimmering golden pollen, shed chitinous dust, and fibrous spores that catch the light like motes in a sunbeam. The scent is of baking bread, ozone, and a thousand strange, spicy-sweet resins. Life here is not animated by H₂O, but by a thick, viscous, amber-colored fluid—a kind of biological sap or ichor that circulates within the living architecture. This is the world's blood.

Light is everywhere, bathing the cavern in the honest, even glow of a cloudy afternoon. It does not come from above. The source is the flora itself. High on the colossal trunks, and studding the arching canopy of interwoven branches, are huge, bladder-like organs of bioluminescence. Some are sacs of cool, white light, others are pulsating nodules of warm, yellow-orange energy. They are the suns of the Bellows, and their collective luminance is steady, constant, and bright. They hum with a high-frequency thrum that adds a searing upper-register to the deafening chorus of the place.

And the chorus is made primarily of the fauna. Life is not just present, it is an overwhelming, crushing saturation. The air shimmers with insectile swarms so dense they form liquid-like clouds of iridescent bodies, their collective wing-beats a monstrous, high-pitched stridulation. Larger creatures, skittering things built of chitin and salvaged plant-fiber, crawl across every surface. They are the scrapers, the harvesters. They are not grazing on leaves, but boring into the living wood with rasping mandibles and drill-like probosces to tap into the veins of precious ichor within. Their grinding noises are like a thousand mills at work. Draped from the higher branches are colossal, bag-like organisms, blind and pale, that rhythmically inflate and deflate, filtering the spore-choked air for sustenance. Their collective exhalation is the deep, whooshing sigh that gives the Bellows its name. Flocks of leathery-winged predators, more like bats fused with dragonflies, dart through the clouds of lesser creatures, their snapping jaws adding a sharp, percussive crackle to the soundscape. Everything is moving, everything is hunting or feeding or fleeing or growing. It is a turbulent, hyperactive ecosystem in a state of perpetual, frenzied climax.

Faint signs of another kind of presence exist, scattered and almost lost in the chaos. There are shelters, not built, but carved. A hollow scooped out of a colossal, fallen root, its entrance marked with chiseled symbols that have already begun to blur as the living wood slowly works to heal itself. You might find a ladder of lashed-together femurs and chitin plates, leading up the side of a trunk towards a hidden alcove. These are the marks of the Weavers. They are hunched, quiet things, moving through the pandemonium with a practiced economy of motion. They carry tools not of metal, but of sharpened bone and the diamond-hard husks of a certain beetle. They don't build structures to last; they create temporary pockets of relative stability, weaving screens of living vine to baffle the noise, carving channels to divert a slow-moving flow of plant-pulp, or harvesting the amber ichor with delicate siphons. They are nomads in a world that moves around them, their existence a constant, desperate act of maintenance against the eruptive force of the Bellows. Their artifacts are ephemeral, soon to be swallowed by the next wave of growth.

The rules of this world are simple and brutal. There is no stillness. Stasis is death. The ground you stand on may not be here in an hour. The passage you used yesterday might be a solid wall of new growth today. Survival is not about strength, but about an intimate understanding of the rhythms of growth, the ability to read the groans and creaks that signal an impending "growth-quake." There is no thirst, only the deep, biological craving for the viscin that fuels all motion. Here, geology and biology are one and the same, and the defining force is not erosion, but a chaotic, deafening, and unending storm of creation.
