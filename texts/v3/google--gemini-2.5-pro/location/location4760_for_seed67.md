The first thing is the warmth, and the wet. It is not the clean, direct heat of a sun, but a heavy, saturated warmth that clings to the skin like a second layer of clothing. The air is thick enough to feel, a soupy medium that beads on any cool surface. Which is to say, no surface. Everything here is the same temperature as the air. Water does not condense so much as it is perpetually present, a slick pellicle on all things, a constant, silent weeping from the world itself.

There is no soil underfoot. The ground is a vast, unending plain of off-white ceramic tiles, perfectly fitted, their edges still sharp as if they were laid only yesterday. They are faintly slick to the touch. This plain is not entirely flat. It is a landscape of low, broad swells, like a frozen ocean, so immense that the curvature of the world is a tangible thing you can feel in your bones. The horizon is a clean, sharp line in every direction, broken only by the haze.

Across this ceramic expanse, at regular and repeating intervals, rise the structures. They are not buildings in any recognizable sense. They are more like the functional organs of some planetary-scale machine. Squat, dark, metallic cubes the size of warehouses hum with a bass note so low it is felt rather than heard. From these cubes, colossal pipes of a dark, greenish alloy run in perfectly straight lines for miles, some flush with the tiled ground, others raised on thick pylons. Grates of an unknown composite material, arranged in intricate geometric patterns, interrupt the ceramic plains, breathing out slow, rhythmic pulses of even warmer, wetter air. Nothing is rusted. Nothing is cracked or worn. The edges of every grate, every tile, every pipe fitting, are perfect and unworn. This world is young in its state of repair, but ancient in its unchanging nature.

The quiet is profound. It is the defining feature against the industrial backdrop. The thrum from the cubes is a constant, a pressure in the ears that is soon forgotten, becoming the baseline of silence itself. The only other sound is the drip. Slow, fat, syrupy drops of water fall from the undersides of the raised pipes, from the joints and knuckles of the vast plumbing. They fall onto the tiles with a soft, resonant *plock*, a sound that is not sharp but dense, like a water drop falling into honey. There is no wind, ever. The hazy air is perfectly still.

Daily, a sun-like light source passes overhead. It is a naked, white light, offering no comfort, only illumination. It reveals the true density of this place, which is not in the structures, but in the life that covers them.

There are no plants. The overwhelming fauna of this world are the reason for its perfect, clean stasis. They are everywhere. On close inspection, the off-white tiles are not uniform in their color; they are tessellated with millions upon millions of tiny creatures, each the size of a grain of rice. They have soft, translucent bodies and move with an imperceptible slowness, their minute rasping mouthparts polishing the ceramic, consuming the microscopic accretions that the mineral-rich water deposits. They are a living, self-replicating cleaning crew.

Clinging to the pipes and pump-housings are larger forms. They resemble slugs or mollusks, their bodies a vitreous, shimmering grey, but their backs are armored with articulated plates of the same greenish alloy as the pipes they inhabit. These are the grazers. They move in silent, peristaltic waves, their single purpose to lick the weeping moisture from the metal surfaces. Their passage leaves behind a gleaming, pristine track. They cluster so thickly around the joints and valves that the structures themselves seem to be made of living, breathing flesh. They have no eyes. They have no voices.

In the shadows beneath the raised pipes, one might glimpse the collectors. They are larger, perhaps the size of a dog, and their form is a disturbing hybrid of the organic and the built. They have many-jointed limbs of the dark composite material found in the grates, but their core is a soft, pulsating sac. They move with a slow, deliberate purpose, gathering the smaller creatures that have died or fallen, or sipping directly from pools of leaked lubricant near the base of the pylons. They are the recycling system.

This entire world is a closed loop, a perfectly balanced terrarium on a continental scale. The machinery generates the heat and the nutrient-rich vapor. The fauna consume the outputs and maintain the machinery. Nothing is wasted. Nothing ever changes. The sun comes, the sun goes. The light shifts, the shadows lengthen and retreat, but the low hum remains constant. The slow drips keep their patient rhythm. The silent, numberless creatures continue their work.

To be here is to be an anomaly, a foreign object in a system that has no place for you. You are the only thing that is not part of the function. The creatures, though dense enough to brush against with every step, take no notice. They have no concept of predator or prey, only of surface and substance. It is a world of immense, quiet, and unending industry, where life is not an expression of chaotic evolution, but a distributed, biological tool, performing its single, eternal task.
