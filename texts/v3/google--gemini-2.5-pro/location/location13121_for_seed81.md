This is a city built on a principle of absence.

You arrive on a high, sprawling plateau, and the first thing you feel is not a sight but a sensation: a deep, profound heat that radiates not from a sun, but from the ground itself. The air is thick enough to drink, super-saturated with water, clinging to your skin in a hot, heavy film. It's a perpetual, global steam room. The light is a dim, bruised twilight, filtered through a permanent, miles-wide ceiling of self-generated vapour, tinged a faint, chemical ochre.

The ground is not soil or rock, but a vast, near-perfectly flat plain of sintered, dark material, like obsidian slag, fused into a seamless floor that stretches to a misty, indefinite horizon. It is flawlessly sterile. Nothing grows here. There is no dust, no grit, no hint of biological decay, because there is nothing biological to decay. The plain is gridded with faint, almost invisible lines, a geometric ghost etched into its surface.

From this plain rise the buildings. But they are not buildings. They are skeletons, frameworks, immense industrial scaffolds of a scale that dwarfs cathedrals. They are composed of a dark, corroded copper-like metal, streaked with lurid green and turquoise verdigris, and vast, smooth plates of what looks like white, unglazed ceramic. These are not towers with floors and windows, but colossal, hollow lattices, pipes the width of roadways, and radiator-like fins that climb for hundreds of feet into the soupy air. They are separated by immense distances; you could walk for ten minutes across the slag flats before reaching the base of the nearest structure. This is the city's great paradox: it is unmistakably urban in its complexity and organization, yet it is defined by an astonishing, near-absolute emptiness. The density is approaching zero.

The world is active, but its activity is not that of life. It is the activity of a single, city-sized machine. The entire plateau is an engine for processing water. Deep below, geothermal fury boils a mineral-heavy aquifer, and this city is the exhaust, the condenser, the distillery.

Water is the only moving thing. It weeps constantly from the ceramic plates, which are cool to the touch despite the ambient heat. This condensation, heavy with dissolved minerals, trickles down the vast structures in slick, dark rivulets, collecting in the grid-lines on the floor. The faint etchings are, in fact, channels. The water flows sluggishly along these geometric pathways, eventually converging on great, circular sumps where it vanishes back into the planet's depths with a quiet, periodic gurgle. Immense, silent plumes of steam vent from the tops of the tallest towers, rising to replenish the ochre cloud-ceiling. The cycle is slow, relentless, and has clearly been in motion for millennia. The weathered state of the metal and the smooth, water-worn edges of the ceramic speak to an ancient equilibrium.

The soundscape is the echo of this emptiness. There is a deep, subsonic hum that you feel in your bones more than hear with your ears—the vibration of colossal, slow-moving pumps or turbines buried miles below. Otherwise, the city is a library of drips. The soft patter of condensation on the slag floor. The slow, resonant *plink... plink...* of a heavy drop falling a hundred feet inside a hollow pipe. Occasionally, miles away, a sound like a vast sheet of metal contracting with a low, resonant *BWWWONG* that echoes for a full minute across the plain. It is the sound of machinery sleeping, not dead.

Life is an afterthought, an anomaly. For days you might see nothing. Then, a flicker of movement high up on a distillation lattice. It is a Scaffold-Skitter. Not an insect, not a reptile, it looks like an articulated shard of porcelain, the size of a human hand. Its legs are needle-thin metallic filaments, and it moves with an unnerving, fluid speed across the vertical surfaces. They are rarely seen, seeming to exist only at the periphery of vision. They do not hunt or eat in any recognizable way; they seem to scrape at the mineral crusts left by evaporating water, or perhaps they draw energy directly from the thermal and electrical gradients of the city itself. They are as much a part of the machinery as its nuts and bolts, a rare and specialized form of maintenance or perhaps a tolerated parasite.

Looking out from the edge of the plateau, you see nothing but the unbroken ochre ceiling of cloud extending forever. There is no world below, only a void of steam. This place is a world unto itself, high and isolated. It is a monument to a purpose you cannot comprehend, built by hands that are long gone, or perhaps were never here at all. It is a factory that produces only filtered water and silence. It is a city without citizens, whose primary law is a slow, hot, and eternal cycle of hydrological physics. It doesn't feel malevolent or benign. It simply is, an engine running in the quiet dark, utterly and profoundly indifferent.
