The first thing to understand is the sound. It is not a sound you hear with your ears alone. It is a physical presence. It is a bass note that has found the resonant frequency of your skeleton, a constant, grinding hum that vibrates up through the soles of your feet and settles deep in your teeth and sinuses. This is the baseline. Layered over it, a million times over, is the high, chaotic skittering of fracture. It is the sound of a world being continuously broken and re-made, the sound of stone tearing like fabric, of immense pressures finding sudden, violent release. It never, ever stops. It is the defining law of this place, a deafening, perpetual roar that makes spoken language an impossibility and hearing a useless sense.

This valley is a basin of turmoil. The ground is a floor of shattered, obsidian-like rock, all sharp angles and fresh, glittering facets. Nothing here is old. Nothing is weathered smooth. The landscape is in a constant state of youthful, violent formation. Great slabs of black stone, kilometres long, groan and shift against each other, their edges grinding into coarse sand and then into fine, dark dust. Fissures split the ground without warning, a sharp crack that is felt more than heard, a brief, high-pitched scream swallowed by the omnipresent din. Then, over minutes or hours, the fissure will grind itself shut again, pulverizing everything caught within.

The air is thick, cold, and heavy with water. It is not raining. Instead, the atmospheric pressure and the constant sonic vibration force moisture to precipitate directly out of the ether. Every surface weeps. The air itself is a condensing fog, reducing visibility to a hundred metres in any direction, a swirling grey murk that muffles what little light exists. Your clothes are instantly soaked with a frigid, pure water that leaches warmth from your body. Water runs in slick sheets over the faces of the black rock, gathers in trembling pools in every depression, and seeps through the thick carpet of life that covers everything.

And it is covered. Despite the cold and the dark and the chaos, vegetation is overwhelmingly lush. It is not a forest of trees, but a sprawling, planetary-scale bog-mat. A thick, resilient layer of what could be mosses, liverworts, and immensely scaled-up lichens smothers the knife-edged geology. This flora is a deep, bruised purple and an electric, phosphorescent blue, colours adapted to capture the faintest traces of energy. This covering, several metres thick in places, acts as a vibration damper. To step off the rock and onto the mat is to feel the world's frantic shuddering lessen from a jackhammer to a deep, full-body massage. The vegetation thrives on the energy, its cellular structure perhaps using the constant mechanical stress for some unknown metabolic process. Puncturing its skin releases a gush of clear, cold water and a faint, organic smell like crushed ferns and ozone.

Across this landscape, scattered at vast but regular intervals, are the source of the chaos. From the purple matting rise pylons, though that word is insufficient. They are spines of a tarnished, coppery material that show no seams or welds. Each is perhaps fifty metres tall, a blade-thin monolith that tapers to a point lost in the churning fog above. They hum with a focused intensity that makes the air around them shimmer like a heat haze, though the air is freezing cold. A faint, internal blue-white light pulses deep within them, the only source of light other than the bioluminescence of the flora. These are the nodes of the engine that is running this world. They are not buildings, but scattered components of a single, valley-sized machine of incomprehensible purpose and power. Its function may be terraforming, or power generation, or a form of computation that uses planetary geology as its substrate. The effect is what matters: the constant, deafening, turbulent creation.

Life is abundant, but it is life adapted to a world of vibration and darkness. There are no birds. There is nothing that flies. The fauna is largely subterranean or semi-aquatic, living within the saturated biome of the moss-mat. Grazing creatures, like enormous, armoured slugs the size of sheep, move in slow waves across the purple plains. Their shells are made of laminated, vibration-dampening plates, and they feed by scraping the sonically-charged flora with radulae of diamond-hard cartilage. Their trails are not worn by footfalls, but by the abrasive passage of their wide, low bodies. Hunting them are things that move like centipedes, long and low to the ground, with dozens of legs that act as seismic sensors. They do not see or hear their prey; they feel the subtle interruption in the world's vibration as a grazer moves, and they hunt by that sense alone. They are all silent, their existence entirely beneath the notice of the overwhelming sound.

To exist here is to recalibrate your senses. The world is dense, layered. Below your feet is the groaning, shattering rock. Above that, the thick, wet, living cushion of the sonophytes. Slinking through it are the silent, abundant fauna. Above that, the frigid, condensing air. And piercing it all, the humming pylons that march in a grid across the entire region, vanishing into the grey distance. The rules are simple: the ground is not stable. The sound is absolute. The cold is relentless. The only way to navigate is by the rhythm of the quakes, by the faint glow of the pylons, by the texture of the living mat beneath you. It is a normal world, in its way. Its physics are consistent, its ecology is in balance. It has its own logic. The anomaly, the truly terrifying thought, would be silence. If the pylons were to one day power down, the sudden quiet would be the most violent event of all.
