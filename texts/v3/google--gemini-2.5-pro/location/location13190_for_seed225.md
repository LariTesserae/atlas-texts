This place is not found by delving, but by thinning. You walk a passage of ordinary, damp limestone, and the rock begins to lose its solidity. First it becomes porous, like pumice, then it turns translucent, the grey stone paling to the colour of milk quartz. The walls are no longer solid barriers but shimmering curtains of mineral light. Stepping through them, you are not in a cave, but inside a geode.

The space is a single, vast, ovate chamber, perhaps the size of a grand ballroom or a small cathedral nave. Its walls are a continuous, unbroken surface of nacreous, opalescent crystal. They are the source of the light, a brilliant, shadowless, and utterly silent luminescence that fills every cubic inch of the volume with the clarity of a mid-morning in June. The light is not white, but the colour of mother-of-pearl, shifting in vast, slow pulses of pastel pink, sea-foam green, and soft lavender over the course of hours. The air is warm, humid, and still, holding the heat of the glowing walls like a breath held in lungs. It carries the clean scent of ozone and wet minerals, a smell like a flash of lightning after a long rain.

Despite the brightness, the chamber feels profoundly empty. The air is so clear, so devoid of motes or haze, that distances are deceptive. The opposite wall seems both impossibly far and intimately close. The scale of the room is definite, yet the lack of contents gives it a quality of near-infinite space. The floor is made of the same polished, glowing material as the walls, but it is dusted with a fine, silver-grey powder that glitters faintly. It is soft and cool underfoot. Scattered across this expanse are shallow, perfectly still pools of water, clear as glass, their surfaces mirroring the pearlescent dome above.

Vegetation is sparse, and not of a kind you would recognize. Growing from the silvery dust are thin, branching spires of what appears to be flawless crystal. They are like skeletal glass trees, reaching a few feet into the air. They are not inert; they are alive. The warm, humid air condenses on their intricate, fractal branches, and you can see droplets of water tracing slow, hesitant paths down their surfaces until they drip with a soft, resonant *plink* into the pools below. This is the dominant sound of the chamber: a quiet, arrhythmic, and gentle percussion of water touching water. These crystal-flora are the source of the chamber's moisture, patiently harvesting it from the air itself. They are widely spaced, each a solitary sculpture in the bright emptiness.

And yet, life is abundant. Look closely at the glowing walls. What at first appears to be the texture of the nacre itself is a living lacework. Millions of tiny, translucent creatures, like glassy barnacles or flattened sea-stars, cling to the dome. They are almost perfectly camouflaged against their luminous substrate. They are the lithophanes, the wall-jellies. They filter nutrients directly from the seeping mineral wall or perhaps subsist on the light itself. They are the source of a secondary, much quieter sound: a faint, almost subliminal chiming, as if a billion microscopic bits of glass are gently shifting against each other. They do not move, at least not on a timescale visible to the human eye, but their collective presence gives the wall a subtle, living shimmer. The vast space of the air is left empty, while life crowds the boundaries.

There is other movement, however. On the floor, moving with a slowness that is hypnotic, are other creatures. Tall, three-legged beings, their bodies shaped like slender, polished earthenware vases. Their skin is a hard, ceramic shell of muted ochre and grey. They have no visible head or sensory organs, but move with a clear intentionality. These are the Tripods. Each one carries a tool: a long, sharp shard broken from one of the crystal plants, held in a delicate, multi-jointed appendage that unfurls from its upper body. They move between the glass-flora and the pools, using their shards to scrape at the silvery dust on the floor, gathering it into small, neat piles. The sound of their work is a soft, dry *shff, shff, shff*, the only other disturbance to the quiet dripping. Their purpose is unclear. Perhaps they are gardeners, tending the dust from which the crystal-trees grow. Perhaps they are feeding. Their actions are too slow, too patient to be called labour; it is more like a ritual, ancient and unchanging.

This is a world in equilibrium. A sealed system running on light, mineral, and water, cycling through its slow, deliberate processes. The great age of the place is evident in the polished smoothness of every surface, the settled grace of the crystal forms. Nothing is new here. Everything is as it has been for millennia. It is a hollow of bright, warm emptiness, where life is not a presence that fills the space, but a delicate film that lines its edges, and the only technology is a shard of glass used to patiently scrape the dust of ages.
