The first thing you notice is the floor. It is not soil or rock in any conventional sense, but a vast, undulating plain of a black, vitreous material, broken only by razor-sharp fault lines that run for miles. The material is cool to the touch, like glass, but not slick. It has a micro-texture that grips the sole of a boot. This is a young world, geologically speaking, or perhaps post-geological. The features are not worn smooth by time and water; they are crisp, as if they were cast from a mold yesterday and are still cooling. Low, serrated ridges rise like the backs of sleeping dragons, their angles too perfect, their surfaces too uniform to be natural.

The air is warm and heavy, thick with the smell of petrichor, ozone, and something else—the faint, clean scent of hot electronics. It’s a greenhouse atmosphere, constantly damp. There is no standing rain, but a perpetual, silent condensation coats every surface. Water beads on the black substrate, gathers in the sharp-angled gullies, and drips constantly from the life that clings to the world above. The sky, or what passes for it, is a low-hanging, uniform ceiling of bruised pewter light. There is no sun, no moon, no discernible source of illumination; the world is simply lit, dimly and eternally.

Despite the sterility of the ground, life is everywhere else. This is a vertical jungle. Immense, architectonic structures rise from the plains, separated by vast distances that make them feel both sparse and overwhelmingly grand. They are not mountains, and they are not buildings, but something in between. Some are slender, impossibly tall spires that scrape the lid-sky, wrapped in thick shrouds of glowing moss. Others are massive, blocky ziggurats, their terraced sides overflowing with vegetation.

This vegetation is a riot of form and subdued color. Thick, cable-like vines, with the texture of ribbed rubber, hang in great loops between the spires or snake across the glassy floor. Their leaves are not green but a deep, luxuriant indigo, some edged with phosphorescent tracery that provides a soft, local light. Broad-leafed ferns, the size of blankets, unfurl from crevices in the structures, their undersides coated in a velvety, copper-colored fuzz. Here and there, clusters of fungi sprout directly from the black substrate, their caps shaped like inverted bells that pulse with a slow, biological rhythm of bioluminescence, cycling from blue to violet and back again. The air is so still that the constant, silent dripping from leaf to leaf to ground is the loudest and most persistent sound, a form of water clock measuring a quiet eternity.

The quiet is profound. There are no bird calls, no insectile chittering. The fauna is vanishingly rare, and not what one might expect. You might go for days seeing nothing, and then, a flicker of movement. A small, six-legged scavenger, its body a mercury-like blob, flows across a patch of the black ground, consuming dust or spilled energy before pouring itself into a crack. Or you might see, high up on a vine, a "syntax-worm"—a creature that appears to be a shimmering line of code, an inch-thick glitch in reality, moving in impossible, non-Euclidean paths as it feeds on the information flowing through the vine-cables. These creatures make no sound. The ecosystem here is not one of predator and prey, but of symbiosis with the substrate itself.

Because the world *is* a substrate. This is a city, a single, contiguous piece of urbanism built on a scale that dwarfs human comprehension. The black ground is a computational plate, the ultimate solid-state hard drive. The warmth in the air is the waste heat of incalculable processes running beneath the surface. The constant dampness is the exhalation of a colossal cooling system. The towering spires and ziggurats are processing nodes, data archives, server farms of unimaginable density and complexity. Their forms are not static; over a period of weeks or months, one might notice a new ridge extruding from the plains, or a spire slowly twisting an extra few degrees into the sky. The sharp-edged terrain is not a result of tectonics, but of algorithmic reconfiguration. The world is actively, dynamically rewriting its own physical form.

The sounds, when they do come, are not of nature, but of the machine. Occasionally, a deep, resonant hum will build from beneath your feet, vibrating through the soles of your boots and up your spine. It is the sound of a major process initiating, a query being run on a planetary scale. Sometimes, a section of the plains will begin to shimmer with a rainbow iridescence, a visual indicator of a massive data transfer. To step on such a patch would be... unwise.

The rules of this world are simple but absolute. The vegetation is not merely decorative; it is integral. The glowing mosses are biological monitors, their color and intensity indicating the operational status of the structures they encrust. The indigo vines are living conduits, data cables with a metabolism, drawing energy from the flow of information they carry. The entire place is one vast, interconnected system, a circuit board dreaming it is a jungle. It is a city, but its inhabitants are processes. It is a work of engineering so far beyond the modern that it has looped back around to become an ecosystem, a place of strange beauty, profound quiet, and a purpose that is utterly, magnificently alien.
