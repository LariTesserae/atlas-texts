The first thing you register is not sight, but sound. It is a single, immense, and unvarying chord. It is the sound of a billion glass legs skittering on a billion crystal floors, the sound of a trillion chitinous wings vibrating in resonant harmony, the sound of a world-sized bell struck once at the beginning of time and left to ring forever. It is a noise so total and monolithic that it ceases to be noise and becomes a form of pressure, a physical presence that fills the air and pushes against your bones. There are no distinct calls, no rhythms, no conversation of creatures. There is only The Sound.

Your eyes adjust to the dim, bruised-purple light. You are in a cavern, a geode cracked open deep below the surface of the world. The scale is intimate, not a sprawling underworld but a single, vast chamber whose walls are always dimly visible. The air is warm and utterly still, thick with the dry, electric scent of ozone and hot rock. There is no wind, no draft, no change. The temperature is as constant as the sound.

The walls are not smoothed by age or water. They are a riot of sharp, fresh fractures, of black basalt cleaved to reveal glittering veins of quartz and pyrite. It is a young space, geologically speaking; the breaks in the rock are crisp and uneroded. From the floor and walls, growing in a way that defies biology, is the vegetation. It is lush but sparse, each specimen given its own space like a sculpture in a gallery. These are not plants of chlorophyll and cellulose. Great, fan-like structures of milky, semi-translucent crystal rise from the ground, their edges sharp as obsidian shards. Through their vitreous surfaces, you can see dark, mineral veins pulsing with a slow, internal light, the source of the cavern's violet glow. Thin, wiry stalks that look like spun copper writhe in complex, frozen patterns, terminating in bell-shaped flowers of pure, resonant salt. Nothing here thirsts. Nothing here drinks. There is no water, not a single drop. The life here is a thing of lithic accretion, of slow mineral growth fed by the geothermal heat and the faint, unknown energies that hum through the rock.

And crawling over this crystalline flora, scuttling across the faceted floor, and vibrating in the thick air, is the fauna. They are abundant. They are the source of The Sound. Legions of insectoid creatures, none larger than a human hand, move with a purpose that seems to have no goal. Some have carapaces of polished agate, their multiple legs like tuning forks that tap and scrape and chime against the ground. Others are winged beings that hover in place, their wings not beating but vibrating at an impossible speed, generating the overwhelming drone. These are the grazers, their serrated mandibles scraping films of mineral dust from the crystal leaves. There are predators, too: sleek, centipede-like hunters with bodies of fulgurite glass, who move in straight, unvarying lines until they intercept a grazer, consume it with a dry crackle, and then continue their rigid, geometric patrol. There is no fear, no chase. There is only the enactment of a process.

Scattered amidst this strange ecology are the only signs of another intelligence. They are not ruins, for that would imply a past. They are as static as everything else. Here, a slender, impossible tower of balanced stones, each one chosen for its specific resonant frequency, adding a barely perceptible undertone to the cavern's drone. There, etched into a flat wall of basalt, are geometric patterns—spirals and branching fractals that mimic the mineral veins in the crystal plants. They were made with simple, primitive tools: a harder stone scratching a softer one. These are not dwellings or fortifications. They feel more like instruments, or prayer stations—attempts by some long-gone visitor or native to understand, to harmonize with, or perhaps to endure the crushing, eternal state of the chamber.

This is the fundamental rule of the place: it is in perfect, frozen equilibrium. Time passes, but nothing changes. The sound does not modulate. The light does not dim or brighten. A crystal fan may grow imperceptibly over a thousand years, but another will be eroded to dust by the grazers at the exact same rate. A creature is born, lives its entire life in rigid, repetitive motion, and is consumed or crumbles, its material reabsorbed into the crystalline substrate. There is no cycle of seasons, no day or night, no sense of becoming. It is a system that achieved a perfect, steady state and locked itself in. The strangeness is not in the waterless life, the crystal plants, or the droning fauna. The true weirdness is the absolute absence of dynamism. It is a pocket of eternity, loud and warm and full of life, but utterly, terrifyingly still.
