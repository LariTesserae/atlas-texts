This place is defined not by sight, but by sound. The sound is first, last, and everything in between. It is a physical presence, a pressure on the skin and a vibration in the skeleton. It is the deafening, unending, planet-spanning roar of wind. Before you see the landscape, you feel its voice in your jaw, a deep and resonant hum that makes your teeth ache. This is not the shriek of a gale, but the bass-heavy, impossibly loud drone of a billion tons of air moving as one across the roof of the world.

You stand on the edge of the Great Table, a plateau so high and vast that the curvature of the planet is a discernible, gentle arc on the horizon. The mountains that once stood here have been worn away by this same wind, their teeth ground down over geological time into colossal, sleeping domes. They are not peaks but "wells," gravity-slumped giants of wind-polished basalt, so smooth they gleam a wet, dark grey. Their scale is immense; a single dome might form the horizon for a day's walk. The landscape is ancient, settled into its final, most stable form. The wind is the only thing left with the energy to work it, and it does so molecule by molecule, a polishing cloth the size of a continent.

The ground is not rock, but a living skin. A deep, lush carpet of vegetation covers everything, a continuous mat of emerald and olive and lime. It is known as the Shuddering Green. It is not grass, but a billion billion tightly-packed, succulent-like organisms, with waxy, overlapping leaves no bigger than a thumbnail. They grow no more than a foot high, an adaptation to the constant, crushing force of the wind. To stand on it is to stand on a firm, damp sponge. The Shuddering Green lives on the air. There are no rivers, no lakes, no rain as it is commonly understood. The world’s water is here, but it is a prisoner of the wind, atomized into a perpetual, high-speed mist that streaks horizontally across the plateau. The green skin of the world is cool and slick to the touch, its leaves expertly designed to comb this kinetic moisture from the air, funneling it down to their roots. The air smells of wet stone and crushed green things.

The place is a contradiction: it is intensely lush, yet profoundly empty. The green carpet is total, unbroken, but nothing stands *above* it. No trees, no shrubs, no spires of rock that have not been humbled and smoothed. The space between the ground and the high, often clear sky is a hollow void, an emptiness made tangible by the sheer force of the wind that occupies it. To look out is to see a gently rolling, endless plain of green, marked only by the darker, titanic curves of the mountain-domes. Things are not dense; they are singular, separated by immense, wind-scoured distances.

And the wind is turbulent. It is a chaotic, living system. Though the primary direction is constant, the air is full of invisible eddies, vortices, and pressure waves. You see them manifested in the Shuddering Green, which does not wave but quivers in vast, spreading patterns. A patch a mile away will suddenly darken as a pressure-front passes over, the billion leaves flipping in unison. The entire landscape is a canvas for the motion of the unseen, a constant, silent, large-scale shimmering.

Life, other than the Green, is profoundly rare. The wind scours the air clean. There are no insects, no birds. To survive here, one must be low, heavy, and patient. Burrowed deep within the damp, peaty soil of the Green live the Groaners. You may never see one. They are armored, slug-like creatures the size of a human torso, with heavy, calcified plates. They spend their lives consuming the soil and the roots of the Green, moving at a glacial pace. Their name comes from the low, infrequent vibrations they emit, a sound utterly lost in the oceanic roar of the wind. They are the sole fauna of any significant size, islands of slow metabolism in a world of hyperactivity.

Scattered across the emptiness are the only marks of intelligence: the Howling Pits. These are simple, circular depressions dug into the Green and the stone beneath, varying from ten to fifty feet in diameter. The edges are lined with hand-smoothed, wind-eroded stones. There are no roofs, no doors. They are not shelters from the wind, for that is impossible. They are tools for dealing with the sound. By some trick of acoustics, standing in the center of a Howling Pit focuses the wind's roar into a single, pure, almost musical note. It is still deafening, but it is coherent, a fundamental tone instead of a chaotic wash of noise. To the people who dug them, this must have been a form of meditation, of sanity. Near some of the pits, one might find a stone knife, its edge worn to a curve, or a bowl-like stone with a shallow depression for grinding the tough leaves of the Green. These are the artifacts of a people for whom the primary struggle was not for food or water, but for a moment of sonic peace.

The light is normal, a simple sun in a simple sky, but the atmosphere gives it a strange quality. The high-speed mist acts like a lens, and on the rare occasions the wind's velocity shifts, light can bend and create fleeting, silent mirages on the horizon: an upside-down mountain dome, a swatch of green reflected in the sky.

This is a world ruled by a single, violent force that has paradoxically created a landscape of immense stability and peace. It is a place of profound solitude, where one could walk for a week and see nothing but the quivering ground and the horizon. But you are never alone. The sound is always with you, a companion that fills your entire being, a constant, turbulent reminder that even in the most ancient, settled, and empty of places, the universe is still screaming.
