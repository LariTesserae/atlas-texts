This is the Atrium City, perched in a caldera at the apex of a world. The mountains below are not jagged spires but colossal, worn-down domes of grey and black rock, the result of an erosion so ancient it has ground the planet's verticality into a softened, final form. The caldera itself is not a volcanic maw but a vast, shallow basin, miles across, a pockmark on the face of a continental-scale plateau. The air is thin here, achingly dry, and holds a perpetual, geothermal warmth that rises from the stone underfoot. There is no water. No rain has fallen in a billion years, no river has carved a path. Any moisture would sublimate instantly into the thin, high atmosphere.

Despite the absolute aridity, the caldera is choked with a forest. It is a jungle of stone. Crystalline growths, in a hundred silent, slow-motion varieties, fill the basin from rim to rim. These are the lithophytes, the mineral flora of this world. Great, pillar-like trunks of hexagonal basalt, fluted and dark, rise towards the dim light, their surfaces filmed with a powdery, verdigris moss of pure malachite dust. From their tops, they do not sprout leaves, but fracture into vast, flat, interlocking plates of translucent selenite. These plates, paper-thin on their edges but feet thick at their base, overlap to form a canopy that catches the weak sun and shatters it into a million shifting mosaics of amber and grey on the forest floor.

The understory is dense, a tangled chaos of form. What should be ferns are brittle, arcing structures of frosted gypsum. Vines are not green and supple but are long, fibrous threads of spun quartz, strong as steel cable and sharp as glass, that drape themselves over every surface. The ground is not soil but a deep, soft carpet of crushed mica and a rust-red mineral dust that puffs up in clouds with every footstep.

The light is a permanent, luminous twilight. The sun is a small, unfocused pearl in the thin sky, and its already weak rays must first penetrate the high, hazy atmosphere, and then filter down through the dense canopy of selenite plates. The effect is that of being inside a cathedral of unimaginable size, lit only by its own stained glass. The air is never truly dark, but never bright. Colors are muted, desaturated, save for where a stray beam catches a crystal facet and explodes in a momentary, sharp-edged rainbow.

The city is not built upon the landscape, but carved within it. The inhabitants, a patient and enduring people, have excavated their civilization directly into the basalt pillars and the bedrock of the caldera floor. Their settlements are not towns of discrete buildings, but a continuous, interconnected warren of chambers, galleries, and plazas, all subterranean or endolithic. Entire communities exist within a single, hollowed-out stone trunk. Highways are tunnels, polished smooth by millennia of shuffling feet. Windows are openings framed by the natural crystal growths, or portals cut to look out into the dim forest, often glazed with a carefully cleaved sheet of mica. The density is startling. There is no private yard, no open space that is not also a thoroughfare. Every cubic foot of easily-worked stone has been hollowed and inhabited, layered one community atop another in a vertical labyrinth. The technology is one of percussion and abrasion. Using chisels of obsidian and hammers of diorite, they slowly, methodically, expand their world. The timescale of their construction is geologic. A new hall might take a thousand years to complete, its progress marked by the steady, patient *tap-tap-tap* of a hundred generations of crafters.

And the sound is immense. It is a constant, overwhelming chorus. The highest layer is a ceaseless, glassy chiming. The thin, dry wind, forever circulating within the basin, makes the selenite canopy plates shift and scrape against one another. It is not the rustle of leaves, but the sound of a million glass knives being sharpened at once—a high, sharp, yet somehow soothing symphony. Beneath this is the sound of life. The fauna here is abundant, and entirely lithovorous. Beetle-like creatures with iridescent carapaces of chalcedony crawl over the crystal growths, their hardened mandibles making a constant grinding, crunching sound as they scrape away microscopic layers of mineral nutrient. Flying things, like moths with wings of stiff, stretched membrane, flit through the dim air, their wings creating a dry, leathery flutter. Their calls are not whistles or songs, but a series of rapid clicks made by striking their forelegs against their silicate bodies. The sound echoes endlessly through the stone forest. And woven through it all is the sound of the city: the distant, percussive echo of carving, the murmur of voices in the enclosed spaces, the scrape of pottery made from fired mineral dust, the clatter of tools. It is the sound of a million creatures, great and small, living their lives in near-contact, a soundscape so dense it becomes a presence, a tangible pressure in the air.

This world is slow. The crystal forest grows, but the change is imperceptible to a single generation. A new trunk might rise a foot in a century. The city's expansion is equally patient. The cycles of the dim sun bring only subtle shifts in the quality of light. There are no seasons, no storms, no sudden events. There is only the long, slow, grinding process of existence. Life here is a conversation with stone, a deep and layered present built upon a foundation of near-infinite time. You can feel the age in the warmth of the rock, see it in the smoothed-down edges of every carved doorway, and hear it in the endless, chiming friction of the canopy far above.
