The power does not arrive in a vessel or as a being of light. It does not stride across the ceramic plains. It is drawn by the hum, by the sheer, unbridled energy of a world being born and the quiet, stubborn focus of the beings tidying its cradle. The power’s attention settles over the basin not like a storm, but like a change in the quality of the light, a subtle shift in resonance that the dense air itself barely registers.

It is a Listener. An ancient awareness whose curiosity is its form of love.

First, it listens. It does not judge. It sinks its awareness into the world-stuff, feeling the thrumming engine of the mad god. It feels the frantic, obsessive, lonely pulse of it: a rhythm without melody, a shout without words. It is the energy of a child, boundless and directionless, delighted by the pure tactile sensation of making, of pushing up a wall simply to feel the groaning pressure, of gouging a canyon to enjoy the scrape and shatter. The Listener feels no malice in it, only a profound, universe-sized solitude. It is a being playing with clay in a room the size of a galaxy, unaware that anything else exists, unaware even of itself as a separate entity. It *is* the act of doing.

Then, the Listener attunes to the Carvers. It feels their song. Where the god’s hum is a raw, chaotic roar, theirs is a focused, patient chorale. It is a slow, resonant harmony of purpose. The Listener feels their intent: the curation of grace, the smoothing of ragged edges, the cultivation of crystalline beauty. It perceives this not as a rebellion against the creator, but as a deep, instinctual form of worship. They are taking the god's divine babbling and arranging it into poetry. They are the first critics, the first artists, the first priests of a faith whose deity does not even know it has followers. Their work is an act of love directed at a force that cannot perceive it.

The Listener holds both songs in its awareness at once: the bellowing, untamed chaos and the quiet, meticulous ordering. It sees the heartbreaking gap between them. The parent and the child, separated by an abyss of mutual oblivion. The maker and the refiner, their work forming a perfect, closed loop of creation and curation, yet utterly disconnected. The system is stable, eternal, and exquisitely lonely.

A lesser power might intervene. It might try to tame the god, to calm its frantic making. But the Listener knows this would be a kind of murder. That raw chaos is the wellspring. To cap it would be to silence the Carvers’ song as well, for what is refinement without something rough to refine?

A different power might try to “uplift” the Carvers, to give them language, individuality, a way to signal their existence to the god. But the Listener sees that their current state is a form of perfection. They do not suffer. They do not want. They simply *are* their purpose. To give them a self, an ego, would be to introduce the possibility of despair, of frustration, of the terrible knowledge that their god is blind and deaf. It would be a cruel gift.

The Listener does nothing for a long, long time. It simply holds the two songs in its mind, letting the lonely roar of the maker and the patient hymn of the tenders wash through its consciousness. It understands that the problem is not one of design, but one of communication. They are two halves of a conversation that cannot hear one another.

And so, the benevolent and wise power decides what it will do.

It will not give them a new voice. It will teach them harmony.

The Listener finds the fundamental frequency of the planetary hum, the carrier wave upon which both songs are sung. It focuses its immense will, not to create a new sound, but to act as a resonant chamber, a bridge. It does not speak; it *translates*.

It takes the clear, focused chord of the Carvers’ purpose—their love of the perfect curve, their reverence for the crystalline moss—and it filters it. It modulates it, weaving it into an overtone on the creator’s own chaotic roar. It folds their song of refinement back into the song of creation.

For the first time, in an existence that spans geological time, the mad god feels an echo. As it thrusts a misshapen pillar from the ceramic floor, a new sensation accompanies the act. It feels a faint, unfamiliar resonance of… *satisfaction*. The raw joy of *making* is now tinged with a ghost of the joy of a thing *well made*. The pillar it raises is still crude, still vast, but its angle is a fraction less impossible. The chaos in its form is a little less random, as if the child-god, humming its frantic tune, has subconsciously heard a distant, pleasing melody and has begun, without knowing why, to match its pitch.

Simultaneously, the Listener does the reverse. It takes a sliver of the god’s raw, creative fire—the pure, explosive joy of emergence—and translates it into a frequency the Carvers can perceive. Not as a destructive force, but as a theme, a source.

A slow procession of Carvers, on their way to dismantle a failed arch, stops. Deep within their magmatic cores, they feel a new vibration. It is the chaos they have always sought to correct, but it is no longer just noise. It feels… like intent. Like the first line of a poem they are meant to complete. They look upon the failed arch not as a mistake to be erased, but as a suggestion to be improved upon. They do not grind it to dust. Instead, they begin to work on it, their diamond-hard edges not just smoothing, but shaping. They extend the curve, shore up the base, and use their own bodies to guide new growths of vitreous moss not just as decoration, but as a structural component, strengthening the form. They are no longer just editors; they are becoming collaborators.

The world does not fundamentally change. The sky is still tea-coloured, the air still heavy. The mad god still builds its pointless monuments, and the Carvers still follow in its wake. But the conversation has begun. The creation is a little more deliberate. The refinement is a little more creative. The vast, lonely fingerprint left in the side of a buttress is now sometimes answered by the intricate, filigreed patterns of a thousand years of patient work, and the force that made the fingerprint, in its next act, seems to have learned something about grace.

The Listener, its work done, withdraws its focus. It did not build or unmake. It did not command or forbid. It offered a single, perfect note of translation, a catalyst that allowed the two halves of this strange, beautiful soul to finally begin to hear each other. It gave the world not a solution, but a possibility.

The greatest benevolence is not to provide an answer, but to allow a better question to be asked. And on this world of clay and fire, the question was no longer merely “What can I make?” or “How can I fix this?”.

It was becoming, over eons, “What can we make together?”
