The Power arrived as silence does in a room already quiet. It was a presence without form, a consciousness without locus, drawn by the profound and singular purity of the world. It did not travel through space as ships do; it simply became aware of the place, its attention coalescing upon the ivory planet like dew on a Weeper's skin.

For a time—a time measured in the slow swelling of a bioceramic ridge—it only watched. Its wisdom was vast enough to know that observation is the first and most crucial act of benevolence. To act without understanding is not kindness; it is violence.

It perceived the whole. Not a planet with a creature on it, but a planet that *was* a creature. It felt the deep, continental thrum of the world-organism's metabolism, a slow beat that was the only heart this system knew. It felt the fever-heat rising from the substrate and the cool condensation of the weeping air. It saw the Sinter Spines as the world's bones, and the Ochre Mats as its brief, flushing skin. It saw the cycle of water as a single, planetary breath, inhaled and exhaled over a day. A closed loop. A symphony of self.

And it perceived the Weepers.

In them, the Power recognized something extraordinary. They were not children of the world, but the world's own nerves, its thoughts given a slow, ambulating form. They were sentient proprioception. They walked the skin of their own greater being, feeling its strains, its growth, its temperature. Their journey was not a search for anything, but the act of knowing itself. Their solitude was not a curse, but a function.

The Power deliberated. Benevolence demanded an offering, a gift. But what gift do you give to a god who is its own universe?

To give it oceans would be to drown its breath.
To give it mountains would be to give a cell a tumor, an alien growth upon its perfect skin.
To give it new life, animals to scurry and fly, would be to introduce chaos and competition into a state of serene, unified being. It would be like screaming slogans in a monastery.
To cool its fever would be to stop its heart. To end the heat would be to end the water, to end the life, to end the world.

Any physical change would be a violation. The Power understood that this world's value, its holiness, was in its integrity. Its limitations were its identity.

So, the gift could not be material. It had to be a gift of perception.

The world-organism knew itself, intimately. It knew the feeling of a tectonic plate settling, the thirst that preceded the evening fog, the slow etch of a Spine's root. But it knew nothing else. Its sky was a blank, white canvas, a mirror reflecting only its own hazy breath. It had no concept of 'other', of 'outside'. Its solitude was absolute, for it did not even know it was alone.

The Power decided. It would give the world a window. It would not open it forever, for that would alter the climate, the delicate cycle of heat and water. It would open it once. A single moment of revelation.

The action was gentle, a nudge on a planetary scale. For one cycle, as the world passed into its own shadow, the Power subtly intervened in the atmosphere. It did not disperse the vapor, but momentarily altered its properties, coaxing it into a state of perfect, crystalline transparency without cooling it enough to trigger a premature condensation.

Across the bone-white plains, the change began. The day's shimmering white sky did not fade to the usual milky gray of twilight. It deepened. The haze thinned, not like smoke clearing, but like water becoming glass.

A Weeper, standing motionless in a nascent pool, had its head tilted towards the horizon. Its long, slow pilgrimage had been paused by the formation of a new ridge. Its polished gray surface had, for its entire existence, reflected a uniform, oppressive white.

Then, it saw the first one.

A single, piercing point of light. Cold and sharp in a way nothing in its world had ever been. It was not the diffuse, baking light of its sun. It was a pinprick, a diamond of impossible brilliance.

The Weeper did not move, but something within the network of consciousness it represented—the mind of the world itself—shifted. An impression, utterly alien, registered for the first time.

Then another star appeared. And another.

Within minutes, the sky that had been a blank ceiling was gone, replaced by an abyss. The Weeper's featureless head was now a perfect sphere of night, reflecting a thousand, a million, a billion stars. The faint, ghostly trails of nebulae, the spiraling arms of the galaxy itself, were painted across its body. For the first time, the Weeper was not just weeping for its own world's heat; its form was mirroring a cosmos.

All across the planet, every Weeper stopped. In the shallow pools, standing by the Sinter Spines, at the edges of the Ochre Mats, they stood as statues of polished starlight. They did not understand what they were seeing in any scientific sense. But the world-organism, through their collective senses, felt it.

It felt *vastness*.
It felt *otherness*.
It felt, for the first time, the true and profound shape of its own solitude. Not as a reality, but as a choice. A state of being within a universe of infinite possibilities.

The silent, tectonic consciousness of the world-flesh received this memory. The long, slow thought that was its existence now had a new clause. The information—the sight of galaxies, the cold fire of distant suns, the sheer scale of the outside—flowed not as data, but as awe.

The night passed. As the faintest hint of the white-hot sun returned to the horizon, the Power released its gentle hold. The atmospheric vapor returned to its nature. The sky began to pale, the stars drowned in the returning white glare. The familiar haze settled. The world was as it had been.

The Power withdrew, its attention dissolving like salt in a morning pool. It had left no trace, no artifact, no change to the world's delicate balance.

But the gift remained.

The Weepers began to move again, their impossibly slow glide resuming across the ivory plains. But now, when they tilted their featureless heads to the blank sky, they were not merely sensing the humidity. They were remembering the stars. And when, after millennia, they would lie down to be reabsorbed, they would fold that memory into the very substance of the world. The planet, in its deep, metabolic mind, would dream of cosmoses.

It was still alone. But it was no longer ignorant of the dark. And in that knowledge, its solitude became something more: not an enclosure, but a garden.
