The Power came to know Iskar not as a destination, but as a resonance. In the grand, silent symphony of cosmos, where worlds spun out songs of heat and life and violence, this place was a single, impossibly deep and sustained bass note. It was a chord of cold, pressure, and endurance. The Power, which perceived existence as a weave of cause and consequence, of matter and meaning, turned its attention to this anomaly. It listened.

It did not see with eyes, but perceived with the totality of being. It felt the immense, slow grind of the splintered continent, the groan of rock settling into a configuration that would not be stable for a million years. It felt the razor-edges of flint, the infinitesimal shift of scree under a Ghor’s hoof, the thinness of the air that was like a constant, gentle suffocation.

It experienced the city, Iskar. It knew the terror of the drop, the bone-deep chill, the strain in the sinew-bridges. It comprehended the slow, painstaking labor, the decade spent to carve a single room a family would call home. It tasted the bitter lichen tea and the rich, fatty Ghor milk. It felt the damp seeping through stone, the preciousness of a single drop of water condensing on a cool wall.

And it knew the Khatt-i-Ghor.

This was the heart of the resonance. The Power sank its awareness into them, not as an intruder, but as a quiet guest in the halls of their collective soul. It wore their bone masks and felt the warmed air enter their deep chests. It felt the slow, powerful beat of their hearts. It stood on a ledge with an elder and looked into The Great Below, and understood that it was not an act of boredom, but of communion. It held an antler pick in a calloused hand and felt the language of the stone travel up the arm, a grammar of fracture and cohesion.

The Power perceived their highest value: *dûr*. Not just survival, but endurance. The quality of being that persists, that holds, that is worthy of trust. It saw that their Ledge-Families were not just social structures, but physical truths. Their interdependence was as real as gravity. Their spirituality was not a set of beliefs, but a set of practices born from necessity and honed into reverence. They did not pray to the Stone-That-Is for mercy; they listened to it for guidance.

And in this deep, quiet knowing, the benevolent and wise Power asked itself a question. What does one give to a people who have everything they need, and nothing they want?

To give them warmth would be a cruelty. It would melt the glaciers, sending catastrophic floods into the canyon. It would rot the Ghor hides and the sinew-ropes. It would make their barrel chests and slow hearts obsolete, a biological burden. Their bodies would betray them.

To give them soil and sun would be an apocalypse. To turn their vertical world horizontal would be to erase their sense of self. To take away the drop would be to take away their neighborly reliance. They would forget the vertical sense; they would learn the fear of falling, a fear they had never needed.

To give them an easier source of water, a roaring river at the canyon’s base, would be to steal their patience. It would erase the quiet ritual of the cistern, the communal joy of a weeping glacier. It would make them careless.

To give them magic to shape the stone with ease would be the gravest insult. It would devalue generations of toil. It would silence the rhythmic *chink-chink* of the hammers, the sacred music of their existence. It would make the concept of *dûr* meaningless. What endures if it was never tested?

The Power saw that the Iskari were not poor, or struggling, or in need of rescue. They were complete. They were the perfect expression of their environment, a testament to the power of slowness and attention. Their hardship was the whetstone that gave their spirit its edge. To give them ease would be to hollow them out, to make them brittle.

Wisdom dictated that any gift must not alter them, but affirm them. It must not solve a problem, but deepen a virtue. It must be a gift that only they, in their quiet patience, could truly receive. It had to be a gift that felt like a discovery.

The Power did not act upon the city or the people. It acted upon the Stone-That-Is.

Deep within the planet’s throat, in the core of the mountain range from which Iskar was carved, the Power found a specific vein of basalt. It was a strata of rock denser and older than the rest, a seam of the world’s original skeleton. And into this stone, the Power sang a song. It was not a song of sound, but of cohesion. It was a resonance of impossibly perfect structure, a quiet hum of atomic bonds reinforcing one another, a vibration that tightened the basalt’s crystalline lattice. It did not change the stone’s nature; it simply made it more perfectly itself. It made it the ultimate expression of *dûr*.

The Power did nothing more. It withdrew its attention, leaving behind only that silent, resonant hum in the deep rock, a hum that would be imperceptible to any but the most patient and attentive hand.

Generations passed. The city grew downwards, as always. Then, a Ledge-Family, quarrying a new chamber deeper than most, struck the vein.

The mason, an old woman with knuckles like river stones, felt it first. Her antler pick struck the wall and the shock that travelled up her arm was different. It was not a dull thud or a sharp crack, but a clean, ringing *tone*. The stone did not flake or shatter. A perfect, palm-sized sliver sheared away, its edge like cut glass.

She paused. She placed her bare, calloused hand on the dark face of the rock. It was cold, as all stone was, but there was something else. A stillness. A density of being that felt ancient and absolute. She spent the rest of the day just listening to it, her touch a form of prayer.

When her family returned, she did not speak. She simply handed them the sliver. They felt its weight, its impossible solidity. They saw its flawless grain. In their slow, rasping tongue, they gave it a name: *Dûr-Khatt*. The Enduring Stone.

Word did not spread quickly, for that was not the Iskari way. But over centuries, as other deep-carvers found the vein, the *Dûr-Khatt* became a legend, and then a reality. It was impossibly hard to work. A normal basalt maul would shatter against it. It demanded new tools, forged from its own slivers. It demanded more patience than any stone before it. Haste would gain you nothing but a broken tool. But for the mason who listened, who worked with the stone’s grain, who matched its patient rhythm, it yielded surfaces smooth as frozen water and edges that would never dull.

It was too precious for mere dwellings. The *Dûr-Khatt* was used for foundation keys, for the anchor points of the greatest bridges, for the lintels above the entrances to their sacred Ghor-rib halls. It was used to carve the blades that would dress the sacred Ghor, blades that never needed sharpening and performed their work with profound respect.

The Iskari never knew a god had visited them. They believed they had simply, through their own enduring patience, reached a deeper truth within the Stone-That-Is. They had earned the right to work its heart.

And the Power, in its distant, knowing silence, was content. The gift had been received. The city of Iskar, built in the throat of the world, was now anchored to its very heart, its endurance made manifest. The people of the stone and the Ghor, a quiet hymn of resilience, now had a new and deeper note to sing.
