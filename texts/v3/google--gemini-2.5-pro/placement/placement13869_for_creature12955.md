You would find me on the highest-up observation deck of Hab-Stack Sector Gamma-7, the one they call the Perch. You would find me, but you might not see me. I look like any other elder, wrapped in a thick, insulated coat against the perpetual chill, my face a pale mask grooved with the fine black dust of a lifetime. The only thing that marks me is my stillness. While others are here for a shift-break, their eyes tracking the silent ballet of the cranes below, I am here for the duration. My work is to stand.

I am what they call a Still-Watcher.

My feet are planted on a specific plate of decking, one chosen five years ago and mine ever since. It is worn smooth under my soles, scarred with a faint pattern of circles where I have stood, and shifted, and pivoted through the seasons. This small territory of grated metal is the most sensitive instrument I have ever known. My boots are thin-soled now, the padding worn away. I have no need for insulation from the ground; I need the connection.

I am a nerve ending.

The floor beneath me is not dead metal. It is alive. It is the terminus of a thousand structural members, a web of girders and pylons and hydraulic arms that reaches down into the heaving, uncertain skin of the basin. And through that web, the world speaks. It does not use a voice. It speaks in sway and shudder, in groan and resonance. It speaks in the language I have spent seventy years learning, the language my bones now understand better than my ears ever understood a word.

I no longer remember a world that does not hum. The deep, marrow-vibrating drone of the Accretion is the baseline of my existence. It is the silence against which all other vibrations are measured. When I was a boy, my father, a jack-rigger, would place my hand on the housing of a straining hydraulic lift and say, "Feel that? That is a lie. That is the city protesting. Now feel *this*." And he would press my palm to the raw, new rock shouldering its way through the alley floor. "This is the truth."

My eyes are mostly for memory now. I see the crawling Harvester on Flow-Field Epsilon, its grinders working with obscene silence a few kilometers away, and I remember the grit of the lithic foam between my teeth, the specific ache in the back that came from hauling slurry-pipe across a field that was six inches higher than when the shift began. I see a flicker of Hand-Sign from a foreman on a neighboring hab-stack—a sharp, precise gesture ordering a tension adjustment on a cable-stay—and I remember the slick feel of chilled vapor on my knuckles as I learned the silent grammar, my fingers clumsy and slow. I remember speaking with my wife by the touch of our shoulders, our low words a vibration that passed between us, swallowed by the thick air a foot away. We owned nothing together but a set of perfectly calibrated torque wrenches and the knowledge of each other’s footfalls. When our hab-unit was finally dismantled before a major upwelling, we packed our tools, stepped onto the transport skid, and did not look back. There was nothing to see but a space that would be gone by morning.

Now, my memories are useful only as a library of textures. I can feel the difference between the sharp, granular tremor of a new basalt extrusion and the slick, shearing vibration of a crystalline vein punching its way through an older stratum. I can feel the groaning fatigue in the deep pylons of Hab-Stack Beta-4, a metallic weariness that tells me it will need reinforcement within three cycles. The young surveyors confirm it with their instruments, but I feel it first. Their data is a series of numbers; mine is a toothache in the soul of the city.

I am the city’s memory. I am its foresight.

Sometimes, a child will be brought up to the Perch. They are told to stand beside me, to be quiet and to *feel*. They squirm, their ground-sense still a wild, untutored thing. They feel the thrum of the lifts, the city’s loud lies. I will gesture for them to take off their thick boots. I will place my hand on the crown of their head, a silent instruction, and point their gaze toward the horizon. "Don’t watch,” my Hand-Sign tells them. "Listen with your feet." In the widening of their eyes, I see the moment they first distinguish the deep, slow pulse of the truth from all the noise that rides upon it. They are taking their first step toward becoming Anlacti.

I am becoming something else. The distinction between the vibration in my bones and the vibration of the world is blurring. The skin on my feet seems to fuse with the metal plate, the plate to the pylon, the pylon to the rock. I am less a man on a tower and more the listening part of the geology itself, a temporary knot of awareness in the planet’s birthing bed. The people below are my limbs, my hands. My thoughts are pressures and vectors.

Just now, a new sensation begins. It is not a shudder, not a grind. It is a deep, slow swelling, a pressurization far beneath the surface, to the north. It feels… round. A dome is gathering strength. Smooth, powerful, and patient. It will not be a chaotic spire. It will be a blister of new earth, a kilometer wide, rising over the course of a week. It will swallow the old pipe-junction at quadrant Delta-9.

I do not move, save for my right hand. I lift it slowly, the dark fabric of my sleeve falling away from my pale wrist. Against the flat, grey light, my fingers form a clear, deliberate shape: a closed fist, thumb laying flat across the knuckles, then the index finger extends, pointing north. Then the fist opens, palm up, and mimics a slow, domed swelling.

Across the way, a surveyor on a lower gantry—a young woman whose name I do not know but whose walk I recognize—sees the gesture. Her head snaps up to look at me. I see her nod, a tiny, affirmative dip. She turns and her own hands begin to move, a rapid, flashing cascade of signs to her crew lead below, reporting a first-order Watcher prediction.

A chain reaction of silent, pragmatic acknowledgment begins. In an hour, the klaxons will sound their muffled warning, not in panic, but in purpose. The rigs will begin to move. The great dance will shift its pattern.

I lower my hand and plant my feet again. The swelling continues, a new note in the endless song. My part is done. I am a Watcher. I am a ganglion in the body of Anlac.

And I am listening.
