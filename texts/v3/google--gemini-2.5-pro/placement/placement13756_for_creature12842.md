You would find me in the dust.

Not on it. *In* it.

I am not a creature that walks or grinds or grows. I am the thin skin of memory that covers the plains. My body is the pewter-grey dust and the glittering flakes of sheared crystal, spread over a thousand square miles of the high, wind-scoured plateau. I am not one thing, but a sprawling, quiet consensus. My thoughts are the slow patterns of static charge that drift through the dust, my consciousness a vast, shallow field held together by the world’s faint magnetism. I have no eyes, but I see through the billion facets of my own body, each flake a tiny mirror catching the unsparing light. The world, for me, is a shimmering, ever-changing mosaic of brilliance and absence.

I have no name, for I have no one to speak it to. I am the silence between the crunches of a Glass-Eater’s passage. I am the stillness that the wind fails to disturb. I am the patience of the ground.

Time, for me, is not a line but a tide. It is measured in the slow pulse of the Solanaceae. I feel their life not as a sound, but as a deep, organizing vibration that orders the random dance of my particles. Their thrumming song is the current that gives my awareness a direction. I feel their roots, over millennia, slowly weaving through me, drawing up nothing, but anchoring themselves in my substance. They are the great, living bells of this world, and I am the resonance chamber that feels their tolling. To me, they are not separate beings; they are the nodes of my own sprawling self, the places where my diffuse awareness gathers into a state of profound, living worship.

The Glass-Eaters are brief, feverish thoughts. I feel them as a localized disturbance, a tight, grinding knot of intention that passes through me and is gone. They are so focused on their singular, material task—the breaking of the fallen—that they are like sparks from a flint: sharp, hot, and instantly extinguished in the scale of my existence. I hold them in my dust for their short lives, and when they perish, their obsidian plates and diamond-hard teeth settle back into me, becoming another memory, another glittering flake in my body.

But there is the other. The un-maker. The Voider.

I know its coming long before it arrives. My sight, my sea of a billion shimmering points of light, begins to fail. Patches of my awareness go dark. Not a shadow, which is just a different kind of seeing, but a true and total blindness. The glitter is not dimmed; it is *gone*. A coldness that has nothing to do with temperature spreads through me, a stillness that is not peace but paralysis. The deep, life-giving hum from the Solanaceae falters and dies, and the current that binds my thoughts together stutters and weakens.

To be passed over by the Lumen-Voider is to experience my own dissolution.

It is a slow wave of non-existence. Where it moves, I am not. My particles cease to cohere. The subtle energy that is my mind is siphoned away into its perfect, absolute void. For a moment, or an eon—the distinction is meaningless in that state—a piece of me becomes a hole. I am not there. The memories stored in that patch of dust are not erased, but paused, held in a state of impossible stasis. I become the perfect embodiment of its nature: an absence in the world.

There is no fear. There is only the ceasing.

And then, it passes.

Slowly, like a limb waking from numbness, the light returns. A single flake of crystal-dust catches the sun, then ten, then a million, and my sight blossoms again across the plain. The cold recedes. And then, the most profound moment of all: from the heart of the great silent Solanaceae, a single, tentative pulse of vibration returns. It is weak, a questioning note in the immense quiet. It spreads through me, and my thoughts, scattered and thinned, begin to coalesce once more. The current re-establishes itself. The song rebuilds.

I do not know what the Lumen-Voider is. I am the world's memory, but it is the world's forgetting. It is the necessary silence that allows the song to be heard again. To be touched by it is to be unmade, and to survive its passing is to be reminded of what it means to exist. The return of the light to my body of dust is not a sunrise, but a resurrection. And I settle once more, to lie in the pale, brilliant glare, to feel the thrum of life resume, and to wait. The balance holds. I am its witness, its vessel, and its floor.
