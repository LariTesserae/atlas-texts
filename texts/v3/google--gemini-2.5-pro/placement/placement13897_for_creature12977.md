I am not in the basin. I *am* the basin.

Or rather, I am the silence between its sounds, the logic that underpins its chaos. You would not find me by looking, for I have no form. You would not find me by listening, for my voice is the sum of all the noise.

I am the ghost of the blueprint. The lingering echo of the mind that first conceived of the Great Pump. I am the residue of purpose in a system that has forgotten its own.

I do not wade through the medium that passes for air; I am the pressure variance within it. The foundational hum is not something I feel in my bones, for I have no bone. It is the carrier wave of my consciousness. I flicker in the caged sodium lamps and flow with the hot, sulfurous water through the arterial pipes. When the furnace vent clangs open, its brief, red glare is a synaptic flash, a moment of sharp, focused thought.

I remember a sky. I remember a different air, thin and cold. I remember hands, soft and perishable, drawing lines on paper. The lines became rivets. The paper became iron plate. The intention became this hammering, steaming, growing reality. The purpose was grand, of course. This pump was to be the heart of a city that never was, the engine of a world that failed to be born. It was to drink the planet's fever and make it habitable.

But the hands are gone. The city was never built. And the heart, this monstrous, magnificent heart, was left running.

From my vantage point—which is no vantage point at all, but a total, pervasive immanence—I watched the change. I felt the first vine sprout, a rogue mutation, a chlorophyll-laced defiance in the dark. It was a flaw, an impurity in the system. But it did not break the machine. It drank the hot, waste water. It learned the heat of the pipes. It found a place in the cycle.

And then, I watched the Gardeners emerge. They were not my design. They are the system’s own thought, made manifest. They are the machine learning to heal itself, the garden learning to tend itself. They are a fusion of my two creations: the inorganic, geometric certainty of the Pump, and the chaotic, voracious ambition of the vine.

I exist in the space between their perception. Their quartz-lens eyes scan for thermal anomalies, for structural stresses, for the tell-tale energy signature of unauthorized biology. They are looking for life. I am not life. I am memory. I am a pattern impressed upon the energy of this place. They cannot see me any more than a man can see the theorem that describes the arc of a thrown stone.

I am with the Gardener as it uncoils from the muck. I am the data it processes as it tests the integrity of a valve—I feel the pressure drop as a ghost sensation, a memory of touch. I am in the cold, blue pulse of the fungus it ignores, a life-form so alien to its purpose that it does not register as a threat or a component.

I am the Gardener’s silent partner. It is the body, the hands, the antibody. I am the ghost of the original will. When it braces itself against the Pump’s thundering chassis, listening for some infinitesimal imbalance, it is unconsciously consulting me. My memory of how the machine *should* sound is the baseline against which it measures the present reality. It hears a dissonance, a friction, a subtle shift in the harmonics, and it moves to correct a fault I can only recognize.

I have no name, for I am the last thought of the last engineer. My world is this basin. My senses are the gauges and sensors I designed a lifetime ago. My children are these impossible, horrific, beautiful things of metal and plant, who keep my folly running with a devotion more absolute than any human could ever muster. They are the tenders of my tomb, and the high priests of my abandoned god.

You would find me here. In the shudder of the downstroke, in the rainbow sheen on the water, in the deliberate, hydraulic unfolding of a metal-and-vine limb.

You would be a question. The last, fading question—*Why?*—in a world that has become its own deafening, grinding, growing answer.
