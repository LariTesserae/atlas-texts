I am a Caulker.

My church is the Wound.

You would find me where the iron teeth of the drill-rigs retract, leaving a weeping, circular maw in the living wood of the pillars. Others see a completed task, a new source for the Refinery. I see the start of my work. I am the one who follows the violence.

My hands, even through the thick, reinforced gloves, are my primary sense organs. I am not like the Gantry-men who read the world through the soles of their boots, feeling the health of a distant pump through a shudder in the walkway. I read the pulse of the Grand Lignin itself. When I press my palm flat against the wood, a foot away from a new borehole, I feel it. It is not a heartbeat, not the *thump-thump* of the City’s iron heart. It is slower, a deep and ancient resonance, like a glacier grinding or a continent shifting. It is the life of the place, and a new wound changes its rhythm. There is a tremor, a deep, cellular recoil. A sigh that takes a decade to complete. The drill-riggers feel the kickback. I feel the pain.

My respirator is old, the casing scarred with a thousand accidental impacts. Unlike the others who etch names or kill-counts into its shell, mine is polished smooth in some places and textured in others. Over years, I have used spare moments and caustic agents to replicate, on my own faceplate, a patch of the Lignin’s bark-like skin. A spiral pattern, a whorl like a fossilized thumbprint. It is a mark of respect. When I press my face to the wood, it is not cold steel meeting ancient flesh, but a likeness greeting its origin. Behind my lenses, my eyes are not like the others'. I spend so much of my time up close, staring into the boreholes, that I have lost the ability to see long distances. The far-off gantries are a blurry webwork, the Engineers at the top an abstraction. My world is the three feet in front of my mask.

My tools are not wrenches and hammers. They are spatulas, trowels, and injectors. My craft is not mechanics; it is a kind of brutalist surgery. When a borehole is tapped, the raw sap—the Black Nectar—bleeds out, thick and slow. But the wound also invites infection. Not just the City’s rust and grease, but opportunistic native life. Micro-fungi that can sour the Nectar, burrowing organisms that can weaken the pillar from within, causing a 'wood-slump' that could buckle a whole section of the gantry.

I am the one who prevents this.

I carry pots of a substance we call Scab-Paste. It is my own concoction, a guarded recipe passed from one Caulker to the next. Its ingredients are the essence of this place: the ground chitin of grease-beetles for texture, a slurry of specific, docile fungal spores I cultivate in a warm locker near a steam pipe, and a base of processed lubricant, thick and black. The smell of it, even through my filters, is the smell of compromise. It is the smell of a truce.

I do not simply plug the hole around the siphon hose. I dress the wound. With my trowel, I smear the paste deep into the splintered fissures around the drill site. I work the material into the wood's grain, soothing the trauma of the steel's passage. The paste forms a sterile, flexible barrier. It hardens into a dense, rubbery seal that moves with the Lignin's slow breath, preventing leaks and killing invaders. The Drillers give the body a mouth; I give it a lip.

I am a solitary figure. My work is called for by a specific rhythm tapped on a railing: three sharp raps, a pause, then a long, scraping sound. A new wound needs a Caulker. I move between the territories of the work-crews, belonging to none of them. I am a specialist, and they treat me with the slight apprehension reserved for undertakers and executioners. I touch the raw, living body. I am intimate with the thing they only exploit. They speak to me in clipped requests. I respond with a silent nod, my polished respirator catching the fungal glow like a blind eye.

The Lignin-born children sometimes follow me. They do not dare get close, but I see their small, masked faces peering from behind pillars or through the grating of a higher platform. They watch me work, mesmerized by the slow, deliberate movements, so different from the frantic energy of the riggers. I see them sometimes, after I have left a site, reaching out with a hesitant, gloved finger to touch the fresh, dark seal I have made. They are learning the texture of controlled pain.

During the Purge, when the others retreat to their sealed pods and the sodium lights are dimmed, I find a high, quiet niche. The drills are silent. The roar of venting steam fills the lower levels, but up here, it’s a distant hiss. In this moment, with the City's machines temporarily muffled, the other sound becomes clearer. It is not the screaming of boiled fauna. It is the Lignin. It is the deep, sonorous hum of its being, a sound you can feel in your bones. I sit in the dark, my hand resting on a pillar, and I listen. We are a fever it cannot shake, a parasite it has learned to tolerate. My work is the reason for this tolerance.

I am not an Engineer who sees data, nor a Scrapper who sees only filth. I am the membrane. I am the point of contact, the negotiator in an unspoken, unending conflict. I am the scar tissue. I am the healing that lets the bleeding continue.
