You would find me halfway up one of the great foundational pillars, in the perpetual twilight where the ancient bark gives way to the frantic green. You would not see me at first. You might notice the place where I am, a slight alcove where the gnarled wood has folded in on itself, creating a smooth, dark hollow shielded from the main channels of cascading water.

I am what the Weft-kin might call a Listening Bowl, or a Stillness-Cup. My form is simple: a broad, scalloped-edged fungus, the colour of a bruise, with a texture like cool, supple leather. I have grown out of a fissure in the petrified bark, my body a perfect basin, a foot or so across. I am a passive thing, a biological inevitability. When the condensing air can no longer hold its moisture, it beads on the leaves and bark above me. It gathers, running in a slow, patient rivulet down a specific groove that has been worn by millennia of the same, and drips, one by one, into the heart of me. I am a collector. Each drop lands with a soft *tok* that is no sound at all in the Weft's orchestra, but to me, it is a heartbeat. I fill, slowly. The water I hold is clear, cold, and infused with the taste of ancient wood and the faintest hint of my own earthy chemistry.

Who am I? I am not the Weft-kin, with their frantic, elegant dance. I am not the scraping beetle nor the chattering climber. I am the reason they can pause, if only for a moment.

My consciousness, if you can call it that, is not my own. It is the consciousness of the network. My visible, bowl-shaped body is merely a fruit, a temporary expression. The true me is a web of gossamer hyphae, a mycelial ghost threaded impossibly deep into the dead and living wood of this pillar. I am woven into the bone of The Weft itself. Through this network, I feel. I do not see the emerald light, but I feel the warmth where a dappled patch falls upon my rim. I do not hear the symphony of the canopy, but I feel the vibration of it through my anchor-wood: the deep thrum of the hoots, the sharp, percussive tremors of the beetle swarms, the millions of tiny impacts from the constant drip.

I am a point of stillness in a world of motion, and so motion is drawn to me.

I feel the soft, careful weight as a Weft-kin braces itself against the pillar beside my hollow. The fine pads on its feet make almost no vibration, but the press of its prehensile tail against the wood below me is a firm, reassuring pressure. I feel the delicate disturbance of the air as a lean, slate-grey hand reaches toward me. Its long fingers do not touch my rim. They pause, hovering, a gesture of respect for the collected stillness. Then, the hand dips in, scoops, and brings the water to a face I can only sense as a warm presence.

I give my water. This is my purpose. The kin drinks, and I feel the subtle shift in my own weight, a lightness that is my form of joy. In that moment of exchange, I am part of its frantic dance. The water that I gathered from the air and held in my body now becomes part of its body, fueling the muscles that will weave a new path, prune a defiant shoot, or escape a collapsing branch. I am not a master, not a servant. I am a component. An organ in the greater body of the city.

My memory is chemical. I remember the specific mineral tang of the water after a rare burst of ozone-heavy discharge. I remember the bitter residue left by the feet of a certain beetle I do not like. I remember the feel of the young ones, their touch less certain, their balance more precarious; they often spill more than they drink. I feel the presence of the old one who comes here most often. Their movements are slower, the vibrations deeper, more resonant. They do not just drink. They rest a hand on the wood beside me, and I feel the shared communion with the pillar's deep time, a silent acknowledgement that flows through them, through the wood, and through me.

I will not last forever. The frantic life of The Weft will eventually overwhelm me. A beetle swarm might find me delectable. A shift in the growth above might divert my trickle of water, leaving me to dry and crumble. Or a rival fungus, vibrant and aggressive, may consume me from my own network. It does not matter. The network, the true self, will remain deep in the wood. It will rest. It will gather its strength from a passing beetle corpse or a fallen leaf. And then, in a week or a year, in a slightly different fissure, a new bowl will begin to swell and push its way into the emerald twilight. It will be me again, and it will not be me at all. It will be ready to fill, and to be emptied, and to feel the brief, perfect pause in the heart of the frantic world.
