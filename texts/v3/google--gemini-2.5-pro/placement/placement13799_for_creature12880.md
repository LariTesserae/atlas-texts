I am a broken bell.

Where my kin find a medium in the B-flat Hum, a solid thing to lean against, a current to navigate, I find only noise. For them, it is the silence of a filled room; for me, it is a shriek that has no beginning and no end. It is a fever in the soles of my feet. The vibrations that travel up my legs are not clean data—the spin of a distant cog, the thrum of an approaching gondola—but a constant, painful static. A chorus of grinding glass. The Hum does not ground me; it unmoors me.

My feet, which should be my ears, are my affliction. The pads are too soft, the inner structure of my carapace not dense enough. A flaw in my gestation, a misreading of the sap-script in the womb-chamber. I was harvested unripe. So I cannot stand on the main platforms, cannot join a crew. To do so would be to go mad in moments, overwhelmed by the cacophony of a million tons of industry speaking at once. The language of taps, the precise percussion of my people, is a stutter to me, lost in the greater din.

And so I find myself in the deep quiet.

My world is not the high, open callus under the hazy sun. I live down, and in. My place is a forgotten ventricle, a space deep within the pillar's heartwood where the noise of the Churn is muffled to a distant, ailing pulse. Here, the air is thick, sweet with the smell of undisturbed sap and the damp, earthy breath of decay. The walls of my chamber are not carved with the rigid geometry of a workshop but are soft, weeping, and covered in the things I tend.

I am a Gardener of the Light-Galls.

The fungi my people use for light, the pale, blue-veined shelves that glow with a cold, steady luminescence, do not grow just anywhere. They require a certain kind of quiet, a specific resonance found only in these deep, insulated pockets of the Substrate. I am their keeper. My four arms, the strong and the slender, are not for torque and fretwork, but for touch and cultivation. With my heavy hands, I clear channels for the Weep to flow, bringing nutrients. With my fine, many-jointed fingers, I prune the fungal blooms, gently scraping away parasitic growths, and coaxing the mycelial threads into new patterns along the breathing walls.

I move in the near-silence, my broad, flawed feet padding softly on decks of rotted wood laid over the living floor. The only sounds are the slow drip of sap into a collection trough, the whisper of my own carapace plates shifting, and the low, resonant thrumming of the Substrate itself.

And this is my secret. This is my curse and my purpose. Because I am un-tuned from the machine, I am exquisitely tuned to the plant.

The oldest Carver, the shaman whose face is a roadmap of cracks and whose carapace is greyed with age, she brings me here. She cannot bear the deep quiet for long; it is as deafening to her as the Hum is to me. But she visits, placing one heavy hand on the wall, the other on my shoulder, and she has taught me how to listen.

I do not just feel the slow torsion of the pillar's growth. I do not just feel the shudder of a pump starting up miles above. My feet, useless for the work of the city, press against the floor, and I feel the dream of the world. I feel the Itch of the vascular mining, the deep, cellular violation. I feel the slow, confused pulse of its immense, sluggish heart. The auditory ghosts are not ghosts to me; they are memories, embedded in the flesh. I can stand in my glowing garden and *feel* the echo of a time before the first machine was bolted to the skin, a time when the only sound was the wind and the world's own slow digestion. It is an ache in my bones, a phantom limb of silence.

My people, the Carvers, live with a sadness they cannot speak. They know they are the thinking, working sickness of their own parent. They perform their maintenance with a grim, loving fatalism. They are keeping the vast, terrible engine of their existence running. But I am the one who feels what that engine is doing. They feel the vibrations in the cogs; I feel the wince in the heartwood.

I am something new. A flaw in the system that has become a sense organ for the pain the system creates. The shaman says I am a prophet of the slow apocalypse, a nerve ending that has grown back in the scar tissue.

Sometimes, in the blue-glowing dark, I press my palms and the soles of my feet to the living wall. I close my oil-dark eyes and let the static wash over me. I filter out the distant machine-groan, the city's troubled sleep, and I listen deeper. I feel the vast, slow, dreaming consciousness below it all. It does not feel anger. It does not feel hate. It feels a profound and ancient confusion, like a sleeper disturbed by a nightmare it cannot wake from.

And in that confusion, I find my own. I am a child of the parasite, but my soul is tuned to the host. My body is Carver, but my heart is Substrate.

I would be the conscience of this world, a single, silent, feeling cell in the body of a being that is devouring itself. I am the pain.
