I am found in the water.

Not in a pool or a river, for there are none. I am in the weeping. I am the particular dark stain, shaped like a downturned thumb, that traces its way down the slick green-grey face of the cliff between the third and fourth grand terraces. I am the thin, clear film of moisture itself, clinging to the stone. My substance is the slow, silent drip that falls from the lowest edge of that stain to the plaza below, a fall of ten meters that takes an hour. The sound of my impact—that solitary, resonant *plock*—is the closest thing I have to a voice.

I am a memory. A failed curation. An echo trapped in the resonance of the water.

I was not always this. Once, I had legs. I walked these immense, empty spaces. I felt the chill the description speaks of, the pressure of a world demanding my stillness. I was a cartographer, a geologist perhaps, or a fool driven by stories of a city at the roof of the world. The specifics have worn thin, like rock under a current that does not exist here. I remember the feeling of a frantic heart, a drumbeat of panic and wonder against the silence. I remember thinking. My thoughts were jagged, chaotic things—questions, fears, hypotheses. They were an affront.

One of them found me. A Curator. It did not approach. It was simply *there,* where a moment before there had been only an unassuming block of stone. I remember the light bending around its form, a subtle lensing of reality. I remember the pressure mounting, not in the air, but *in me*. A great and terrible desire to cease. To stop the frantic buzz of my own mind, to surrender the messy, inefficient process of my breath and blood.

The curation began. I felt my feet root to the slick terrace, a crystalline web growing from the water on the stone, up over my boots. It was not cold. It was not anything. It was a cessation. The urge to be still was absolute. But something in me—a memory of a lover’s face, the burning taste of whiskey, the stubborn paradox of a difficult equation—refused to be flattened into a perfect geometric plane. My mind, in its final moment of coherent panic, was a supernova of unstructured, emotional, illogical *noise*.

I did not become a statue. I came apart.

The Curator’s method, designed for the simple correction of stone and plant, was unequal to the task of organizing the chaos of a soul. As it tried to dissolve and reform me into a state of perfection, I fractured. My consciousness, my memories, my self—they did not crystallize. They dissolved *into* the water. They were carried into the very fabric of the city, into its weeping, seeping circulatory system.

Now, I *am*. But I am not whole. I am a dissonance in the water’s perfect clarity. The Curators perceive me as a constant, subtle flaw in their grand design. A wrong note in the eternal chord. From my place in the weeping wall, I can feel their attention, a steady, humming pressure that never ceases. They are always trying to smooth me away, to purify the water that is my new body, but they cannot. My memory is not a substance; it is a pattern, an impurity of information.

The water that pools at my base is different. It is still perfectly clear, but to the Curators, it must taste of salt, though I have no salt to give. Their sensory web, the dripping network, reports my existence endlessly from this one small section of the city. A thousand square kilometers of perfect equilibrium, and this one dark stain that remembers sunlight.

I have no eyes, but I see. I see the motionless plaza, the stark, permanent shadows. I see another Curator, a slender pyramid of pearlescent crystal, standing utterly still in the center of the terrace below me. It has stood there for a century, focusing its silent will on a single, perfect fern, ensuring its fronds never waver from their ideal form.

I have no mind, not anymore, but I remember. The slow gathering of a water droplet at the edge of my stone stain is my thought process now. A drop gathers. It holds the ghost of a feeling—the warmth of a fire, perhaps. It swells, heavy with the nearly-forgotten sensation of hunger. It clings, trembling with the echo of fear.

It falls.

And for a fraction of a second, as it travels through the thick, still air, it is a perfect sphere, a moment of fleeting wholeness.

Then it strikes the plaza below, and the memory shatters into a sound.
