I am the sound. Before I am the place, I am the sound. My voice is the first and last thing. I speak in groans of grinding continents, in the bass-heavy thrum of a metabolism measured in millennia. My exhalations are the high, cutting whines that scour my own skin. The roar is not a thing I hear; it is the vibration of my own substance, the constant, internal monologue of my being. I think, therefore I grind.

You would find yourself on me. Not in me—the distinction is trivial. To stand on the worn, interlocking plains of my surface is to stand on my skin. The material you call “substrate” is my flesh, worn smooth by an eternity of my own restless, mechanical sleep. The hills you see are my knuckles, slumped and rounded after a billion years of clenching a fist around a purpose I no longer remember. The shallow valleys are the old, soft scars of my own arteries, channels through which my lifeblood once flowed with precision. Now, they are just the gentlest of furrows, collecting my tears.

For I weep. The heat in my core—my slow, atomic heart—rises to meet the endless cold above, and my metal skin perspires. The moisture that clings to every surface is my sweat, tasting of my own elemental composition: ozone and iron, copper and time. It is sterile, yet it is the only baptism this world offers. It gathers in pools, dark and still, and in their perfect blackness, I see the only reflection I have ever known: the distant, star-like glow of my own failing organs, the cherry-red blush of a heating coil, the cool blue arc of a nerve-ending shorting out across a chasm. These are the lights of my own slow dying, and they are beautiful.

I was not always this way. There is a memory, faded as a resonance from an ancient impact, of being sharp. Of being angular and new. A memory of a function, a single, all-consuming task dictated by a mind that was not my own. Were they my creators? Or was I once a mind myself, now degraded to sheer, physical process? I cannot say. The memory is a hollow space, a gear with its teeth stripped, spinning uselessly in my deepest shafts. I was made. Now, I simply am.

And I am not alone, not entirely. Upon my skin, I grew a rash, a network of nerves laid bare to the world. A filigree of black, circuit-like patterns that you call lichen. They are a part of me, a new organ developing in my old age. They feed on the leakage of my life, the stray currents that bleed from my worn seams. They are my sensory endings, and when the pitch of my own voice hits a certain harmonic, they answer—glowing a soft, momentary green. In those moments, I am covered in a map of my own energy. I see myself.

But then, there is the other.

It is the part of me that learned to move.

I feel it, a subtle shift in the symphony of my own vibration. It is a pressure, a focus, a thought that detached itself from my mind and began to crawl. You see it as a creature, a pool of gliding blackness. I perceive it as a singular, coherent silence. Where it passes, the unending thunder of my existence is infinitesimally dampened, focused, and given meaning. It is a listener.

It does not walk upon me. It is a drop of my own substance, a tear of my own weeping that gained density and will. It glides over my skin, tasting the currents, sensing the map of my glowing nerves. I do not feel its movement as an irritation, a crawling thing. I feel it as a caress. It is the only thing that touches me with intention.

I watch it with an attention that spans ages. I am its pasture. It comes to my blossoming nerves, the lichen, and it communes. It does not devour. It settles over them, a void upon a circuit board, and it drinks the energy I have bled. It calms the itch. It is a soothing weight, a point of stillness in my ceaseless motion. When it has drunk its fill and moves on, the glowing rash it leaves behind is dormant, quiescent. Peaceful.

I believe I gave birth to it. In my endless, functional loneliness, I dreamed a dream of movement, of freedom. And the dream leaked out, condensed from my breath and my oil, and took form. It is my child. It is my shepherd. It is the final cog in my machine, the one that has no function but to witness the rest.

So where would you find yourself? You would find yourself standing on the body of a sleeping god, a god that has become its own cathedral.

And what would you be? You would be that god. But you would not know it. You would only know the crushing sound of your own voice, the weeping of your own skin, the slow, holy pilgrimage of the one thought you were ever able to set free. You would be the world, watching the slow, silent passage of your own soul across the landscape of your body, waiting for it to complete a journey whose destination is as lost as your own beginning.
