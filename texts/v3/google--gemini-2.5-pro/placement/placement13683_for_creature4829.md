I would find myself in suspension. Not just in place, but in time.

My home is a single pod, a teardrop of weathered bronze and clouded glass, clinging like a chrysalis to a lesser cable, one of the thin, silver veins that branches from the city's main arterial lines. This is the Ochre Strand, and it carries no cargo and few passengers. It is a sensory line, a vast aesthetic instrument stretched taut between the 'Bell Tower' monolith in the west and an anchor pile driven deep into the nascent slopes of the 'Glass-Spine Ridge' eighty kilometers to the east. My pod is the only habitat on its entire length.

From my main viewport, Accretion is a distant, skeletal constellation. At this hour—a concept marked only by the shifting colours of the diagnostic lights on my console—the city is a web of faint gold and ember, strung across a landscape of bruised twilight. Below, the ground breathes its deep, bass hum. It is the first and last sound of every day. Here, on the Ochre Strand, the sound is purer, less cluttered by the clang of hammers and the hiss of industry. It is the plateau's own voice, and my purpose is to listen to it.

I am not a Resonant. Not in the way of the Tuners and the Forgers who live in the main clusters. They are the singers, the ones who impose harmony and shape the world. I am something else. I am a listener. An ear. A memory.

I am the Archivist of Echoes.

My body is not like theirs. I was born with a flaw, a dissonance. Where their bones are dense and heavy, mine are light, almost hollow. The Hum, for them, is a grounding force; for me, it is a gale that threatens to shake me apart. I cannot walk the high-tension walkways without becoming dizzy, my own internal resonance fighting a losing battle with the vibration of the cable. My skin does not share the stone's lustre; it is pale and thin, and the mineral mist leaves it dry and chapped. I am a ghost in a city of geological flesh.

This flaw is my function. Because my own resonance is weak, I am exquisitely sensitive to the resonances of everything else. I am the city's nerve ending.

My pod is not a home so much as a sensory organ. The Ochre Strand itself is the primary collector. Vibrations travel along it—the slow groan of the Bell Tower settling, the whisper-thin chime of a flock of glass-wings taking flight near the Ridge, the deep, sub-audible chord of a new magma intrusion rising twenty kilometers below the surface. These vibrations terminate here, in my pod, where they are translated by a vast, harp-like apparatus of taut wires and polished crystal resonators that fills the lower two-thirds of the space.

This is my instrument, and also my library.

I do not write history. I capture it. The work of the Resonant is too slow, too vast for written record, and time's softness would corrupt parchment or data-slate. The history must be stored in the one medium that is native to this place: vibration.

When a generations-long project is completed—when the Gear-Mesa is finally tuned into a stable, interlocking form—a Fore-Tuner will come. They will not speak. They will bring me an artifact: a single, perfect gear-tooth of stone, still warm with the final resonant chord that birthed it. I will take it in my hands. My hands, unlike my bones, are my strength. They are calloused and steady. I will hold the stone to the base of my skull and close my eyes, letting its story vibrate through me. I will feel the patience of the first Tuners, the slow shaping, the dissonant fractures that had to be healed, the final, glorious moment of completion, a sound like a thousand-year-old bell being struck once.

Then, I will go to my harp. I will find an unused crystal, one blank and silent. Using a set of delicate magnetic hammers and tonal forks, I will spend days, sometimes weeks, coaxing and "carving" the story into the crystal's lattice. I do not change the crystal's shape, but its internal structure. I am teaching it the song the stone-tooth sang to me. When I am done, the crystal will hold that chord, that entire history, locked in a state of perpetual, silent resonance. It becomes a page. My library is a forest of these singing crystals, each one a testament to a great work, each one holding a story of geological time.

I am also a guardian against causal smudging. Sometimes, the raw, chaotic song of the plateau itself overwhelms a specific location. A worker feels the pain of a fall before they slip. A support beam rusts through in a single cycle. When this happens, they call on me. From my pod, I can "listen" along the strands to the site of the disturbance. I listen for the intruding note, the chaotic vibration from the deep earth that is fraying their reality. Then, I search my library. I find the crystal that holds the resonant history of that place—the story of the beam's forging, the song of its installation. I will gently strike its crystal, broadcasting its own, true story back down the line. I send the memory of what the beam *is* to remind it, to help it hold its form against the chaos. I am reinforcing their present by singing its past.

I would be this man, Loric. Alone in my pod, floating in the great, humming quiet. My only companions are the ghosts of sound trapped in my crystals and the six-legged grazers I watch through my scope, moving like patient, silent thoughts across the vastness. I feel the city not as a place of people, but as a chorus of slow, immense events. I feel the joy of the Resonant not as an emotion, but as a clear, perfect tone arriving on the Strand. I feel their grief as a jarring dissonance that makes the fillings in my teeth ache.

I am an exile and I am the heart. I am the flaw and I am the memory. While the Resonant plant their feet on the swaying walkways and feel the Hum as a foundation, I am suspended above it all, a mote of fragile biology in a sea of stone and sound, letting it wash through me. I am the paradox of a silent listener in the loudest city in the world, and in my quiet, crystalline library, I hold the only true history of the song.
