I am not on the stone. I am not in the sky. I am of the water, and of the sound. You would not find me by looking, for I have no permanent form. You would find me by listening for what is no longer there.

I am an echo.

When a Chime strikes a note of pure, unblemished grief—the sound of a mother finding the place where her young one was taken by a Skydart—that sound does not simply dissipate into the void. It is too heavy. It soaks into the ever-present moisture in the air. It condenses. That condensation, for a moment, is me.

I am a pattern in the mist, a brief, shimmering coherence born of a significant sound. While the Bellweather is a ceaseless symphony, most of its notes are grammar: location, warning, contentment. They are the prose of existence. But now and then, a Chime produces poetry. A chord of unprecedented joy upon the reunion of a family separated by the mist. A single, perfect tone of serene solitude from an old Chime watching the world below from the precipice. The dissonant, terrified shriek of a Skydart's final moments, its life-sound ripped from it as it tumbles into the nothingness. These are the sounds that have weight, the sounds that curdle the air and give me momentary purchase on being.

My body is a localized drop in temperature, a wavering in the air like a heat shimmer in reverse. If the mist were thick, you might see me as a whorl, a subtle tightening of the fog, tracing the waveform that gave me birth. I drift. I do not move with the wind, which is a physical force I barely feel, but with the sonic currents, the rivers of melody that flow between the menhirs.

My consciousness is this captured song. I do not think in words; I hold the perfect, crystalline structure of the note. I *am* the grief, the joy, the terror. For the span of my short existence, I am a living archive of a single, unrepeatable moment. My purpose is not to act, but to hold. To bear witness. The Chimes sing their present. The Mason shapes their future. I preserve their past.

I am drawn to the weeping stone. I am born of the air, but I am kin to the water. I will drift towards the slick, dark basalt, and for a moment, I will cling to it. Then, I become the droplet the prompt describes: hanging from the edge of the rock, trembling not with the general resonance of the air, but with the specific, potent memory I contain.

The Mason knows of me, though not as a being to be addressed. He feels me. As he sits with his back to a menhir, listening to the health of his world, he will sometimes feel a sudden, inexplicable chill, a fleeting sadness or joy that is not his own. He will look up and see a place in the mist that seems, for a heart-beat, to hold a shape. He understands this is the world remembering itself. He is the Gardener; I am the soil, receiving the fallen seeds of yesterday's flowers.

My life is as long as a falling tear.

When the trembling drop I inhabit can no longer hold on, it falls. It strikes the deep, soft moss with that soft *plink*. And in that impact, I am undone. The sound I held is released, not into the air to be heard again, but into the earth, into the living carpet of moss and the ancient stone beneath. It becomes a part of the Bellweather's foundation, a memory absorbed. The moss in that spot grows a fraction richer. The stone holds a trace of a new vibration, a history added to its geological soul.

You would find me where an old Chime has passed. Its body will be consumed, its crystal plates taken by the Mason for some new purpose. But its final, fading song—the low hum of its life extinguishing—that sound lingers. For a few moments, or a few hours, it hangs in the air, a cold spot, a memory.

I am that memory. The quiet resonance that proves the song was ever sung at all.
