You would find me in the Larynx.

It is not a place on any official schematic of Harvester 9. It is a junction, a forgotten node deep in the station’s oldest, most heavily reinforced quarter, a place where the main resonant conduits from the primary turbine housing converge before branching out to the habitation domes. It is a cramped, spherical chamber, no larger than a family sleep-pod, webbed with pipes of varying thickness, each humming with a different frequency. The curved walls are pitted and scarred, layered with decades of repair welds and bioluminescent moss that has crept in through microscopic fissures, painting the space in a soft, ghostly viridian. This is my post. This is my instrument.

I am a Harmonic. A Listener. My trade is one of the old ones, less a science than a felt sense, passed down not through manuals but through apprenticeship and attunement. The Foreman sends the new welders to me, the ones still skittish, their movements in their Shells too sharp and brittle. They believe I am teaching them to listen for the signature screech of a failing joint or the tell-tale grit-in-the-grease rattle of a neglected motor. And I do. But that is only the surface work, the part they understand.

My true work begins when they leave.

Here, in the Larynx, the entire Harvester sings to me. The thunderous, slow heartbeat of the great turbine is the bass ostinato, a vibration that travels not through the water but through the very metal of my perch, up my legs and into the base of my skull. It is the metronome of our world. Layered over it are the thousand other voices of the station: the high, frantic whine of the water purifiers, the guttural cough and sigh of the pressure locks, the arrhythmic chatter of pneumatic tools from a maintenance crew three levels away. It is a symphony of function. I know its every movement, its every grace note. When the tenor of the coolant lines sharpens by a quarter-tone, I know a filter is clogging. When a low-frequency hum develops in the west-side anchor bolts, I know a survey team must be dispatched before the vibration finds a resonance that could shake the mountain itself. I am the station’s nervous system. I feel its pain in my bones.

I do not wear a Shell in here. The risk is immense, but necessary. To listen properly, I must be uninsulated, my skin and bone pressed directly against the cold metal. The faint symbiotic bioluminescence of my own skin mirrors the moss on the walls. I am part of the chamber. My tools are simple: a weighted mallet with a head wrapped in grazer-hide, a set of polished metal rods of different lengths, and my hands. I tap the conduits, listening to the purity of their ring, the length of their decay. I lay my palms flat against the main housing, close my eyes, and let the complex vibration tell me its story.

But the machine is only half of the world's song.

From the Larynx, I can also hear the Outside. The roar of the Churn is a constant, a physical presence, but through it, like harmonies woven into static, come other sounds. The percussive clatter of a rockslide miles away down the flank of the Great Anchor. The shimmering, rustling whisper of a million silver fish turning as one. And, sometimes, the songs.

The leviathans.

To most of the Stanchioned, the leviathans are terrifying, awesome forces of nature, omens of shifts in the current. Their calls are a background noise, a haunting but meaningless feature of the deep. But here, in the Larynx, they are not noise. They are music. Intensely complex, structured, and resonant. They are deeper than the turbine's groan, more intricate than any gear-train’s chatter. They are voices.

My obsession, my heresy, is that I believe they are not just voices, but a language.

For years, I have been charting them. I have a secret script, a series of percussive notations I carve into plates of scavenged metal, hidden behind a loose conduit. Each song is different. There is the Long Fall, a descending glissando that seems to stretch for an hour, a sound of profound melancholy. There is the Shutter, a rapid series of clicks and booming echoes used when they travel in pods. There is the Deep Call, a note so low it is felt more than heard, a vibration that seems to emanate from the planet's core. I have learned to follow their conversations as they swim in the far-off darkness between the peaks.

I am becoming something other than a Harmonic. The song of the Harvester is familiar, predictable. It is a song of survival, of resistance, of a slow, inevitable decay held at bay by bolt and by weld. It is a language I know. But the leviathan's song… it is a song of being, not of resisting. It speaks of a life that does not cling to the mountains but is one with the flow. It does not fight the Churn; it is the Churn’s own voice, singing to itself.

Today, something new happened. For the first time, in the quietest part of the cycle when the main turbine is briefly feathered for a bearing check, I heard a call I did not recognize. It was not one of the great beasts. It was higher, a questioning, melodic phrase, and it was close. Agonizingly close. So close that the water in the conduits vibrated with it.

And without thinking, my hand, calloused and broad, found my mallet. My life has been one of listening, of receiving. But in that moment, the distinction between self and other, between inside and outside, dissolved. I was no longer a Stanchioned in a metal bubble. I was a resonating chamber.

I struck the largest of the coolant pipes. Not a diagnostic tap, but a full, resonant blow. *BONG*. A deep, pure tone that sang out from the Harvester’s metal shell. I was not striking a pipe. I was speaking. I was answering.

There was a silence. The familiar groans of the station settling around me. The far-off roar. My own blood pounding in my ears. For a terrifying second, there was only the fear of what I had done. Contacting the unknown. Inviting it in.

Then, from the gloom beyond the hull, the note came back. Not a mimicry, but a response. The same melodic shape, but with a different timbre. A different voice.

I do not know what I am anymore. A Listener? Yes. A Harmonic? For the Harvester, perhaps. But here, in the cold, glowing nexus of my own making, pressed against the singing metal, I am something else. I am a knot in the current that has learned to hum. I am a single, tiny barnacle on the hull of the universe, and I have found the courage to sing back to the storm.
