They are the Stone-Singers. It is not a name they would use for themselves, for their language has no word for what they do that is separate from what they *are*. Their every action is a note struck against the profound, foundational silence; the work of their hands is the only song the world will ever know.

Their physical form is a reflection of their world, an echo of its static verticality. They are unnaturally tall and slender, with limbs that seem stretched, as if by a lifetime of reaching. Their skin is pale, the colour of a wet river stone or the underside of a mushroom, and it holds the permanent, clammy dew of the air, glistening in the unwavering light. Their joints are prominent, like the nodules on the bone-white trees, and their movements are slow, deliberate, and utterly economical. There is no haste in them, for time is a currency they have in endless supply. Their faces are long and gaunt, with high cheekbones and deep-set eyes. The eyes are the most notable feature: they are the colour of the dark, petrified water in the riverbed, wide and unblinking, accustomed to staring for hours, for days, at a single unmoving stone, discerning the shape that sleeps within.

Their hands are their most expressive feature. They are large, with long, spidery fingers, yet the palms and fingertips are covered in a landscape of calluses as hard and smooth as polished agate. These hands are instruments of impossible patience, capable of guiding a bronze chisel with micrometre precision or hauling on a rope as thick as a man's waist. To watch their hands at work is to watch a form of devotion.

The Stone-Singers are the sole dynamic principle in this arrested world. They are born, they grow, and they die. This simple, biological fact makes them utterly alien to their surroundings. They are the only things that change, the only things that wither. This mortality, set against the backdrop of an eternal, unchanging landscape, is the engine of their entire civilization.

They build because they must. Their building is not for shelter, not for glory, not for defense. It is an act of memory. In a world where the sun casts the same shadow every day, where a footprint left yesterday will remain perfect for a thousand years, there is no natural record of an individual's passage. The seasons do not turn; the mountains do not erode. Without their work, a life lived would be a breath taken and held, leaving no trace upon release.

So they carve their story. The city is their collective autobiography. Each colossal tower, each impossibly thin bridge, is a testament to generations. A grandfather may begin the shaping of a single, house-sized block at the base of a pylon; his grandchild may see it hoisted into place a hundred feet in the air. They do not write history books; they build it. A name is not carved in letters on a tomb, but in the ten thousand precise taps of a chisel that shaped a particular merlon on a parapet a thousand feet high. Their identity is subsumed into the work. When asked who they are, a Stone-Singer might simply point with a calloused finger to a specific stretch of smoothly finished wall and say, "I am that."

Their culture is one of profound quiet. Sound is a sacred and powerful force. Speech is rare, reserved for instruction, for warnings, and for what might be called prayer—short, resonant phrases uttered before a great work is begun. The true language is the percussion of their tools. An apprentice learns to listen to the song of the stone, the way the *clink* of a master's chisel changes as it nears a flaw or finds the perfect grain. A crew of a hundred workers, balanced on a timber scaffold against a sheer cliff face, moves not to shouted orders but to a shared, internalised rhythm, their mallets rising and falling in a slow, hypnotic syncopation that is the world's only heartbeat. A dropped tool is a moment of shocking, profane dissonance. The sound of its clatter down the rock face is a blasphemy that causes all work within earshot to cease, the silence rushing back in to reclaim the space, heavy with shame.

When a Stone-Singer dies, they become the only thing in the landscape to truly decay. Their body, so long an agent of change, undergoes its final, most rapid transformation. They are not buried in the unyielding stone. They are carried in a silent procession to one of the great, house-sized cushions of moss. They are laid upon its deep, emerald surface, and the living moss, velvety and saturated, slowly, over the course of weeks, envelopes them. Their transient, dynamic biology is given back to the landscape's most vibrant living element. Their physical form dissolves, feeding the green, while their true essence—the work of their hands, the facet they shaped, the bridge they spanned—remains, perfect and permanent, a single, clear note held forever in the silent city. They do not fear this end; it is simply the moment their song becomes stone.
