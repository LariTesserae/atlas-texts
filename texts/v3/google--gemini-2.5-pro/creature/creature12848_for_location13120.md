They who live here are the **Arborians**, though they have no such name for themselves. In their own clicking, resonant tongue, they are simply *The People*, as what else could there be? To them, the vast, groaning wood is not a place to live *in*; it is the body of which they are a part, like mites on a sleeping giant.

Their form is a perfect answer to the questions the Arbor poses. They are slender and tall, with bones that are light but incredibly dense, like the toughest heartwood. Their bodies are long-limbed and wired with muscle, built for tension and leverage, not bulk. They possess six limbs: two legs, and four arms. The lower pair of arms, set just above the hips, are shorter, more powerful, and end in broad, four-fingered hands with tough, calloused pads, used for gripping, bracing, and the slow, careful work of weaving their living nests. Their upper arms are longer, more graceful, ending in delicate, five-fingered hands suited for fine manipulation—for flaking a sap-knife, for carving a chime, for reading the texture of the bark. Their legs are muscular and end not in feet but in elegant, two-toed hooks, like a perching bird’s, with a third, opposable talon at the heel. They can lock these talons into the bark, allowing them to hang suspended for hours, perfectly stable, even in a gale. When they move, it is not a walk or a run, but a fluid, six-limbed brachiation that is bewildering to watch, a silent, spider-like dance across the boughs.

Their skin is the color of bruised plums or the pale green of new shoots, a shifting camouflage that matches the strange light of their world. It is covered in a fine, downy fur, like the coat on a new bud, that traps a thin layer of air against their skin, insulating them from the gnawing cold. Their faces are narrow and elongated. They have no discernible nose, only a series of gill-like slits along their high cheekbones that open and close, tasting the air for spores, humidity, and the subtle chemical signals of growth and decay. Their mouths are small and thin-lipped, primarily for eating, though they can produce a range of soft, resonant hums and sharp, percussive clicks. But it is their eyes and ears that define them. Their eyes are enormous, perfectly round, and utterly black, like pools of frozen sap. They reflect no light, only absorb it, granting them a profound ability to see in the gloom, to distinguish between the soft luminescence of a leaf-continent and the predatory glimmer of a hunter's eye.

Their ears, however, are their primary organs of sense. They are not simple external structures but complex, fan-like membranes of cartilage that can fold and unfurl from behind their jawline. Delicately veined and semi-translucent, they are in constant, subtle motion, twitching, tilting, and cupping, sampling the world’s symphony. An Arborian does not simply hear the Arbor’s groan; they *read* it. They can feel a coming collapse as a dissonant sharpening of a specific frequency in the deep thrum of a bough miles away. They can differentiate the sound of wind over empty space from wind flowing over a dense cluster of new growth. They use a form of passive echolocation, judging the distance and nature of objects by how they distort the omnipresent sound of the world. To them, the world is not a picture, but a chord. Silence is the only true blindness.

They live in small, nomadic clans, forever migrating along the great boughs. Their lives are governed by the Listeners—elders whose ears have grown wide and exquisitely sensitive with age. These Listeners spend their days in meditative stillness, talons locked to a major bough, their fan-like ears unfurled, their entire being focused on the deep song. They are living seismographs. It is the Listener who declares that a branch is "souring," its song becoming brittle, and that the clan must move on. It is the Listener who hears the "sweet" hum of a new, stable branch-system ripe for habitation.

Their culture is one of profound symbiosis and impermanence. They do not build, they *encourage*. Their spherical nests, woven from living shoots, are a collaboration, a process of guiding the Arbor's own impulse to grow. They tend these nests like gardens, pruning and shaping them over generations until the home itself is a living, breathing part of the world. They understand that everything they have is a loan. The hide of a glider keeps them warm for a time before it returns to the Arbor as rot. A sap-knife holds its edge for a thousand cuts before it shatters and is reclaimed by the ice.

They are a quiet, patient people. Their conversations are sparse, conveyed through a combination of soft clicks, hums, and a complex sign language gestured with their upper hands. Their thoughts are long. They do not hurry, because the Arbor does not hurry. To be born is to join the song. To die is for your personal melody to end, your body given back to the void—a fall not of terror, but of release. There are no graves, only a final, lingering echo. When one of them falls, their clan will turn their heads to listen, not to watch, tracking the percussive series of impacts on the boughs below until the sound fades completely into the Arbor's bass note. Then they will continue on, carrying the memory of that person's sound. The chimes they leave behind are not just decorations; they are prayers to the wind, epitaphs, and messages. They are a declaration that says, *We were here. We lived in harmony with the great song. And we, too, made a beautiful, fleeting sound.*
