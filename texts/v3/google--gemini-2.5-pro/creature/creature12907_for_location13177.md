They are the Silenti.

Their form is a study in verticality and adaptation. They are tall and unnervingly slender, their limbs elongated as if stretched by a lifetime of reaching across voids. They possess not four limbs, but six. The lower two are legs, ending in wide, three-toed feet with an opposable digit at the rear, allowing them to grip branches of any diameter with the tenacity of a hawk's talon. Their two primary arms are long and dextrous, with fine-boned, seven-fingered hands that can weave the most delicate filaments or flake the hardest sap-stone. The final pair of limbs rests high on their back, just below the shoulder blades. In stillness, they appear to be little more than a folded mantle of leathery skin. But when they leap from a perch, these limbs unfurl into a pair of patagia, broad, translucent membranes stretched between a frame of cartilage-like bone. These are not wings for flapping flight—the air is too thin, the distances too great—but for gliding. A single Silenti, catching a thermal rising from a new growth-surge, can cross a mile-wide chasm in a silent, controlled descent that lasts for hours.

Their skin is not skin as we know it. It is a cool, smooth integument that mimics the textures of Yggr. Its color is a shifting mottle of lichen-gray, deep green, and the pale, shadowy blue of a vein under skin. It is a perfect camouflage, but it is not static. When they are still, their skin-tone slowly adapts to the light and color of their perch, rendering them nearly invisible. More than camouflage, their skin holds a faint, controllable bioluminescence. In the deep quiet, they communicate not with voice—for they have none—but with slow, gentle pulses of light from their hands and faces, a language of shifting intensity and color that resembles the vast, slow thoughts of Yggr itself.

Their heads are narrow, their faces dominated by enormous eyes. These eyes are pools of liquid black, with no visible sclera or iris, designed to gather the faintest glimmer in the darkest chasms. They are covered by a double-lidded system: an outer lid for sleep, and an inner, nictitating membrane that is semi-opaque and crystalline. This inner lid slides across during a light-storm, protecting their sensitive vision from the blinding glare of a new growth-surge while still allowing them to perceive the world as a dazzling map of overexposed light and shadow. They have no visible nose, only a series of small slits below the eyes that contract and expand, tasting the chemical composition of the air, discerning the health of a branch or the proximity of a ripe fungus. Their hearing is rudimentary; the world is silent, so their primary sense beyond sight is touch. Through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, they feel the infinitesimal vibrations of Yggr's life, the thrumming of a nutrient flow deep within a bough, the groaning tension that precedes a growth-eruption or a death-fall.

The Silenti do not think of Yggr as a place, a god, or even a thing. To them, Yggr is the verb *to be*. They are a part of its grammar. They are utterly transient, living in small, familial clutches known as "weavings," so named for the woven pod-nests they inhabit. They follow the "seasons" of the Undervault—not of cold and heat, but of light and dark, growth and decay. They migrate for months at a time towards the promise of a "Blue Bloom," a massive new growth that provides the tenderest shoots and the most potent fungi. To subsist, they drink the sweet, nutrient-rich dew that condenses on the leaves, and use their hooked poles to harvest the glowing, gelatinous fungus-caps that are their primary food. These fungi are their only source of intense flavor, and a clutch will guard a fertile patch with quiet, unwavering vigilance.

Their culture is one of deep, patient observation. To a Silenti, the most profound event is not a birth or death, but witnessing the "Unfurling" of a new primary branch. They will gather at a safe distance and watch for days as the spear of new life extends across the void, their own bodies lit by its fierce blue-white glow. Conversely, they mark the "Letting Go" of a dead section. They see it not as a tragedy, but as a necessary release, a journey. They will watch the continent of deadwood begin its centuries-long, slow-motion tumble into the mists below, a silent funeral for a piece of the world.

Their understanding of time is not linear, but cyclical and tidal, like the pulses of light that sweep through the branches. They have no spoken history, only a tactile one. The notches carved into a branch are not a map of distance, but a record of a sensation—"here the light was warm," "here a branch sang with life," "from this perch we saw the fall." Their tools are extensions of their world. The blade of petrified sap holds the memory of ancient light; the hooked pole of stiffened vine is shaped by its former life.

You will never see a crowd of Silenti. You will never hear their laughter or their cries. You might, if you are impossibly still and lucky, glimpse a lone glider, a dark shape against a green-glowing aurora, its membranes rippling in the slow currents. Or you might stumble upon an abandoned nest, the woven filaments still holding the faint warmth of life, a half-eaten fungus cap resting beside a discarded sap-knife. They are not conquerors or builders. They are Yggr's dreaming nerve-endings, a fleeting, transient consciousness born of the great plant's own substance. They are a quiet question the world-tree asks of itself, written in gestures of light and silent flight, before being reabsorbed into the all-encompassing answer.
