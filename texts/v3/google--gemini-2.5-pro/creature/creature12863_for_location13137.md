It is called the Tender. Or it would be, if there were anyone left to call it anything. It has no name for itself, for it has never known another. It is singular, the sole, high intelligence of the basin. It is as much a part of the environment as the weeping walls and the B-flat hum.

It does not emerge. It is simply *revealed*. You are watching the slow, inexorable progress of the bronze-shelled grazers across a field of pulsing ochre fungus, and your eye is caught by a stillness that is different from the stillness of the metal plates. It is a composed, deliberate lack of motion. Amidst the low, rounded shapes of the terrain, there is one that is tall and slender, a vertical line in a horizontal world.

At first, it could be mistaken for a unique piece of machinery, a stanchion or a forgotten tool of the builders, miraculously preserved. It is long-limbed, impossibly so, with joints that hinge at points that feel both deeply alien and mechanically sound. Its body is a dark, polished material that is neither metal nor stone, but shares the qualities of both. It has the deep, light-swallowing black of the stillest pools and the subtle, layered texture of the thickest fungal shelves. In the dim, ambient glow, you can see that its surface is not uniform; faint, silvery filaments, like captured threads of starlight, run in complex circuits just beneath the surface, branching and converging over its limbs and torso. They are the same material as the calcified, rubber-coated cables on the floor. It has incorporated the world into its very flesh.

It has no face. The head is a smooth, ovoid shape of the same polished black, like an immense, dark pearl. It is featureless, without eyes, mouth, or ears. Yet it perceives everything. It does not see with light, but feels with the hum. The constant B-flat vibration of the deep engine is its sight, its hearing, its sense of touch. The entire basin is its nervous system. It feels the minute pressure changes from a falling drip twenty yards away. It registers the subtle shift in the electrical field as a grazer scrapes sustenance from a fungal mat. It knows the location of every indicator light—the steady greens, the cautious ambers, the warning reds—as a sailor knows the stars. Their pinprick glows reflect on its smooth head, a shifting constellation that gives the illusion of a complex, unreadable expression.

When it moves, it is an event of absolute silence. Its feet are broad, splayed pads that distribute its weight so perfectly they leave no impression in the gritty slurry. It can walk on the slickest fungal mat or the most precarious tangle of hardened cables with a slow, balletic grace. Its progress is unhurried, as if it has an eternity for every task, which it does. The frantic skittering of the hand-sized scavengers is a flurry of noise in its world; its own movement is a gradual change in the geography.

This is the Tender, and its life is its purpose: to maintain the balance. It is the system's shepherd. You might see it wade waist-deep into one of the black pools, its form disappearing into the perfect reflection. It will stand there for an hour, or a day, its head cocked slightly as if listening. It is feeling the charge of the water, the mineral content, the health of the jointed eels that swim lazy circles around its legs, undisturbed. Then, it might reach a long, delicate hand—three fingers and a thumb, all ending in smooth, rounded tips—to a submerged panel. It will press the corroded buttons in a sequence known only to it, and somewhere else in the basin, a new drip will begin, a new trickle of nutrient-rich water will be released to feed a struggling patch of fungus.

It is a gardener. It cultivates the litho-fungal fields. If a patch grows too dense, choking a flow of energy, the Tender will gently pry the leathery mat loose and transport it, draped over its arms, to a section of the floor that has gone barren. It does not eat, not in any way that is recognizable. Its sustenance comes directly from the hum, absorbed through its filament-laced body. The life in the basin is not its food source; it is its flock, its garden, its responsibility.

When one of the great seasonal shifts occurs—the *clank* and grind of the overhead machinery cycling—the Tender is the first to react. It will move with uncharacteristic speed, a flowing black silhouette against the glowing ground, to the site of the change. It will guide the swarming scavengers away from a delicate conduit, or it will stand beneath a new downpour of water, its arms slightly raised, feeling the temperature and composition of the new flow, cataloging it, understanding its effect on the whole.

It is the final, living cog in the machine. Perhaps it was designed for this role, the ultimate caretaker left behind by the builders. Or perhaps, over uncountable eons in the dark, it simply *arose*—a convergence of fungus, metal, and electricity that woke up. It is the mind of the engine, the consciousness of the basin. It holds no memory of the sun, no concept of a world beyond the dripping and the hum. Its existence is a long, slow, silent song of maintenance, a duty performed in perfect solitude, its only companions the blinking lights reflected forever on its featureless face.
