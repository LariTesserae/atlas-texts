Those who live here are the Sinter-Carvers. It is not what they call themselves, for they have no name for their own kind. A name is a wall built to separate "us" from "them," and in their world, there is no "them." There are the grazers, the stalkers, the flyers, and themselves, all cogs in the same roaring machine. They are simply... *the people*.

To them, the world is not loud. The constant, vibrating roar is silence. It is the absence of information, the blank canvas of existence. Their hearing is not structured to parse it, any more than a fish is structured to parse the qualities of water. It is the medium in which they are suspended. What they hear are the deviations, the textures upon the silence: the close, rhythmic *scrape-scrape-scrape* of a grazer herd, a high-frequency vibration in the stone that speaks of a new layer hardening, the dull, deep *thump* of a rockfall in a forgotten lower level. Their ears are small, simple, and mostly internal, protected by flaps of thick skin. True hearing happens through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands.

Physically, they are formed by their world and their work. They are stooped, their spines having a permanent, graceful curve from a lifetime spent in tunnels and shafts carved just high enough to serve their purpose. They are not built for striding across open plains, but for moving through the dense, organic lattice of their excavated city. Their skin is a pallid, doughy grey, like that of a cave fish, almost translucent where it is thin at the belly and throat. But on the knees, the horny soles of the feet, the elbows, and especially the hands, it is thick as tanned leather and stained the deep ochre and rust of the sinter.

Their hands are their most defining feature. They are broad, oversized, with thick, powerful fingers. Their fingernails are not keratin but a bio-mineral adaptation, growing thick and hard like flint, with a sharp, resilient edge. In infancy, these nails are soft, but they harden with age, chipped and worn down by the constant work of carving, only to grow again, stronger. These biological tools are supplemented by simple chisels and scrapers made from the hardened carapace of grazers and the leg-bones of the six-legged stalkers.

Their faces are simple and wide, with soft features. Their mouths are small and rarely open. Their noses are little more than two vertical slits, capable of closing to filter the mineral-heavy air. Their eyes are their most haunting aspect. They are huge, round, and of a solid, liquid black, like drops of crude oil. They possess no visible sclera or iris. These eyes are not for seeing in the way we understand it. In the perpetual dim twilight, they are exquisitely sensitive to the faintest movements and shifts in light, but they do not perceive color or fine detail. To a Carver, the world is a monochrome ballet of deeper and lighter shadows. They navigate the city as much by memory and touch as by sight. They can feel the heat radiating from a newly hardened wall, the subtle drafts of air that signal a passage, and the familiar texture of a thousand-year-old carving under their fingertips.

They do not speak. The roar would swallow any sound they make. Their language is a silent, tactile one, a complex grammar of touch and pressure. A questing touch of the fingers to another's arm is a question. A firm, flat palm on the back is a reassurance. A rapid series of taps on a shoulder communicates urgency. Stories are told by tracing patterns on a companion’s skin in the dark, the teller’s rough fingers conveying the scrape of a grazer herd, the silent pounce of a stalker, the slow, patient work of the ancestors in the levels below. It is a language of profound intimacy, and the concept of personal space is unknown to them. They live, sleep, and work in a constant, flowing proximity, their bodies brushing past each other in the narrow corridors, a constant, silent conversation of contact.

Their lives are one of ritualized labor. They are the agents of the city's growth, which is also its burial. When a new layer of sinter hardens on the terraces and plazas—a process that happens over a cycle of what might be weeks or months—the Carvers descend upon it. They work in teams, their movements synchronized not by a foreman's call but by a shared, felt rhythm that passes through the stone. The *tap-scrape* of their tools is a communal song only they can feel. They carve not from a plan, but from an inherited instinct, a deep grammar of form. They hollow out new living chambers, link them with passages, and carve steep, spiraling stairways down into the now-older levels. Their architecture is a living thing, an extension of their bodies.

Their relationship with the world is not one of dominion, but of function. They harvest the dinner-plate-sized grazers, cracking open their shells for the dense, protein-rich flesh within, leaving the empty carapaces to be broken down and reabsorbed by the biofilm. They are farmers of a sort, managing the slow-moving herds. They pay no mind to the six-legged stalkers, seeing them as another part of the system, a check on the grazer population. A fellow Carver taken by a stalker is a sad but necessary event, no different than a rockfall. The individual is subsumed by the process.

The concept of self is secondary to the concept of the collective, and the collective is secondary to the work. Time for them is not a line, but a depth. The past is physically, tangibly beneath their feet. To speak of an ancestor is to point downwards, into the layered darkness of the under-city. The future is the soft, wet, uncarved sinter above.

When a Carver’s life ends, there is no grave. There is no monument. A monument would be an absurdity in a world that perpetually buries itself. The body is carried to a fresh, wet plain of newly precipitated sinter, a place not yet ready for carving. It is laid upon the soft, chalky ground. There, the same shimmering biofilm that feeds the grazers goes to work. Slowly, gently, it envelops the body. The flesh and bone are broken down, their minerals un-knit. They are dissolved and given back to the super-saturated world, to be precipitated once more as the ochre stone from which they came. They do not merely live in the city; they become it. They are born of the stone, spend their lives shaping it, and in death, they return to it, their substance ready to be carved anew by the generations to come, in the eternal, roaring silence of the world’s single, unchanging moment.
