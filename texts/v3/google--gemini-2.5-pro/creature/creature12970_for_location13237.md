The beings who live here are called the Still. This is not a name they chose, for they have no concept of names as sound. It is a description of their state, translated into a framework of noise and silence they could never comprehend.

They are beings of density.

At first, you do not see them. They are as much a part of the landscape as the lithocrete blocks and weeping stone. Their forms are low to the ground, broad and heavy-boned, with a low center of gravity. They move with a profound, unhurried deliberation that seems to emerge from the geology itself. Their skin is pale, the color of wet cement or weathered quartz, but it is often streaked with the same mineral deposits that stain the monoliths. Runnels of rust-red and verdigris crawl down their limbs and across their backs, mapping the paths of condensation that has trickled over their bodies for a lifetime. Their skin is cool and perpetually damp, slick to the touch like a river stone.

Their heads are smooth and dome-like, with features that seem pressed into the flesh rather than sculpted from it. There are no external ears, only sealed, almost invisible whorls of cartilage where they once might have been. To them, the air is a silent medium, useful only for its moisture and for breathing. Their eyes are wide and dark, pupils fixed into deep, black pools that absorb the pearlescent, shadowless light. They rarely blink, their gaze patient and unnervingly steady, protected by a secondary, translucent membrane that sweeps across the eyeball with the slow regularity of a radar dish.

The true locus of their perception is in their feet. They are barefoot, always. Their soles are immense, splayed wide and covered in a thick, yet supple, layer of callus that is ridged and patterned like a fingerprint the size of a dinner plate. These feet are not for walking so much as for listening. Through them, they read the world. They feel the constant, baseline thrum of the deep turbines as a kind of foundational truth, the ever-present hum of existence itself. Silence, to them, would not be peace; it would be the terrifying void of non-being, the failure of the world's heart.

Upon this foundational hum, they perceive everything else as pressure and texture. They can feel the subtle shift in vibration as the colossal pumps deep underground begin a new cycle. They can discern the location of a hairline fracture weeping water fifty yards away by the faint, high-frequency resonance it adds to the drone in the stone. They feel the approach of others not as footfalls, but as a complex, traveling wave of pressure moving through the vast, solid medium of the plateau. Their world is a symphony of vibrations, a tactile landscape of immense subtlety and depth.

Their communication is a language of touch and pressure. When they meet, they press their palms or foreheads together, a slow, firm contact that lasts for many seconds. In this stillness, a silent conversation unfolds. It is a language of minute, controlled tremors generated within their own musculature, of subtle shifts in weight, of changes in skin temperature and the electrical potential of their own bodies. It's a form of communication more intimate and far slower than speech, a direct transfer of information from one nervous system to another. From a distance, they might communicate by pressing a hand flat to the ground and sending a patterned pulse through the stone, a message that arrives as a faint, meaningful tremor under the feet of its recipient miles away.

They are the tenders of the great machine. Their lives are spent in slow, methodical maintenance. You might see one, and then another, moving across the wide avenues with a glacial grace. They carry no tools of clatter and bang; their implements are resonant tuners, pressure calibrators, and tubes of viscous, conductive paste. They work in unison, but without signal or command, each seeming to know their role through a shared, constant awareness of the system's state. One might spend an entire day slowly polishing a conduit seam with a fine, abrasive gel, its movements rhythmic and hypnotic. Another might stand for hours, palm pressed against the side of a Compressor House, "listening" to the deep, slow rhythm of its inner workings, a physician monitoring a patient's breath.

They do not seem to have homes in the way we understand them. They rest in shallow, carved-out alcoves within the monolithic blocks, niches no larger than their bodies, where they fold themselves into a state of minimal activity, their bodies still absorbing the life-thrum of the city. They consume the iridescent, metallic lichen, scraping it from the stone with keratinous growths on their fingertips. They drink the weeping water directly from the sheet-pools, cupping it in thick, heavy hands.

Their relationship with the tiny, glass-like crustaceans is one of passive coexistence. The creatures are too small to register in their vibrational world, and the Still are too slow and deliberate to pose a threat. They are simply another part of the slow, wet, humming equilibrium.

To see one is to see an embodiment of the place itself. A solitary figure stands on the edge of a vast, mirror-flat pool of water, its form perfectly reflected in the surface. It is utterly motionless, its broad feet planted on the rose-quartz stone, its head tilted as if concentrating. It is not listening for a sound. It is feeling the pulse of a world-engine, the slow, inexorable turning of a gear the size of a continent, the constant, life-giving hum that flows from the stone, through its feet, up its dense bones, and into the core of its being. It is a part of the circuit. It is home.
