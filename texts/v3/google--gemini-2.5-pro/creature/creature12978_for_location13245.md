They are the Rim-Walkers. They are also called the Quiet, though this name is a paradox, for they are born into, live within, and die in the most profound and constant noise imaginable.

To see one is to almost miss them. Their bodies are long and slender, with an economy of form that speaks of scarcity and efficiency. They have no fat, only a ropy, resilient musculature stretched over a light, bird-like frame. Their average height is greater than a man's, but their weight is less than half. Their limbs are elongated, especially their fingers and toes, which are prehensile and tipped not with nails but with tough, textured pads like a gecko's. These allow them to move with uncanny silence and security across the sloping, furrowed skin of the Great Boles, even on near-vertical surfaces.

Their skin is their first and most obvious adaptation. It lacks uniform pigment, instead showing a mottled, variable pattern of ochres, greys, deep greens, and blacks. The pattern is not static; it shifts slowly over hours and days, like lichen growing or a shadow moving, allowing them to blend almost perfectly with the ancient bark-ground they walk upon. A stationary Rim-Walker is little more than a trick of the light, a slight anomaly in the landscape's texture.

Their faces are long and narrow, dominated by large, dark eyes. These eyes are set wide apart, giving them an immense field of vision, and are protected by a transparent inner eyelid that flicks across the cornea to clear away the constant, fine rain of dead insect matter. In the perpetual, soft light, their pupils are permanently wide, absorbing every possible photon. They see in patterns. Where another might see a chaotic, shimmering cloud of fauna, a Rim-Walker sees the currents, the eddies, the hunting lanes, the mating plumes. They can track a single eagle-sized glider through a swarm of a million smaller ones, not by focusing on it, but by observing the pattern of its disturbance.

But their most unique feature is their sensory relationship with the sound. They have no external ears. The sides of their skull, just behind the jaw, are covered in smooth, taut membranes of skin, like the heads of tiny drums. These are their tympana, and they do not "hear" in the way we do. They do not perceive pitch or melody. To them, the great drone is a medium, not a noise. It is their water, their air, a carrier of information.

The constant vibration is their baseline, their sense of "normal." Through minute shifts in pressure and resonance felt through these membranes and the soles of their feet, they can sense phenomena miles away. A subtle change in the drone's frequency tells of a shift in air pressure, an approaching mist-front. A deep, resonant pulse that travels through the body of the Bole itself signals a bark-fall in a distant quadrant. The frenzied, high-frequency "sheen" on the sound's surface indicates a massive predator-prey event in the open air. They do not listen to the world; they feel its texture in real time.

Spoken language is impossible here, and they have none. Their communication is a combination of slow, deliberate gestures—a language of form and posture—and direct, tactile transmission. They press their palms or foreheads together, and through the skin and bone, a complex pattern of minute, controlled vibrations passes between them. It is a silent, intimate language of pure feeling and intent, a direct transfer of state. A "conversation" might be one Rim-Walker placing their hand on another's back and conveying the feeling of a cool, full water basin, a feeling of contentment and security.

Their psychology is one of profound patience. Their perception of time is tied to the geological life of the Boles. Generations are counted by the wearing down of a particular groove or the slow peeling of a sheet of bark. A human life-span is an eyeblink. Their own lives, though long, are seen as fleeting contributions to the whole. They do not think of themselves as individuals in the way we do, but as temporary, sentient motes on the skin of the great organism. The concept of a singular ego is as faint and useless as a single spoken word in the drone.

They are beings of immense, tranquil awareness. They do not seek to create or to change, but to perceive and to understand. Their art is observation. A Rim-Walker might spend a full day sitting on an overhang, not in meditation, but in rapt attention, watching the way mist condenses on the fuzzy filaments of a fungal mat. This is not idleness; it is an act of communion. Their most sacred creations are memory-strings, long cords woven from the sinew of gliders, onto which they tie knots of varying complexity. These are not records of events, but captures of sensation—a knot that, when held, mimics the texture of a specific type of pollen, or another whose shape recalls the sweeping curve of a particular dry riverbed.

They are not hunters. They are scavengers and gardeners of a sort. They eat the softer, spore-rich fungal felts and the sweet, gelatinous secretions of the Boles. When they need a tool, they find a large glider that has died of old age and fallen to the rim, respectfully harvesting its sharp bones and iridescent chitin. Nothing is taken from a living thing.

When a Rim-Walker dies, there is no great ceremony. Their kin carry the light, slender body to one of the wide, shallow basins where the fungal mats are thickest. They lay the body down among the filaments and let it be. They watch as the fungus, the decomposer of this world, begins its slow, quiet work of breaking the body down, returning its substance to the Great Bole that sustained it. To the Rim-Walkers, this is not an end. It is merely the last, most peaceful act of participation. They are a quiet thought in the mind of a sleeping god, and upon death, the thought simply dissolves back into the dreamer.
