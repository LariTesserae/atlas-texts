They are called the Still-Folk, though they have no name for themselves that is spoken aloud. Names are concepts too sharp, too definite for this soft world. They are simply *us*, just as the Arch-Bole is *here* and the Quiet Fall is *below*.

To see one is to see a living piece of the twilight. They are tall and willowy, their forms elongated as if stretched by the immense verticality of their world. Their skin is smooth and cool to the touch, poreless like the surface of a river stone, and it glistens with a perpetual, fine sheen of moisture, much like the weeping bark-wood they call home. Its color is a mottled, shifting palette of charcoal, moss-green, and damp loam-brown, a perfect camouflage that makes them seem to dissolve into the background when they are motionless. And they are often motionless. Stillness is their natural state, a form of communion with the profound quiet of their world.

They are hexapodal, possessing two long, graceful legs and four arms. The lower, primary arms are strong, with broad, four-fingered hands. The palms and fingers are wide and soft, padded with a tissue that offers incredible grip on the damp surfaces, yet is gentle enough not to harm the living wood. These are the arms for climbing, for carving, for the slow, patient work of generations. Above these, nestled just below their collarbones, are a pair of smaller, more slender arms, with long, delicate fingers perfect for intricate tasks: weaving moss-thread, tending to the fragile blooms of epiphytes, or for the quiet, complex gestures that form a significant part of their language.

Their faces are narrow and serene, with wide-set, liquid-dark eyes that are entirely pupil. They have no whites, no irises, just two pools of polished night, perfectly adapted for the dim, silver-grey light. They see movement and pattern more than detail and color. They have no external ears; the muffling air makes them almost useless. Instead, they sense vibration through the soles of their feet and the pads of their hands, feeling the thrum of the Arch-Bole's deep, slow life and the subtle shifts in air pressure that are their only winds. Their mouths are small, simple slits that rarely open, as their primary mode of communication is not vocal. Speech is a tool for intimacy, a murmur of soft, vowel-heavy whispers shared only in the closest of quarters. For all other communication, they use the silent, balletic poetry of their four hands and, in the deeper dark of their hollows, the shifting constellations of faint bioluminescent nodules that dapple their chests and forearms, pulsing and changing color to convey mood, intent, and complex ideas.

They are not individuals in the way a creature from a louder, faster world would understand it. The fluidity of memory in this place has shaped their consciousness. A Still-Person's memories are like water beads on a leaf—present, clear for a moment, then prone to merging with the whole or evaporating entirely. They do not trust the "wet thoughts" inside their skulls. Certainty resides outside the self. Their history, their laws, their sense of who they are, is not remembered but *read*. It is carved into the very floor they walk on. Vast, intricate patterns of looping, branching, and spiraling grooves—worn smooth by the touch of a million hands over a thousand years—are their libraries and their ancestors. To know their lineage, a Still-Person traces a specific carved line with their fingertips. To understand a past event, they walk a path, feeling the story unfold as a sequence of textures under their feet. They live in a state of communal, externalized memory, trusting the un-fading wood over the fickle mists of the mind.

Their "technology" is an expression of this profound patience. To create a bridge, they do not fell a tree; they find a suitable vine and, over the course of a century, gently guide its growth across a chasm, whispering encouragement to it with careful pruning and offerings of nutrient-rich water. To create a tool, they find a place where the Arch-Bole's sap weeps and hardens, and they wait for years until the globule is large and dense enough to be flaked into a sharp, obsidian-like edge.

They are the world's self-awareness. They are parasites in the same way a nerve ending is a parasite on a body—an integrated part of a whole, translating the world to itself. They are the tenders of the great, dreaming giant. They clear fungal blights that could harm the Bole. They channel the weeping waters to dry patches. They act as the living architecture's gentle, guiding consciousness.

When one of them feels the end of their life approaching—a slow fading they feel as a loss of connection to the Bole's thrum—they are not mourned with tears, for the world already weeps for them. Their kin will weave a simple, open-work raft of moss and broad leaves. The dying person is laid upon it, and the raft is gently pushed from the edge of the plateau. It does not plummet. It drifts into the Quiet Fall, descending with impossible slowness, a silent vessel on a silent sea of air. They watch until it is a mere speck, and then nothing, not consumed by distance, but absorbed back into the great, quiet dream that is the world. For the Still-Folk, this is not an end. It is simply a memory, a story, finally evaporating.
