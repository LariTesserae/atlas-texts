What lives here is patient. It must be. The pressure and the cold have leeched all hurry from the cells of being, leaving behind only the slow, inexorable certainty of geology. Consciousness, if it can be called that, is not a flash of lightning but a sedimentary process, an accretion of sensory moments over a timescale that would render a surface-dweller's life a mere spasm.

They are expressions of the medium, not invaders within it.

The most substantial are the ones who know the false floor of the silt basins, the Crawlers. To call them creatures is to imply a separation from their world that does not exist. A Crawler is a piece of the Underside's geology that has decided to move. Its carapace is not grown but *accumulated*. Over centuries, its slow, cold metabolism draws minerals from the digested grit, binding them with organic polymers into a shell that is indistinguishable from the polished bedrock of the Drones. It is a living stone, its surface scoured and grooved by the same currents that shape the world. Some are ancient, their backs hosting their own ecosystems of mineral-fixing bacteria, small, dark patches that are the Underside’s equivalent of moss.

A Crawler does not see. Eyes would be useless, sand-blasted follies. It does not hear in any conventional sense, for the world is a constant roar of grinding particles. Instead, it *feels*. Its entire body is a receptor, a kilometer-long nerve ending pressed against the world. It reads the grain of the currents, the subtle shifts in pressure that signal a coming surge or the settling of a long calm. It tastes the medium, not for flavor, but for information: the distant signature of a freshly-shed particle of a Glass-Knot, a trace of the rare minerals that bubble from bedrock fissures. Its mind is a map of pressures and chemistries.

Its existence is a slow pilgrimage between states of being. In the deep quiet between currents, it moves. It does not walk so much as flow. Heavy, shovel-like limbs, themselves mountains of compacted armour, push through the loose sediment of the basin. It is plowing a furrow in what passes for soil, seeking the faint chemical rain that falls from the distant, flashing constellations of the Glass-Knots, or the even older, richer detritus buried in the silt from ages past. When its body-sense detects the first deep tremor of an approaching current, a change in the omnipresent hum, it ceases its journey. It finds purchase, either by digging its anchor-limbs deep into the denser sediment below or, as observed, by bracing itself against a larger, un-living boulder—a temporary alliance with the inanimate. And then it waits. For a day, a week, a decade. It becomes stone again, enduring the scouring storm of grit that would strip flesh from bone, but which is to the Crawler merely weather. It does not think. It does not hope for the calm. It simply *is*—a knot of endurance in a universe of friction.

Far above, in the perpetual midnight of the mid-body, are the Phantoms. They are the antithesis of the Crawlers—formless, weightless, and tragically brief. A Phantom is the medium’s dream of itself. It is a near-invisible shimmer, a momentary sac of organized water and gel, its boundaries constantly dissolving and reforming. It is a creature born of the current, shaped by it, and often killed by it. To be a Phantom is to have no anchor. Its life is a dance of utter passivity, captured by the great invisible rivers, tumbling through the darkness for the entirety of its existence.

It feeds by being open. It unfurls membranes so thin they are merely a concept, webs of molecules that trap the rarest of treasures: a spore from a Glass-Knot, a sloughed-off cell from another Phantom, the ghost of an organic compound. Most of what they catch is useless grit, which they must slowly, inefficiently expel. Their existence is one of profound starvation, punctuated by microscopic feasts.

The green flash of a Phantom is its only act of defiance. It is an expenditure of energy so costly it can shorten a lifespan, but it is their only language. It is a warning to a predator—though what preys on a ghost?—a desperate mating call to another nearby shimmer, or a final, pointless scream as the creature is torn apart by a shear current. For a Crawler, patient in the silt below, this momentary emerald star in the upper blackness is a meaningless celestial event, a flicker in a sky it will never know. It is the death of a god of whom the Crawler is not even aware.

And there is something else. Something smaller and more fundamental. In the deepest, most stable silt of the basin floors, below where even the Crawlers plow, is a kind of life that has no individuals. It is a diffuse, microbial consensus. We might call them the Murmurs. They are not organisms, but a slow, planet-sized chemical reaction that has achieved a state of nascent being. They are the ones who perform the first, most patient digestion of the raw minerals. They are what creates the subtle chemical gradients that the Crawlers follow like scent trails. They do not move. They do not grow. They simply persist, a collective hum of metabolism that is a register below even the grinding of the silt. They are the reason the Underside is not entirely dead, the thin, vital line between a world of inert particles and a world that can dream of crawlers and phantoms.

The Glass-Knots, then, are the saints of this world. They have achieved the impossible: stability. By finding a crack in the bedrock, a place sheltered from the worst of the currents, they have anchored themselves to permanence. Their slow, blue-white pulse is a signal not of life, but of *place*. It is the universe's only proof that a "here" can exist in the Underside. Their crystalline bodies are a testament to order, a fragile geometry in a world of chaotic abrasion. When they die and their fragments break away, they become holy relics, pieces of solid meaning that fall into the shifting doubt of the basins, providing, for a moment, a truth for the Crawlers to consume before resuming their endless, patient journey through the grit.
