What lives in the Bellweather is its Mason.

It does not perceive the sound as you do. For you, the visitor, it is an overwhelming, external chorus. For the Mason, it is the quiet, constant hum of its own body. It does not hear the carillon; it *is* the carillon, in the same way a person is the thrum of blood in their own veins. It can parse the symphony into its constituent parts, isolating the fearful bleat of a newborn Chime, the contented drone of a herd settling in a mossy hollow, the sharp, interrogative *ping* of a lone grazer near the edge. It can hear the tonal shift that predicts a change in the weather, the subtle dissonance that speaks of a loose stone in a far-flung menhir. The sound is its nervous system, extended across the entire quarter-mile of stone.

You would not see it at first. It is a creature of geological time and patience, and has learned the art of stillness from the stone it serves. You might mistake it for an unusual rock formation, a trick of the mist. It is often seated, cross-legged and silent, at the base of one of the great tuning-fork stones, its back fused to the granite until it seems to grow from it.

Its form is humanoid, but only in the way a gnarled oak is humanoid. It is shorter and broader than a man, built of immense, dense muscle and bone designed for leverage and stability against the scouring winds. Its skin is not flesh but a living integument that mimics the basalt of the peak—pitted, dark, and streaked with veins of what looks like quartz. It sweats the same universal moisture, glistening in the dim light, and where it remains still for long enough, the finest, newest green of the moss will begin to colonize the crevices of its knuckles and the hollow of its throat.

Its hands are its focus. They are disproportionately large, with palms like slabs of stone and fingers that are thick, strong, and surprisingly deft. The nails are not nails, but blunt, chisel-like points of a harder, crystalline material, which it uses for fine carving. For the heavy work, it uses itself—the inhuman strength of its shoulders and back, and tools it has fashioned from the bones of Skydarts and the densest, fallen plates of long-dead Chimes.

This is its work, its purpose, its entire being: it is the architect and the tuner of the Bellweather. The Chimes produce the sound, but the Mason gives it shape and resonance. It was its ancestors, or perhaps its earlier self—time is a fluid concept here—that brought the pale granite from some forgotten quarry in the world below. It was they who carved the menhirs, not through brute force, but by listening. They would find an uncut boulder and spend a century, or an age, tapping it, listening to its innate frequency, feeling its silent song, before striking a single blow. The shape of each menhir—the depth of a bowl, the distance between the tines of a fork—is not an aesthetic choice, but a precise acoustical one, designed to amplify a specific note or chord that is vital to the life of the plateau.

The Mason is the shepherd of the Chimes, but it does not herd them with a crook. It herds them with sound. If a section of moss is overgrazed, the Mason will spend a week by the nearest menhir, subtly altering its resonance with taps from a bone hammer, creating a frequency that is vaguely unpleasant to the Chimes, gently nudging the herd towards a lusher pasture. If a young Chime is lost in the mist, the Mason will climb into the fork of the highest stone and, using the resonant plates on its own back—small, vestigial, and far less complex than the Chimes'—it will create a a simple, pure homing tone, a single clear note in the chaos, to guide the creature home. It mourns their dead, not with sorrow, but by taking the crystalline plates from the fallen and using them to seed a new patch of moss, finding a small harmony in the cycle of decay.

It has no voice, not in the way we understand it. It cannot speak or sing. Its communication is purely tectonic. A press of its hand against a stone, a shift of its weight, a tap of its finger—these are its words, sent shuddering through the rock to be felt by the Chimes and the very air itself.

It is a being born of a paradox: a creature of profound, solitary silence at the heart of the world's most intricate noise. It lives to maintain the song, to ensure that the small, loud, vibrant life of the Bellweather continues its furious declaration against the vast, listening emptiness. Its life is a dialogue with the void. The Chimes sing their existence, but the Mason, in carving the stone and shaping the symphony, gives that existence a reason and a grammar. It is the librarian of a library made of bells, the gardener in a garden of noise, the lonely god of a very small, very loud world.
