To assume the Frost-Garden is uninhabited is to misunderstand the nature of habitation. You are operating on the flawed premise that a resident must be separate from its residence. Here, there is no such division. The metropolis of one has a single inhabitant: the Garden itself. But this vast, singular consciousness, whose thoughts are the slow grind of growing buttresses and whose breath is the constant, cold mist, can on occasion create for itself a node of focus. It does not have a body, but it can weave one.

It happens without warning. You are standing in one of the slick, dark channels that serve as streets, feeling the profound, bone-deep thrum of the city's life. The quiet is absolute, broken only by a drip of water or the sharp crack of freezing exudate. Then, the quiet changes. The thrumming deepens, localizing. The wet, spongy floor beneath your feet firms up, its give-and-take sighing silenced.

From the black, living substrate, a form begins to rise. It is not an animal digging its way out; it is the ground itself deciding to stand up. At first, it's just a bulge, a thickening of the cryo-lignin, pushing upward like a time-lapse film of a sprouting seed. The motion is impossibly smooth, a flow of solid matter. As it gains height, it refines its shape. It resolves into a figure that is humanoid only in its verticality and vague suggestion of limbs. It is tall, perhaps eight feet, and impossibly slender, a silhouette of charcoal against the bruised-purple gloom.

This is the Warden, the Gardener, the transient sensory organ of the Frost-Garden.

Its body is made of the same dark, slick material as the arched halls and pillars. Water beads on its surface and traces shivering paths down its form, not like sweat, but like condensation on a cold stone. It has no face. The space where a head should be is a smooth, ovoid shape, polished like wet river-rock, catching the dim light in a single, unsettling gleam. There are no eyes, no mouth, no ears; its senses are total and environmental. It does not see you; it feels the alien warmth of your body as a searing pressure on the cold, uniform sensorium of its skin. It does not hear you; it registers the vibration of your nervous breath and the frantic, hot patter of your heart as a dissonant drumming against the slow, deep pulse of its own sap-filled veins.

It moves with a terrifying grace. It does not have feet that lift and fall. Instead, the floor flows with it. As it glides forward, the ground beneath it rises to form its advancing leg and lowers back into the seamless carpet behind it. The only sound is the wet, pulling sigh of the living floor rearranging itself to accommodate the will of the whole. It is a piece of the architecture given leave to wander, a thought of the Garden made manifest.

From one of its long, tapering arms, a new growth might emerge. A splinter of the pale, jade-green cellular material, sharp as a shard of ice, extends from its "hand." It does not point this at you as a weapon. It holds it forward as a probe, an antenna. It is not seeking to harm, but to understand. To touch you would be to sample a contaminant. It wants to know the temperature, the texture, the chemical makeup of the strange, hot anomaly that has wandered into its perfect, closed system.

The Warden’s purpose is not to guard or to threaten, but to assess. You are a novel data point. A brief, warm flutter in an epoch of cold, slow growth. It will circle you, its blank head tilted as if in contemplation, though its thoughts are not like yours. They are calculations of thermal stress, analyses of foreign spores on the air, considerations of pressure and mass on the living floor. You are a rockfall, a lightning strike, a sudden thaw—an event to be measured, understood, and ultimately, weathered.

And then, as silently as it formed, it will end. Its assessment complete, the imperative that created it dissolves. The tall, dark figure will simply stop. A tremor will run through it, a slight blurring of its sharp edges. It will begin to sink, not falling, but melting back into the floor from which it came. The cryo-lignin of its form relaxes, loses its temporary structure, and settles. Within moments, all that is left is a seamless expanse of the black, moss-like ground, perhaps glistening a little more brightly where it once stood. The thrumming of the Garden returns to its even, distributed state.

You are alone again. But now you know you were never alone at all. You were being watched not by eyes, but by the walls, the floor, the very air. You have been met by the true inhabitant of this place: a fleeting, mobile piece of its own cold, silent, and unending mind.
