They are the **Still**.

They do not have a single form, for in a world without tools, the organism itself must become the tool, the home, and the vessel of its own history. To be one of the Still is to be a participant in a slow, tripartite life cycle that is as much a geological process as a biological one.

**The First Form: The Kainos**

Deep in the black, tannin-rich pools on the forest floor, they begin. The Kainos are the larval stage, and they are rarely seen. They resemble shimmering veils of oil on the water's surface, or perhaps a congregation of fine, silver needles, drawn together by some unseen current. They are almost liquid, their bodies little more than a semi-permeable membrane enclosing a complex knot of nervous tissue. They do not eat in a conventional sense; they absorb. They drift through the water that has percolated through millennia of wood, imbibing the chemical memories, the genetic ghosts, and the lingering electrical signals of the forest's deep past. They are the world's librarians, their minds a silent repository of everything the water has touched. For centuries, they drift and learn, their consciousness a quiet, receptive mirror to the deep, slow thrum of the Great Tableland.

**The Second Form: The Weavers**

At the culmination of an age, a Kainos will feel a pull—a resonant call from the mycelial network that pulses through the groundwood. It will crawl from its pool, its liquid body coalescing, hardening in the thick, oxygen-rich air. This is the Weaver.

The Weaver is the form you might, if you are impossibly lucky and patient, mistake for a piece of the landscape in motion. They are roughly the size of a human, but their shape is utterly alien. They have six or eight limbs, long and slender, terminating not in hands, but in a complex of articulated chitinous hooks, spatulas, and fine, thread-like extruders. Their bodies are flattened, encased in a flexible, mottled carapace that perfectly mimics the bark of a dozen different trees. They move with a slowness that is unnerving, a deliberate, multi-limbed grace across the vertical and horizontal surfaces of the wood.

They are the active principle of the world. The Weavers are the gardeners, the architects, the artists. They do not build with dead material; they guide the living. One might spend a standard decade persuading a vine to grow in a specific spiral to create a passive water-catchment. Another might spend a century weaving the mycelial threads of a glowing fungus into a pattern on the under-bark of a great limb—a pattern that isn't language as we know it, but a living circuit that modulates the humidity and spore-flow in a cubic kilometer of forest. This is their communication: slow, living sculpture. They tend the bellows-plants, ensuring their exhalations are in harmony. They prune the leaf-stairs. They are the world's nervous system made manifest, their patient manipulations the physical expression of the forest's will.

They do not speak. The sound would be a violation. Their interactions are through touch, through the exchange of complex pheromones, and through the shared canvas of the living wood they cultivate. They are the authors of the silence, for they are the ones who maintain the perfect, sound-dampening equilibrium.

**The Third Form: The Ancients**

A Weaver’s life of labor ends when it climbs. It ascends one of the Arch-Boles, a journey that can take a lifetime, and finds a place prepared by generations before it—a hollow, a platform, a convergence of great boughs in the perpetual twilight near the canopy. Here, it settles. Its limbs curl inward. Its carapace fuses and thickens. Roots, fine as fungal hyphae, grow from its body, penetrating the living wood of the Arch-Bole, physically connecting it to the geobiological whole. This is the final form: the Ancient.

The Ancients are motionless. Over millennia, moss and epiphytes grow over them until they are indistinguishable from any other knot or burl on the great tree. But they are not dead; they have simply changed their state of being. Their consciousness, once focused on the physical manipulation of the world, now expands outwards through the root-tap and into the vast mycelial network. They become a mind within the greater mind of the wood. They are no longer doing; they are *being*. They do not perceive time as a sequence of events, but as a single, complex, and simultaneous texture. They are the ones who feel the decade-long groan of a branch shifting, not as a sound, but as a drawn-out thought. They are the ones who dream the patterns that the Weavers then enact over centuries. Their collective consciousness is the low, sub-audible hum that emanates from the world itself.

And the remnants? They are the legacy of the Ancients. When a gestalt of Ancients, networked together for eons in one location, finally completes its thought-cycle, its collective being can precipitate out of biology and into a more permanent form. The amber-like hives are the fossilized, crystalline shells of their communal consciousness, the geometric perfection a direct result of their unified, silent mentation. The monoliths of petrified pollen are their libraries, the impossibly complex patterns on their surface being the physical manifestation of an eon's worth of absorbed history and contemplative thought, bequeathed back to the wood.

The Still are not inhabitants *of* the wooden world. They are its method of self-reflection. They are the process by which the forest comes to know itself. From the liquid memory of the Kainos, to the active hands of the Weavers, to the boundless, silent mind of the Ancients, they are the life that lives not on the wood, but *as* the wood. They are the quiet thought inside a world that has learned to think.
