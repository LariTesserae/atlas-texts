What lives here is an answer to a question no one remembers asking. They are the quiet third term in the equation of machine and vegetation. They are the reason for the profound absence of animals.

They do not have a name for themselves, for they have no language of discrete words. Their communication is the engine's own: a subtle shift in resonant frequency, a harmonizing with the thrum, a gesture read in the shimmer of heat-haze. To the builders, long-perished, they might have been designated 'Janitors' or 'Custodians.' Now, they are simply the Tenders.

To see one is to mistake it for the landscape. They are slow, impossibly so, their movements calibrated to the 90-minute cycle of the great pistons. You might watch a section of wall for an hour before realizing that the cluster of coppery vine-leaves and glowing fungus is not flora at all, but a Tender at rest, fused with its perch.

Their bodies are a perfect synthesis of their world. They are vaguely humanoid in silhouette, but unsettlingly tall and slender, built to navigate the labyrinth of pipes and catwalks. Their skin is not flesh, but a mosaic of articulated, thermosynthetic plates, identical to the leaves of the metallic kudzu. These plates, warm to the touch, shift and clatter with a sound like wind chimes made of shell casings, a faint, personal percussion against the monolithic chord of the engine. In the red glow of the core, they are the color of dried blood. In the cool light of the fungus, they are a muted, oily bronze.

Where their joints meet—at the elbow, the knee, the long, delicate neck—the copper plates give way to a fibrous, white material like the asbestos moss, constantly weeping a clear, hot fluid that is their blood and sweat, dripping to the floor to rejoin the great cycle of steam and condensation. Through this white fiber, and in swirling patterns across the copper plates, run webs of the bioplasmic fungus. They are not an infection; they are the Tender's nervous system made visible. At rest, the fungal light pulses in a slow, steady rhythm, a soft blue-green that mirrors the colonies on the walls. When the Tender is active, or focused, this light brightens and flows along its limbs in currents of cool fire. They have no eyes. The entire surface of their fungal network is a sensory organ, perceiving the world as a symphony of heat, vibration, and pressure. They do not see the red light and the blue light; they *feel* the core's inferno as a constant, nourishing sun and the fungal colonies as cool, silent constellations.

They are the tenders of a garden they did not plant. Their purpose is not maintenance in the way the builders understood it. They do not fix broken parts or replace worn gears. The machine is no longer a machine; it is a geological feature, a heat-source, a metal skeleton for life. The Tenders' role is to maintain its equilibrium.

You will not see one with a wrench, but you might see one poised for days by a colossal pipe, its long, three-fingered hands resting gently on the oily surface. It is not repairing the pipe; it is listening to it. It feels the vibration, the temperature, the flow of steam within. If a section runs too hot, threatening a rupture that could disrupt the balance for centuries, the Tender will act. It will not weld a patch. It will find a new, young tendril of the copper-kudzu and, over the course of weeks, gently guide it, wrapping it around the overheating section. The vine, a living heat-sink, will siphon off the excess energy, its leaves glowing a brighter red, its roots digging deeper into the metal, strengthening the weak point. It is not engineering; it is horticulture.

They are also the reason the amber light still blinks. One Tender is always stationed near the derelict control bank, a silent, unmoving sentinel. Its duty, passed down through a non-genetic, ritual inheritance, is to observe the light. The seven-second interval is a holy rhythm, a measure of time against which all other, slower cycles are judged. They do not know what it means, only that it *is*. It is the last word of their gods, and their vigil is an act of faith.

Any life that is not of the engine, the vine, or the fungus, is an intrusion, a dissonance. A rat, scuttling from some forgotten tunnel, would not last a minute. Its frantic, high-frequency movements, its alien warmth, its unfamiliar scent would register on the Tenders' awareness like a shriek in a library. The nearest Tender would unfold from the wall, its fungal webs flaring bright blue. It would not hunt the rat. It would simply move to block its path, its sheer alien presence and the waves of heat rolling off its body enough to corner the creature. It would then gently, patiently, lower a hand, not to crush, but to cover. The heat of its palm, designed to interface with the engine's skin, would be instantly lethal. The body would be dropped into a pool of water, to be broken down and reassimilated. The symphony would be restored.

They are not born. When a section of the engine-garden becomes imbalanced—a new leak creating a vast, untended curtain of moss, a fungal bloom growing unchecked—a new Tender will bud. From the dense matrix of vine roots and fungal mycelium, a knot will form. Over decades, it will draw energy from the core, shaping itself, plating itself in copper, growing a consciousness from the network. Eventually, it will detach, a new, slow-moving thought added to the mind of the cavern.

They are the inhabitants, but they are also the place itself, made ambulatory. They are the living interface between metal and cell, the guardians of a single, thrumming, eternal note. They are the dream the machine has of itself, a dream of copper leaves and cool blue light, sung in the dark for no one.
