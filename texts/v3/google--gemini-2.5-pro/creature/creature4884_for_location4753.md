Here live the Weft-kin.

They have the deep coloration of a river stone, smooth and dark and cool. Their skin is hairless, a mosaic of slate-greys, deep mossy greens, and the brown of wet mulch, pattered in a way that makes them disappear against the gnarled bark of the foundational pillars. In the shifting, dappled light, a faint, oily iridescence sheens on their surfaces, mimicking the play of light on a beetle's carapace or a slick, wet leaf. They are perpetually damp, their skin secreting a thin, clear mucilage that keeps the Weft's condensing humidity from leaching the life from their bodies. It gives them a constant gleam, as if they have just emerged from a deep pool. To see a Weft-kin drying out is to see a corpse in the making; their skin cracks like parched earth, the vibrant colours dulling to a lifeless ash.

Their bodies are built for the vertical world. Long of limb and slender of torso, they move with a disconcerting fluidity, a silent, controlled grace that is a stark counterpoint to the chaotic energy of their home. A Weft-kin in motion is a ripple in the overwhelming noise, a study in efficiency. Their hands and feet are long and dextrous, with an extra, opposable digit on their feet, and tipped not with claws but with tough, ridged pads of callous that can find purchase on the most treacherous, algae-slicked surfaces. Their most striking feature is a long, muscular, prehensile tail, as thick at the base as their arm and tapering to a fine, sensitive tip. It is their third hand, their anchor, their counterbalance, used to brace against a swaying vine or to delicately prune a shoot just out of arm's reach.

Their faces are narrow, their features subtle. They have no visible outer ear, only a smooth, spiraled hollow at the side of the head, covered by a thin membrane that vibrates to select frequencies. It allows them to filter the chaotic din of the Weft, to isolate the specific *snap* of a weakening branch, the tell-tale *scrape* of a destructive beetle colony, or the soft, sibilant call of a fellow kin. Their eyes are very large, dark, and seem to be composed almost entirely of pupil, designed to drink in the emerald twilight. A secondary, translucent lid sweeps across the eye horizontally, not to blink, but to wipe away the ever-present moisture. When they communicate, it is in two modes. Up close, it is a language of soft clicks, whistles, and whispery exhalations, a private soundscape carved out of the thunderous background. From a distance, they use their entire bodies, a silent, expressive sign language of hand gestures, body postures, and the articulate curl and lash of their tails, a dance of meaning easily seen across a vine-choked plaza.

The Weft-kin are not a people of stillness. Their existence is one of constant, frenetic motion, an endless dance of cultivation and repair. They are the nervous system of the Weft made manifest. They sleep in short, deep bursts, curled in hollows within the great wooden pillars or suspended in cocoon-like hammocks woven from living vine, often in groups, their bodies a warm, damp tangle of limbs and tails. But to be awake is to be working. Their society is not built on concepts of property or hierarchy, but of function and immediate need. The thought of "owning" a branch, a tool, or a space is absurd; the branch might be eaten tomorrow, the space overgrown. All is flux. Their community is a single, fluid organism responding to the greater organism they inhabit. A team of kin might spend a day weaving a new walkway, their movements so coordinated they seem pre-choreographed, only for another team to silently dismantle it a week later to harvest the swollen, edible fungus that has erupted along its length.

They are born gardeners, their knowledge symbiotic and ancient. They carry simple tools: razor-sharp flakes of obsidian for precise cuts, smoothed river stones for grinding pulps and salves, and elegant hooks of carved bone for pulling and guiding pliant growth. They can read the Weft's moods in the turgor of a leaf, the scent of a new blossom, the pitch of a buzzing insect. They are the city's frantic, tireless antibody, swarming to a point of damage—a beetle infestation, a wind-torn canopy—to mend and regrow. They are also its cultivators, grafting fruiting fungi onto structural limbs, training water-channeling grooves with painstaking patience, and weaving living screens of foliage for privacy and shelter.

Their psychology is a reflection of their world. They possess a deep, calm reverence for the foundational pillars—the "bones" of the Weft. This is the only permanence they know, a deep time they feel in their own bones. They might press a hand to the ancient, petrified bark in a gesture of respect, a silent communion with the ages. Yet, their conscious mind is a thing of the frantic present, utterly absorbed by the chaotic, metabolic rush of the upper levels. They are not sentimental. They don't mourn a collapsed pathway or a devoured harvest; they simply react, adapt, and build anew. Loss is an expected part of the process. Their joy is not in possession, but in participation: the taste of a sun-warmed fruit from a freshly grafted branch, the feeling of a newly woven vine holding strong underfoot, the shared rhythm of working together in the emerald twilight.

When a Weft-kin dies, they are not buried. Their body is carried to a special hollow, a place of decomposition known as a "Giving Cradle." Here, they are laid down, and the Weft that sustained them in life reclaims them in death. The fungi, the insects, the dripping water, and the deep humus break them down, returning their substance to the whole. To the Weft-kin, this is not an end, but a transformation. They become the nutrient that feeds the root, the strength that thickens the bark, the energy that fuels the next frantic, beautiful, chaotic explosion of life. They become The Weft itself.
