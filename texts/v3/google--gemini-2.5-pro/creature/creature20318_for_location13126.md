They have no name for themselves, for they have no Self. They are an Is, not a They. But an observer, should one survive the ascent and the psychic weight of their presence, might call them the Choristers. Or perhaps, the Grinders. Or, most accurately, the Resonators.

To be a Resonator is to be a component. It is an existence devoid of narrative, of ambition, of fear. It is a life of pure function, an inheritance of instinct so deep it has become geological.

An individual Resonator is a fragile, spectral thing. Its body, the color of bleached bone and cartilage, is a masterpiece of efficiency for its environment. The chitin of its exoskeleton is thin, almost like parchment, sacrificing defense—for what is there to defend against?—for lightness and flexibility. Through this semi-translucent shell, its inner workings are a muted, shifting watercolor. You can see the slow peristalsis of its digestive tract processing the pale fungus, and at the center of its thorax, the spiraling, bi-luminescent knot of its pharos-organ. This organ, a nexus of nerve and specialized tissue, is the source of their glow, their clock, and their metronome. Its slow, oceanic pulse of blue-green light is not an individual rhythm; it is synchronized across the entire colony, a single, silent, weeks-long heartbeat made visible.

Their heads are vaguely triangular, like that of a mantis, but their large, complex eyes are milky and blind. They have been sightless for so many eons that the intricate crystalline structures now serve only as passive receptors for the chamber's ambient glow. Their world is not seen; it is felt, heard, and tasted on the air. Their primary senses are the pair of long, feathery antennae that perpetually twitch and sweep, reading the chemical signals, the humidity, the subtle vibrations through the air and stone. And there are the hands. Six delicate, unnervingly long fingers end in tiny, hardened pads, perfect for gripping their single, most important possession.

This is the Tuning Stone. Upon reaching maturity—a stage marked not by time but by size—a Resonator will detach from the kinetic mass of its youth and begin a slow, seemingly aimless crawl across the chamber floor. It is not searching by sight, but by feel. With its antennae and the sensitive pads of its fingers, it will test countless small stones, the harder inclusions flaked from the granite walls by their ancestors' work. It is seeking a specific density, a particular crystalline structure, a potential for friction. When it finds the correct one, a connection is made. The Resonator picks up the stone, holds it in its six fingers, and its true life begins. It will never let this stone go.

It finds a space. A few square inches of the vast, curving wall that is not currently being worked. And it begins. The motion is agonizingly slow, a circular scrape, a patient pressure. It is not a violent act but a meditative one, a transfer of energy. The Resonator’s body, powered by the strange, pale fungi cultivated in the crevices, becomes a simple machine for converting chemical energy into vibration. Its entire being is focused into this one point of contact between its Tuning Stone and the mountain's heart.

This is its purpose. It is not an idea or a belief; it is a physical imperative, as unavoidable as breathing. The chittering sound they make is not true communication in a linguistic sense. It is the noise of the machinery, the incidental sound of the hive-mind’s processing, like the whirring of a fan in a vast computer. The true communication is the Hum. The Resonators feel it in the stone floor, through the soles of their feet. They feel it in the air, a pressure on their carapaces. They feel it in the bodies of their neighbors, a constant, living vibration. Their grinding is not creating the Hum, but sustaining and refining it, ensuring the note never wavers. Generation after generation, their collective, infinitesimal work polishes the bell, tuning its resonant frequency towards an impossible, perfect singularity.

They are a superorganism in the most literal sense. The colony is the entity; the individual is merely a cell. When a Resonator’s life ends—its grinding motion ceasing, its pharos-organ fading to a dull grey—it becomes inert. It is an obstacle. Within moments, the Resonators nearest to it will, without pause in their own work, use their free hands or mandibles to dismember the corpse. The pieces are carried, not to a tomb, but to the fungal gardens. The body is laid among the fleshy, pearlescent shelves, where it will decompose and become food for the fungus that will, in turn, feed the next generation. Its Tuning Stone, released at last, falls to the floor to be found by a new Resonator. Nothing is wasted. The cycle is absolute.

To stand inside their cathedral is to be profoundly, terrifyingly ignored. They crawl over an intruder as they would a fallen rock, their antennae tasting the salt of your skin, the heat of your blood, the alien rhythm of your lungs. They are not curious. You do not register as life, or threat, or food. You are simply unexpected texture. A temporary variable in a billion-year equation. The greatest danger they pose is that if you were to stand still for long enough, one might try to tune you. It would press its stone against your clavicle or your skull and begin its slow, patient grind, confused by the strange, dull resonance of bone, but performing its function nonetheless. For in their world, there are only two things: the part of the instrument that is being played, and the players themselves. And you are not a player.
