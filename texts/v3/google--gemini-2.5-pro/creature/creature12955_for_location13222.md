The people who live in Anlac are called the Anlacti, though they rarely use the term themselves. They are simply the riggers, the surveyors, the harvesters, the moss-binders. To call yourself an Anlacti is to state the obvious, like a fish announcing it lives in water. They are a people shaped by silence and motion, existing in a state of perpetual, quiet flux.

Physically, the Anlacti are wiry and dense with muscle, built for leverage and endurance rather than brute force. They move with a low center of gravity and a certainty of foot that comes from a lifetime of walking on unsettled ground. Their boots are thick-soled and padded, not just for grip but to insulate them from the constant, low-frequency vibration of the city. Years of this thrumming resonance live in their bones; they can feel a distant flow-event through the soles of their feet long before any sensor registers it, an instinctual tremor that tells them the world is about to shift. This "ground-sense" is their most vital organ. An Anlacti who loses it is considered deaf, blind, and in terrible danger.

Their faces are often pale from the diffuse, shadowless light, a pallor that makes the fine, dark dust of the Accretion stand out starkly in the lines around their eyes and mouths. Many wear simple filter-masks hanging around their necks, pulling them up not out of habit, but in response to the thickness of the air on a given day. Their eyes are their most expressive feature, necessarily so. They are wide and quick, accustomed to scanning for the subtle sheen of a growing crystal vein or the almost imperceptible bulge in a street-plate that signals an imminent upwelling. Their gaze seems to measure weight and stress in everything it falls upon.

In the profound, sound-swallowing quiet of the basin, the Anlacti have developed a language of proximity. They do not shout. To raise one's voice is a sign of panic or madness, a useless spray of energy into the heavy air. They speak in low tones, standing close, often with a hand on the other's arm, as much to feel the vibration of their words as to hear them. This gives their interactions an Earthen-surface intimacy that is entirely pragmatic. Public discourse is a collection of tight knots of people, murmuring. For distance, they rely on a fluid, complex sign language—a "Hand-Sign" that is all flashing fingers and precise gestures, a silent grammar understood by every citizen from childhood. You can watch a rigger foreman on one hab-stack direct a crane operator a thousand meters away with a silent, elegant ballet of hand signals that is as detailed as a spoken paragraph.

They are a people without heirlooms. They own nothing they cannot carry in a single trip. Sentimentality is a fatal flaw. A child’s first toy is not a doll but a miniature, working hydraulic jack. Their most treasured possessions are their tools: a perfectly balanced multi-wrench, a personal strata-sonde, a durable set of cutting lenses. These are often the only things that pass from one generation to the next. Their homes, the modular hab-units, are functional, impersonal pods, their interiors organized with a sailor's efficiency. Decoration is seen in the quality of a weld, the elegance of a reinforced joint, or the careful cultivation of a patch of rust-colored moss in a window box, its rhizoids helping to deaden vibrations.

An Anlacti elder is not someone with frail bones, but someone whose ground-sense has become preternaturally acute. The old do not retire; they become the "Seers" or "Still-Watchers." They spend their days on the highest observation decks, their feet planted firmly on the metal, their eyes half-closed. They are not watching, but feeling. They predict the major flows, map the subterranean pressures, and sense the "dead zones" falling quiet or, more dangerously, waking up. Their wisdom is the city’s long-term survival plan.

The children of Anlac learn to walk on shifting skid-platforms. Their games are "Tower-Stack" and "Flow-Dodge." A rite of passage is not a birthday or a religious ceremony, but the first time a youth serves on a "shift-crew" during a flow-event, their small hands helping to lock down a swaying pipeline or guide a hab-unit onto its new pylons. They don't fear the ground; they view it with a kind of competitive reverence. It is the opponent in a city-wide game, a partner in a dance, a field to be plowed.

They have an ailment they call "Still-Sickness," a profound anxiety and disorientation that afflicts those who spend too long in the rare, truly stable dead zones, or who leave Anlac for the outside world. They become agitated by the lack of the familiar, bone-deep hum of the Accretion, unnerved by the permanence of a world that does not breathe beneath their feet.

They are not miners in their own minds. They are more like midwives. They assist in the birth of the world, and their payment is the strange, valuable material this genesis leaves behind. They live in symbiosis with the planet's creation, their quiet, pragmatic, and ceaseless labor the only thing that allows a human city to persist on the surface of an unfinished earth. They are the silent, symbiotic intelligence of the Choked City, as much a part of its strange ecosystem as the cable-vines and the six-legged scavengers.
