What lives here does not arrive; it resolves.

You have been watching a particular expanse of moss between two of the smaller, hut-sized stones for an hour, your senses lulled by the unchanging hum and the pearlescent light. You look away for a moment, tracing the slow drip of water down a black, weeping face. When you look back, it is there. It did not walk into the space. It simply became distinct from it, as if your eyes have finally learned the language of the place and can now distinguish this second, deeper layer of existence.

It is a Weeper.

Its form is one of profound and unsettling simplicity. It is tall and slender, perhaps twice the height of a person, but its posture is a permanent, patient stoop, a bow of listening. Its body has the same smooth, rounded, and water-polished quality as the monoliths. Its skin is not skin, but a semi-porous, basalt-black integument, slick and glistening with the same perpetual dew that coats the stones. It is faceless. The head is a smooth, dark ovoid, like a perfect river stone,featureless save for two shallow, concave depressions where eyes might be. These depressions do not gather light; they gather resonance. It does not see you. It feels the minor dissonance your solid, water-proof body creates in the basin's perfect chord.

It moves with a slowness that redefines the word. Each motion is a study in millennia. A leg lifts, water streaming from it, and for a full minute it hangs in the air, a dark pendulum in the mist. When it sets its wide, soft foot down, it happens with the silent deliberation of a root finding its hold. Its passage leaves no trace on the moss, for it is as much a part of the water-saturated world as the ground itself. Water is drawn up through the porous soles of its feet, circulating through the capillary network of its body, and seeping out from every point on its surface. The Weeper is a walking, living monolith, a smaller, mobile participant in the basin's great song. Its own body hums, a quiet, cellular counterpoint to the deep thrum of the stones.

For long periods, it is utterly still, a black statue amid the emerald moss. In this stillness, it is listening. It is not listening for sound, but *to* sound. It is monitoring the health of the basin, the pitch of each stone, the rhythm of the water's flow. Its entire existence is one of custodial attention.

Then, it acts. Its long, slender arm, ending in fingers that are not flesh but tapered spars of the same vitreous black stone as the scattered shards, reaches for the base of a monolith. One of its fingers, impossibly sharp, touches the stone. It begins to scrape. It is not a violent or hurried motion. It is a slow, methodical tracing, removing a fine powder of mineral accretion that has begun to clog the stone's microscopic pores. It is cleaning the stone's throat. As it works, the pitch of the monolith's hum shifts, infinitesimally to a human ear, but to the Weeper, it is a note brought back into perfect harmony. It is a tuner of mountains.

It tends the glass-frond ferns as well. Sometimes it will stand beneath one for days, its head tilted, feeling the stress-vibrations in the fern's crystalline stalk. It might then select a sharp-edged flake of weeping stone from the moss and, with the same geological patience, score a line across a single, colossal frond. The cut is molecularly precise. It is not an act of destruction, but of pruning. Over the next week, the frond will slowly yellow along that line, its structure failing with graceful deliberation until it detaches and falls, becoming nutrient and water for the moss below. The Weeper ensures the light is never too blocked, the flow of water never too impeded.

It has no culture, no language, no art. The basin is its temple, its instrument, and its body. It does not reproduce in a biological sense. When a great stone becomes so ancient that its song grows erratic and fractured, a piece of it may fall away—not a dead shard, but a node of living resonance. This node will lie in the moss for a century, gathering water, slowly shaping itself into the stooped and patient form of a new Weeper, which will then rise and begin its endless, silent work of listening and tuning.

They do not die. They erode. Over eons, the constant passage of water through their bodies wears them down. They grow thinner, more translucent, their hum fainter. Eventually, a Weeper will simply dissolve back into the landscape, its final notes merging with the great, thousand-year chord, leaving behind nothing but a handful of black, sharp-edged tools for its successor, and a silence that sounds exactly like everything else.
