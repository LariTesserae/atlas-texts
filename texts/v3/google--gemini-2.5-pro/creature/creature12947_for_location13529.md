While the Harvesters are the planet's metabolism, they are not its only inhabitants. Their existence, a constant process of deconstruction, creates the niches for a second, and even a third, order of life. These are beings not of flesh, but of mineral and process, each one an echo of the world's fundamental nature.

On the vast, continent-sized carapaces of the Harvesters live the **Geometers**.

If a Harvester is a living mountain, the Geometers are the intricate, self-assembling instruments that tend to it. They are not parasites; their relationship is more akin to that of a coral polyp to the reef it builds. They are an extension of the Harvester’s own process, but with a different mandate. Where the Harvester deconstructs, the Geometers construct.

They appear as spindly, multi-limbed beings, ranging from the size of a human hand to that of a small car. Their bodies are woven from filaments of conductive metals—platinum, gold, and other rare elements—drawn directly from the Harvester's internal, pre-sorted streams. They move like mechanical spiders, their legs ending not in feet but in sensitive, resonant forks. They do not see with light; they "see" by thrumming their forks against the Harvester's shell, reading the subtle vibrations of the colossal sorting process occurring kilometers below them. They feel the flow of nearly pure iron, the slow pulse of silicon, the deep hum of carbon being separated. They exist in a sensory world of geologic frequencies.

A Geometer's life is one of ceaseless, intricate work. They tap into the Harvester's elemental streams through microscopic pores, siphoning off minute quantities of purified matter. This matter is not for sustenance in a way we would understand it, but for creation. They are artisans. On the dark, obsidian plains of the Harvesters' backs, they build.

They erect astonishingly complex structures: delicate, miles-long lattices that catch the faint atmospheric light; towering, needle-thin spires that vibrate with the Harvester's internal hum; intricate, clockwork-like arrays that slowly re-orient themselves in response to the planet's faint magnetic field. These are not cities or homes. They are expressions. They are a form of mineral thought given physical form. The back of an ancient Harvester is a forest of these silent, metallic sculptures, a garden of pure geometry growing on the skin of a moving god. Reproduction for the Geometers is a function of their art. When one of their creations reaches a certain resonant complexity, it sheds a "seed"—a small, tightly-wound knot of metallic filaments that unfurls, tests its connection to the Harvester's hum, and begins to build.

In the vast, sorted plains left in the Harvesters' wake, another form of life exists: the **Sifters**.

They are not solid beings. A Sifter is a colonial intelligence, a cohesive but amorphous cloud of magnetised dust particles. From a distance, they might be mistaken for a patch of denser ground fog, a slow-moving stain on the landscape. Up close, they have a shimmering, fluid quality, like a shoal of microscopic fish made of iron filings. They have no limbs, no organs, no centre. They are a distributed consciousness, their form held together by a complex interplay of internal magnetic fields.

The Sifters are the world's refiners. The Harvesters sort on a massive scale, leaving behind strata of elements that are pure by industrial standards, but not by cosmic ones. The Sifters move slowly across these plains, particularly along the boundaries where one element gives way to another—the grey edge of iron oxide bleeding into the paler grey of silica. They flow *through* the dust, their collective magnetic field attracting and binding specific trace elements the Harvesters deemed insignificant. They are gleaners, perfectionists. A Sifter might spend a century moving along a single elemental border, patiently extracting a few hundred kilograms of germanium or cobalt from a trillion tons of iron dust.

They are utterly silent, their passage marked only by a faint disturbance in the dust and a slight sharpening of the color gradient in their wake. When two Sifters meet, they do not fight or communicate in any overt way. They simply merge, their collective fields overlapping and exchanging information—a shared "memory" of mineral concentrations—before peeling away from one another, subtly changed, to continue their endless, fastidious work.

Finally, in the shallow, hydrocarbon pools that collect in the Harvesters' tracks, there is the most abstract life of all: the **Lacunae**.

These are not creatures; they are self-sustaining, self-organizing chemical reactions. In the cold, inert liquid, which is a complex soup of water and long-chain hydrocarbons, the Lacunae manifest as slow-moving vortices of shimmering iridescence. They are patterns that think. They possess no solid form, existing only as a localized, temporary defiance of entropy.

Their "metabolism" is the slow catalysis of the hydrocarbons in the pool. They break down the complex molecules vented by the Harvesters into simpler, more stable compounds, releasing faint whispers of energy that sustain their own intricate, swirling patterns. To observe a Lacuna is to watch a slow, silent ballet of chemical symmetries forming and dissolving. A single Lacuna might be a meter across, a delicate, rotating mandala of liquid crystal, and a pool might contain dozens of them, drifting and interacting like clouds. Their "lifespan" is the lifespan of their pool. As the inert water slowly evaporates over millennia, or if a Harvester's path returns to the same location, the pool is drained, and the Lacunae simply... cease. Their work is done. The final, most subtle stage of the planet's processing is complete. Their existence is a fleeting, beautiful calculation in a forgotten chemical sea.
