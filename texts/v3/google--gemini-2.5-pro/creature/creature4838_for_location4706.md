They are the Still.

They are not named by themselves, for they have no spoken language and no one else to speak of. The name is an observer's guess, an attempt to capture the defining quality of a people who have found a centre of absolute calm within a universe of deafening motion.

To see one is a rare and startling event, for they are beings of the low places, shaped by pressure and patience. They are humanoid, but only in the way a river stone is still a stone. They stand no more than four feet high, but they are immensely broad, with a low-slung, heavy pelvis and thick, columnar legs that end in wide, splayed feet. Their posture is a permanent semi-crouch, a posture of submission to the wind that is, in fact, the secret to their defiance of it. To stand upright would be an act of suicidal ego. Their entire form is a study in aerodynamics and ballast. They are heavy, dense-boned, and carry their weight in their hips and thighs, an anchor against the gale.

Their skin is not uniform. On their backs, shoulders, and the crowns of their heads—the surfaces most presented to the endless horizontal flow—it has thickened into a pale, greyish carapace, smooth and seamless like the polished basalt of the mountain-domes. It is cool to the touch and possesses a faint, waxy lustre, grown not from keratin but from a silica-rich biology, a living shield of stone. On their undersides and inner limbs, the skin is softer, paler, almost translucent, crisscrossed with a network of fine blue veins. It is here, pressed against the damp Shuddering Green, that their bodies breathe and absorb moisture. They wear no clothes, for clothes would be flayed from their bodies in moments. Their skin is their only garment, a perfect, living union of armor and interface.

The face of a Still is what truly marks them. It is wide and flat, with a broad, low nose that is little more than two crescent-shaped slits that can be squeezed shut. Their mouths are wide but thin-lipped, rarely opened. But it is the absence of ears that is most profound. There are no external structures, no pinnae, no lobes—nothing to catch and shred the chaotic wind. Where an ear would be, there is only a smooth, uninterrupted curve of bone and carapace. They do not hear as we do. The roar of the wind is not an auditory event processed by a delicate cochlea; that organ would have been destroyed generations ago. For the Still, sound is a tactile, total-body experience. They *feel* it as a constant, grinding vibration through their dense skeletons. The hum in the jaw is their baseline reality. Their entire being is a resonating chamber.

Their eyes are deep-set beneath a heavy, bony brow, and they are small and dark. But across them, blinking with a slow, reptilian rhythm, slides a second, milky eyelid. A nictitating membrane that is their constant shield against the high-velocity mist and microscopic grit of the air. They see the world through this perpetual, translucent filter, a world of softened shapes and blurred distances, where the shivering of the Green is more felt than seen.

A Still lives a life of profound and patient solitude. They might be found near a Howling Pit, their temple and their tool. For hours, they will sit motionless at the centre of the pit, legs crossed, thick-fingered hands resting on their knees. Here, embraced by the coherent, pure note of the focused wind, their faces achieve an expression of ecstatic release. This is not meditation as an escape from the world, but a direct and holy communion with its fundamental voice. The chaotic, grinding vibration that rattles their bones across the plateau is focused here into a single, clean frequency. In the Pit, their skeleton does not shudder; it sings. This is their form of thought, of prayer, of art. The chaotic noise of the world is madness; the pure tone of the Pit is sanity, consciousness itself.

They travel, but it is a slow and deliberate pilgrimage. One might walk for weeks to visit a distant Pit, or to find a new vein of stone suitable for a knife. They move with a low, rolling gait, their body a single, smooth muscle against the wind. When two Still meet, a rare and momentous occasion, they do not speak. They approach slowly and, with great solemnity, press their palms together. They stand like this for a time, eyes closed, feeling the shared, synchronous vibration of their skeletons, the song of the wind passing through them both. It is a communication more intimate than any word, a confirmation that another being shares their unique, resonant existence.

They are herbivores, their diet consisting of the tough, succulent leaves of the Shuddering Green. They use simple, palm-sized stones, worn smooth into shallow bowls, to grind the leaves into a palatable mash, using a smaller, egg-shaped stone as a pestle. Their artifacts are few and precious, worn down by use and the polishing wind until they seem like natural formations. A knife is a treasure, its edge a lifetime's work of patient sharpening against finer stones.

They are born, they live, they find clarity in the Pits, and they die. They have no concept of building, for anything built is an affront to the wind and will be erased. Their only construction is a hole in the ground that celebrates the very force that defines them. When a Still's life ends, their body is carried by a partner or a child to an open expanse of the Green. They are laid upon the living carpet, and the wind, which shaped their entire life, begins the slow, patient process of reclaiming them. It scours the soft flesh, polishes the bone, and over years, seasons, and ages, returns them molecule by molecule to the world, until nothing is left but a faint, calcium-rich patch in the soil, from which the Shuddering Green grows just a little more vibrantly. They do not fight the wind; they are a vessel for its voice, a temporary knot of stillness in its eternal flow.
