In this world of mud and sky, there live the Skimmers. Some call them the Mud-Saints.

They are the dialectic of this place, the answer to the paradox of frantic sound and geological stillness. They are creatures of the air, the one part of the world that seems empty. They do not belong to the mud or the water, but they are utterly dependent upon it.

To see one is to witness a contradiction. Its body is a long, slender shard of what looks like polished, unbleumatized bone, or perhaps sun-bleached porcelain. It is shaped like a flattened spindle, about the length of a human arm, tapering to a fine point at either end. There is no discernible head, no obvious sensory organs from a distance. From this smooth, hard body unfurl two pairs of wings. They are not feathered or leathery, but are membranes of an almost-impossible thinness, like a sheet of oil on water given form. They are transparent, and in the bright, hazy light, you do not see the wings themselves so much as the distortion of the world behind them—the heat-shimmer of their passage.

They move in a way that is not quite flight. The air is so thick and heavy that they seem to rest upon it. With slow, rhythmic pulses of their diaphanous wings, they row through the humidity, propelling themselves in long, effortless glides a few meters above the glistening mud. They are called Skimmers because that is what they do: they skim the boundary layer between the wet earth and the wet air.

Hanging from the underside of their bone-white bodies are three legs, long and impossibly thin, like the glassy reed-thorns that dot the landscape. They are made of the same amber, silica-like material, rigid and internally ribbed. The Skimmers do not walk on these legs. The legs hang straight down as they glide, their tips hovering centimeters above the muck. They are not for locomotion; they are for hunting, and for listening.

The Skimmer is the apex predator of this simple food web, and its consciousness is built from sound. The featureless white body possesses no eyes. It navigates the world through the overwhelming sonic tapestry. The constant static of the billion clickers is not noise to it; it is a high-resolution map, a constant update of the terrain and the life within it. Each *tick* is a pixel of information. The deep, resonant booms of the bellowers are its landmarks, its cardinal directions, the slow, rhythmic sonar pings by which it orients itself across the vast, featureless flats.

It hunts with an unnerving patience. It will glide for hours, its three glassy legs extended, listening. It is processing the cacophony, filtering the signal from the noise, isolating the specific sequence of clicks from a single crab-like organism just below the surface. When it has fixed on its prey, its movement changes. The slow rowing of its wings ceases. It holds itself perfectly still in the thick air, a suspended needle of white. It is judging depth, density, and the faint electrical field of the creature's life.

Then, it strikes. It is the only truly fast thing in this world. It drops not with a dive or a flutter, but as if a cord has been cut. It falls vertically, and its central leg, a perfect spear, punches through the surface of the mud. There is no splash, only a sharp, brittle *crack* as the silica tip pierces the clicker's iridescent carapace, followed by a wet, definitive *pop*. The sound is instantly swallowed by the greater chorus. The Skimmer then rises, just as swiftly, a slight tremor in its wings. It retracts the leg, drawing the impaled clicker up into an orifice on its underside, and resumes its slow, patient glide.

This is the life of the Skimmer. But it is not the life of the Mud-Saint.

For hours, or for days, a Skimmer will hunt, a ghost of purpose drifting over the flats. But eventually, it will seek out one of the great, worn domes of primordial clay, the oldest parts of the world. It will ascend the gentle slope, its wings beating a little harder in the slightly thinner air, and it will come to rest on the summit.

Here, it enters its other state. It settles, and its three legs, which moments ago were instruments of death, now serve a different function. It drives them deep into the ancient, damp clay, anchoring itself to the very foundation of the world. Its wings fold tightly against its body, becoming almost invisible. Its bright, porcelain-like sheen dulls to the color of wet ash. It becomes indistinguishable from a strange, tripodal reed-thorn. It becomes still.

And in this stillness, it listens.

It is here that it becomes a Mud-Saint. It is no longer hunting for the sound of prey. It is absorbing the totality of the world's voice. Its whole being, a resonant, hollow chamber, becomes an antenna for existence. It listens to the patter of the afternoon rain across the whole plain. It feels the deep, infrasonic thrum of the bellowers not as individual calls, but as the slow, collective heartbeat of the basin. It registers the static of the clickers not as a map, but as the fizz of life itself, the sound of a billion tiny mineral engines turning over.

But it hears more. Anchored to the oldest earth, its glassy legs become geological sensors. It hears the slow, inexorable creep of the silt. It hears the sound of water percolating through strata of clay laid down a million years ago. It hears the faint, deep groan of the planet's crust settling into its final, most stable form. It is a consciousness defined not by thought or action, but by perfect, total reception. It is a living seismograph for a world that has mostly stopped shaking. It does not build, or speak, or dream. It *attends*.

These are the Mud-Saints. They are the nervous system of the sleeping leviathan. They are the point where the frantic, fleeting noise of life meets the immense, silent weight of time. And as the diffuse sun wheels through the white sky, they stand in their silent trances upon the earth's oldest bones, bearing witness, their entire being a quiet vessel for the deafening song of their finished world.
