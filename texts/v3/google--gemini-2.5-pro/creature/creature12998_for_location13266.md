The assumption that the world is uninhabited is correct, but incomplete. It is uninhabited by anything that is *separate* from it. You are right: there is no ghost in this machine. There are only the machine's own, transient antibodies; its living, mobile tools. They are the closest this world comes to having occupants, and to mistake them for life in any familiar sense would be a grave error.

They have no collective name, for there is no one to name them. They do not have species, for they are not born of lineages. They are *precipitated*.

You see them first not as a creature, but as a change in the texture of the ever-present vibration under your feet. Where you feel the steady, bass thrum of the world's deep engines, a new frequency will appear—a high, stressed whine, like a bearing about to fail. Look toward the source of that sound. Watch a section of the purple-green substrate.

A shiver will run through it. The interwoven cellulose will begin to writhe, not with growth, but with organization. Fibers will pull taut, thicken, and braid themselves into cords. Mycelial threads, the world's nerves, will swarm the area, their faint blue light intensifying as they form a concentrated network. A bulge appears. It is a knot of potential, a temporary convergence of the world's resources and will.

From this knot, something will emerge. It does not crawl out; it is extruded. If the need is for demolition—if an ancient, failing archway needs to be brought down before it collapses unpredictably—what emerges is a thing of brutalist function. It might be a dozen feet tall, a tripod of thick, lignin-armored legs that hammer into the ground, anchoring it. Its body is a dense cudgel of petrified wood, and its "head" is a battering ram, a calcium-hardened burl. It will move with a crushing, mindless rhythm, slamming itself against the weak points of the structure, guided by the vibrations of stress fractures it senses through its feet. It feels no pain, no exhaustion. It is a living sledgehammer. When the arch finally crumbles into steaming mulch, the creature simply… stops. Its legs unlock. Its body goes slack. And over the course of an hour, it deliquesces, its complex form dissolving back into the pulpy substrate, its resources reabsorbed, its purpose fulfilled.

If the need is more delicate—a leak in one of the great, pulsing pipelines—what condenses from the substrate is entirely different. It will be smaller, perhaps no larger than a person. Its form will be spindly, almost skeletal. It will be a thing of long, many-jointed limbs and fine, tendril-like manipulators. It will move with an unnerving, insectile precision, its feet—pads of sensitive, adhesive tissue—finding purchase on the slick, curved surface of the pipeline. It approaches the leak, the drip-drip-drip of precious, sweet fluid. It does not see the leak; it *feels* it as a drop in pressure, a chemical anomaly in the air. Its manipulators, fine as spun glass, will begin to work. One will exude a fast-acting solvent to clean the area. Another will weave a fresh patch of cellulose fibers, pulling them directly from its own body mass. A third will secrete a resin, a biological epoxy that hardens into a seal, tinged with the same tarnished copper as the veins in the ground. The work is silent, efficient, and utterly devoid of thought. Once the pressure is equalized and the chemical signature returns to normal, this technician, too, will dissolve. Its brief, specialized existence is over.

These are the world's primary inhabitants: temporary, purpose-built automata, condensed from the very fabric of the planet to serve as its maintenance crew. They are born of the spore-vents. The clouds of shimmering particles are not merely for propagation; they are a haze of pluripotent stem cells, a biological aerosol that settles everywhere. When the world's network of mycelial nerves detects a need—a pressure drop, a structural failure, a chemical imbalance—it sends a signal. This signal triggers the spores in that location to activate, drawing matter and energy from the substrate to assemble a creature perfectly designed for the task at hand. They are the world's hands, its teeth, its scalpels.

They never interact. A demolition brute and a repair technician could pass within inches of each other and would not register the other's existence. They are on different frequencies, programmed for different tasks. They have no society, no history, no hunger, no fear. Their only drive is the imperative encoded into their brief assembly.

And yet.

The system is not perfect. The chaos that is its equilibrium sometimes produces a flaw, a ghost note in the symphony of function. Very rarely, a creature will be formed that has no immediate purpose. Or, more commonly, a creature whose purpose is complete will fail to completely dissolve. A trace remains.

These are the Wanderers.

They are degraded, incomplete versions of the functional beings. A demolition brute might lose its mass and density, leaving a tall, gaunt form that strides aimlessly across the plains, its battering-ram head now a hollow, useless casque. A repair technician might lose its specialized tools, its fine manipulators fraying into listless, trailing threads.

These Wanderers are the source of the rumor of fauna. They are no more sentient than the tools they once were, but they are untethered from purpose. They are motes of memory in the world's mind. They move in feedback loops, tracing the paths of their original tasks. A skeletal brute might walk a slow, endless circle around the base of a smokestack it once helped to build. A frayed technician might endlessly stroke a perfectly sealed joint on a pipeline, its movements a faint echo of a job long since finished.

They are the closest thing this world has to ghosts, and they are why the silence feels so heavy. They are the evidence that there is a difference between a process and a memory. They are the lonely, walking, disassembling monuments to a million forgotten tasks, the only beings here that have a past, and the only tragedy in a world that is otherwise too perfect, too functional, to know suffering. They are what lives here, in the brief, beautiful, and utterly pointless interval between function and decay.
