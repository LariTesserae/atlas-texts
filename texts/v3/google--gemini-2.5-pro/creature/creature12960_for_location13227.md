They who live here are of the basin, born of its damp quiet and its patient, grinding time. To call them "people" is a convenience of language; they are the city's living ganglia, the conscious part of its slow, metabolic process.

They are the Tenders.

One emerges from the gloom between two weeping bronze towers, and what you see first is not a face, but a texture. Their skin is smooth and cool to the touch, with a pale, greyish-green cast like the underside of a fungus stalk. It is not slick with water so much as it is inherently non-porous, a surface that encourages the atmospheric damp to bead and run off rather than soak in. Across their shoulders, down their spines, and along their outer limbs, this skin is often mottled with patterns of deep maroon and ochre, a dappling that so perfectly mimics the ancient moss-carpets that a still Tender can seem to dissolve into the landscape. They are hairless, and their forms are lean, economical. There is no surplus to them, no flesh held for vanity or abundance; every muscle is a dense cord of necessity, adapted for slow, deliberate climbing and for maintaining balance on slick, narrow walkways.

Their movements are the antithesis of haste. They walk with a low, shuffling gait, their feet planted flat and wide. Their boots are not heavy, but have soft, thick soles of a felted, fibrous material that grips the damp ceramic and metal. They do not need to see the ground to know it; they feel it. The constant, low-frequency hum of the city's atmospheric pumps and electrochemical vats is their sixth sense. It travels through the soles of their feet, up their dense bones, and resonates in their chests. Through this vibration, they read the city as a living text: the change in rhythm that signals a blockage in a master condensate pipe three levels down, the subtle shudder of a quarrying machine reaching a new stratum of silicified moss, the approaching footsteps of another Tender on the same walkway, long before they could be seen or heard.

Their faces are broad and placid, their features subtly flattened as if by a constant, gentle pressure. Their mouths are wide and thin-lipped, rarely used for shouting; speech is a low, humming murmur, meant only for those within the immediate pool of one's lamplight. Their most striking feature is their eyes. They are large and dark, the pupils permanently wide to swallow every stray photon in the perpetual twilight. They possess a profound, liquid depth, but they are not the primary organ of perception. A Tender will often tilt their head, pressing a cheek against a wall or laying a hand flat on a railing, to better feel the city's thrumming life, their dark eyes unfocused and placid.

They are born into a purpose that is as much a part of them as their own bones. There are families of Pipe-Welders, generations who know the unique resonance of every joint and flow-valve; there are the Quarry-Scribes, who carve the living moss-stone in silent, methodical patterns that will not destabilize the mats; there are Condensate Chanters, whose low, harmonic humming is believed to quiet the molecular turbulence within the collection vats, improving the purity of the yield. These are not jobs; they are states of being. They wear clothing of the same waxed, heavy canvas described, not for warmth—their metabolism is slow and cool—but as a second skin against the constant drip and the abrasive surfaces of their vertical world. The tools they carry are ancient, works of art in their own right, with handles of polished, petrified moss-root and heads of dark, non-corroding bronze, passed down through generations until they are worn into perfect ergonomic extensions of the hand.

They have no concept of day or night, only of the city's cycles. The time for the great quarterly cleaning of the Condensation Fins is a season. The slow, decade-long advance of a quarry face is an Age. Their lives are long, measured in centuries, and their temperament reflects this. They are possessed of a profound, geological patience. Anger is a fleeting, inefficient heat; joy is a quiet, deep resonance, a sense of rightness with the city's hum. They huddle not for warmth, but for communion, their small pools of lamplight overlapping in a corridor to create a temporary, larger island of society.

When a Tender's life ends, they do not leave the system. There are no graves, no tombs. They are given back to the city they served. Their bodies are taken to the deepest, warmest chambers of the industrial core. Here, through a final, sacred electrochemical process, they are rendered down. The water of their bodies is returned to the condensate cycle. The minerals of their bones and flesh, rich with a lifetime of breathing the basin's air, are added as a vital catalyst to the vats that process the quarried moss. They become part of the nutrient slurry that may, in time, be used to cultivate new strains of bioluminescent fungi, or be returned to the great moss-mats themselves. They dissolve back into the hum. They are the city, and the city is them, a singular, symbiotic organism living in the final, quiet breath of a dying world, forever tending the slow, damp, and truly sacred work of existence.
