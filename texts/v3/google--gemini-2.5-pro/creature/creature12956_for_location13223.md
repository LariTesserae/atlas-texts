The inhabitants are the Stanchioned. They are not born of this place, not truly, though it has been so many generations since their arrival that the memory of sky and sun is less than a myth; it is a neurological artifact, a phantom limb of the soul. They have been shaped by the Churn as thoroughly as the mountains they cling to.

Physically, they are dense. Generations of pressure have favored a specific morphology: short, wide-set bodies with thick, calcified bones and ropey, powerful muscle. They are built low to the ground they will never stand on, their center of gravity a point of internal focus against the endless push of the water. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, often with a faint, fish-belly luminescence of its own, a result of a symbiotic biofilm that offers a marginal resistance to the cold. Their eyes are large and dark, the pupils permanently dilated to drink in the shifting twilight of the deep. Many possess a nictitating membrane that slides across the eye instinctively, a milky-white shield against sudden flares of industrial light or the disorienting strobes of plankton blooms. Their hands are their most distinctive feature: broad, calloused, with an opposable thumb that seems double-jointed, evolved for an unbreakable grip on a tool, a railing, a safety line. A web of fine white scars covers their bodies, each a story of a slip, a near-miss, a moment the Churn almost claimed them.

They do not swim. To be in the open water without a Shell is a death sentence, delivered in moments. Their movements outside the pressurized habitats are a form of controlled falling, a brutalist ballet performed in heavy, articulated exoskeletons they call their Shells. These are not sleek suits of armor. They are hulking, crab-like machines, pocked with dents and scarred by abrasion, often passed down through generations. The joints scream in protest; the hydraulic lines weep fluid; the internal displays flicker and ghost. A Shell is an heirloom, a second body, a personal history worn externally. Its every repair patch is a memory, its every custom-welded tool mount a signature of its owner's trade and temperament. To be born is to be measured for your first, undersized training Shell; to die is to have your Shell stripped for parts, its most intact pieces grafted onto those of the living.

The Stanchioned mind has been rewired by the environment. They suffer from a collective form of agoraphobia—not of open spaces, but of stillness. A complete lack of motion, the absence of the Churn's deep groan, induces a vertiginous panic they call the "Stillness Sickness." Silence feels like suffocation. Their language is clipped and guttural, designed to be understood over the roar of water and machinery. But their non-verbal communication is a rich tapestry of subtle gestures, pressure shifts within a Shell’s grip, and a complex percussive language tapped out on pipes and bulkheads.

Their hearing is their primary sense. They can distinguish the growl of a failing bearing in Turbine Seven from the resonant song of a passing leviathan a mile away. The Churn is their weather, and they are its most intimate meteorologists. They can feel a shift in a current's vector hours before it arrives, sensing the change in the harmonic vibrations of their Harvester's structure. Their world is a symphony of sound, and they are its most attentive listeners.

Culture, for the Stanchioned, is synonymous with maintenance. Their art is not found in galleries but in the elegant, almost organic pattern of a perfect weld, in the polished grazer-plate that serves as a communal table, in the intricate scrimshaw carved into the handles of their tools. Their music is percussive and resonant, played on hollowed-out conduits and stretched leviathan gut, mimicking the thrumming of the turbines and the deep moans from the abyss. Their rituals are tied to the machines. A new turbine blade being lowered into place is a holy event, attended with silence and reverence. The "Sending" is their funeral rite: the body of the deceased, wrapped in kelp fronds and weighted with scrap metal, is committed to a maintenance lock and released into the currents. To be consumed by the Churn and the things that live in it is not an end, but a dissolution, a final act of becoming part of the whole.

They are a people defined by their relationship with the leviathans. They do not hunt the great beasts; the energy cost is too high, the danger absolute. They are regarded with a mixture of terror and awe, as itinerant gods whose passing can alter currents and threaten the foundations of a Harvester. A sighting is an omen. Their mournful calls are woven into the Stanchioned's own songs, a constant reminder of a life that endures the Churn not by bracing against it, but by being a part of its immense, fluid self.

The Stanchioned are not conquerors. They are barnacles, tenacious and enduring. They live in a state of perpetual, high-stakes symbiosis with the Harvesters that shelter them, and in a parasitic relationship with the ancient mountains that anchor them. They know that every rivet will one day fail, every dome will eventually implode, every turbine will finally be sheared from its mount. The ruins of failed stations are their most sacred texts, lessons written in shattered metal and drowned hopes. They are the knot in the current, the momentary interruption in the flow. They do not conquer the Churn. They do not tame it. They inhabit a single, continuous moment of resistance, and in that moment, they are alive.
