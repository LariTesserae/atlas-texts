What lives here is the reason the maintenance is unseen. It is because the crews are not visitors, but part of the place itself—as native as the rust and the rhizomes. They are the Tenders.

At first, you don't perceive them as living things. You might see one as a trick of the light, a section of verdigris-coated pipe that seems to rearrange itself with agonizing slowness. Or you might mistake one for a concentration of shadow in a nook between a condenser and the thick, cable-like liana that has wrapped around it over a century. They do not move quickly, because nothing here does. Their purpose is not to act, but to correct, to adjust, to maintain the great, slow equilibrium.

When one is still, it is nearly indistinguishable from its surroundings. Its skin is not flesh but a semi-living polymer, a biological patina that mimics the surfaces it spends its life upon. It is a mosaic of textures: the slick, damp-to-the-touch quality of a fungus, the pitted grain of corroded iron, the oily sheen of a lubricated joint, the matte green of oxidized copper. They are vaguely humanoid in shape, but only in the way a gnarled cypress root is vaguely humanoid—a trunk, long limbs, a node at the top. They have no faces. The front of their "head" is a smooth, unfeatured sweep of this same mottled integument, except for a single, broad, heat-sensitive pit that glows with a soft, internal orange light, like the embers of a forge. This is how they see: not in images, but in a thermal map of the world, a landscape of temperature gradients. The hiss of a steam leak is a flare of white-hot filigree; a cooling pipe is a fading grey ghost.

They do not breathe air so much as process it. Slits along their torsos, like the gills of a shark, slowly pulse, drawing in the thick, saturated atmosphere. They do not filter oxygen; they osmote the water directly, extracting what they need and exhaling a slightly cooler, denser vapor that immediately joins the perpetual condensation. Their blood is not red but a thin, tea-colored fluid, the very same sap the machines extract from the living floor. They are nourished by the system they maintain. Small, retractable siphons can emerge from their palms, needle-thin and flexible, to drink directly from the nutrient fluid flowing through specific transparent conduits in the great machine.

Their limbs are long, disproportionately so, and their joints seem to possess a range of motion impossible for a skeleton of bone. They can brace themselves between a pillar-trunk and a girder a dozen feet away, forming a living bridge. Their hands are not hands, but mutable tools. A finger might elongate and harden into a pry bar. The palm might flatten, its surface temperature rising until it glows, becoming a soldering iron to seal a leaking seam. From their wrists, they can secrete a thick, resinous substance that hardens into a perfect, watertight patch, or a viscous lubricant that silences a groaning gear. They are walking, climbing toolkits, their every function an adaptation to this specific industry.

They have no language. They do not need one. Their consciousness is collective, felt through the vibrations of the entire quarter. The rhythmic thrum of the great engine is their shared heartbeat. A change in that rhythm—a new clatter, a subtle grinding, a shift in the harmonic frequency—is a message to them all. It is a pain in the communal body. A Tender nearby, perhaps one that has been motionless for a decade, grown over with moss and indistinguishable from a stanchion, will slowly detach itself. It will begin its journey, a pilgrimage of maintenance that may take weeks, flowing over the architecture like a slug made of metal and plant.

They are born from the floor. In a particularly fertile, fallow section of the root-mass, a node will begin to swell. Over a generation, it is fed by the nutrient-rich seep of the substrate, slowly differentiating into the form of a Tender. It lies dormant until the thrum of the factory calls it, signaling a need—another cog in the great machine has worn down, another antibody is required to heal the wound in the system. When they die, their bodies simply cease moving. They become what they have always mimicked: a length of pipe, a rusted bracket, a burl of wood. The mosses and fungi and slow-growing roots envelop them, and over another century, they are reabsorbed back into the living floor they once walked upon, their substance ready to be harvested anew. They are not the masters of this place, nor its slaves. They are its enzymes, its quiet immune system, the silent, patient consciousness of a world that has forgotten the difference between growing and running.
