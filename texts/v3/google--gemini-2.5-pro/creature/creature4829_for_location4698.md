The inhabitants of Accretion are called the Resonant. They do not call themselves this; it is not a name but a state of being, a simple descriptor of what they are. To be born here is to be born into the Hum, to have it as your first lullaby and your final dirge.

They are, by the standards of other human stock, slow. Their movements are deliberate, economical, and possess a strange, fluid grace, like that of a glacier or a river of cooling lava. They walk with a low center of gravity, their feet wide and planted, sensing the texture and vibration of the surface beneath their thick-soled boots. An outsider might see them as sluggish, almost somnolent, but this stillness is a form of listening. Their every action is predicated on the information they receive through the soles of their feet, the palms of their hands, the base of their skull. They feel the planetary drone not as a sound, but as the fundamental texture of existence.

To be Resonant is to be dense. Their bones, over generations of exposure to the lithogenic vibrations and mineral-rich air, are thicker and more robust than is common. There is a subtle, unsettling weight to them, a physical presence that seems to have more to do with geology than biology. Their skin has a peculiar lustre, ranging in tone from the charcoal-grey of the hardening plains to the bruised purple of a fresh extrusion. It is perpetually cool and slick to the touch, not with oil, but with a fine condensation of the same minerals that bead on the iron towers. Their hair grows coarse and dark, often catching and holding a fine, glittering dust of crystal and metal, so that in the dim light their heads seem to crown themselves with faint nebulae.

Their senses are re-tuned. Their eyes, often a pale, milky grey or a deep, liquid violet, are accustomed to vast distances and low light. They do not blink often. Their gaze is steady and sweeping, taking in the slow progress of a mesa's formation a hundred kilometers away with the same patient attention a farmer might give a growing cornstalk. But it is their hearing, and the feeling that is coupled with it, that is most alien. The Hum does not travel *through* their bones; it is the marrow of their bones. They can distinguish infinitesimal shifts in its frequency and timbre, reading the state of the plateau as a musician reads a score. They can feel the dissonance of a coming fracture half a kilometer down, or the perfect, satisfying chord of a Resonance Pile settling a field of silicon into crystalline purity. They speak to each other in low, resonant tones, their voices often lost in the ambient drone to an outsider's ear, but for them, each word is a clear, plucked note in a constant symphony.

They are not without emotion, but it is a thing of geological scale. Joy is not a fleeting smile but the quiet, bone-deep satisfaction of seeing a harmonic hammer, set by their grandfather, finally strike the note that fractures a ridge along a planned line. Grief is not a spasm of tears, but the heavy, leaden silence that follows the collapse of a mis-tuned formation, a project of generations lost to a moment of dissonance. Love is the silent, shared understanding of a work crew on a suspended platform, moving in perfect, synchronized rhythm without a word exchanged, every member an extension of the others, all tuned to the same task.

The softness of reality on the plateau has shaped their minds. Causal smudging is not a hazard to them; it is a tool. The old and the skilled, the Fore-Tuners, learn to feel the "echo" of an action before they take it. They might feel the phantom pain of a future burn and pull their hand back from a pipe that is not yet hot, or taste the metallic tang of a successful forging before the ore has even been brought to the Pile. They experience time as a texture, not a line. Their genealogies are not lists of names but living maps of work. "My hand," a worker might say, gesturing with a calloused, mineral-stained appendage, "was laid on this anchor by my great-grandmother. My eyes are watching for the sign she expected to see."

Their children are born with the Hum in their ears. They learn to balance on the vibrating, swaying walkways before they learn to speak. Their toys are discarded lenses of pure crystal, resonant scraps of metal that chime when struck, and the polished, hexagonal plates from the carapaces of the grazers. They are taught not a history of kings and wars, but the long, slow story of the Whirpool Valley's turning, or the generations-long effort to cultivate the single, perfect note of the Spire Ridge.

The Resonant embody the paradox of their home. They are a loud, industrial people whose defining characteristic is a profound quietude. They are sculptors of the planetary flesh, yet their touch is one of listening and response, not force. They live suspended over an abyss of nascent, chaotic creation, held aloft by cables and girders, yet they are the most grounded beings imaginable. They are what happens when humanity stops trying to conquer a world and instead decides to join its choir, becoming a living, breathing, generational instrument, adding its own slow, patient, and deeply resonant verse to the song of the stone.
