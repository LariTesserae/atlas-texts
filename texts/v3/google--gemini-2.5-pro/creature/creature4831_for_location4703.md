They who live in Murkfast are inseparable from it, a specialized organ of the great, living tower. They are not its masters, nor are they its prisoners. They are its thoughts, made ambulatory. They are its hands, made purposeful. To the occasional, horrified scholar who has glimpsed them and survived, they are known by a whisper: the Lorn.

At a distance, a Lorn is a trick of the light, a flicker of motion that your mind insists was merely water trailing down a calcified pillar. They are unnervingly tall and slender, their forms echoing the ascending lines of the towers they inhabit. Their posture is a permanent, graceful stoop, as if perpetually listening to the ground or bowing in deference to the immense organism around them. Their limbs are long, with an extra joint in both arm and leg, allowing them to brace themselves within arches or fold themselves into impossibly small crevices. Their movement is the source of the silence, not a break in it. They do not walk so much as they flow across the root-mat, their wide, soft-soled feet making no sound, displacing nothing. They move with the unhurried, inexorable purpose of a vine seeking light, or a root seeking water.

To see one up close is to witness a creature of exquisite, horrifying symbiosis. Their skin is not skin, but a pale, unctuous membrane, like the cap of a mushroom that has never seen the sun. It is cool and damp to the touch, and in the shifting bioluminescence, it shimmers with a faint, pearlescent quality, mirroring the beetles they tend. They have no hair. Their head is a smooth, ovoid shape that flows seamlessly into a long, flexible neck. And they have no face, not in a way a human would recognize.

Where a face should be is a smooth, bone-white plate of chitin, like a mask grown from within. This mask is not entirely blank. It is dominated by two enormous, round, compound eyes, black and vitreous, like polished obsidian or the multifaceted eyes of a moth. They do not blink. They absorb the faint light with an unnerving totality, reflecting nothing back. They have no visible mouth. For sustenance, a delicate, segmented proboscis, like that of a butterfly but tipped with a needle of sharpened bone, uncoils from a hidden slit beneath the chin-plate. With this, they feed, sipping the sweet ichor from mature beetles, drinking the nutrient-rich ooze that collects in certain fungal cups, or drawing sustenance directly from the "veins" of the Bog-Tower itself. Their silence is absolute because they have no organ for speech, no lungs to heave air, no throat to form words.

Their hands are their most expressive and complex feature. They have six fingers, long and arachnidan, with the same extra joint as their limbs. The tips are not padded with flesh but end in hard, chitinous points, perfect for gripping the slick tower walls. These fingers move with a drowsy, hypnotic slowness as they work. You might see a Lorn spend a full cycle of the pulsing light gently stroking a new, crystalline shoot with its polished bone hook, its head tilted as if listening, coaxing the growth to curve into the arch of a new doorway. Their senses are not our own. They see in shades of blue and green light, but they also "smell" moisture gradients in the air, "taste" the health of the mycelial floor through their feet, and "hear" the subsonic vibrations of the Tower's slow, geologic growth.

They are solitary by nature but networked in consciousness. They do not speak, but they communicate. When one makes a decision—that a silk net needs repair, that a clutch of eel-eggs is ready for harvest—the others seem to know. They operate with the silent, emergent intelligence of a slime mold, a consensus that arises not from discussion but from a shared, all-encompassing sensory input from the Tower itself. The Tower is their externalized mind. They are rarely seen together, yet you never get the sense they are truly alone. A Lorn tending a gut-line ladder on one level might be perfectly aware of another, half a mile away, harvesting the silvery dust from a carpet of dead moths.

They do not seem to possess individuality as we know it. There are no great Lorn leaders, no artists, no rebels. There is only the work, the endless, patient gardening. They are born as much as they are grown. In certain humid, dark chambers, pods hang from the canopy like pale, leathery fruit. In time, these pods swell and tear, and a young Lorn, small and gelatinous, unfurls and drops silently to the root-mat below. It is born knowing its place, its purpose already woven into the fabric of the Tower's being.

When a Lorn dies, its body does not rot in the conventional sense. It finds a quiet place, folds its long limbs, and becomes still. Its membranous skin hardens, calcifies. Fungi and glowing mosses, the very same that form the Tower, colonize it. It is reabsorbed. Over decades, it becomes a small, gnarled pillar, another knot in the living architecture, its bone-white faceplate staring out with its dead, black eyes until it is covered over, becoming just another feature in the landscape it spent its entire life tending. To happen upon one is to stumble into a cathedral and find the saints and the gargoyles are one and the same, and they are still silently, patiently, at work.
