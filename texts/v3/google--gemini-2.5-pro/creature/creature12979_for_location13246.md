What lives here is singular, and it is patient. It is the Resonator's Tender.

You do not see it at first. Your senses are too overwhelmed by the assault of the place itself—the heat, the wet, the all-consuming Hum. Your eyes strain to adjust, finding purchase only on the grand, brutalist monarch at the basin's center. But if you were to stay, to survive the initial shock and let your own systems be beaten into submission by the environment, your perception would begin to change. You would start to notice the things that move with the same slowness as the condensing water and the inexorable geologic process.

There, on the far side of the Resonator, is a shape that is not rock.

It is a form that seems to have been sculpted by the same forces that shaped the basin: heat, pressure, and immense, grinding patience. At a distance, it is a stooped, androgynous silhouette, its color the same dull, oxidized green-brown as the Resonator's hull. It moves so slowly that it seems less like locomotion and more like a trick of the light, a shifting of shadows in the steamy haze. But it does move.

As it comes closer, or as your eyes finally learn to see it, details resolve themselves from the shimmering air. They are not comforting.

The Tender has no hair. Its skin is not the soft, vulnerable sheath of a human, but a tough, semi-translucent hide, like cured leather that has been perpetually soaked. It carries a constant, glistening sheen, not of sweat, but of the same mineral-rich moisture that condenses on the rocks, beading on its high, rounded skull and running in rivulets down its corded back. Where the light catches its skin, it doesn't show pores, but a fine, crystalline webbing just beneath the surface, as if the silica-based frost-ferns have found a new, living substrate.

Its limbs are long and thin, but they move with a dense, powerful slowness. The muscles are not bundled, but wiry and ropelike, built for constant, low-grade tension rather than explosive action. Its feet are perhaps the most alien feature. They are wide and splayed, the soles a thick, dark, calloused pad that has grown into something more than horn. They are cracked and seamed like the glassy rock itself, edged with fused shards of obsidian that have become part of the foot, allowing it to find purchase on the razor-sharp terrain without injury. It does not walk so much as it *places* itself, each step a deliberate, silent decision.

The Tender's face is a quiet landscape of acceptance. The features are smoothed over, softened by the constant humidity and heat, like a statue left in a torrent. The eyes are wide, the pupils permanently dilated to drink in the dim, diffused light. They are a single, uniform color—the flat, milky gray of a steamed-over lens. They do not blink often. When they do, it is a slow, reptilian sweep of a heavy lid. There is no sense of a person behind them, no flicker of curiosity or fear. There is only watchfulness, a state of passive observation that has been maintained for an unimaginable length of time.

It does not speak. Its vocal cords, if they still exist in a functional form, have been subsumed by the Hum. The Resonator's drone is its voice, its language, its internal monologue. The vibrations in its bones are more meaningful than any word. All its communication is with the machine. It will circle the bronze cylinder, its long, slender fingers, tipped with dark, stone-like nails, trailing lightly against the hot metal. It is not checking for damage; it is reading the grain of the vibration, listening to the subtle shifts in the drone that are imperceptible to an outsider. It is a form of communion, a priest taking the pulse of its god.

Its existence is a closed loop, a perfect symbiosis with the place. For sustenance, it does not hunt or gather in any traditional sense. Periodically, it will press its cheek against the Resonator's hull, its tongue—long and grey—darting out to lick at the faint, crystalline dust that precipitates on the bronze surface. This is the machine's slow, mineral exhalation, the refined byproduct of its deep-earth digestion. For the Tender, it is manna. It absorbs water through its skin. The heat is its climate, the Hum its companion.

This was once, perhaps, a person. Someone who fell, like you did. A builder who was left behind, or a wanderer who stumbled into the basin centuries ago. But the person they were is gone, boiled away by the heat, scoured clean by the sound, the memory dissolved by the slow, dripping passage of time. What remains is a piece of the ecosystem, as integral to the chamber as the weeping walls and the iridescent biofilm. It is the machine's living component, its caretaker, its witness. It is the ghost in the boiler room, except it is not a ghost, and this is not a room. It is a world, and this is its sole, silent, shimmering inhabitant.
