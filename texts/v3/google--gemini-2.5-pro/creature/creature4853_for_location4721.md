They are the Weep-born, the Dwellers. You will not see them at first, and even when you do, you may not recognize them as living. They are the color of a fresh bruise on old iron, a deep, shifting purple-grey that is a perfect camouflage against the weeping plates. They are beings born of condensation and corrosion.

To glimpse one is to see a flicker of wrongness in the gloom. It is a shape that should be a static piece of discarded machinery but is not. They are gaunt, impossibly so, their frames little more than bone and sinew stretched taut over an angular skeleton. They move in a low, patient crouch, their long, four-jointed limbs allowing them to scuttle with unnerving speed or remain perfectly still for hours, folded into the shape of a discarded support bracket. Their skin is not skin, but a smooth, cold, rubbery integument, slick and iridescent like the water in the gutters, perpetually beaded with the basin's fine, metallic rain.

They have no faces in a way you would understand. Where a face should be is a smooth, swept-back casque of chitinous material, the same color as their bodies but harder, resembling the slagged and cooled shell of a faulty weld. They do not have eyes. Instead, set into this faceless head are two deep pits, like acoustic sensors, that are preternaturally sensitive to the basin's cacophony. They navigate by the echoes of their own quiet clicks, by the subtle shift in the sub-bass thrum of the floor, by the doppler wail of a distant crane. They can read the architecture of the basin by its sound alone, mapping the world in pressure waves and vibrations. They hear the approaching crawler-drones as a growing shadow in the symphony, a dissonant chord of death.

Their hands and feet are their most intricate feature. They are long and spidery, ending not in fingers but in clusters of metallic, hook-like claws and sensitive, gecko-like pads. These allow them to find purchase on treacherous smooth plates, cling upside-down to the scaffolding far above, or brace themselves against the percussive shockwave of a nearby piston stroke. They can tell the age and composition of a plate by touch, the virility of the crystalline oxides by their texture. These needle-like turquoise and white shards are, for them, a vital resource. They harvest them, cracking them from the seams with their claws and consuming them. The mineral salts are essential to their strange biology, fortifying their skeletons and honing the metallic edges of their claws.

The Dwellers exist in two distinct states, driven by the brutal pragmatism of their existence.

The most numerous are the Scuttlers. These are the young, the active, the foragers. They are the ones who patrol the iron plains, who climb the static machinery, who risk the open ground between the cones of light. They are perpetually hungry, relentlessly resourceful. They feed on the aggressive oxide-salts, on the bioluminescent lichen that they scrape from the pools, and, most prized of all, on the discarded energy and matter of the great machine. A spent power cell, leaking its last charge, is a delicacy. A broken repair bot, its circuits fried, is a feast of rare metals and polymers. They work in small, silent packs, communicating through subtle shifts in posture and the slight vibration of their claws tapping on the iron floor. They are scavengers in the guts of the leviathan.

But this frantic life cannot last. The basin is too harsh, the crawler-drones too efficient. As a Dweller ages, as its limbs grow stiff and its senses dull, it does not simply die and become food for its pack. It seeks a different path. It seeks stillness.

This is the second state: the Anchorite.

An aging Dweller will find a secluded nexus, a place of power and relative safety—a deep recess behind a humming transformer, a sheltered alcove beneath a steam-vent, or a quiet bank beside a pool of glowing lichen. There, they settle. They drive their claws deep into the seams of the iron plates, or brace themselves against a conduit humming with power. And they begin to change.

Their metabolism slows to a state barely distinguishable from geology. The mineral salts they have consumed throughout their lives begin to crystallize *on* them, not just *in* them. Their bodies become encrusted with the virulent turquoise and bone-white shards, their limbs fusing to the structure of the basin itself. Their smooth, dark skin hardens and cracks, becoming another texture in the industrial landscape. They become living statues, part creature, part corrosion.

They are no longer foragers; they are conduits. They draw sustenance directly from the machine's waste energy—from the heat of a pipe, the stray voltage of a cable, the kinetic vibration of the floor. Their faceless heads, now immobile, are cocked as if listening to a secret. And they are. For years, decades, they sit, and they *listen*. They absorb the rhythm of the basin, the grand, inhuman symphony. They learn its cadences, its prophecies. The changing pitch of the deep thrum is a seasonal shift. The shriek of a specific lathe is an omen. The pattern of the sirens is a scripture.

The Scuttlers revere the Anchorites. They are their oracles, their memory, their culture. They bring them offerings—not of food, but of information. A rare metal fragment found near a new excavation, a piece of a strange drone, a shard of crystal with an unusual hue. They place these things before the silent, encrusted figures. The Anchorite does not respond in words, for they have no mouths. But the Scuttlers watch the faint, residual light that plays across the Anchorite's crystalline shell, a light drawn from the machine's own energy, and in its patterns, they read their guidance: where to forage, where danger lies, which rhythms are changing.

They are the indifferent god's unwanted children, born in the dampness of its lungs and nurtured by the poison of its blood. They have no concept of the sun, of green, of soft earth. Their universe is a grid of iron, their sky a web of steel, their stars the status lights of a machine that does not know they exist. They live and die by its rhythms, and in the deepest, quietest moments of the basin, when the pistons pause and the steam recedes, one might almost hear a sound that is not machine—a faint, resonant clicking, a chorus of a thousand tiny, patient heartbeats, singing the terrible hymns of their iron god.
