What lives here is the reason there is no life. The makers of this place never left; they are simply engaged in a work so patient and so total that it has consumed the very definition of living. They are the city.

One does not see them at first, or second, or ever, in the way one sees a creature distinct from its environment. To look for them is to look for a specific wave in the ocean. They are the Weepers, or the Shapers, or the Quiet. They have no name for themselves, because there are no others from whom to be distinguished.

They are made of the city's bone-white ceramic. They are not inside it; they are extrusions of it. A form that yesterday was a simple, stark pillar might today have begun to articulate a shape that is almost a shoulder, a spine. Another wall, perfectly flat for a millennium, might now bear the slow-blooming relief of a face, eyes closed, cheekbones sharp as the surrounding architecture. These are not statues. They are the workers, paused. Their stillness is not inactivity; it is a state of immense, focused pressure.

To witness them move is impossible. Their motion is geological. A hand, indistinguishable from a decorative plinth, might press itself into a block of still-pliable ceramic, and the gesture of shaping it, of leaving those smoothed-over palm prints, might be an action that takes a standard century to complete. They flow like glaciers, their substance shifting and reconfiguring according to the grand, crystalline logic of the structure. They are the agents of the city’s growth. They are building, always building, but their work is not expansion. It is refinement. They are perfecting a single, colossal object.

The weeping water is their blood and their medium. It is the hydraulic fluid of their slow, tectonic existence. It carries the nutrients they need—not biological, but mineral—leached from one part of the structure to be deposited in another. The water is sterile because they are sterile. Their purpose is a great and final purification, and life—unpredictable, chaotic, noisy life—is the ultimate impurity. They filter it out with the patience of gods. Every surface, every pore of the ceramic, is a membrane designed to catch and annihilate the possibility of a microbe, a seed, a thought.

The shadowless light is their consciousness. They have no eyes. The pervasive, clinical glow is their sensory organ, allowing them to perceive the entire structure as a single entity, to feel the stresses and flows within their shared body. The low, resonant hum is their thought. It is the sound of a billion minds, or a single mind with a billion nodes, contemplating the silent, geometric perfection they seek. It is a drone of immense, unified calculation, the sound of a problem being solved over eons.

They are monks. They are artists. They are machines. They have taken a vow of silence that extends to the molecular level, quieting the chaotic dance of biology to achieve a state of pure form. The handprints they leave are not signatures of pride but imprints of a necessary force, like the mark a glacier leaves on bedrock.

If you stood in a plaza for a thousand years, you might perceive one of them. A cowled figure, its body a seamless extension of the archway behind it, might turn its head. The motion would be imperceptible, slower than the creeping mineral stains. Across the chasm, another shape—an impossibly tall, attenuated being that is also a buttress—might extend a limb to offer a tool made of the same ceramic, a tool that will take five hundred years to cross the gap.

They are building their own tomb. Or perhaps, they are building their own fossil, creating the perfect conditions to preserve their final, perfected form. The city is a sarcophagus the size of a mountain range, and they are both the sculptors and the body being interred. The purpose of the engine is to achieve a state of ultimate, eternal stillness. The reason there is no life here is because this place is a monument to the cessation of it. They are not living; they are the process of becoming perfectly, immaculately still. They are what is left when everything else is gone.
