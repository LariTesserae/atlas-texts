The beings who live in Amun-Aur are the Stone-Singers.

They are not named this because they have voices, for they have no mouths to speak, no lungs to push air. They are named for the way they listen, and the way their work answers. The hiss of the wind is the world’s breath, and the hum of the groves is its heartbeat. To the Stone-Singers, these are not background noises; they are the two halves of a conversation, and their lives are the long, patient response.

To see one is to see a piece of the world given slow, deliberate leave to move. They are tall, impossibly slender, and their form suggests something between a wind-scoured stone column and the ossified stem of some giant, extinct plant. Their outer surface is not skin but a ceramic carapace, the color of the grey-black stone swells, polished by ages of existence. It is not a uniform grey; like the rock of the plateau, it is a tapestry of interlocking mineral grains, some catching the pale light like flecks of mica, others absorbing it completely.

Running through this stony integument are veins, not of blood, but of the same deep, living matter that animates the calciform flora. These veins, a network of viridian and cobalt, pulse with a light so slow it is almost imperceptible. A single throb, a wave of faint luminescence moving from a foot to a hip, might take a full minute to complete. This is the source of their vitality. They do not eat. They do not drink in the conventional sense. They are symbiotic resonators. They subsist on the world’s fundamental instability, the same hum that warms the groves. To live, they must be partly rooted in the city's resonant chambers, or spend long periods of stillness in the valleys, their long, finger-like digits resting upon the waxy leaves, absorbing the vibration and the condensation. They metabolize entropy.

Their heads are smooth, ovoid, and featureless, save for two shallow depressions on either side where ears might be. These depressions do not hear sound as we do; they feel pressure and vibration. A Stone-Singer can feel the echo of their distant companion's chisel-fall through the soles of their feet, distinguishing it from the background hum as a distinct, rhythmic thought. They have no eyes. Their perception of the world is a complex fusion of thermosensation, resonant imaging, and a direct, tactile awareness of the stone. They navigate the city’s lightless inner chambers by feeling the way the hum of the world changes its tone and texture as it bounces off a wall, flows down a channel, or is absorbed by an uncarved section of rock. They can sense the soft spots in causality the way an animal senses a change in the weather, and they move with an intuitive grace that guides them around a patch of air where time might be flowing backward or where gravity has momentarily lost its conviction.

Their life is The Work, and The Work is the city. They are born not from a womb but from the world itself. In certain deep, resonant grottos, a stone geode, saturated with the hum for millennia, will begin to change. Over a century, it softens, and a new form is slowly, patiently, carved from it by elder Singers—an act of midwifery that is also an act of sculpture. The new Singer emerges fully formed but unpolished, their surface rough, their inner pulse weak. Their childhood is an apprenticeship of centuries, learning to sand their own limbs to a frictionless gloss, to attune their inner resonance, and to listen to the stone.

There is no distinction between art, engineering, and worship. To channel water is a prayer. To polish a floor until it reflects the vague light from a surface shaft is a philosophical statement. To spend a lifetime with a single obsidian chisel, slowly flaking away at a block of stone to reveal the curve of a wall, is the entirety of one's purpose. They are not a culture of grand gestures, but of infinite, patient refinement.

Their "conversations" are actions. When a master carver wishes to teach a pupil, they do not speak. The master will strike the rock, a single *chink*. The pupil, standing a hundred yards away, feels the precise vibration of that impact travel through the city's floor, noting its frequency, its force, the way it rings in the stone. They will then take up their own hammer and, after a long moment of contemplation, attempt to replicate the exact resonance. This exchange might be the only communication between them for a day. Their history is not in books but in the walls themselves; the style of a particular era's carving, the depth of a channel, the specific curve of an archway—these are their records.

To stand on a balcony in Amun-Aur and watch a Stone-Singer work is an exercise in perception. You might see one at the far end of a vast causeway, seemingly motionless, one long, three-fingered hand resting on a megalithic block. For an hour, nothing happens. But if you could feel as they do, you would know that they are in communion with the stone, sensing its internal stresses, its resonant frequency, its willingness to be moved. When the moment is right, when the hum of the world aligns in just such a way, they will lean, and the great block, weighing thousands of tons, will scrape forward a single inch with a sound that echoes across the valley for a full minute. The Singer will then rest, perhaps for a day, perhaps for a week, waiting for the world, the stone, and their own being to align again.

They are the world's final inhabitants, but they do not mourn its decay. They are its instruments. The world is a bell, struck at the dawn of time and now ringing its final, long, deep note. The Stone-Singers are not the listeners, waiting for the sound to die. They are the bell itself, vibrating with the note, and their work is just that resonance, made manifest in stone and silence.
