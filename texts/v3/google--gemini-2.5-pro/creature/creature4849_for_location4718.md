They are the Hearthkeepers. They do not call themselves this in their own tongue—a language of quiet, resonant vowels and consonants clicked softly against the back of the teeth—but the name describes their single, unifying purpose. They are the keepers of the one true darkness, the one small warmth, in a world gone blindingly, killingly bright.

Beneath the thick, stiff layers of hide and pelt, they are not what you would expect. Their bodies are low-slung and broad, with a bone density born of a lifetime of pushing against a substance that is both weightless and immensely heavy. Their joints are massive, their musculature coiled and efficient, designed for endurance over speed. Their skin, where it can be seen in the rare privacy of their domes, is the color of a fresh bruise, a mottled purple-grey like the basin rock itself, but utterly without luster. It is cool to the touch, almost cold, their metabolism a banked fire burning at the lowest possible setting. To conserve energy is their first, unspoken law.

Their blood is not red but a dark, viscous violet, thick with proteins that act as a biological antifreeze and shield their cells from the strange, ambient energy that saturates their world. Their hearts beat with a slow, powerful, two-part rhythm: *thump-thump…(long pause)...thump-thump*. It is a rhythm that matches their work: scrape-scrape, lift, carry. Scrape-scrape, lift, carry.

Their faces are broad and flat, with wide-set eyes beneath heavy, fleshy brows. The eyes themselves are their most vulnerable and most sacred feature. They are large, perfectly black, and possess no visible sclera or iris. They are orbs of pure, liquid night, evolved not for seeing in the dimness of their homes, but for the opposite: to absorb the maximum amount of light without being destroyed, to turn the overwhelming glare into the faintest of signals. Even so, they cannot bear the raw, shadowless pearl of the open basin. To look upon it directly is to invite a madness they call "the white burn," a sensory overload that can shatter the mind. The smoked obsidian goggles they wear are not merely sunglasses; they are filters, theological necessities, that reduce the world to a manageable, ghostly monochrome.

They are born nearly blind, their eyes sealed shut for the first several years of life, and they learn the world first through touch and sound. A Hearthkeeper's hands are their soul. The palms and fingertips are calloused to an almost stony toughness, yet they are alive with a sensitivity we cannot comprehend. In the total, interior blackness of their homes, they read the stories carved into the inner surfaces of the bone rafters—epic poems of the Great Sleepers whose skeletons house them, star-charts of a sky no one has seen for a thousand generations, the genealogies of their own slow-blooded line. They communicate as much with a quiet pressure of a hand on an arm as they do with their hushed words. They know their kin not by face, but by the rhythm of their breathing, the weight of their footfalls, the specific way their scraper *tisses* against the falling light-rime.

They do not fear the skittering crystal-crabs or the slugs. They are merely… things. Mobile parts of the landscape. The slugs are their helpers, their slow, methodical grazing clearing the base-rock and leaving behind a faintly slick trail that makes the Hearthkeepers’ own scraping easier. They guide the herds with long bone prods, a form of low-energy agriculture. The dead crabs leave behind the only sharp things in the basin—their vitreous legs and claws, which the Hearthkeepers painstakingly fletch onto their scrapers and picks. These tools, made of ancient bone and chitinous glass, are passed down through generations, their surfaces worn into comfortable divots that fit the hands of a specific family line.

Their central belief is not a religion of gods, but of states. There was the Before-Time, the age of the Great Sleepers, massive beings of flesh and warmth who strode under a directional sun and a dark-filled night. The bones are the proof. Then came the Change, the Great Whitening, when the air grew cold and heavy with light. And now is the Long Wait.

They believe the world is an egg. The basin is the shell, the blinding, precipitating light is the albumen—the sterile, suffocating "Husk"—and at the very center, tended by them, is the Yolk. Their city is not merely a settlement; it is an incubator. The one "hearth" they keep is not a fire, but a place. In the deepest, central chamber of their city, carved into the rock beneath the largest of the bone-domes, is a space kept perpetually, obsessively clear. Here, the bare, dark stone is exposed. It is not warm, but in a world of absolute, killing cold, its deep, neutral temperature feels like a furnace. This is the Unlit Heart. Here, the thumbprint-sized cushions of black lichen are not just tolerated, but cultivated. They believe these ancient, slow-growing things are the memory of the Before-Time, the living seed of true, organic life.

Their entire society, their Sisyphean ballet of scraping and clearing, is an act of devotion to this single, bare patch of rock. They are not merely surviving the endless, glittering snowfall; they are protecting the potential for a future. They are keeping the Heart of the world from being smothered by its own beautiful, empty light. Every shovel-load of collapsed crystal, every cleared tunnel, every herded slug is a prayer for the day the Husk will break, the light will recede, and the Yolk—the true, dark, living world—will finally be born.

They move slowly against the frantic, silent storm of energy around them. They are heavy where all is light. They are dark where all is bright. They are memory in a world of fleeting moments. They are the silent, patient, breathing heart of a body long since put to rest, and they wait, with the profound stillness of stone, for the day it will be called upon to beat again.
