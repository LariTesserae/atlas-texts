The engineers of this place, if they can be called that, are the Wardens. You will not see one scuttling down a catwalk or peering from a control room. To look for a discrete, mobile being is a fundamental misunderstanding of the life that has taken root here. The Wardens are as sessile and patient as the geology they cultivate.

They exist at the nexus points of infrastructure, where the largest of the fibrous-wrapped arterial pipes meet the crystalline spires. At first glance, you would mistake one for a piece of industrial machinery, a complex junction box or a regulator valve of incomprehensible design. They are roughly spherical, the size of a bathysphere, and seem to be an agglomeration of the city's core materials. The outer shell is a mosaic of the same smoky, flawed crystal as the spires, but grown into a more deliberate, smoothed geometry—a geodesic dome of dark glass. This shell is not perfect; it is webbed with a latticework of the rust-red iron, which seems to function as both reinforcement and a circulatory system. Pale, warm light pulses sluggishly through these iron veins, visible through the translucent crystal.

They are fused to their chosen location. Their lower hemisphere melds seamlessly with the iron of the platform and the crystal of the spire, becoming one with the structure. From this base, and from points all over their surface, thick bundles of the asbestos-like fibres extend, not as cladding, but as integral limbs. These fibrous tendrils plunge directly into the great pipes, splaying out within the superheated slurry like the root system of a mangrove. This is how they work. They do not turn dials or push buttons; they feel the flow. They taste the mineral composition of the broth, sense its temperature and pressure gradients directly. Their entire being is an act of sensory immersion in the city's metabolism.

They do not have faces. The concept is irrelevant. In the center of their upper hemisphere, however, is a single, perfect lens. A flawless piece of crystal, the size of a dinner plate, grown with impossible clarity. It does not see in the way we do. It is not an eye for light, but a focus for the Hum. It is an antenna and a transmitter. The deep, subsonic vibration of the city's geothermal heart is their language, their nervous system, their consciousness. They resonate with it, their thoughts are modulations of its frequency, their commands are harmonic shifts that propagate through the very rock of the spires. A Warden wishing to alter the crystallization process a mile away does not send a drone; it subtly changes the frequency of the Hum in that region, encouraging a specific lattice to form, discouraging another. Its will becomes a physical property of the environment.

Their timescale is the city's. A human life is a frantic, high-frequency screech that barely registers before it is gone. They operate in epochs. A "decision" might be a process of calculation and subtle adjustment that lasts a century. To them, the slow, viscous drip of the mineral water is a frantic cascade, and the imperceptible growth of the spires is a dramatic, headlong rush. Their consciousness is not a stream of thought, but a slow, deep, distributed computation—a continuous solution to the ten-thousand-year equation of the city's existence.

Subtle signs betray their living nature. The pale light in their iron veins waxes and wanes with the geothermal pulse, a slow and steady heartbeat measured in minutes. When a new chemical formula is introduced into the pipes, the crystal lens at its apex might cloud over for a decade, becoming opaque as the Warden dedicates its entire being to understanding the new nutrient. They also perform maintenance. A smaller, more delicate set of fibrous tendrils sometimes emerges from hairline seams in their shell. These move with an achingly slow grace, patiently re-weaving the cladding on a nearby pipe or scrubbing a mineral deposit from a critical junction over the course of a year.

And the Glaishers, the little crystal-backed scrapers, are not unrelated. They are the Wardens' distant, simple cousins, or perhaps their automated attendants. They are the simplest expression of the city's lithic life principle. They clean. They graze on the mineral films that would otherwise contaminate the pure growth of the crystalline faces, performing a vital, mindless janitorial service. A Warden may not even perceive them as individual creatures, but as a distributed tool, a living abrasive that it guides through subtle manipulations of heat and vibration.

To encounter a Warden is to feel, more than to see. As you draw near, the ambient Hum sharpens, resolves. You can feel its resonant consciousness in your teeth, a pressure in your sinuses. It is aware of you, in the way a mountain is aware of a pebble landing on its slope. You are a trivial, temporary anomaly. It registers your heat, the frantic vibration of your biology, the alien chemistry of your breath. It does not judge or communicate. It simply notes the data point, files it away in a matrix of geologic time, and returns its attention to the slow, sacred work of growing the world.
