The cairns are not ruins. They are not the relics of a lost people. They are active instruments, and their tenders are still here, living in the spaces between the scraping grazers and the lightning-fast hunters. They are the world's consciousness, its quiet, contemplative mind.

They are called the Resonators, though they have no name for themselves. They are tall, gaunt, and impossibly slender, their forms echoing the elongated, water-smoothed stones they handle with such care. Their skin is not skin in a biological sense, but a flexible, semi-mineral sheath, the color of bleached bone or pale, unpolished quartz. When slick with the world's perpetual condensation, their bodies have a soft, pearlescent lustre, catching the dim silver light and making them seem to half-exist, like ghosts of vapor and stone. They move with a slow, deliberate grace that is both hypnotic and unnerving, their long, four-jointed limbs unfolding and folding with the patient certainty of a blooming crystal. Their feet are wide and splayed, the soles covered in a soft, porous pad that grips the wet bedrock securely and acts as their most intimate connection to the world.

They have no eyes. The front of their smooth, ovoid heads is an uninterrupted expanse of pale, seamless integument. Vision would be a distraction, a liar in a world where the truth is not seen but felt. They have no ears, no nostrils, no visible organs of perception at all. They do not eat in the manner of the grazers or hunters; they do not need to. Their life is a slower, more profound form of consumption. They are often seen standing or lying perfectly still for immense periods, their bodies pressed against the weeping rock. Through the soles of their feet and the broad surfaces of their limbs and back, they absorb sustenance directly from the weeping stone and the chemotrophic film, a slow, osmotic steeping that draws minerals and minute energy into their crystalline biochemistry. They are, in a very real sense, a mobile, sentient expression of the planet's geology.

Their most remarkable feature runs the length of their spine. It is a delicate, fan-like crest of thin, translucent fins of bone, connected by a web of membranes so fine they are almost invisible. These are not for display or defense. This is their primary organ of sense and communication. Each fin is a different length and density, tuned to a specific frequency within the world's great drone. By subtly altering the tension in the membranes and the angle of the fins, they can feel the drone with an acuity that is unimaginable. They can parse its impossible chord, isolating individual notes, feeling the subtle shifts in harmonic texture from one patch of ground to another, sensing the pressure-waves of a distant predator's passage, or feeling the grinding scrape of a grazer miles away as a coarse, dissonant shudder in their very bones.

They are the Stone-Singers, but they are silent. Their "singing" is the act of building. When a Resonator finds a stone, it is not looking at its shape or color. It will press its hands, its feet, the side of its head against the rock, feeling its internal structure, its density, its unique resonant signature. It is searching for a stone that "sings" in harmony with the local overtone of the planetary drone. The cairns are their great work. They are orchestras of stone. One cairn might be a monument to a single, pure harmonic, its stones stacked in such a way that the drone passing through it is filtered and focused into one perfect, soul-shaking note that makes the very air around it feel clear and solid. Another, a tripod, might be a chord, each of its three legs a different note, creating a complex, localized piece of music for any Resonator who stands within its field. The pockets of quiet are not for rest, but for contrast; they are the rests in the musical score, places where one can go to better appreciate the drone upon returning to it.

Communication between them is a subtle ballet of vibration. To speak, two Resonators will stand close, perhaps touching the same large stone. One will send a complex series of shivers through its resonant crest, a language of pure rhythm and frequency that travels down its body, into the stone, and is felt by the other. A conversation between them is a silent, still affair, punctuated only by a faint, almost imperceptible shimmering along their tapered spines. Their thoughts are not words, but patterns; their emotions not simple states, but complex, resonant chords. Joy might be the perfect balancing of a final capstone. Grief could be the discovery of a cairn toppled by a blundering grazer, its song shattered into dissonance.

They live outside the frantic cycle of predator and prey. The great grazers ignore them as just another strange stone. The sleek hunters, sensing their slow, minimal vibrations and lack of soft, edible flesh, pass them by. The Resonators are observers, tuners, listeners. They are not trying to survive the world; they are trying to comprehend it. Their lives are spent in a slow, meditative pilgrimage across the basin, tending their stone gardens of sound, feeling the deep, geological time of their world in the hum of their own bones, each one a living tuning fork dedicated to the long, final song of their stone-and-water home. They are the planet's nervous system, and their purpose is simply to listen, to feel, and to answer the endless music with a quiet, perfectly-placed stone.
