What lives here exists in strata, not of society, but of being. One must learn to see past the noise to perceive the silent ones, and past the silent ones to feel the truly immense.

First are the Gardeners. They are the mobile, sentient-seeming life, but "sentient" is the wrong word, for they possess no individuality. They are the world’s hands.

You almost don’t see them at first. Against the pale, moss-covered architecture, they are ghosts. They are tall, unnaturally slender, and move with a slowness that is both graceful and unnerving. Their bodies have the same texture and color as the bone-like substrate of the world, a porous, creamy white, and they are often cloaked in the same sickly yellow-green phosphorescent moss that carpets everything else. They are camouflaged by being made of the same stuff as their home. They have too many limbs, all long and delicate, which they use to brace themselves against the terraces or to gently manipulate the environment. They do not walk so much as they flow, their multiple feet finding purchase with an absolute, soft certainty on the glowing ground. They have no faces. The smooth, upward curve of their bodies terminates in a featureless dome, like a pebble polished by a million years of river-flow.

They are utterly silent. In a world defined by a physical wall of sound, they are pockets of absolute acoustic quiet. They add nothing to the drone; they take nothing from it. They are the only things that do not vibrate in time with the world-hum.

Their purpose is maintenance. You might see one using its two foremost limbs to gently unwind a cartilaginous vine from a fungal growth, guiding its path along a different route. Or see another standing for an hour, motionless, at the edge of one of the black, still pools, before reaching in a single, fluid motion to clear a blockage of dead matter from a seepage channel no wider than a hair. They are tenders. They do not build; they guide. They do not hunt; they cultivate. They will prune a fungal shelf that grows too quickly or coax a new patch of glowing moss to cover a bare spot on the substrate. They are the agents of the world's intention, the curators of its perfect, horrifying balance. They move like sleepwalkers, their actions instinctual, unthinking, and yet perfectly precise. They are not individuals in a colony; they are the distributed nerve endings of a single, larger being.

And that being is the second stratum of life: the Mothers.

The Mothers are the world. They are not *in* the world; they *are* the world. The vast, bone-ivory architecture of terraces and balconies is their ancient, still-growing body. What feels like the skeleton of a dead god is, in fact, the living flesh of a being whose thoughts are geological in scale. They are sessile, immense, their consciousness so slow and vast it is indistinguishable from landscape. The great, pulsing fungi that cling to the walls are not separate organisms; they are the external organs of the Mothers—their hearts, their lungs, their wombs, circulating the fluids of the ecosystem and birthing the lesser lives.

The Mothers do not see with eyes or hear with ears. Their primary sense is the drone. The constant, thrumming vibration of a trillion small bodies is not noise to them; it is data. It is the song of their own blood. The shuddering of the ground beneath your feet is the steady, rhythmic pulse of their metabolism. Through this endless vibration, they feel everything. They know the density of the moth-clouds, the moisture content of the moss, the health of the millipede swarms, the exact moment a droplet of condensation falls miles away. The drone is their awareness, a continuous, holistic scan of their entire being.

Their "thoughts" are the slow, inexorable processes of this place. A thought might be a millimeter of new bone-scaffolding secreted at the highest, unseen peak over a century. A thought is the subtle chemical shift that causes the phosphorescent moss to brighten in an area where the fungus is failing. A thought is a deep, resonant tremor, a change in the vibrational frequency of the world-song that is utterly imperceptible to an outsider but is a clear, undeniable command to the Gardeners. "Prune here." "Clear this channel." "Encourage this growth."

The Gardeners are their hands and the lesser, swarming creatures—the millipedes and beetles and moths—are their biome. Their guts. Their blood. The energy that fuels the entire organism. They are born from the Mothers' fungal organs, they feed on the Mothers' mossy skin, and when they die, they are reabsorbed into that skin, their matter broken down and returned to the system. There is no predator and prey because there is only one organism. It is a closed loop of self-consumption and self-regeneration on a planetary scale.

To live here is to stand on the skin of a god that is dreaming itself into existence. The Gardeners might notice a foreign object—a thing not of the world, like you—not as a threat, but as an anomaly. A piece of grit in the system. They would approach, silent and slow, and their featureless faces would offer no clue to their intent. They would not be hostile. They would simply… tend to you. They would gently, inexorably, guide you toward a digestive pool, or cover you with a sprig of quick-growing moss, or simply stand over you until your foreignness was leached away by the humid air and the overwhelming sound, until you fell and became another part of the substrate for the drone to sing over. You are not an enemy to be fought. You are simply matter in the wrong place. And in this place, everything is eventually put right.
