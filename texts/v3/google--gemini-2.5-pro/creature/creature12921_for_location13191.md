The place is inhabited by the Geode-Kin.

They do not consider themselves separate from the basin. To them, the extruding black rock is their distant kin, the slow, elder part of their own being. The water that weeps from it is the same water that flows in their veins. The constant, percussive chime is the only language they have ever known, the rhythm of their own slow-beating hearts amplified a thousandfold.

To see one without its cloak is rare, for the damp and chill are persistent, but it is not a matter of modesty. The thick, pale wool is a practical husk, a layer of insulation against a world that is permanently cool. Beneath it, their form is both familiar and alien. They are broadly humanoid, but dense and low to the ground, with a solidity that suggests immense weight. Their bodies are not built for speed or suddenness, but for inexhaustible patience and the application of steady, relentless pressure.

Their skin is the colour of wet slate, ranging from a pale, misty grey to a near-black, and it is entirely hairless. It is not smooth, but possesses a fine, crystalline texture, like unpolished stone. In the diffuse, pearlescent light of the basin, it has a subtle lustre, and when they move, it can seem as if fine-grained fractal patterns shift just beneath the surface. Their joints do not bend with the soft pliancy of flesh and cartilage, but with the clean, precise movement of fitted stone, articulating with a faint, grinding sound that is lost in the greater chorus of the valley.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. They are wide, the fingers thick and seemingly fused at the second knuckle, ending not in nails but in hardened, chisel-like pads of biological obsidian. They are living tools, evolved for their singular purpose. They can feel the faintest stress-line in a rising pillar, sense a flaw deep within a slab, and know, through touch alone, the exact angle at which a chip will break clean.

The face of a Geode-Kin is a study in stillness. They have no visible ears; the entire surface of their skull seems to act as a resonant chamber, receiving the vibrations that fill the air. What they perceive is not sound as we know it, but a complex, tactile symphony of information: the growth rate of the mesa to the east, the water pressure in the western fissure, the precise, rhythmic signature of the tapping team three tiers below. This is their conversation, their history, their song. Their eyes are like pools of mercury, large, dark, and lidless, adapted to the perpetual soft light and holding an unnerving depth. They do not blink, only occasionally sheathing themselves behind a nictitating membrane of translucent, smoky quartz that slides up from below. They have mouths, but they are simple, slit-like openings used for one purpose only: to drink.

They do not eat. Their sustenance comes directly from the landscape. They drink the mineral-rich water that seeps and drips from the living rock, absorbing the silicates and trace elements that lend their bodies their unique hardness. They metabolize the ozone-charged air, their slow lungs extracting some unknown nutrient from the electrical tang. They are a closed system, a biological expression of the caldera's unique geochemistry.

Their life is the Great Work. They are born not from a womb, but from a geode. A child is found in a newly-split rock, curled in a crystalline hollow, small and pale and still. The community will raise them, teaching them not with words, but by guiding their hands, letting them listen to the rhythms, showing them how to feel the song of the stone. A Geode-Kin’s life is measured in the completion of tasks that defy human comprehension. One might spend five hundred years polishing a single twenty-foot square of rock until it becomes a perfect black mirror. Another may devote a millennium to scoring a single, elegant curve into a rising cliff face. Individuality exists only in the quality of one's work, the subtle signature in the rhythm of a hammer-fall, the unique perfection of a finished surface.

When a Geode-Kin feels their long life ending, when their inner structures begin to calcify beyond repair and their hands can no longer strike true, they do not die in a grotto. They find an unfinished wall or an unscored slab and, with their final reserves of strength, carve a niche for themselves. They enter it, assume a final pose, and the nictitating membranes slide up over their eyes for the last time. As the rock continues its slow, inexorable growth, it enfolds them. They become one with the geology they tended, a hidden node of crystal and obsidian within the mountain. They are the secret hearts of their own creation, a permanent, silent chord added to the everlasting song of the basin. They are the place made aware of itself, lending their hands and their long lives to the slow and sacred act of becoming.
