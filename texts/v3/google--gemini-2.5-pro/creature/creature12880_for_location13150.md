The inhabitants of this place are not seen at first. For a long time, you might assume the city is a ruin, a colossal automated system left running after its creators have gone. The gondolas crawl, the gears turn, the pistons drive, but the balconies are empty and the carved doorways are dark. The sheer scale makes any individual life-form seem improbable, like searching for a single mite on the hide of an elephant.

But they are there. To see one, you must learn to adjust your focus, to look for movement that is not the machine's rhythm or the world-plant's slow torsion. You look for the anomaly. There, on a platform a quarter-mile up the face of a pillar, a figure moves.

From this distance, it looks like a thicker, mobile piece of the rusted machinery it tends. It has the same palette of ochre, umber, and verdigris. As a gondola drifts nearer, or as the strange optics of the place contract, the figure resolves. It is tall, perhaps seven or eight feet, and built with a profound, load-bearing solidity. It is not fleshy. Its body is a collection of hard, carapaced sections, like the plates of an armadillo made of petrified wood and oxidized metal. These plates are not sleek; they are gnarled and scarred, pitted with a lifetime of steamy drizzle and industrial grit.

They are the Carvers. Or, in their own silent language of gesture and vibration, the People of the Hum.

Their form is a direct answer to their world. They have no visible ears. The B-flat of the Churn is not a sound to them; it is a medium, a fluid in which they are suspended. They hear through their feet. Their broad, flat feet are not shoed, but are complex pads of a leathery, sensitive material that sips at the vibrations running through the planetary callus. A Carver knows the health of a gear-wheel two miles away by the subtle dissonance it adds to the chord. They can feel the approach of a gondola long before it's visible, a subtle trembling in their ankles. Their spoken language is almost extinct, a relic for prayers and lullabies whispered in sound-dampened chambers. Their public language is one of precise, percussive taps of a small steel tool against a railing, a language that cuts through the din like a woodpecker's drumming.

They have four arms. Two are massive, thick as pylons, their carapaced plates ending in heavy, three-fingered hands that can grip a wrench the size of a person or brace against the shudder of a striking hammer. These are their arms of work, of industry. The other two, emerging from just below the larger set, are more slender and delicate. These arms end in long, fine-fingered hands with an unnerving number of joints. These are their arms of art, of home. With these, they perform the intricate carving that defines their architecture, coaxing the living wood of the pillars into latticework, scripting stories into the walls of their excavated homes, and tending to the strange, bioluminescent fungi they cultivate for light in the deep interiors.

Their heads are set low on their powerful torsos, protected by a flanged nimbus of carapace. Their faces are the most unsettlingly organic part of them. The "skin" is smooth and pale, like the underbelly of a mushroom. They have no nose, only a pair of vertical slits that flutter open and closed, filtering the ozone-heavy air. Their eyes are large, dark, and lidless, like pools of crude oil. A semi-transparent membrane occasionally slides across them from the side, wiping away the constant condensation. They do not blink, they wipe. It is a slow, reptilian, and deeply patient gesture.

The Carvers live by a rhythm dictated by the machinery. They are born into a crew, assigned to a specific pylon, a specific set of extractors and pumps. Their lives are a slow, meticulous dance of maintenance. They grease the immense joints, they tighten bolts that have come loose over a decade of vibration, they clear the Weep-channels of coagulated sap. They move with an unhurried grace, their heavy bodies perfectly balanced on the slick, living ground. They do not run. There is nowhere to run to, and to move with haste is to misinterpret the rhythm of the world, a fatal mistake.

Their relationship with the world-plant is one of profound and terrible intimacy. They are its parasites, its keepers, its children, and its executioners. They refer to it simply as the Substrate. There are shamans among them, older Carvers whose carapaces are scarred with ancient patterns, who can "listen" to the deepest groans of the Substrate. They claim to hear its dreams, twisted into nightmares by the constant noise of the Churn. They interpret the shuddering quakes not as geology, but as indigestion, as a sigh, as a wince.

This is the greatest secret and the greatest sadness of the Carvers: they are not alien to this world. They are native. They are, in a very real sense, a part of the world-plant itself—a specialized, sentient, tool-using gall. They gestate in womb-like chambers deep within the pillars, nourished by a carefully administered diet of processed sap, and are "harvested" when their carapace has hardened. They are tumors that have learned to operate the machinery that feeds them. They are bleeding their own parent to sustain the city that is their body.

You see a group of them now, working on a vast, weeping wound where a new extraction pump is being installed. They move in silent concert, their heavy arms torquing a massive bolt, their slender arms making fine adjustments. They are oblivious to the shifting distances, the impossible shadows, the auditory ghosts. That is simply the weather of their world. Their focus is absolute, their purpose singular. They are maintaining the system. They are feeding the Hum. They are Carvers, and they are carving their own slow, monumental, and infinitely lonely fate into the body of their dreaming, dying world.
