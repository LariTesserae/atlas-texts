They are the Silent Crew. They are the Janitors of the Great Root.

They move like ghosts within the machine, their forms delineated not by flesh but by the bulky, insulated exosuits they inhabit. These suits are not sleek instruments of exploration; they are ponderous, utilitarian things, much like the platforms they tend. The outer layer is a patchwork of reinforced, charcoal-gray fabric and scuffed ceramic plates, designed to ward off the apathetic cold and glancing blows from falling crystalline shards. Their helmets are featureless ovoids of polished black, the faceplates permanently polarized to a mirrored orange that reflects the sodium lamps, making them indistinguishable from one another at a distance. They are anonymized by their own life support, their humanity sealed beneath layers of technology.

They do not speak. The roar makes vocalization a fool's errand, a waste of precious air. Over countless generations born into this thrumming silence, their language has migrated from the larynx to the hands. They communicate in a complex, rapid-fire sign language, a flurry of gloved fingers and precise palm rotations performed close to the chest. It is a language of pure economy, stripped of flourish, designed for clarity in the dim light. A supervisor might convey a complex diagnostic with a few sharp twists of the wrist. Two tenders, passing on a gantry, might share a silent observation about a pressure fluctuation with a brief, intricate dance of their fingers. Their conversations are islands of precise, silent motion in a world of deafening, monolithic vibration.

To see one without their suit is a rare and almost unsettling intimacy. Underneath, they are pale, with the chalky complexion of beings who have never felt natural sunlight. Their eyes, wide and accustomed to the omnipresent dimness, seem dark and deep. They are slender, almost gaunt, their musculature honed for the specific tasks of their existence: the wiry strength in their arms and hands for climbing and manipulating machinery, the endurance in their legs for standing long shifts on the vibrating steel decks. They are physically marked by the environment; a permanent, faint tremor can be seen in their hands when at rest, a neurological echo of the world’s constant shudder.

Their relationship with the great being they live upon is one of profound, geological indifference. They do not think of it as a creature. It is simply "The Floor" or "The Wood." Its life operates on a timescale so far removed from their own that it is, for all practical purposes, inanimate. The sap they harvest is no more alive to them than oil is to a roughneck or iron ore to a miner. It is a resource, the very foundation of their existence, the purpose for the machines that give them warmth and light. The roar of the drills is not the sound of a wound; it is the sound of home. It is the steady, reassuring heartbeat of the world.

Their lives are ritualized and slow, dictated by the glacial pace of the harvest and the meticulous demands of the machinery. They are born on the platforms, their first sensation the deep, bone-felt thrum that will accompany them until their death. They learn the cant of the machinery like a catechism, the meaning of a shift in the harmonic overtone of a specific pump, the feel of a bearing beginning to wear through the sole of a boot. Their society is cellular, each platform a self-contained clan, a family bound by the shared duty to their particular drill. Travel between platforms is a major expedition, a dangerous trek across the slick, treacherous root-mat, pursued by the absolute cold and the disorienting dark.

They are keepers of a process whose original purpose is likely lost to them, a forgotten directive from a mythical "Up." They know only the cycle: maintain the drills, monitor the flow, repair the conduits, replace the worn parts. The viscous, glowing sap is pumped into vast, silent holding tanks, and from there... it goes. Up. Through colossal pipelines that run alongside the taproot pillars, ascending into a darkness so complete it has become abstract. They do not question where it goes, or why. The question itself is a foreign concept, as meaningless as asking why the cold is cold. Their universe is the platform, its light, its warmth, its ceaseless, thunderous noise.

Sometimes, a tender on a maintenance check will walk to the edge of a gantry and look out. The beam from their helmet-lamp cuts a fleeting, dusty cone into the oppressive black. It illuminates the intricate, frozen weave of the roots below, the sharp, glittering edges of a fresh crystalline fracture on a nearby pillar. Beyond the beam’s reach is the rest of their world: a space filled not with emptiness, but with the physical presence of the sound, and the immeasurable, patient mass of the wood. In that moment, the Weaver is a spark of contained life and warmth, a conscious anomaly afloat on a slow, dreaming, subterranean sea, feeling the pulse of their two symbiotic lives—the machine's and the root's—vibrating together as one. Then they will turn, their mirrored faceplate reflecting the orange glow of their home, and get back to work.
