They are the Humbled. Not because they are pious, but because the Grand Lignin permits no other state of being. They are the human fauna of this place, as adapted to its rhythms and dangers as the scale-moths are to the sodium lamps. To see one is to see a silhouette of pragmatism, a body sculpted by necessity.

They are broad, but not with muscle so much as with a low-slung, balanced density. Their movements are sure-footed and economical, born from a lifetime of navigating slick, narrow gantries hundreds of feet above a floor that seethes with life. Their skin, where it is ever exposed, has a pale, waxy sheen, an evolutionary response, perhaps, to the perpetual damp and a constant rain of chemical byproducts and organic detritus. It doesn't tan; it stains. Generations of exposure to the unique atmospheric compounds have left faint, permanent mottling on their faces and hands, patterns that mimic the fungal blooms on the walls, a kind of slow, human verdigris.

Their true face is the respirator. It is the first thing they don as a child and the last thing removed from them in death. These are not simple masks, but complex, self-contained breathing apparatuses, their filters and cartridges customized for the specific toxins of a worker's station. An Engineer's might be studded with extra sensors for ozone spikes, while a Scrapper's, who works the lower levels, will have heavy-duty scrubbers for boiled biological matter. The respirator is the center of their identity. The hard-shelled casings are often the only canvas for personal expression: a scratched-in sigil, the name of a child, a kill-count of raptor-flies, or a splash of scavenged pigment. Behind the thick, goggle-like lenses, their eyes are wide and accustomed to the half-light, flinching involuntarily in the direct, brutal glare of a work lamp.

They are a people whose primary language is not speech. The incessant thrumming of the pumps and the shriek of machinery make conversation a taxing, shouted affair. Instead, they communicate through a rich, percussive sign language. They rap knuckles on iron railings in complex rhythms, a code that can travel the length of a gantry. They use precise, economical hand gestures honed to be visible in dim light or through swirling steam. Their spoken words are short, clipped, and technical—"Pressure drop, B-line," "Scale-fall heavy," "Purge warning." It is the language of a ship's crew in a permanent storm.

The Humbled see the world through vibration. They can feel the difference between the shudder of a failing pump bearing and the foundational thrum of normal operation through the soles of their cleated, often magnetized, boots. They know the distinctive *skitter-thump* of a fist-sized grease-beetle losing its grip and falling from the ceiling, and they can sense the subtle, collective tremor of an approaching ant-swarm. This tactile awareness is their most crucial sense, a constant flow of information that warns them of industrial failure or biological threat long before their eyes or ears can.

There is a stark caste system written into the architecture of the City. At the bottom are the Sump-Workers and Scrappers, who live and work on the lowest, wettest levels, closest to the floor. They are the ones who dredge the drainage channels and clear the intakes of chitinous debris. Their gear is perpetually slimed, their life expectancy measured in shifts. They are grim, silent figures, often seen moving through the blue-green fungal light below the main platforms. They have a closer relationship with the native fauna, born of proximity and necessity; they know which slugs are edible when boiled, and how to use the glowing fungi to create light-traps for protein.

Above them are the Drill-Riggers, the Pipe-fitters, and the Gantry-men, the core workforce. They inhabit the bustling middle strata, their lives dictated by the twenty-hour cycle of extraction. They are the ones who wrestle with the machinery, who bleed the Wood, and who maintain the iron parasite. Their communities are small, tight-knit pods of corrugated sheds bolted around a pillar, their 'neighborhoods' defined by the specific system they maintain.

At the very top, in the highest, driest, and most sealed sections of the Refinery, live the Chemists and Engineers. They are the aristocracy of the Grand Lignin. They rarely descend, living in pressurized, climate-controlled modules with recycled air and hydroponic food. They are paler, thinner, their bodies not bearing the same dense, grounded stamp of the lower workers. They interact with the Lignin and its lifeblood as pure data, readings on a screen, pressures in a pipe. To the workers below, they are as alien as the idea of an open sky.

The children, the Lignin-born, are a strange and beautiful terror. They learn to climb the iron lattice before they can walk on a flat surface. Their games involve mimicking the frantic scuttling of beetles or seeing who can hold their breath the longest during a minor steam-vent. They wear tiny, functional respirators and are taught to identify the shrieks and clicks of the ecosystem as other children are taught animal sounds. They have a feral grace, utterly at home in the vertical world of iron and noise. For them, this is the whole universe: the warm, breathing Wood; the hard, hot City; the constant rain of scales; the thumping heart of the pumps; and the promise of the Purge, a time to huddle together in the high-up dark and listen to the world scream.

These people are not conquerors. They have no sense of dominion. They are survivors, tenders of a machine they may no longer fully understand, lodged in the flesh of a being whose scale they cannot comprehend. They extract the sap, process it, and ship it... somewhere. To them, the 'why' is irrelevant. All that matters is the cycle. The thrum of the pump, the hiss of the steam, the clicking of life in the shadows, and the constant, humble negotiation for one more shift, one more breath, inside the body of a sleeping god.
