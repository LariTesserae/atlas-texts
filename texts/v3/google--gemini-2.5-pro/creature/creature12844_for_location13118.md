What lives here are the Weepers.

They are not a species, for that implies a population of separate individuals born of evolutionary competition. They are more like manifestations, mobile aspects of the world-organism itself. To a traveller, they might first appear as mirages, heat-shimmer given form. They are tall, impossibly slender figures, their shape vaguely humanoid only in its verticality and bipedal grace. Seeing one on the horizon is like seeing a line drawn in charcoal against the searing white sky.

Their substance is a refinement of the world’s own bioceramic shell. Where the ground is rough, pocked, and bone-like, their bodies are smooth and polished to a finish like sea-glass, lustrous and cool gray. Their forms are seamless, flowing from long, delicate limbs to a torso that is little more than a thickening of the stem, topped with a featureless, ovoid head that tilts and turns with an unnerving slowness. They have no face, no eyes, no mouth, no sensory organs that we would recognize. Their surface is utterly blank, a mirror for the oppressive sky.

The Weepers are the cool counterpoint to the world's fever. While the ground bakes, their bodies are perpetually cool, acting as natural condensers. This is their most defining characteristic and the source of their name. Even at the height of the day's heat, their surfaces are slick with moisture, coalesced from the thick, humid air. Tiny rivulets of pure water are always tracing paths down their limbs, dripping silently from their fingertips to the hot ground below, where each drop sizzles into steam in an instant. They are walking points of precipitation, figures of living dew.

Their movement is their purpose. They are solitary pilgrims on an endless journey across the face of their own greater body. They do not walk so much as glide, their feet barely seeming to press upon the substrate. They leave no footprints. Their pace is agonizingly slow, a serene and measured drift that might take them a full day to cross a visible mile. They move in straight lines for immense distances, their paths changing only when they encounter a newly forming ridge or a significant shift in the substrate. In these moments, they will pause, sometimes for days, their heads tilted as if listening to the deep, tectonic groans of the world-flesh. They are the world's itinerant senses, its proprioception made manifest, wandering to feel the subtle strains and pressures of its own slow growth.

They are drawn to water. When the evening fog settles and the great, shallow pools form, the Weepers will alter their course to stand in them. They stand motionless in the inches-deep water, sometimes in groups of two or three that have converged from different headings, though they show no sign of communication or recognition. They are not drinking; they are absorbing. The water is drawn up into their porous structure, replenishing what they lose to the day's heat. This is also how they feed, filtering the sparse mineral residues and organic compounds left behind by the last evaporation cycle. They are sustained by the world's exhalations.

They have a relationship with the other, simpler forms of life. They are drawn to the Ochre Mats, and will stand at the edge of the rust-red plains for long periods, passive observers to the slow, pulsing life of the biofilm. They seem to find solace near the Sinter Spines. A Weeper might end a long journey at the base of a great quartz-like thorn, resting its smooth head against the cool, crystalline structure. In these moments, it is as if a nerve is touching a bone, the conscious part of the world connecting with its skeletal framework.

Their genesis and end are as quiet as their existence. They are not born. They emerge. In a region of deep geologic stillness, a hairline fissure might weep a different kind of fluid, a thicker, pearlescent gel. Over a cycle of cooling and heating, this gel hardens and grows, taking shape vertically. Eventually, a Weeper will uncurl from the ground like a fern, detaching itself from its point of origin to begin its silent ambulation.

They do not die of age or sickness. When their form is somehow compromised—perhaps caught in a major plate shift and fractured, or after millennia of slow travel—they seek a final resting place. They find a deep, stable depression and simply lie down. Their form does not decay. Instead, over decades and centuries, the living substrate grows over them, reabsorbing their refined material, their substance dissolving back into the planetary whole. The information they have gathered—the feeling of a million years of walking, the subtle shifts in temperature and pressure, the memory of water—is folded back into the world's deep, metabolic mind.

To encounter a Weeper is a profound and unsettling experience. It is to witness a thought in the mind of a god. They are solitude given form, the quiet, lonely consciousness of a planet-sized creature, walking its own skin and weeping for its own heat.
