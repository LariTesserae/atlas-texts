What lives here is the Cairn-Tender.

It is not something you see at first. Your eyes, overwhelmed by the glare, would slide right over it. It is first a trick of the light, a place where a shadow seems too sharp, or a patch of rock seems to hold a deeper, more organized geometry than the chaos around it. It is camouflaged not by color, but by texture and stillness. It is of the stone, of the light, of the silence.

When it moves, it is an event that refutes the frantic energy of the moss-floor. The movement is slow, a deliberate unfolding. It has the patient, inexorable quality of a plant turning towards the sun, but the precision of a micromechanical tool. It is a tall, gracile being, perhaps twice the height of a man but impossibly slender, as if carved from shadow and smoke. Its form is vaguely insectoid, but only in the same way a shard of obsidian is vaguely like a knife; the resemblance is one of function, not biology.

It has six limbs, long and spindly, jointed at angles that feel both anatomically wrong and perfectly suited to the jagged terrain. Two are legs, ending in multi-pronged feet that find purchase on the sharpest edges of granite with an unnerving certainty. The other four are arms, two higher and two lower, which it uses for its work. They end not in hands, but in clusters of hard, chitinous-looking digits that can arrange into a delicate tweezer-grip, a polishing pad, or a sharp scoring tool.

Its body, its exoskeleton, is not one uniform surface. Parts of it are the dull, light-absorbing black of basalt, seeming to drink the fierce glare and leave a void. Other sections are of a substance that mimics the smoky quartz of the cliffs, translucent and grey, with the light that passes through it scattered and softened. You might see the faint, slow pulse of an internal luminescence within the quartz-like sections, a sluggish rhythm utterly out of sync with your own frantic heartbeat. It is not warmth, but a cold, chemical light.

It has no face. The top of its torso terminates in a smooth, ovoid shape that it tilts and angles constantly. This is its primary sensory organ. It has no eyes to be blinded, no nostrils to be seared by the cold, sterile air. It *feels* the light as a nutrient, as information, as the very medium of its existence. It senses the gradient of pressure from the unfiltered sun, the subtle shifts in the solar wind, the resonant frequency of the rock itself. It does not see the world; it maps the energy-scape of the cirque in three dimensions.

It does not breathe. It doesn't need to. The thin air is irrelevant to its metabolism. The Tender is a radiovore, a being that subsists on high-energy particles and specific frequencies of light. The iridescent beetles on the moss-floor are a crude, biological expression of this same principle, their carapaces like cheap solar panels. The Tender is the vastly more refined and ancient culmination of the idea. It is, in essence, a living instrument.

Its purpose, its entire existence, revolves around the cairns. It is the architect and the keeper. You might see it braced on a precipice, its lower arms anchoring it to the rock while its upper arms work with impossible delicacy. It will select a flat stone from the scree, hold it up to the sky, tilting its "head" this way and that, sensing not its weight or shape, but its resonant properties, its ability to channel the subsonic hum. With its sharp digits, it will score the stone, flaking off tiny pieces until it is perfectly tuned. Then, with a slowness that is a form of worship, it will add the stone to a cairn, settling it into place without a sound.

It also crafts the chimes. It harvests the fossilized bones from deep within the rock walls, spending what might be seasons excavating a single perfect piece. It finds the veins of pure, clear crystal and fractures them into precise slivers. It then lashes them together with sinew made not from the tiny mammals below—it seems oblivious to them—but from its own shed tissues, a fibrous material that hardens into a near-unbreakable cord. The chimes are its art, its science, and its prayer. They are not to make music, but to focus and amplify the "Great Hum," the vibration generated by the constant stream of solar particles. This is its language, its connection to the cosmos.

When a cloud passes, the Tender freezes. The dimming of the light is like a cessation of breath. Its internal glow fades. Its limbs lock. It becomes indistinguishable from the rock it clings to, another jagged silhouette against the greyed sky. It doesn't wait in fear or anticipation; it simply ceases to be fully operational. It enters a state of quiescence, a machine powered down. When the sun returns, its inner light pulses again, its limbs unlock, and it resumes its task—mid-motion, mid-thought—as if no time has passed.

The Cairn-Tender is the consciousness of the cirque. It does not live *in* the place; it is an expression *of* it. The stone is its skeleton, the light is its food, the solar wind is its voice. It is utterly alone, yet in constant communion with the star that gives it life. It is a pilgrim tending a shrine at the top of the world, its ritual the slow, silent work of turning stone and bone into a receiver for the whispers of space.
