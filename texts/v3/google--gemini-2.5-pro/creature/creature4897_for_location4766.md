They are the Weavers.

You do not see them at first, and perhaps, you never will. To see a Weaver requires a letting go of the time scale of your own life, an adjustment of perception so profound that it borders on a geological empathy. They are not creatures *on* the landscape; they are a slow, living modulation *of* it.

Imagine lying with your cheek against the warm, weeping stone, letting the bass hum of the Conduits vibrate through your bones. You close your eyes, and for a week, or a month, you do not move. You feel the slow flow of water, the pulse of the subterranean heat. In that stillness, you might begin to perceive them. A shadow in a wide, placid pool that is not a shadow, but a form. A length of the black, slick stone that seems, over the course of a day, to have become more curved.

A Weaver is a long, sinuous being, sometimes hundreds of feet from end to end. They have no face, no limbs, no discernible organs of the kind you would recognize. Their bodies are a seamless fusion of the organic and the geologic. Their skin is a living, flexible variant of the black glass-stone of the plateau, possessing the same faint internal lattice that refracts the hazy light. It is cool to the touch, not with the cold of dead stone, but with the deep, self-contained coolness of a functioning machine. They are neither amphibious nor reptilian; they are something other, a life form born of a technobiological process.

For most of their existence, they are still. They lie submerged in the great basins of warm, mineral-rich water, their forms barely distinct from the contoured floor. They are not sleeping. They are working. Their entire bodies are a sensory organ, attuned to the network of this world. They feel the hum of the Condugits not as sound, but as a constant stream of information, a language of pure frequency. They taste the subtle shifts in the water's composition as it weeps from the stone and flows over their vast surfaces. They are the living regulators of the terrarium.

When a Weaver moves, it is an event on the scale of a season changing. It does not crawl or swim. It flows. One end of its immense body will begin to lift, peeling itself from the stone with an impossibly slow grace, like a tongue lifting from a frozen surface. It moves with a peristaltic ripple, a liquid reconfiguration of its own mass, pouring itself over the rolling hills of the plateau. The sheepdog-sized amphibians will placidly move out of its path, sensing the shift in the stone's resonance hours before the movement becomes visible. The journey from one basin to another might take a decade.

Their purpose is maintenance. They are the partners of the Conduits. While the ancient ceramic engines provide the raw heat and the foundational hum, the Weavers provide the finesse. They are living capacitors and catalysts. When they lie in a basin, they are drawing in ambient energy, filtering the water through their very being. Their metabolism is a silent alchemy, precipitating the vital salts and organic precursors that the dense flora requires, re-tuning the water’s memory. When an area of the jungle begins to tax the system, growing too voraciously or showing signs of mineral deficiency, a Weaver will eventually stir. It will begin its long, slow journey, a pilgrimage of redistribution, to lie within that region and restore the balance. It will wrap its body around the base of one of the banyan-like trees, its skin making a perfect, wet seal with the grasping roots, and for a century it will feed the tree directly with subtle electrical potentials and tailored mineral solutions.

They do not communicate in any way you could record. Their dialogue is the landscape itself. A change in the water chemistry in one basin is a message. A subtle fluctuation in the Conduit hum is a question. The movement of a Weaver is a response. This conversation has been ongoing for millions of years, a slow, planetary thought process.

They do not breed. They emerge. When a Conduit has functioned for an eon, and its energetic output has reached a certain state of unfathomable complexity, a new Weaver will begin to form at its base. It starts as a flaw in the bone-white ceramic, a place where the pervasive moisture finally seems to gain purchase. Over millennia, this "flaw" will darken and grow, budding outward, a crystalline structure taking on the properties of life. It grows with the speed of a stalactite, indistinguishable from the surrounding geology until, after ten thousand years, it has reached a critical length. Then, one day, it will detach and begin its first slow, sinuous flow into the world, already knowing its purpose, already part of the hum.

And when a Weaver's long work is done, it will find a deep, isolated basin, coil itself into a perfect spiral, and become still. Over the next hundred thousand years, its living energy will dissipate back into the ceaseless hum, and its body will slowly calcify, becoming a formation of black glass-stone indistinguishable from the rest of the plateau—a new hill, a new contour in the weeping world it spent its long life tending.

You are a flicker to them. A momentary, high-frequency vibration in the air, too fast to be data, too brief to be real. Should you touch one, you would feel only the slick, cool, unyielding surface of the world itself. They are the gardeners in a garden the size of a nation, the keepers of an engine whose purpose is its own continuation. They are the quiet, unending will of the plateau made manifest. They live here.
