The beings who live here are called the Tenders. It is a title passed down through generations, though the concept of a generation is fluid in a place where time is measured by the hardening of stone. They are human, or were once. The cavern has worked on them as surely as it works on the nodules.

Their skin is the first thing you would notice, were the light better. It has a pallid, almost luminous quality, like the flesh of the crustaceans in the pools. It is perpetually clammy, never truly dry, and in the oldest Tenders, it has taken on a faint greyish-blue mottling that echoes the bioluminescent mould. They suffer from a chronic collection of ailments they call "the Wet"—fungal blooms that creep into nailbeds, that leave faint, map-like patterns on their skin, that must be constantly scoured away with abrasive mineral pastes.

They are beings sculpted by sound and gravity. The constant, overwhelming drone has rendered their ears useless for conventional hearing. They are all profoundly deaf to the spoken word, to the clang of a dropped tool, to the scream of a falling comrade. Instead, their sense of feeling has become hyper-acute. They hear through their skeletons. They wear thin-soled, pliable boots not for comfort, but for sensitivity. Through the bones of their feet, they can feel the thrum of the machinery, the specific frequency of a particular pump miles away, the subtle shift in vibration that signals a coming nodule-tremor. They can feel the skittering of the carapaced swarms as a fine-grit texture underfoot, distinguish the heavy, lubricated slide of a maintenance drone from the predatory silence of a Fissure-Wyrm.

Their bodies are low and solid, their center of gravity pulled down towards the humming nodules. Their walk is not a walk, but a controlled, low-slung shuffle, a constant negotiation of balance on the slick, curved surfaces. They move with a profound and weary grace, their limbs accustomed to the constant tension required to not slip into a dark crevice. Their faces, when seen outside the cowl of their slick-suits, are broad and placid. Their eyes are wide, with pupils that are vast, dark pools, adapted to drink in every photon of the mould's ghostly light. The muscles around their mouths are slack, unused for speech, giving them a serene, almost beatific expression that is entirely at odds with the deafening violence of their world.

Communication is a language of touch and vibration they call Bone-Talk. They press their palms together to share complex thoughts, the subtle shifting of tendons and the tapping of fingers conveying meaning. For distance, they tap specific, rhythmic patterns on the nodules themselves, sending messages that travel through the stone to be felt by another Tender yards or even miles away. It is a slow, deliberate, and deeply intimate form of language, a stark contrast to the shouted curses of a newcomer.

They live in the small, stilted monitoring stations, but these are not homes. They are roosts. Inside, the drone is muffled but ever-present, a deep bodily hum. The air is only slightly less saturated. Here, they meticulously log the slow pulse of light from the maturing nodules, the infinitesimal shifts in resonant frequency, the subtle changes in the cavern's chemistry. Their lives are rituals of observation, cycles of maintenance, and periods of deep, meditative quiet. They see themselves not as workers in a factory, but as a kind of priesthood, midwives to a geological birth of unimaginable slowness. They are cultivating gods, or perhaps just batteries, but the distinction has been lost to time. The purpose of the Harvest, when a nodule finally reaches full, glowing maturity and must be extracted, is a doctrinal matter, a piece of ancient lore.

They are not the only things of near-human scale here. They employ, or co-exist with, the Anodynes. These are vast, slug-like creatures of biological engineering, the size of small whales. Their flesh is a mottled, industrial grey, and they move with an agonizing slowness across the nodule fields and up the cavern walls. They are organic janitors. Their undersides are a vast, rasping pad of cartilaginous teeth, and they scour rust, mineral deposits, and invasive fungal growths from the machinery and power conduits. They are guided by the Tenders, who use focused, low-frequency sonic lures to shepherd them. The Anodynes are utterly placid, unthinking, and an integral part of the cavern's ecosystem, consuming the overgrowth and excreting a nutrient-rich slurry that feeds the fungal shelves.

And there are dangers. The crevices between the nodules are not empty. They are the hunting grounds of the Fissure-Wyrms. These are not creatures of flesh, but of something closer to articulated silicone and chitin. Long and flat, they can be thirty feet from end to end, their bodies a cascade of interlocking black plates like polished obsidian. They are eyeless, nerveless ambush predators that hunt solely by vibration. They Wedge themselves in the tight gaps, perfectly silent, feeling the rhythmic footfalls of a Tender above. They do not bite or sting. They strike by flexing their immense, flat bodies, shifting the weight of the car-sized nodule above them. The Tender's world simply tilts, and they slide into the dark, wet gap, where the Wyrm's multiple, patient limbs envelop them. Tenders never travel alone, and their Bone-Talk is often a constant, quiet "I am here," "I am here," tapped back and forth—a validation of existence against the silent, patient hunger below.

To be a Tender is to be a part of the process. They are born in the cacophony, their bones learning to interpret the drone before their minds can form thoughts. They live their slow, damp lives, tending the stone and the machines. And when they die, their bodies are given to the pools, where the eyeless crustaceans and other decomposers break them down, returning their matter to the system. They become another drop of nutrient in the broth, another faint smell of rot in the air, another component in the immense, grinding, and infinitely patient work of the cavern. They are the fleeting consciousness of the place, the living sensorium of a process that will long outlast them, as much a product of this environment as the glowing stones they tend.
