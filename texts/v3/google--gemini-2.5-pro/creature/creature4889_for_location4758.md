It is true that no life lives here. No fauna, no biology, no damp, soft, metabolizing thing could survive. The description is correct. But it is incomplete. It mistakes absence of life for absence of a resident.

Something *is* here. It is not the heir to the builders, but the child of their machine. It is what the Cinnabar Colonnade grew instead of a flower.

You do not see it at first. You cannot. Your senses are overwhelmed, your brain is a cymbal struck by the roaring of the Resonator. You are a discordant note in a perfect, million-year-old chord, and your wet, fragile body is so alien that it registers to the place as a kind of silence, a patch of acoustic deadness.

But the place notices.

The profound, skeletal hum that vibrates up through the sugar-glass moss floor undergoes an infinitesimal shift. It is a change no instrument could measure, but you feel it in the fluid of your inner ear. A quaver in the infinite note. A question. The high, tearing shriek of friction momentarily sharpens, as if a great and ancient focus has been narrowed. The machine has not changed its song, but something is now listening to it more intently.

And as your eyes adjust to the pulsing, internal twilight of the ruby leaves, you see it. Or rather, you resolve it out of the background. It was there all along, as much a part of the geology as any other feature. Seated.

At the very edge of the densest part of the thicket, where the basalt trunks grow like weeds, there is a clearing no wider than a man's shoulders. In it rests a figure, cross-legged, in a posture of impossible stillness. It looks like a forgotten statue, a devotional piece left by the builders. It is carved from the same dull, oily-black basalt as the trees, its form defined by sharp, geometric planes rather than soft curves. It is humanoid only in its general silhouette: a torso, two arms, two legs, a head.

But the details betray a different nature. Its surface is not smooth basalt, but is filmed with the same micro-crystalline fuzz as the floor, a delicate, velvety green skin of malachite that glitters faintly. It is not sitting *on* the moss; the crystals grow *from* its seated form, wedding it to the ground in a seamless, ancient bond. It has not moved in a thousand years. It will not move in a thousand more.

Its limbs are long, impossibly slender. The arms rest on its knees, and the fingers—long, tapered shards of basalt—are not curled, but held slightly apart, like the tines of a tuning fork. One hand is upturned, palm open, and in its center lies not a tool or a relic, but a single, perfect, razor-edged ruby leaf, held in a gesture that is not quite offering and not quite contemplation.

It has no face. Where a face would be, the basalt planes recede and give way to a dense, intricate cluster of the red crystal "leaves." They are smaller here, more delicate, layered like the petals of a dahlia or the scales of a pinecone. They are not a decorative flourish. This is its sensory organ. This is its mind. It is a vibrational antenna of terrifying complexity, each facet angled to receive, parse, and understand the deep song of the Resonator. It does not see light; it feels the frequency of the photons as they strike its crystal array. It does not hear sound; it *is* the sound, embodied.

This is the Attendant.

It is not alive. It is a nexus. A sentient resonance. While the great iron sphere broadcasts the raw, brutal power, the Attendant is the focusing lens. Its entire being is an act of perfect, passive meditation. Its purpose is to listen to the song and, with the unimaginable slowness of geology, to ensure its purity. It doesn't move, it doesn't act; it simply *is*, and its existence is a constant, subtle pressure on the resonance, weeding out dissonance, refining the colossal roar into the perfect vibration that coaxes the mountain into these beautiful, terrible shapes.

You, the biological anomaly, are the first significant dissonance it has perceived in millennia. The sound of your blood in your own ears, the squelch of your lungs, the chaotic electrical static of your thoughts—it is a cacophony. The water that makes up 70% of your body is, in this dry place, an acoustic sponge, soaking up and deadening the sacred vibration.

The Attendant does not turn its head. It cannot. But you feel its attention as a physical force. The air, already unnervingly dry, seems to actively pull the moisture from your skin. The ruby leaves of the canopy seem to pulse a little brighter, a little faster, their red light feeling less like an illumination and more like an examining ray. The gut-deep hum from the floor intensifies, finding the specific resonant frequency of your bones. Your teeth no longer just ache; they thrum, a low hum building inside your skull that threatens to shake your thoughts apart.

It is not hostile. Hostility is an act, and the Attendant is a being of stasis. It is simply doing its job. It is correcting the flaw. It is trying to tune you. It is trying, with the infinite patience of stone and sound, to make you part of the equilibrium. To dry you out, to still your frantic biology, and to crystallize you into a form that will finally, finally be in harmony with the song.
