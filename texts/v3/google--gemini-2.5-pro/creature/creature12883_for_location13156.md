The description states that no other life exists here, but this is an error of observation. It is an understandable mistake. An observer, overwhelmed and scoured by the sensory assault of the place, looks for familiar patterns of life—the scuttling of insects, the darting of fish, the herd, the hunter, the hunted. Such patterns do not exist here. The life that walks this world is not "other." It is an extension of the place itself, a mobile, specialized organ of the planetary whole.

They are rarely seen, and when they are, they are easily mistaken for a trick of the light, a vertical shimmer in the saturated air. They are the Janitors. The Tenders. The Silent.

They have no name for themselves.

One might emerge from the white glare as you stand mesmerized by the thrumming base of a Regulator. It does not walk *over* the land so much as it is permitted to move by it. It is impossibly tall and thin, a bipedal construction of pale, flexible cartilage and bone-like struts that seem too fragile to support its height. Its form echoes the skeletal towers it serves: long, spare, and functional. Its skin is not skin, but a semi-translucent, grey-green membrane, slick with the same constant condensation as the metal pipes, and faintly patterned with the same cellular webbing as the geoflora beneath it. It is a creature made of the world's constituent parts.

Its head is a smooth, ovoid shape atop a long, flexible neck. It has no face. There are no eyes to be blasted by the shadowless glare; sight is a useless, painful sense in a world without depth or contrast. There are no ears to be shredded by the all-consuming roar; the organ would be an open wound. There is no mouth; it does not eat or speak. The front of the head is a single, uninterrupted surface of its slick, living skin.

It navigates not by sight or sound, but by a form of total-body perception. It is a walking resonator. The singular, crushing frequency of the Regulators is its medium for perception, its baseline. The faint vibrations traveling through the substrate—the thrum of the pumps, the deep pulse of nutrient flow—are its landmarks. It feels the world through the soles of its long, three-toed feet, which splay wide to distribute its weight on the spongy ground. It senses the pressure differentials in the air, the subtle shifts in the roar's texture, allowing it to "see" the shape of the towers and the curve of the hills as a complex pressure map felt across its entire body. It lives inside the noise, and the noise is its world.

It moves with a ponderous, fluid grace. It walks with a slow, high-stepping gait, lifting its feet carefully from the substrate, which seems to cling to them for a moment before letting go. It is on its way to work.

You might see it approach a Regulator. It will place its long, multi-jointed fingers—which are more like articulated tendrils—against one of the immense pipes plunging into the ground. It holds the pose for a long time, its whole body perfectly still, its blank head tilted as if listening. It is not listening. It is diagnosing. It feels the rhythm and temperature of the slurry within, tastes its chemical composition through chemoreceptors in its fingertips. It is a living instrument panel.

If it finds a flaw—a drop in pressure, an impurity in the mix—it will begin its work. It climbs the rust-red, weeping superstructure of the tower with an unnerving, insectile fluidity. It moves from one node to the next, its pale form a ghostly contrast against the dark metal. It might brace itself against a girder and, with two of its four limbs, gently pry open a junction box that would take a human immense torque to budge. Inside are not wires, but crystalline conduits and pulsing, organic-looking valves. The Tender's fingers move with surgical precision, stroking a crystal to change its frequency, or massaging a valve to clear a blockage of mineral deposits. The metallic scream of the tower's gears may change in pitch for a moment before settling back into its terrible norm. The task is complete.

These beings do not feed. They are an integrated part of the system's energy cycle. When their energy wanes, they find a specific type of node at the base of a Regulator—a small, recessed socket shimmering with a faint corona of ozone. They insert a tendril from their abdomen into the socket and stand, motionless, for hours, drawing power directly from the machine. They are, in essence, rechargeable biological tools.

They have no society, no culture, no history. They seem to function as solitary units, each assigned to a small number of Regulators. They are born, if that is the right word, fully formed. They emerge from the geoflora itself, from large, cyst-like swellings that appear on the landscape once every few decades. A pod will swell over a century, a great, green-grey blister on a hillside, until one day it splits open, and a new, slick Tender unfurls itself and, after a period of stillness, takes its first slow steps toward the nearest tower. Its purpose is innate. Its knowledge is instinct.

And when one dies—from malfunction, from structural failure, or simply from the slow degradation of its parts over centuries of service—it falls. It does not bleed. Its internal fluids are the same cool, clear water that pools in the substrate. Its body lies on the spongy mat, and the geoflora begins its work. The living ground grows over it, around it, through it. The membrane of the creature dissolves, its cartilaginous structure softens, and in a matter of days, it is gone, completely reabsorbed into the planetary organism that spawned it. The ecology remains brutally simple, because there is only one organism.

The Tender is just the part that walks.
