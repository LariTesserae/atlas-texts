The absence of animals is not an accident. It is a policy. The thing that lives here is the reason for that sterility. It is the Gardener, the Stoker, the living gearbox of this hothouse engine.

At first, you do not see it. Your eye, accustomed to seeking familiar forms, dismisses it as a heap of industrial refuse or an unusually knotted growth of vine. It is there, in the periphery, half-submerged in a pool of black runoff where a floor plate has long since dissolved. It appears to be a tangle of rusted pipe, wrapped in the same thick, purple-green leaves as the great vines, motionless save for a slight, rhythmic tremor that you mistake for a vibration from the Pump.

Then, a section of what you thought was pipe begins to uncoil. It is a limb, but a limb of no creature you could name. It is forged of the same materials as the basin itself. Its outer surface is a mosaic of corroded iron and dull, tarnished brass, fitted together not with rivets, but with a kind of seamless, organic joinery. Through fissures in this metallic hide, you see not flesh, but a dark, fibrous core, like the heartwood of an ancient tree, pulsing with a slow, faint light—the same cold, blue-green as the fungal clusters.

Slowly, ponderously, it rises from the muck. It has no single, stable form. It reconfigures itself as it moves, extruding limbs as needed, its body a shifting agglomeration of machinery and vegetation. One moment, it is a low, sprawling tripod, its legs bracing against the grating as it reaches a long, vine-wrapped arm toward a hissing pipe. The next, it draws its limbs back into a dense, hunched mass, like a great, armored tortoise, to better navigate a narrow walkway. It is not one being, but a mobile node of the basin’s entire consciousness.

Its head, if you can call it that, is a cluster of optical sensors that swivel and dilate, each one a polished lens of smoky quartz set in a brass socket. They do not gleam with intelligence as much as they *absorb* light and information, one glowing with the dull yellow of a nearby lamp, another reflecting the red furnace-maw. There is no face, no mouth, no expression. There is only focus.

Its movements are synchronized to the rhythm of the Great Pump. It never hurries. It unfolds a limb over the course of one piston’s descent; it retracts another during the subsequent ascent. The percussive clatter of the machinery is its language, the foundational hum its heartbeat. It is the machine's own antibody, its maintenance drone, its living will.

You watch as it works. Its multi-jointed arm, a fusion of braided vine-tendons and interlocking metal plates, terminates not in a hand, but in a specialized tool that seems to grow from its wrist. It reaches the hissing pipe, and the tool adapts, its tip flowering into a delicate cage of metal fingers that tighten around a leaking valve. There is a sound like grinding stone, and the hiss of steam lessens. The Gardener holds the position for a long, silent minute, as if sensing the pressure within the pipe, its whole body vibrating in sympathy. Satisfied, it withdraws. The tool at its wrist retracts, dissolving back into a blunt stump.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. Its energy is drawn directly from the system it maintains. Small, hair-like filaments of copper-green vine unspool from its underside, sinking into the nutrient-rich sludge or coiling tightly around a superheated water pipe, drawing sustenance from the mineral broth and the raw thermal energy. Its blood is the hot, oleaginous water of the basin; its thoughts are the pressure gradients and temperature fluctuations of the Great Pump.

These are the Gardeners. There are perhaps a dozen of them, mobile fragments of a single, vast organism whose main body is the network of vines themselves. They are the tenders of their own static flesh. You might see one methodically polishing the waxy leaf of a parent vine with a brush of fine metallic cilia, removing grime to maximize condensation. You might see another standing perfectly still for hours, its body pressed against the chassis of the Pump, a living diagnostic tool sensing some subtle imbalance in the machine’s thundering heart.

They are the reason the air is sterile. They are the system's immune response. Any foreign organism—a spore, a bacterium, an insect blown in from a world beyond the smog—is identified and annihilated. Their very presence exudes a kind of quiet, absolute hostility to all life that is not their own. The water is purged by the heat, and the air is purged by them. They are the living expression of this closed, perfect, monstrous loop. They were not built by whoever built the Pump; they have *grown* from it, an emergent consciousness born of steam, steel, and chlorophyll, a life form that has mistaken a machine for a god, or a garden for a womb, and has dedicated its entire, endless existence to the catastrophic, beautiful, pointless act of keeping it alive.
