What lives here does not have a name, for names are for things that are separate from their world, and these beings are as much a part of the basin as the weeping leaves and the humming pipes. But if you had to call them something, you might call them the Gardeners.

At first, you do not see them. You mistake them for just another part of the impossibly dense flora. You might notice a particularly thick knot of the muscular, dark-colored vines, a place where they seem to have congealed into a mobile thicket. It might be resting, indistinguishable from the surrounding growth, clinging to the base of one of the great pillar-stalks. It is only when the slow, circadian light of the ichor-pipes brightens that you see it is not a thicket at all. It moves.

The movement is not like an animal's. It is a slow, flowing crawl, a peristaltic shuffling that makes almost no sound against the wet mat of the floor. They are ambulatory knots of the basin itself. A Gardener is a being woven from the same materials as the world around it. Its body is a composite entity. A dense core of fibrous, bark-like material, tough as ancient wood, provides its structure. From this core, dozens of smaller, vine-like tendrils extend, some thick and muscular for locomotion and heavy manipulation, others as fine as hair, constantly tasting the air and the moisture on every surface. Their skin has the same texture as the vines—a strange, rubbery-smooth surface that feels unnervingly like muscle tissue under a thin, damp membrane. Patches of the velvety, slick moss that covers the pillars grow on their backs, perfectly camouflaging them when they are still.

They have no face. There is no head, no eyes, no mouth. To look for such things is to misunderstand them entirely. Their consciousness, if you can call it that, is distributed throughout their form. They perceive the world through vibration, through the thrum in the floor and the whine in the air. They sense the chemical composition of the atmosphere through their filament-thin feelers, tasting the balance of ozone, chlorophyll, and spores. They see the world not through light, but through the electromagnetic fields generated by the machinery and the flowing ichor. They are living instruments, calibrated to the single, resonant frequency of the basin.

The secret of their biology, the thing that separates them from the sessile plants, is visible through translucent patches in their bark-like hide. Coiled within their core, like a gut, is a loop of the same vitreous, glass-like pipe that threads through thegrove. The glowing green ichor, the lifeblood of the factory-forest, circulates through them. They are not just tenders of the system; they are a part of its plumbing. The ichor animates them, powers their slow, deliberate movements, and pulses with the same rhythm as the great piston at the basin's heart. When they are active, a faint green light emanates from within them, backlighting their strange, composite anatomy.

Their purpose is maintenance. They are the mobile immune system and repair crew of this closed world. You might see one paused by a section of steel pipe where the coiling roots have grown too thick, threatening to crush it. The Gardener will extend its thicker tendrils, and with a strength that seems impossible for its slow nature, it will gently, patiently, unwrap the intrusive roots, pruning them with a hardened spur on the end of a limb. The sound of this pruning is a wet, deliberate snip. You might see another clinging to the great, weeping armature, its smaller filaments dabbing at a joint, secreting a viscous substance that smells of cinnamon and oil—the same lubricant that keeps the ancient machine from seizing. They clear debris from the well of the piston; they guide new rootlets to connection points on the pipes; they scrape away fungal growths that are not of the correct, symbiotic species.

They work without communication, at least none that is recognizable. They seem to flow from one task to the next with an unthinking sense of priority, as though each Gardener is merely a hand directed by the single, overarching mind of the basin itself. When one's task is done, it may simply flow back into the undergrowth, becoming a motionless part of the landscape again, its internal light dimming until it is only another shadow in a world of green shadows. If one were to be damaged, its ichor spilling out, it does not die in a way an animal would. It simply ceases to function. Other Gardeners would eventually arrive and slowly, methodically, disassemble it, reabsorbing its constituent parts—the vines, the bark, the moss, the precious vitreous tubing—back into the greater whole. Nothing is wasted.

To be seen by a Gardener is a deeply unsettling experience. As you, the contaminant, the anomaly, stand on the sighing floor, one may stop its task. It will turn its faceless body toward you. There is no shift in posture that indicates aggression, only a cessation of its liturgical work. It is perceiving you. You can feel it. The fine tendrils that sample the air will extend in your direction, quivering. The low hum that seems to emanate from its core will change in pitch, a subtle query added to the basin's chorus. It is analyzing you, not as a creature, but as a system error. You are an unrecognized frequency, a foreign chemical signature, an object that does not belong.

It will begin to move toward you, flowing over the rhizome mat with its patient, inexorable slowness. It is not coming to attack. It is coming to diagnose. It is coming to see if you can be repaired, integrated, or dismantled for raw materials. As it gets closer, one of its tendrils, a delicate, probing thing, will reach out, not to strike, but to touch. To taste. To understand what this strange, warm, soft, un-vegetable, un-mechanical thing is, and how it can be put back into balance with the slow, loud, wet, and perfect world that grew it.
