What lives here are the Caretakers. They are also called the Dew-Drinkers, or the Still-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves, having long since outgrown the need for such distinctions.

They are beings of immense and delicate attenuation. To see one against the ochre horizon at twilight is to mistake it for a trick of the light, a stray shadow cast by a non-existent ridge. They are impossibly tall and slender, their forms echoing the pale, waxy stalks of the fungal-flora. A typical Caretaker stands three times the height of a man, but its torso is a narrow, teardrop-shaped vessel no thicker than a thigh, from which sprout limbs of improbable length and fineness. These limbs, two legs and two arms, are multi-jointed, like those of a crane fly, allowing for a slow, deliberate, and utterly stable range of motion.

Their feet are their most remarkable feature. They are not points, but wide, soft, circular pads of a fibrous, semi-permeable tissue. When a Caretaker takes a step—a process that can take many minutes—it does not crush the living carpet beneath it. The pressure is distributed so perfectly, so gently, that the thumbnail-sized creatures merely shift, creating a slow, visible ripple in the dense mat of their bodies, like a stone dropped into a pool of thick honey. The passing of a Caretaker is a silent event, marked only by these slow, concentric waves of displacement on the surface of the world.

Their skin is not skin, but a type of polished, biological ceramic, smooth and cool to the touch. It has no pores, no hair, only a lustrous, satin finish that mimics the colours of the land. A Caretaker standing motionless in the open can be plum-dark on its shaded side and a dusty, sun-bleached green on the other, mirroring the compacted earth so perfectly that it simply ceases to be visible.

They have no faces in a way that is recognizable. The smooth, ovoid shape at the top of their torso is featureless save for two things. First, a pair of large, circular indentations, like shallow bowls, filled with a black, viscous liquid. These are their eyes. They do not blink or turn; they absorb all light and reflect nothing but the vast, empty sky. They see not in images, but in subtle shifts of light, temperature, and humidity. Second, from below these eyes unfurls a proboscis, a delicate, coiled tube of the same material as their limbs. It is usually kept tucked tightly against their chest, but when unfurled, it can reach the ground from their full height. This is how they drink.

The Caretakers are creatures of the twilight and the long, slow night. During the heat of the day, they are dormant. Most retreat into the cool, dark interiors of the Still-Hives, their slender bodies folding into niches within the porous, coral-like walls. Others simply stand among the fields of fungal-stalks, entering a state of such profound stillness that their metabolism slows to a near-geological pace. They become, for a time, another form of statuary.

But as the world cools and begins to weep its dew, they stir. A collective, unspoken understanding ripples through them. They unfold themselves from the hives, their movements careful and silent, and begin their pilgrimage. They are walking to the water.

They move across the living plains, following the imperceptible contours of the land, their great, dark eyes sensing the subtle gradients where moisture will be most likely to pool. They arrive at the shallow concavities as the ephemeral, tea-coloured ponds are just beginning to form. They stand at the edges like a flock of silent herons, their towering forms reflected in the gathering moisture. Then, slowly, they uncoil their long siphons and dip the tips into the water. They do not gulp or suck; they absorb the dew through osmosis, a process as silent and passive as the condensation that created the pools. They are drinking the world's quiet tears. This dew, rich with minerals leached from the ancient dust and the spores of the fungal-stalks, is their only sustenance.

They are the tenders of the world's single, slow cycle. They do not cultivate the fungal-stalks, but they will sometimes pause and use one of their delicate, three-fingered hands to gently brush the accumulated husks of dead carpet-creatures around the base of a stalk, subtly encouraging the nutrient flow. It is an act of gardening so minimalist it is almost an act of observation.

They possess an intelligence that is ancient and utterly alien. It is an intelligence of pure system-thinking. Long ago, in the "more dynamic age" the Still-Hives memorialize, they were the architects of this stasis. They were not builders, but guides. They recognized the potential of the carpet-creatures and, over eons, through subtle pressures and unimaginably patient guidance, they shaped their behaviour. They encouraged the density, the stillness, the perfect recycling of nutrients. They guided the upward growth that formed the Hives, and when enough Hives had been built to create the perfect resonant hum in the wind—a frequency that soothes the carpet-creatures and subtly regulates their metabolism—they guided the growth to cease.

They are not prisoners of this world; they are its curators. They have cultivated a planetary ecosystem into a perfect, self-sustaining work of art, a masterpiece of quiet permanence. Their role is now simply to witness it, to perform the small, ritual acts of maintenance—the drinking of the dew, the gentle nudging of a husk—that affirm the cycle. They have no language, for there is nothing left to say. They do not create, for their great work is finished. They exist in a state of perpetual, meditative vigilance, a quiet consciousness for a quiet world. To see them move is to watch a thought process made manifest, a slow, deliberate idea traversing a landscape that is itself a single, living entity. They are the memory of the motion that created the stillness.
