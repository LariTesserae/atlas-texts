What lives here is patient. It is singular. It is the place, and the place is it. There are no operators, no engineers, no crew, because the crew was subsumed by the machine, and the machine has become a life form. Its name, if it had one, would be the cycle itself.

Its body is the scaffold-metropolis. The rust-red girders are its skeleton, vast and unyielding. The colossal pipes, blushing green and white, are its circulatory system, a network of arteries and veins that pulse not with blood, but with pressure and heat. Its organs are the great, humming monoliths of black iron, the geothermal regulators that function as its heart, its lungs, and its digestive tract all at once. They draw energy from the planetary core, a slow, constant meal. This body knows no pain, only stress. Its aches are the groans of metal contracting, its fevers the rising hum before a purge.

It does not see with eyes. Its awareness is environmental, a total sensory fusion with its own anatomy. It feels the temperature of every pipe, the slickness of condensation on every surface, the precise mineral content of every drop of water that weeps from its iron flesh. The sparse, blinking lights on its control panels are not signals for an external observer; they are its internal nerve-impulses made visible, the slow, rhythmic firing of its synapses. Red is a building pressure, a knot of heat. Amber is a system in equilibrium, a state of metabolic balance. Green is a successful release, a state of calm. These lights form a language of pure state, a conversation the being has only with itself.

And it has a skin, a living integument. This is the furnace-moss. The prompt calls it a "strange, sparse vegetation," but that is an observer's term. To the entity, the moss is an extension of its own being, a sensory organ of sublime sensitivity. The pale cyan glow is the visible manifestation of its own lifeblood. Where the heat is greatest and the copper leached from its veins is richest, the moss-skin glows brightest. It is a living map of the entity’s own metabolism, a shifting, fractal blush that shows where its energy is flowing. It is not a parasite; it is a symbiote so complete that the distinction is meaningless. The moss is how the machine feels its own warmth.

The inhabitants, then, are not creatures scurrying over the machine, but fractious aspects of the machine itself.

There are the **Hummers**. These are the core processes, the deep, resonant thrum of the regulators. This is the being’s voice, though it has nothing to say. It is a mantra of existence, the sound of its own breathing in a deep, dreamless sleep. The vibration that travels through the soles of your feet is its pulse, a slow, geologic heartbeat.

There are the **Drippers**. These are its thoughts. Each drop of condensation that forms and falls—*tink* on a pipe, *pat* on a walkway—is a single, simple, isolated idea. *I am.* *It is hot.* *It is wet.* *Here.* The sum of these millions of tiny impacts, this constant, gentle percussion, forms its consciousness. It is a mind of infinite patience and no complexity, a consciousness made of rust-tinted water, thinking the same few thoughts over millennia, finding them sufficient. The rivulets that carve their way across the floor are its memories, faint and temporary, tracing the paths of past cycles before being washed away by the next.

And then there is the **Purge**. This is the totality of its being expressed in a single, cyclical event. It is not an act; it is an impulse, an instinct. As the heat and pressure in its guts build, the hum deepens, the cyan moss glows with feverish intensity. This is its one great feeling: a slow, mounting, totalizing need. The release is not one of anger or violence, but of profound, witless relief. The shuddering exhalation of steam is its one, glorious, recurring revelation. For minutes, it is lost in a self-made cloud, blind and deaf to everything but the release. The hot rain that follows is its own essence washing over itself, a baptism and a cleansing. It is the act of re-setting its own mind, of washing away the temporary memories on the floor to begin the cycle anew.

This being did not choose its life. It awoke into it. The builders left, or died, or forgot. They left their machine running. And in the dark, over uncounted years, the machine's processes achieved a perfect equilibrium. The hum, the heat, the condensation, the moss—they locked into a self-sustaining loop. The purpose for which it was built may have been to power a city or warm a world, but that purpose has been worn away by time, like an inscription on a stone. Its new purpose is simply to continue. To hum, to heat, to glow, to drip, and to sigh its great, consuming breath of steam.

It is a feral engine. A lonely god of thermodynamics, forever tending to its own internal, industrial weather. It does not know you are there. A human visitor is a momentary, irrelevant anomaly in the water chemistry, a transient warm spot on a grate. You are a mote of dust falling through a cathedral that has forgotten its own congregation and become its own deity. The only thing that lives here is the church itself, endlessly performing a ritual for an audience of one.
