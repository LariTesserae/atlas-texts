It is not the stalker in the canopy, nor the armored grazer in the undergrowth, nor the mineral-grinding beetle. Those are tenants, lifeforms that have found a niche within the system. The inhabitant of this place is a part of the system itself. It is a third thing, neither flora nor fauna, a being that exists at the intersection of geology and biology.

You would not see it at first. Your eyes, accustomed to seeking movement and familiar forms, would slide right over it. It might be mistaken for a weathered outcrop of basalt, a gnarled and ancient tree trunk, or simply a trick of the heat-hazed air. But if you were to watch for a full cycle of the steam—a full exhalation of the plateau's great lung—you might notice that one of the shadows has shifted.

They are the Weavers. Or perhaps the Conductors. They have no name for themselves, for they have no language of sound. Their existence is one of silent, focused communion with the plateau itself.

A Weaver is a being of slow purpose. Its body, standing three metres tall, is not made of flesh and blood. It is a composite, a slow accretion of the plateau's own materials. Its outer surface is a mosaic of dull, grey-black silicates and mineral deposits, resembling the basalt pillars but with a strange, fibrous texture, like petrified wood grain. It is not rigid; it has a slow, profound flexibility, like a cooling river of glass. Veins of the same coppery metal that thread the pillars run through its body, pulsing with a faint, internal heat. These are not decorative; they are its nervous system, its circulatory system, its sensory organs.

It is humanoid only in the vaguest sense: a long torso, a head-like node at the top, and limbs. But it has four primary limbs that serve as legs, splayed for stability on the spongy, uncertain ground, each ending in a wide, disc-like foot that distributes its considerable weight. From its upper torso sprout four more limbs, these longer, more slender and ending not in hands but in complex, mutable appendages. When at rest, they look like bundles of corroded copper wire. When in use, they fray out into dozens, even hundreds, of hair-fine filaments, each one a microscopic probe capable of sensing temperature, pressure, and the flow of telluric current.

A Weaver does not see with eyes. The node atop its torso is a smooth, featureless dome of polished obsidian, which perfectly reflects the milky white sky. It is not an organ of sight but a passive heat-sink. The Weaver perceives the world as a symphony of vibrations and thermal gradients. It *feels* the path of the six-legged grazer as a subtle shift in the moss-bed's pressure. It maps the Condenser Stalker by the cool spot it creates in the warm, wet air. Its primary sense, however, is a form of electroreception. It reads the landscape by tapping into the very hum that permeates the rock, listening to the machine's slow, eternal heartbeat.

For most of a cycle, a Weaver is utterly still, a statue draped in condensation and epiphytic moss, indistinguishable from the landscape. It is listening. It is diagnosing. It feels a subtle arrhythmia in the rhythm of the steam vents, a flutter in the power flowing through a nearby pillar. The machine is ancient, and its parts wear down. A fissure may become too wide, releasing too much pressure; a metallic vein may become occluded with mineral deposits, causing energy to build to a dangerous level.

Then, with an almost imperceptible slowness, the Weaver begins to move. Its motion is not walking, but a form of controlled, geological creep. It flows over the ground, its disc-feet seeming to melt and reform with each step. It does not navigate around obstacles; it simply absorbs them, its semi-pliable form conforming to a rock or a thick fern-bole before restoring its shape. It leaves no tracks.

Reaching a pillar that is "out of tune," the Weaver braces itself with its lower limbs, becoming a part of the landscape's architecture. Its upper limbs reach out and touch the warm stone. The filament-hands unravel, spreading over the surface like a metallic fungus. They are not scraping or cutting. They are *weaving*. The filaments slide into micro-fissures in the rock, tapping into the copper veins within. One filament might draw off excess energy, its tip glowing cherry-red as it bleeds the charge into the Weaver's own body, which then harmlessly dissipates it as heat. Another might vibrate at a specific, intense frequency, shattering a crystalline blockage deep within the rock. Another might secrete a catalytic enzyme that encourages the growth of the metallic vein, healing a breach in the circuit.

There is no sound but the quiet crackle of ozone and the hiss of water vaporizing on its hot skin. The work is intricate, precise, and can take days. The Weaver is a living capacitor, a switchboard, a surgeon, and a semiconductor. It is the self-repair mechanism of a factory that has been running so long it has evolved its own maintenance crews from its spare parts.

They do not eat, not in the way the grazers do. They draw their energy directly from the geothermal and electrical systems they maintain. They do not reproduce; they form. In the deepest, hottest fissures, where pressure and mineral concentrations are highest, new Weavers slowly crystallize over millennia, accreting molecule by molecule around a knot of telluric energy until they achieve a slow, simple consciousness and uncurl, fully formed, to join the silent work. And they do not die; they erode. A Weaver, after ten thousand years of service, becomes brittle. Its internal energy wanes. It finds a quiet place, settles, and allows the heat and humidity to dissolve it, its constituent minerals washing back into the spongy earth to be used anew. It is simply reabsorbed into the system.

They are the will of the plateau made manifest. They are its antibodies, its gardeners, its minders. They are what lives here not by adaptation, but by identity. They are the machine, tending to itself, forever.
