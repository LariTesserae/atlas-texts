The beings who live here are called the **Klin**. The name might be their own or one given by a forgotten observer, but it fits, echoing the kilns that must have fired the world's bone-white plates. They are not the builders, but the inheritors, as much a part of the engineered ecology as the waxy-leafed ferns and the plack-beasts.

To see a Klin for the first time is to almost miss them. Their skin is not one colour but a shifting palette of functional camouflage. On the blinding white plains, it takes on a pale, matte texture, like unglazed ceramic, scattered with faint grey mottling that mimics the hairline fractures and stains on the plates. In the green, lush planters or against a mossy pylan, their skin darkens, flushing with chlorophyll-like greens and damp stone greys. This is not a magical process, but a slow, biological one, a symbiotic relationship with the same micro-algaes that coat the world's damp surfaces. A Klin who has been still for a long time, listening to the world's hum, might seem to be sprouting a fine, velvety moss along their shoulders and arms.

They are built for the vertical. Long of limb and spare of flesh, their bodies are maps of sinew and muscle, designed for climbing and clinging, not for running on the flat. Their hands and feet are their most remarkable feature: broad, with long, dextrous digits that can splay wide or grip with crushing force. The pads of their fingers and toes are not soft, but are tough, ridged calluses, minutely grooved like the treads of a tire, granting them purchase on the smoothest ceramic. They move with a silent, deliberate grace, a four-limbed scramble up a vertical face as natural to them as walking is to us. To a Klin, a sheer pylon wall is not an obstacle, but a staircase with a billion potential holds.

The glare and the hum have shaped their senses profoundly. Their eyes are deeply recessed beneath heavy, bony brows that act as built-in sun visors. The pupils are often no more than vertical slits, and they are protected by a transparent, nictitating membrane that slides across to cut the blinding light to a manageable level, while also keeping the constant atmospheric moisture from their eyes. They see the world through a perpetual watery film. As a result, sight is not their primary sense.

They live by touch and vibration. Through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands, they read the world. They feel the deep, patient thrum of the pylons not as noise, but as a language. They can discern the health of a distant pump by a subtle shift in its rhythmic vibration, feel the approach of a flock of steel-winged birds through the trembling of a suspension cable, and sense the location of a water-filled cistern by the way its resonance dampens the plate's hum. They are living seismographs, attuned to the mechanical soul of their world.

They are a quiet people. Spoken language is used sparingly, in the sheltered, acoustically predictable spaces of their dwellings, which are often woven pods of vine and metal wire suspended beneath the main plateaus. Out in the open, amidst the overwhelming din, they communicate through a complex language of taps. A rhythmic drumming of fingers on a ceramic plate can be a greeting, a warning, or a question, the message travelling through the structure to be felt by another Klin yards or even miles away.

The Klin are consummate artisans of adaptation. They do not forge iron or fire ceramic; they are not builders on that scale. They are weavers, scavengers, and tenders. They weave the tough fibers of the giant vines into ropes, harnesses, and the very walls of their homes. They collect the moulted metallic shells of the clicking beetles and polish them into sharp cutting tools or resonant chimes that sing in the updrafts. They have a symbiotic relationship with the plack-beasts; they do not hunt them, but follow in their wake, collecting the flakes of mineral-rich algae they scrape from the plates, which forms a key part of their diet.

They are the world's gardeners and its mechanics' assistants. A family of Klin might be responsible for maintaining the flow of a specific network of condensation pipes, keeping them clear of blockages and painting them with a cultivated, rust-inhibiting moss. They tend the great planters, not by tilling, but by carefully pruning, guiding the growth of vines, and harvesting the waxy fruits and tubers. They possess an ancient, inherited knowledge of the system's rhythms. They know when a maintenance drone is due to pass on its millennial-old track and make sure the way is clear. They know which gears in the pylon's heart will shift on the solstice, an event marked by a slight tremor and a change in the world's deep hum.

Their philosophy is one of deep integration. They have no concept of "unnatural." The world simply *is*, and their role is to facilitate its continued existence. A broken pipe is not an inconvenience; it is a wound in the world's body. A silent pylon is not a curiosity; it is a held breath, a moment of cosmic terror. They do not worship the lost builders, for they have no conception of them. The world-machine is not the creation of a god; it *is* the god, the all-encompassing reality. An individual Klin does not see themself as a discrete being so much as a single, mobile cell in a body the size of a continent, a living ganglion whose purpose is to sense, to maintain, and to persist. They are the quiet, patient consciousness of the machine, living and breathing in the gaps left by the architects.
