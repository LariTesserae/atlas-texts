The ones who live here are the cause of the silence, not just subjects of it. They are the reason the air is still. They are the quiet that has settled over the geological noise of creation. They are the Carvers.

To see one is to first mistake it for the landscape. Amid the forest of bone-white pillars, a shape that seemed to be another squat, weeping stone concretion will detach itself from the slurry with a soft, sucking sigh. The sound is thick and wet, a sound of release from the mud's grip. It is the only sound it will make.

It is vaguely humanoid in shape, but only in the way a water-smoothed root or a standing stone might be. It stands stooped, its back a long, gentle curve, its head bowed as if in constant, weary reverence. Its limbs are long and thin, but there is no sense of frailty to them. They are dense, heavy, moving with the deliberate, inexorable slowness of a glacier. The creature's skin is the color of the milky water, a pale, almost luminous gray-white, and it is slick with the same eternal condensation that coats the stone. It has no hair, no discernable ears or nose, only smooth, soft contours where features ought to be. Its surface is not entirely uniform; in places, particularly on the curved spine and the dome of its skull, the skin has thickened and hardened into plates of travertine, ridged and whorled like the mineral growths around it. It is becoming what it tends.

Its hands and feet are broad and splayed, more like webbed paddles than appendages for grasping. They distribute its weight, allowing it to move across the treacherous mud without sinking completely, leaving behind wide, shallow depressions that quickly fill with seeping water.

And then there are its eyes. Where a face would be, set into the smooth plane of the head, are two pools. They are not organs of sight in any way a surface-dweller would understand. They are wide, round, and perfectly black, yet they shimmer with a captured, pearlescent light, like two drops of crude oil on the surface of one of the milky ponds. They do not blink. They do not focus. They seem to absorb the dim light of the basin, taking in the entire pale scene without distinction, registering movement only as a slow change in the overall pattern of things.

It is a Carver. They are the ones who broke the spires to make the paths. That act of ancient, violent creation is long past. Now, their work is maintenance. They are custodians of stasis.

One will move with agonizing patience along the worn channel, its splayed feet making that soft, sucking sound with each step. It will stop at a place where a new, small nubbin of stone, the result of a thousand years of dripping, has begun to impede the way. From a fold of its thickened skin, it will produce a tool: a fist-sized river rock, dark and hard, anomalous in this world of soft, pale stone. It is a tool passed down for generations beyond counting, its surface worn to a perfect, soft polish by the grip of damp hands.

And then begins the work. It is not a chipping or a hammering. The Carver braces itself, its body becoming as still as the columns around it, and it begins to *scrape*. A slow, grinding pass, from top to bottom, shaving away a fine, wet powder of white mineral. The sound is a low, abrasive *shhhhhhhh* like sand on stone, the only interruption to the dripping water. The movement is rhythmic, meditative. A scrape, a pause, a scrape, a pause. It might take a full turn of the lightless day to wear down a growth the size of a thumb. The work is never done, but it also never needs to be hurried. Time is not a resource they spend; it is the medium in which they float.

Their sustenance is the world itself. They will pause at the bowls they have carved, dipping their smooth heads to drink the mineral-heavy water. This is what feeds them, and it is also what slowly transforms them. The minerals build up within their bodies, reinforcing their bones, hardening their skin, gradually slowing their already-languid metabolism. They also, on the rare occasion they find one, eat the pearl-shelled snails. They will lift the creature from the silt, its beautiful shell a perfect orb in their paddle-like hand. With their dark stone tool, they will give the shell a single, sharp, efficient tap. It breaks with a sound like fine china shattering. They consume the soft, gray body within, a rare infusion of complex biology into their geological diet. They do not seem to feel pity or reverence for the creature; it is simply another part of the unchanging system.

They are, perhaps, a dying race, though they would not comprehend the concept. There are no young ones. The Carvers you see are all ancient, their bodies in various states of petrification. The oldest among them no longer move. They can be found at the edges of the paths, fused to the larger mineral columns. Their limbs have become indistinguishable from the travertine branches, their skin a continuous sheet of weeping stone. Their pearlescent eyes are filmed over with a white, calcified crust. But they are not dead. Inside, a heart beats once a minute, then once an hour, then once a day, their internal processes slowing until they are perfectly in sync with the geological time of the basin. They have completed their purpose, not by dying, but by finally, wholly, becoming a part of the place they so patiently served. They have returned to the silence.
