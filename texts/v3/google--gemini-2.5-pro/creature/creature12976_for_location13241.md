The inhabitants of this chasm are known to themselves simply as the People. To an outsider, they might be the Weavers, the Geologists, or the Chasm-Dwellers. They are human, but humanity shaped by gravity, shadow, and stone.

Their bodies tell the first story. They are not tall, as height offers no advantage in a world of confined spaces and low-slung doorways. Instead, they are built with a wiry, compact strength. Their center of gravity is low, their forms dense with muscle that is not for brute lifting but for endurance, balance, and controlled tension. The muscles in their backs, shoulders, and forearms are particularly well-developed, sculpted by a lifetime of climbing, hauling, and holding on. Their hands are their most expressive and capable feature: broad-palmed, with long, strong fingers and calluses as thick and patterned as tree bark. A Chasm-Dweller's handshake is a formidable thing, a grip accustomed to holding their own life, and the lives of others, suspended over a hundred-foot drop.

Their feet are almost as dextrous as their hands. Many choose to go barefoot within their own dwellings or on familiar walkways, the better to feel the texture and integrity of the planks beneath them. Their arches are high and strong, their toes able to grip and feel for purchase. To watch them move is to see a different kind of walking. It is not the simple forward momentum of a surface-dweller, but a constant, fluid dance of balance, a three-dimensional navigation. They step with a deliberate, cat-like grace, their weight always centered, always prepared for a sudden sway of a bridge or the need to shift their body to pass another person on a narrow ledge.

The perpetual, even coolness of the light has left its mark on their faces. Their skin is pale, ranging from a milky white to a soft, mossy grey, often with a faint, translucent quality that reveals the blue tracery of veins beneath. Without a sun to shield against, their skin is vulnerable and soft where it isn't callused by work. Their eyes, however, are their most arresting feature. They are accustomed to the dim, diffuse light, so their pupils are often wide, giving them a look of constant, quiet attention. The irises come in muted, earthy shades—slate grey, damp soil brown, cloudy quartz, and the rare, startling pale green of chasm moss. They are not built for scanning vast horizons, but for judging depth and detail with uncanny precision: the exact distance to a handhold, the subtle fraying of a rope, the hairline fracture in a crystal anchor.

Their senses are tuned to a different scale. Their hearing is acute, filtering out the constant, meditative drip of the Rain to catch the vital sounds: the groan of over-stressed wood, the distinctive *skitter-click* of a dislodged stone far below, the change in the harmonics of a vibrating rope that signals a flaw. They can tell a neighbor's footstep from a stranger's by its weight and rhythm on the planks outside.

They are a quiet people. Voices are rarely raised, as a shout is an alarm, a signal of crisis. Communication across distances is often done not with voice, but with a complex and nuanced language of hand-signals, or by a series of coded knocks tapped on wooden pylons, the vibrations carrying through the city's web. Their spoken language is soft, filled with precise terminology for geology, carpentry, and ropework. They have seventeen words for the way wood can creak, and a different verb for tying a knot that will bear a person's weight versus one that will simply hold a bundle.

Socially, they are as interconnected as their city. The concept of radical individualism is not just foreign; it is nonsensical, a form of dangerous madness. A person is a node in the web, an anchor point, a part of a whole. Your neighbor’s structural failure is your imminent problem. This fosters a profound, pragmatic collectivism. Children are raised by the community, and an elder scolding a child for an unsafe knot is not seen as an intrusion but as a shared responsibility. There is little concept of private land, only private dwellings. The walkways, platforms, and lifts are a shared body, and their maintenance is a constant, shared ritual.

Their psychology is one of patient observation and slow, deliberate action. They do not rage against a geological shift; they note it, measure its progress, and plan the necessary adaptations. They are the ultimate pragmatists. Their art is not found in paintings or grand sculptures, but in the elegant, perfectly balanced weave of a bridge, the flawless faceting of a crystal tool, and the intricate, mnemonic patterns tied into the ends of ropes. A person's status might be quietly displayed by the complexity and strength of the knots on their harness, a subtle testament to their skill and experience.

Their spirituality is a direct reflection of their world. They do not worship gods in the sky, for they have no sky. Their reverence is for the Chasm itself—the two great forces that govern all things. There is the Growth Below, the slow, inexorable, and life-giving thrust of the crystal fields. And there is the Rain Above, the constant, cleansing fall of pure water. These are not seen as conscious or personal deities, but as the fundamental, unthinking processes of life to which one must listen and adapt. Their rituals are a form of collaboration with these forces. When miners descend, it is with a quiet respect, a request to the stone. When a new cistern is blessed, it is an act of gratitude to the falling water. They return their dead to the chasm, lowering the shrouded bodies into the deepest, inaccessible fissures, giving them back to the Growth. In their philosophy, to live is to balance between the push of the stone and the fall of the water. To die is to join one or the other. They are the living hyphen between the two, a patient, enduring community woven into the heart of a world that is still being born.
