What lives here is the Weft’s own attempt at a nervous system. They are what an outsider might call the Anchorites, though they have no name for themselves. They are not a race or a species; they are a function, a temporary and recurring antibody produced by the planetary organism to mitigate the worst of its own fever.

They are impossibly tall and slender, their forms echoing the verticality of the Calyces but possessing a stillness that is the antithesis of The Churn. From a distance, in the grey, spore-thick air, they are easily mistaken for an odd, solitary stalk of ossified fungus or a splinter of petrified wood thrust up from the biomass. Their limbs are long and double-jointed, moving with a slow, deliberate grace that seems entirely at odds with the chaotic surges of the world. They do not walk so much as they *flow* across the Weft, their wide, splayed feet—less flesh than a webwork of semi-rigid hyphae—distributing their weight so perfectly that the ground barely quivers beneath them. They leave no footprints, only a momentary darkening of the sponge-like mat where they have passed.

Their skin is not skin. It is a membrane, pale and luminous like the flesh of a cave mushroom, and it is perpetually damp, beaded with the same cold, clean water that saturates the ground. It is porous, featureless. They have no visible mouths, no nostrils, no ears. Their heads are smooth, oblong structures atop their willowy necks, and where eyes should be, there are only shallow, concave indentations, like thumbprints pressed into cool wax. These are not eyes; they are resonators. They do not see light; they perceive the world as a symphony of vibrations, from the deep, planetary groan of a distant Calyx to the high-frequency crunch of a Shear-Mite chewing on fractal scurf a mile away.

More profoundly, they perceive the fractures in reality. To an Anchorite, a temporal surge is not a confusing distortion but a change in key. They experience the visual echo of a Groaner not as a trick of the light, but as a chord—the *was*, the *is*, and the *might-be*, all held in a single, resonant moment. They feel the fluctuations in gravity as swells and troughs in a tide that flows through everything. This is their native sense, as fundamental to them as sight or touch is to a human. They navigate the Weft not by landmarks, but by following the contours of causality, seeking out areas of stability or, more often, moving toward the fraying edges of a surge to perform their function.

They are the tuners. The regulators. The Groaners are the brute force of the system's feedback loop, their bellows a blunt instrument to shock a shuddering Calyx back into a more stable rhythm. The Anchorites are the fine-tuners. They will gather near a Calyx on the verge of a violent surge, standing in a silent, motionless circle. They will plant their feet deep into the Weft, weaving their hyphal roots into the planetary mycelial network. And they will begin to hum.

It is not a vocalization. The sound emanates from their entire bodies, a low, complex, multi-layered thrum that is almost too deep to be heard, felt more as a pressure in the chest and a tingling in the bones. They are modulating the Weft itself, sending carefully patterned vibrations through the substrate, a counter-frequency to the frantic stuttering of the Calyx. They are, in a biological sense, executing a noise-canceling algorithm on a planetary scale. They do not feed on the reality-scurf like the Shear-Mites; they prevent its formation. They are the calm thought in the fever dream, a moment of lucidity that soothes the spasming machine.

They do not have a society, for they are not truly individuals. They are more like lymphocytes, generated by the Weft where and when they are needed. In a region of prolonged stability, they are few. In a chaotic, surging territory between two warring Calyces, they will be numerous, standing like a silent, spectral forest in the grey rain. They do not communicate with each other in any way an outsider could understand; they are nodes in a single network, acting in perfect, silent concert because they are all extensions of the same deep consciousness.

They have no life cycle in the conventional sense. An Anchorite isn't born. It coalesces. A certain density of temporal stress, a specific harmonic need in the Weft, will trigger a "sprouting." A knot of mycelia will thicken, draw in ambient proteins from the weeping mist, and slowly, over the course of days, push a new form up from the living ground. It unfurls, not like a birth, but like a fern frond uncurling, already possessing all the knowledge it needs, which is the knowledge of the Weft itself.

And they do not die. When their function is complete, or when the energy required to maintain their form is no longer needed, they simply… dissolve. They will find a low, quiet swell on the plain, become still, and slowly soften. Their form loses its integrity, slumping back into the substrate, their luminous membrane becoming indistinguishable from the pale fungi around it, their physical being reabsorbed completely into the whole. There is no loss, only a change in state. The Anchorite is gone, but its awareness, its experience of the last surge it soothed, is now a permanent memory trace woven into the ancient, living fabric of the Weft. They are the world's memory of itself, the quiet breath that follows the scream.
