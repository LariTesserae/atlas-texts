They who live in Am-Thenget are called the Thengeti, though they have no name for themselves as a collective. To them, they are simply *people*, and the paving is simply *the world*. They are as much a feature of the landscape as the cisterns or the moss-beds, and they conceive of themselves in this way.

Their physical form is a direct reflection of their environment. They are of middling height, but built with a low, solid density, their center of gravity pulled down towards the stone they walk upon. Their bodies are not soft. Their skin has a grey, ochre, or dun cast, not as a matter of pigmentation but as a kind of ingrained patina, as if the dust of the flagstones has, over a thousand generations, become part of their flesh. Their skin is cool to the touch and has a smooth, matte texture like a river-worn stone. In the old, it develops a network of fine cracks reminiscent of the crazing on ancient pottery.

Their feet are the most specialized part of them. They are broad and flat, with tough, leathery soles that can read the texture of the pavement as a scholar reads a page. Through their feet, they can feel the subtle shift in moisture that precedes the pre-dawn condensation, the faint vibration of a stone settling, or the almost imperceptible roughness that indicates a surface needs polishing. They walk with a slow, gliding shuffle, their feet never truly leaving the ground, a motion that conserves energy and keeps them in constant communion with the stone. This gait produces the signature sound of Am-Thenget: the soft, rhythmic *shuff-scrape* of leather on rock.

Their hands are wide and their fingers are thick and strong, with squared-off nails that are perpetually worn down from their work. Their primary sense is touch. They do not just see a clogged channel; they feel its gritty resistance against their scrapers. They do not just see a dull patch; they feel its lack of smoothness under their polishing cloths. Their eyes, often a pale grey or a watery hazel, are wide-set and accustomed to gazing over vast, empty expanses of stone, quick to spot the anomaly of a weed's rust-colored spire or the dark line of a new fissure.

They are a people of profound quiet. Their language is sparse and lithic, made of short, declarative statements. It has no words for running, for shouting, for forests, for oceans, for ambition. It is rich, however, in terms for the qualities of stone: its texture, its temperature, its resonance, its precise hue. They have a hundred words for the subtle curve of a water channel, and a different verb for polishing a surface at dawn versus at noon. To a Thengeti, a conversation might consist of a few shared monosyllables and a long, comfortable silence, punctuated by a nod towards a patch of moss that is growing well.

Their minds are like their city: calm, ordered, and cyclical. The concept of an individual "ego" as a separate, striving entity is absent. An individual is a node in the network, a temporary caretaker of a specific quadrant. Their memories are not personal chronicles but accretions of received knowledge, passed down through quiet instruction: "This stone was laid by the Ancestors of the South Slope. It sweats more than the others. It must be wiped last." Their sense of time is geological. The life of a person is a brief season, while the life of the paving is eternal. Joy is not a sudden peak of emotion, but the sustained, humming satisfaction of a basin in perfect balance. Distress is the anxiety of a blocked channel, the deep disquiet of a stone chipped by a dropped tool.

They dress in simple, seamless tunics woven from the fibers of their food-moss. When the grey-green lichen reaches a certain maturity, the toughest outer strands are harvested, dried until they are ashen, pounded until they are soft, and woven into a stiff, incredibly durable fabric. These garments are the color of dry stone and smell faintly of dust and minerals. They offer little warmth, but none is needed; the Thengeti's slow metabolism keeps them at a constant, cool temperature, and they are impervious to the chill of the basin.

The life cycle is one of utter sustainability. The population remains constant. A new child is conceived only when an elder feels their "weight" lessening, their connection to the stone thinning. Birth is not a celebration so much as a quiet acknowledgement that a replacement is being prepared. Children are not raised by parents, but by the community as a whole. Their play is a form of apprenticeship: they are given small, smooth stones to polish, or tiny, blocked channels to clear with their fingers.

Death is a return. When a Thengeti can no longer walk their rounds, when their feet can no longer read the stone, they are led to a small, sun-warmed plaza near the central cisterns. They sit, and they cease to eat or drink. They simply watch the light move across the basin until their life recedes, as slow and inevitable as the evening coolness. Their desiccated body, made light and brittle by the thin, dry air, is then taken by two designated elders. It is placed upon a great, hollowed stone and ground to a fine powder with a stone pestle. This powder, a concentration of the minerals and life they have gathered, is then carefully sprinkled into the moss-beds, ensuring that everything they were is given back to the system.

They are the living consciousness of Am-Thenget. They are not its masters, but its custodians, its nerve endings. They feel the thirst of the stone, the slow scrape of time, the silent promise of water in the cooling air. They exist to ensure that tomorrow, the drops will form, the channels will run, and the cisterns will receive their due. They are the slow, patient, and eternal thought in the mind of the stone.
