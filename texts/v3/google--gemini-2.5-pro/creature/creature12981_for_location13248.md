The things that live here are not individuals in the way you might understand. They are tissues of a single, vast organism that calls itself a city. For them, there is no boundary between self and place, between body and home.

First, you see the city's blood cells, its autonomic functions. The herds of opalescent **Beetle-Grazers** move with the slow, inevitable force of a tide. They are not animals that live *in* the city; they *are* the city's janitorial and digestive systems. Their carapaces, ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a cart, are whorled and opalescent, reflecting the moss-light in shifting rainbows of oil-on-water. They are not intelligent, but they are purposeful. They follow invisible gradients of scent and light, drawn to the thickest carpets of moss, their many-jointed legs ending in soft, absorbent pads that leave no mark. Their mandibles are not for biting, but are complex, porous structures that rasp and slurp the glowing vegetation, turning biological light into the chemical energy that fuels their slow, endless pilgrimage. Their waste is a nutrient-rich paste that glows faintly before being absorbed back into the living floor, fueling new growth. They are the city's gardeners and its gut.

Then there are the nerves, the messengers. Darting along the walls and vine-ways are the **Synaptids**. They are a blur of spindly legs and vibrating antennae, their bodies no larger than a child's hand. Their sleek, chitinous forms are a deep, non-reflective black, broken by stripes and nodes of brilliant, pulsing blue light. They are the city's fast-response system. They don't eat the moss; they tend to it. When a water channel is blocked, they swarm the blockage, their collective weight and subtle vibrations dislodging the debris. When a patch of moss dims, they crawl over it, secreting a single drop of a potent, enzyme-rich catalyst from their abdomens, jolting the life back into it. They are the pollinators of the fungal shelves and the repair-crews for the living architecture. They communicate in flashes of their blue light, a rapid, complex semaphore that directs swarms with terrifying efficiency. A human observer would see only chaos, but it is the chaos of a brain firing, a perfect network of information.

And at the base of it all, mostly unseen, are the city's bones and its heart: the **Makers**. They are the colossal, snail-like beings mentioned, but to see one is to misunderstand. You never see the *whole* of one. You see only a part of it, a glistening, muscular foot the size of a plaza, pressing out from a fissure at the base of a tower. You might witness a section of its vast, coiled shell, a pearlescent dome indistinguishable from the surrounding architecture because it *is* the surrounding architecture. They are not building a city. They *are* the city, growing. Their consciousness is geological, their thoughts measured in centuries. They are not aware of the Synaptids or the Grazers any more than you are aware of the mitochondria in your cells. They feel pressure, moisture, temperature, the slow stress of gravity. In response, they secrete. A new buttress is extruded over a decade to counter a shift in the cavern floor. A spire grows an inch a year towards a new source of weeping moisture. They are the city's oldest, deepest, and most unconscious self.

But there is a mind here. A waking consciousness that walks among the autonomic systems.

They are beings of sublime elegance and terrifying alienness. Let us call them the **Lumin**. They are the city's brain and its soul.

To see one is to see a piece of the city walking. An adult Lumin stands taller than a human, but seems to weigh less. Their form is slender, almost frail, a bipedal creature whose long limbs move with a liquid grace that seems to defy the weight of their own structure. Their skin is not skin, but a smooth, semi-translucent membrane, the colour of pale moonlight, and it is cool and damp to the touch. It is stretched over a delicate, but strong, endoskeleton of the same chitinous material that forms the city. Through this membrane, one can see the slow, rhythmic pulse of their internal organs, which glow with the same soft, blue-green light as the moss. Their light is not static; it shifts, swirls, and brightens with their emotional or cognitive state. A calm Lumin is a river of soft, flowing aquamarine light. A startled one is a flash of brilliant white.

They have no mouths. They do not eat or speak. They receive sustenance directly through the soles of their broad, three-toed feet. When they stand still on the mossy ground, they are photosynthesizing, drawing energy from the collective light of the city. They are feeding from the organism they are a part of.

Their heads are smooth and ovoid, featureless except for two enormous, perfectly black, liquid eyes that reflect the glowing world without revealing anything within. They have no nose, no ears, no mouth. Their primary senses are not for our world of sound and scent. They feel the vibrations of the city through their feet. They read the chemical and energetic state of their surroundings through their skin.

And they communicate. Their language is one of light and touch. They gather in circles, the colours of their bodies swirling and pulsing in silent, complex conversation. A ripple of emerald down a Lumin's arm might be a question. A cascade of cyan from another is the answer. A dance of a thousand pinpricks of light is a story, a history, a mathematical proof. To outsiders, it would be a beautiful, meaningless light show. To the Lumin, it is poetry, science, and governance.

They are the shepherds. They do not command the Grazers with whips or words, but guide them with projected patterns of light, creating corridors of “unpalatable” brightness to divert a herd, or a beacon of “delicious” intensity to draw them to a necessary task. They are the thinkers. They stand for days, unmoving, palms pressed against a growing tower, their own internal light in communion with the deep, slow pulse of the Maker within. They are not a master speaking to a slave; they are a conscious mind listening to the dreams of its own subconscious. They are diagnosing, feeling, understanding the needs of the whole.

They do not fear death; they do not even seem to mark it as an end. When a Lumin's light begins to fade permanently, it will walk to one of the city's high, open gardens. It lies down amongst the glowing ferns, and its light fades for the last time. Its body does not rot; it dissolves, the complex biopolymers of its form breaking down and releasing a final, brilliant burst of life and light, feeding the garden for a hundred years to come. It has returned its energy to the whole. It has returned home, which it never truly left. They are the city made aware of itself, and their life is a perfect, silent, luminous act of maintenance.
