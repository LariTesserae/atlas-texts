The one who comes here is a Harvester. But that name is too clean, too pastoral for the act. They are a Wounder, a Scar-Maker. They are not native to this place of seamless life; they are what happens to it.

They are not always here. Most of the time, there is only the evidence of their passage: the raw, weeping wounds on the wall, the severed filaments dripping their lifeblood onto the floor. The Harvester’s presence is a violence, and like all violence, it is temporary but its marks endure, at least for a while.

When they arrive, they do not belong. They are a dissonance in the chamber’s perfect chord. Dressed in stiff hides cured in salt and smoke, their clothes are a dead, abrasive texture against the living slickness of the walls. Their own scent—of sweat, of the cold stone of the tunnels above, of old blood—is a foul intrusion into the clean, chlorophyll-like breath of the chamber.

The Harvester is human, or was, or mostly is. Their form is stooped, not with age, but with a permanent tension, the posture of a person bracing against a constant, invisible wind. Their skin, where it is exposed, is not pale from darkness but has a strange, bruised-looking patina, flecked with tiny, embedded specks of hardened amber sap, as if they were gilded with their own transgressions. Their knuckles are broad and calloused, their fingernails are thick and yellowed, chipped from prying open the fissure that leads here.

But it is their eyes that mark them as a creature of this place. They are never still, but they do not dart. They drift. They seem to look at several points in the chamber at once, one pupil fixed on a distant, pulsing vein in the ceiling while the other traces a filament near the floor. They have learned to see without focus, because to focus here is to invite vertigo, to be hypnotized by the impossible geometry. Their gaze is a refutation of perspective, a fractured, layered perception that can hold the near and the far in equal measure. They see the web of light not as an obstacle course to be navigated, but as a single, static object they must pass through, like a ghost walking through a wall.

And they are deaf. Not in the conventional sense. The immense, bone-shaking hum of the chamber has long ago destroyed the delicate mechanics of their ears. To the small sounds of the world above—the drip of water in a cavern, a footstep, a spoken word—they are stone. But they are not without hearing. They hear with their whole body. The chamber’s resonance is a language they understand in their marrow. They can feel the tension shifting in a new filament as it grows, sense the subtle thickening of a wall section as a sign of health, and feel the grating disharmony of their own presence in the thrum.

They move with a heavy, deliberate slowness. Each foot is planted firmly, a conscious anchor against the dislocation of the air. They do not glide through the filaments; they shoulder past them, their dead hides scraping against the living strands, which shudder and sometimes snap in their wake.

Their tools are a profanity. They carry a scraper made from a shard of dark, volcanic glass, its edge brutally sharp. When they work, they press this dead stone against the living wall and methodically, laboriously, flay a section of the glowing skin away. The sound is an atrocity—a high, wet tearing that briefly cuts through the deep hum. It is the only sound that truly echoes here, a scream the chamber cannot swallow. They peel back sheets of the luminous flesh, which curl and continue to glow with a dying light, and pack them into a stiff leather satchel.

From a harness on their belt, they take a smaller knife, a sliver of flint, and with a practiced motion, they reach up and sever one of the thicker filaments. They hold a hollowed horn gourd beneath the wound. The glowing, viscous sap, the chamber's purest life-essence, drips slowly into the horn. The sound of these drips is loud in the relative silence of their work—thick, resonant plops of liquid light. Each drop is a heartbeat the chamber will not have back.

They do not seem to hate the place. There is no malice in their actions. There is only a grim, absolute purpose. They are a function, a necessary parasite. The harvested wall-flesh, carried back to the sunless warrens they call home, will serve as lamps—cold, steady lights that will burn for a year without fading. The sap, a single drop on the tongue, can knit broken bones or grant a day of tireless strength. It is a medicine, a sacrament, a currency. They bleed this living god to keep their own people from the dark.

When they are done, they linger for a moment. They stand in the center of the void, their head tilted, their body absorbing the chamber's resonant note. Perhaps this is their form of prayer, of apology. Or perhaps they are simply recharging. The air here changes them. The thoughts in their head are not their own; they are echoes of the chamber's vast, silent, growing mind. They feel their own consciousness dissolve, becoming a momentary eddy in the chamber's flow. For an instant, they are not a Harvester, but a part of the whole, a fleeting cell in the greater body.

Then, with a shudder, they reclaim themselves. The illusion breaks. They are an intruder again. They sling their satchel, heavy with light and life, over their shoulder. They turn and walk back towards the torn membrane, their heavy boots scuffing the living floor. They leave the chamber to its healing, to the slow work of scabbing over the wound they made, to the patient regrowth of its severed veins. They carry the hum with them, a ghost-sound that will vibrate in their bones until they are compelled to return, to crack open the world and wound it again.
