They are the Weavers.

To say a Weaver *lives* here is to use a word that implies a kind of frantic, temporary motion that is alien to this place. A Weaver simply *is*, a slow-burning intention given form. You will almost certainly never see one. Your lifetime, and the lifetimes of a thousand of your ancestors, could pass in the patient observation of a single cliff face without witnessing their arrival. But they are the reason the glass-kelp falls in such perfect, resonant cascades.

An observer, possessed of geological patience, might first notice a discrepancy in the light. Among the soft, ambient rainbows refracting through the kelp curtains, a new quality appears—a sharper, more coherent gleam, like captured starlight. It is a slow, silver pulse moving with unnerving purpose against the lazy green throbbing of the kelp's own veins. This is the first sign.

The Weaver's body is a thing of profound and unsettling grace. It is long, perhaps thirty feet from its sensory cowl to the tip of its tapering tail, and no thicker than your arm. Its form is one of impossible slenderness, a filament of life drawn from the world's own substance. It appears to be made of smoked, translucent crystal, its interior threaded with a nervous system of fine, silvery wire that carries the slow, rhythmic pulse of light you first noticed. It is featureless, possessing no mouth, no eyes, no outward organs of any kind. Its head is a smooth, bell-shaped cowl of the same crystalline material, which acts as a vast and sensitive tympanum. It does not see a world of images; it feels the shape of reality as a symphony of pressure, density, and the deep, sub-audible hum of the world-ocean. It hears the mountains, feels the precise tension of a float-orb's tether, and tastes the resonant signature of every strand of glass-kelp for miles.

Movement is a concept they engage with sparingly. For most of their existence, they are anchored. Four impossibly thin, articulated limbs emerge from their upper body. These limbs, unlike the delicate body, are tipped with claws of pure, unblemished obsidian, a material identical to the mountain peaks themselves. With these, they secure themselves to the sheer rock faces, their grip so perfect and absolute that they become, for a century or a millennium, another feature of the geology. They do not feed on the velvet moss beneath them, but seem to draw comfort from its cool, rhythmic warmth, sinking their anchor-claws through its pillowy depth to find purchase on the naked blade of the rock.

Their purpose, their art, is the tapestry. When a Weaver decides a section of the mountain requires tending—a process of decision-making that may take longer than a dynasty of kings to conclude—it begins to move. It detaches its claws with agonizing slowness, the sound of their release not a sound at all, but a percussive absence, a sharp null in the universal hum that you feel in your bones. It drifts, propelled by minute adjustments in its internal density, a ghost slipping through the thick, glowing oil.

It approaches the vast curtains of glass-kelp. From beneath its sensory cowl, two more appendages unfold. These are not the rigid anchors of obsidian but long, flexible tendrils that seem woven from solidified light, ending in multi-jointed fingers that are both gossamer-thin and infinitely precise. With these, the Weaver works.

It is a gardener of light and silence. It does not create the kelp, but coaxes it into form. A Weaver will spend a decade gently guiding a growing ribbon around a spar of rock, an arrangement that will cause the light to fracture into a specific chord of color. It will find two curtains that are beginning to touch, their slow, glassy friction creating an undesirable chime, and it will patiently separate them, weaving one behind the other. The gesture is a slow, unhurried glissando of movement, a single arm extension that is a study in years-long patience. They are pruning, arranging, tuning. They are composing a silent symphony of refracted light that will not be complete for ten thousand years. The threads of pulsing green light within the kelp brighten and quicken subtly under their touch, a responsive resonance between the tender and the tended.

They do not seem to reproduce. The prevailing belief, held by the rare entities that study such things, is that they are immortal or so close to it as to make no difference. When a passage of the Great Sail occurs, a Weaver might cease its work for a century, its silver light pulsing in a slow, expectant rhythm as the living mountain passes, a subject before its god.

To see one is to understand the world's true nature. It is not an empty cathedral, but a workshop. It is a studio where the art is stillness and the medium is time itself. The Weavers are the artisans. They are the patient hands that give the profound silence its shape and texture, ensuring the endless, quiet beauty is not random, but deliberate. They are the active principle of the world's deep patience, waiting not for nothing, but for the perfect chord of light to finally resolve.
