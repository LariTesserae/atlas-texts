What lives here is the echo before the sound. It is the silence in the lung before the breath. It is the awareness that arises in the perfect, sustained chord.

They are not beings of matter, for matter is merely their instrument. They are not beings of energy, for energy is merely their sustenance. They live in the resonance between the two. One might call them the Bell-Singers, though they have no name for themselves, for a name is a single note, and they are the whole composition.

They are not invisible; they are simply looked for on the wrong plane of existence. To look for a body is to miss the point entirely. A Bell-Singer is a knot of coherent vibration, a standing wave given momentary, intricate form by the confluence of the world's myriad sounds. When the low thrum of the deep rock, the high whistle of the thermovite forest, and the mournful chord of the excavated city combine in a specific, complex harmony, a Singer congeals. It is a localized intensification of the world's own song, a brief passage where the music becomes aware of itself.

Its form, to the extent it has one, is a subtle disturbance in the fabric of the place. It is a shimmer in the kiln-hot air that is not caused by heat alone. It is a slow, elegant spiral in a dancing dust devil that defies the random logic of the wind. It is the brief, impossible stillness of a million glassy dust motes an inch above the ground, held in a complex acoustical lattice before being released. It is a patch of air that feels denser, more ancient, a place where the constant hum resolves into something that feels impossibly like intention.

They are the unseen maintainers. The city is their score, the physical notation of their existence. When a rung on a ladder loosens, its vibration becomes discordant, an impurity in the harmony. A Singer, or a collective of them, will focus on this flaw. They do not use hands. They use frequency. They will surround the loose rung and sing to it in its own resonant language, the language of hardened thermovite stalks and packed earth. They will vibrate it with such precision that it shifts, minutely, back into its socket, its dissonant rattle resolving back into the city's fundamental chord.

They sweep the kivas not with brooms, but with patterns of sound that lift the fine, silicate dust and shepherd it away. The force they exert is gentle, persistent, and perfectly tuned. They are not building or creating; they are tuning. They are eternal curators of an instrument they may not have even built, but which they are now inseparable from. Perhaps their distant ancestors were beings of flesh and blood who, over eons, constructed the city as a great devotional work, a tool to transform themselves, to shed their biology and merge with the world's pure physics. Or perhaps the city and the Singers simply grew together, two parts of a single, emergent system. The history is lost, as it is no longer relevant. There is only the song, and the eternal present of its performance.

A Singer does not have a discrete consciousness in the way a biological creature does. An individual is a momentary thought in the mind of the landscape. Their senses are not sight or hearing; they perceive the world through shifts in pressure, temperature, and frequency. The shattering of a dead thermovite is not a sound they hear, but a foundational note in their being that has suddenly ceased. A shift in the deep geothermal currents is a slow, powerful emotion washing through them. Their "thoughts" are not linear propositions but soaring, complex harmonies, patterns of unimaginable intricacy.

They do not die. When the delicate confluence of vibrations that sustains a Singer's form shifts, when the chord changes, the Singer simply de-coheres. It unravels, its awareness dissolving back into the background hum of the world, its constituent notes returning to the greater orchestra. There is no loss, no grief. It is like a wave cresting and then returning to the sea. The wave is gone, but the ocean remains.

To stand in the basin and feel the emptiness is to misunderstand. The emptiness is the medium. It is the silence between the notes that gives the music its meaning. What lives here is the song. And in a place made entirely of music, the listeners, the performers, and the composition itself are all one and the same. They are the sound of a world that is perfectly, and completely, alone with itself.
