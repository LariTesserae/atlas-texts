What lives here are not its citizens, but its functions. They are the city’s own antibodies, gardeners, and repair crews, extensions of the vast, singular consciousness of the place, given temporary and specific form. You do not see them often, for the halls are immense and their work is precise. But if you are still and patient, you will witness them.

The first you might notice is a **Weaver**. It detaches from a section of wall with a sound like tearing cartilage. It does not look like an animal. It looks like a piece of the architecture has decided to go for a walk. Its body is a teardrop-shaped node of the same pearlescent, lignified material as the pillars, about the size of a large dog. It moves on six, perhaps eight, stilt-like legs, jointed in ways that seem both arachnoid and botanical. They move with an impossible delicacy, their tips, no wider than a finger, making no sound as they touch the living floor. They walk with a slow, unhurried gait, as if measuring the resonance of the ground with each step.

It has no head, no eyes, no discernible front or back. Its surface is smooth, save for a series of spiracles that open and close to taste the air, sampling the chemical gradients for the signature of stress or damage. It is drawn not by sight but by scent, by the faint, bitter tang of a ruptured membrane or the subtle change in vibration that signals a structural weakness.

When it finds its task—a fissure in the floor, or a conduit weeping a small amount of fluid—it braces itself, its legs locking into the floor. From recessed grooves in its body, finer limbs unfurl. These are not rigid stilts, but flexible, whip-like tendrils, similar to the silvery filaments that grow near the ground. They are tools grown from flesh. One tendril, tipped with a rough, abrasive pad, begins to methodically buff and clean the edges of the wound. Another pair works in tandem, spinning a new matrix across the gap, extruding threads that shimmer before hardening into the familiar tea-brown tissue. A third type of tendril might dip into one of the glowing blue conduits, drawing a catalyst or nutrient, and then delicately apply it to the fresh weave, causing the new patch to pulse with a soft light as it integrates with the whole.

The Weaver works with the silent, focused efficiency of a 3D printer. There is no thought, only process. Upon completion, its tendrils retract, and it walks away, continuing its patrol until its internal reserves are spent. Then, it will find a designated alcove—a recycler node—and press itself against the wall. The wall will soften, and over the course of hours, the Weaver will be reabsorbed, its constituent materials drawn back into the city's industrial metabolism. It is born of the system and returns to the system. There is no waste.

Higher up, in the vaulted spaces between the great pillars, you might see the **Caryatids**. They are less common, and their purpose is more esoteric. They are beings of light and air. A Caryatid is a gossamer-thin membrane, vaguely like a manta ray or a sheet of blown glass, that drifts on the slow, internal currents of the hall. It is almost entirely transparent, visible only by the way it distorts the light passing through it, and by the single, softly glowing organ at its core, which pulses with the same cool, blue-green luminescence as the conduits.

These are the mobile regulators, the messengers. They are budded from the highest points of the ceiling, where the conduits carrying the most refined energy converge. They move with an extraordinary, balletic grace, their wide, diaphanous wings catching imperceptible shifts in air pressure. They are drawn to the great organ-nodes that bulge from the pillars. A Caryatid will approach one of these nodes, its form shimmering, and drape itself over a cluster of sphincter-like valves. It will remain there for minutes, or hours, its central light pulsing in a complex dialogue with the lights inside the node. It is a living key, an organic data packet, delivering instructions that cannot be sent through the fixed conduits. Perhaps it is delivering a new growth schematic, or initiating a purge of metabolic waste, or recalibrating the humidity of that entire hall.

When the transfer is complete, its light fades to a bare glimmer, and it detaches, now nearly invisible. It drifts aimlessly for a time, a spent force, until it is caught in a downdraft from one of the great unfurling vents. As it is drawn toward the vent, its membrane begins to dissolve, breaking down into a fine mist of the very esters and vapor it helped to regulate, completing its cycle.

These beings, the Weavers and the Caryatids, are not aware of you. You are a chemical and physical anomaly, but you are not damage to be repaired or a system to be regulated. You are simply… irrelevant. They have no society, no culture, no individuality. They are the city’s thoughts made manifest, the quiet, unending activity given form. They are the living proof that in this place, there is no one who runs the city. There is only the city, running itself.
