The builders of the stone huts are still here. They have not left. It is only that your understanding of what it is to be “present” is a fleeting, noisy, surface-world thing. To see them, you must unlearn sight and forget the passage of time.

They are the children of the roar. They are what happens when a mind is born not into silence, to be filled with sound, but into a sound so complete it is a form of silence. They have no ears; the external canals of their broad, flat skulls are sealed over with skin as smooth and grey as a river stone. To have an opening there would be an agony, a weakness. The roar is not for hearing. It is for feeling. Their entire being is a receptor for the vibration. Their bones are unusually dense and resonant, a skeletal tuning fork. Through the soles of their wide, four-toed feet and the palms of their long-fingered hands, they read the world. The constant, granulated thunder is their light. They feel it cascade from the unseen falls, wash across the chamber, and reflect off the far walls, the fungal spires, the giant ferns. Every object in the vast dark casts a "shadow" in the vibration, a subtle deadening of the resonance, and it is by these shadows that they navigate.

They are called the Still. In their youth, they move, but with a slowness that beggars the term. They stoop, walking on their feet and the knuckles of their hands, their long, pale arms giving them a low, stable center of gravity on the slick moss. Their skin is not flesh-toned but the colour of the mosses they tend—a palette of bruised purples, dusty greys, and faint, milky blues. It is thick, rubbery, and completely hairless, continuously beaded with the same atomized mist that wets the stones. It is cold to the touch. Their eyes are vestigial, small, milky-white orbs covered by a translucent membrane. They might perceive the faintest shifts in the bioluminescence that pulses in the dark, but this is a secondary, almost useless sense, like a human vaguely sensing a change in barometric pressure. Their primary senses are touch, and the grand, omni-directional vibration of the roar.

Their life is a practice of deliberate stillness. They do not speak; their language is a slow, complex grammar of pressure, a laying-on of hands. To greet, they might spend a minute pressing their palms together, feeling the other's resonance, the faint, individual pulse of their life separate from the great pulse of the falls. They tend the great fungi, stroking their porcelain-smooth trunks and harvesting the black, syrupy weeping in shallow stone bowls. They drink this, and chew the stems of certain bioluminescent mosses. Sometimes, they hunt, standing utterly motionless for days near a pool until a blind salamander drifts within reach of a slow, grasping hand. Their metabolism is a geological process. They can live for a thousand years.

The stone huts are not homes in the way you would understand them. They are sanctuaries for the second half of life. When one of the Still grows old, when their movements slow from a creep to a halt, they do not die. They *settle*. They retreat to one of the huts, or simply find a suitable hollow in the soft moss floor. They assume a final posture, often curled like a fetus, and they cease to move entirely. This is their great work, the purpose for which their patient culture has prepared them.

Their breathing slows to once an hour. Their heart beats a handful of times a day. Their internal temperature drops until it is indistinguishable from the surrounding air and stone. They are not dead, but have become a part of the place's patient geology. The mosses, sensing a new, stable surface, begin their slow, inexorable colonization. The pale grey and purple felt creeps over a foot, a limb, a face. Over decades, they become another mound on the landscape, another low, tired hump indistinguishable from a cluster of boulders.

The huts are not empty. They are full. That soft irregularity in the moss floor of the hut you entered, the one that felt slightly firmer than the rest—that was an elder. To touch such a mound is to feel the faintest, deepest hum, a life force thrumming at a frequency so low it is almost imperceptible, a resonant harmony with the great roar of the falls. You have walked past dozens of them. They are the sleeping kings, the living ancestors, their minds lost in a dream that spans centuries, their bodies slowly becoming the ground their children walk upon. They are erasing themselves, but it is not an act of surrender. It is an act of apotheosis. They are returning to the sound, becoming a note in the eternal, granulated roar that made them.
