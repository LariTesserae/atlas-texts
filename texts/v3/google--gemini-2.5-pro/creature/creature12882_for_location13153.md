The life in the Atrium City is as much a part of the stone as the crystal growths themselves. They are not merely inhabitants; they are the active, conscious geology of the place. To a fleeting, soft-fleshed being, they might not even be recognizable as life at first, but rather as another slow, deliberate process of the lithic world.

They are beings of immense density and patience. Their bodies are not made of soft tissue and flowing water, but of a complex, living silicate matrix. Their “skin” is a mosaic of smooth, worn grey like the dome-mountains and crystalline patches that glitter with captured light, like the interior of a geode. On their joints—shoulders, elbows, knees—and down the hard ridges of their spines, these crystals are thicker, forming plates of natural armor that are dull or sharp depending on the individual’s age and role. Their coloration is the palette of the caldera: basalt-dark, mica-silver, rust-red, with startling veins of lapis or malachite that trace the slow, geothermal pulse of their internal systems. They are heavy, and they move with a deliberation that seems impossibly slow, but is merely tuned to a timescale alien to us. Each step is placed with perfect, ponderous balance on the uneven floor of mica-dust and crystal shards.

They have no faces in a familiar sense. Their heads are solid, angular blocks, like uncut stone, with no visible mouth or nose. Their primary sense is not sight, but a form of seismic perception. Their entire bodies, particularly the long, multi-jointed fingers on their four hands, are exquisitely sensitive to vibration. They *feel* the world. They can read the health of a basalt pillar by the way it transmits the chiming of the canopy, discern the location of a skittering chalcedony-beetle from a hundred yards away, and navigate the labyrinthine tunnels in absolute darkness by the echo of their own footfalls. Their language is a direct extension of this sense. They communicate not with voices, but with percussion. By tapping their hardened fingertips against their own stone bodies, the walls, or specialized resonant plates carved into public plazas, they create complex rhythms of clicks, taps, and deep, humming tones. A conversation is a polyrhythmic duet; a public proclamation is a wave of structured vibration that ripples through the very foundation of the city. Their carving is their highest form of communication: a story, a philosophy, a genealogy told in a percussive song that lasts a thousand years, its meaning permanently encoded in the shape of the resulting chamber.

What they have for eyes are not single orbs, but clusters of deep, multifaceted black lenses embedded in their stony heads. These are not for sharp, focused vision, but for gathering the faint, fractured light of the twilight forest. Each facet registers a different angle of the shattered mosaics pouring through the selenite canopy, allowing them to perceive patterns of light and shadow with a sublime, holographic clarity incomprehensible to a creature with simple pupils. They see the flow of light itself, the way it stresses and bends as it strikes crystal.

They possess four arms. Two are thick, immensely powerful limbs used for the heavy work of hammering and splitting stone. Two are slighter, more articulate arms, ending in long, delicate fingers tipped with naturally-formed obsidian that is kept sharp by constant use. These are their hands for fine carving, for reading the story in the stone’s grain, for speaking their silent, clicking language. They are ambidextrous and can carve with all four hands at once, a slow, hypnotic dance of creation.

Their lifecycle is geological. They are not born, but *cleaved*. A new individual is budded off from a parent, a small, stone-like nodule that gestates for decades encased in a specialized pouch of mineral fiber. This "child" is then "birthed" and spends its first century as a small, dense, slowly tumbling stone, absorbing ambient geothermal energy and the vibrations of the city—its culture and language—before it finally cracks open and unfurls its limbs. They do not eat in the way we understand it. They are lithovores, but their consumption is slow and passive. Standing still, they can absorb mineral nutrients directly from the stone floor through their feet, a process that can take days. They may also occasionally use a grinding, mandible-like apparatus hidden within their chest cavity to scrape off and ingest mineral dusts or the powdery malachite moss, much like the local fauna.

And they do not truly die. As they age over millennia, their metabolism slows, their movements become more infrequent, and their internal heat fades. An elder, feeling their end approach, will find a chosen place within the city—a wall, a niche, the base of a pillar—and become still. Over the next few centuries, their body slowly and fully crystallizes, its living silicate matrix locking into a final, permanent form. Their consciousness fades as their vibrations cease, and they become one with the architecture. The city is, quite literally, built from the bodies of its ancestors. The walls are not just memorials; they *are* the memorialized. To walk the halls of the Atrium City is to be surrounded by a silent, sleeping chorus of a billion souls.

They are the Resonant. They are the patient carvers, the silent listeners, the living stone of this slow and eternal world. Their existence is a single, continuous act of becoming, a conversation between life and substance so profound that the two have merged into one. They are not in the city. They *are* the city, and the city is them.
