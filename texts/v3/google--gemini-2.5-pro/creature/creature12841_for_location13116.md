What lives there is not one thing, but a vast, layered symbiosis, a creature whose body is the city itself and whose cells are the beings within it.

At the bottom層, in the hot, wet breath of the machine, is the **Chitinous Bed**. This is the life described—the unending, rustling tide of Ferrites, so named for their iron-rich carapaces that are the colour of cooling embers and corroded steel. They are not individuals. A single Ferrite has no more self-awareness than a skin cell on your arm. They are a single, sprawling biomass that flows like a metallic liquid, responding to stimuli as a collective.

Their forms are varied, not by species, but by function and age, like the castes of a super-colony. The youngest, the **Filings**, are no larger than a grain of rice, born in the nutrient-rich sludge where condensate pipes meet the slag. They are soft-shelled and swarm in thick, shimmering clouds, blindly filtering the chemical soup. As they moult and grow, they become **Scrabblers**, the finger-to-hand-sized generalists whose dry rustle is the planet’s primary voice. Their role is to wander, to consume, to be consumed. They scour the slag pans, their mandibles abrading the mineral deposits, their gut flora processing the complex polymers that drip from the city above. In their final stage, they are the **Ingots**— dinner-plate-sized fortresses of chitin. They are slow, ponderous, their shells thick and pitted. They seek out the hottest thermal vents, basking in the direct heat, their bodies acting as metabolic batteries for the entire colony. When they die, they simply stop, becoming silent, ore-rich lumps that are slowly consumed and broken down by the younger Scrabblers, their bodies returning to the geology they so resemble.

Hunting this bed are the **Geode-backs**. They are not merely predators; they are a culling mechanism, a function of the living landscape. Their long, flat bodies, propped on a dozen spindly, double-jointed legs, are almost perfectly flush with the slag. Their armour is not chitin, but a crystalline silicate, grown in layers. Over the decades of their slow lives, minerals from the hot pools precipitate onto their backs, creating a jagged, glittering camouflage of ochre, rust-red, and turquoise. They do not have eyes. Instead, the front of their flattened head is a single, broad, heat-sensitive plate, a biological thermographic camera that sees the world as a shimmering map of temperature gradients. They move with an impossible slowness, taking a single step over the course of an hour, becoming indistinguishable from the ground itself. The hunt is an act of pure patience. They wait until the ceaseless, random motion of the Ferrite swarm brings a Scrabbler within reach. The strike is a blur of motion too fast for the eye to track—a needle-tipped forelimb, powered by a hydrostatic "muscle," impales the prey. There is no sound. A neurotoxin and a digestive enzyme are injected in the same instant. The Ferrite is paralyzed and begins to dissolve from the inside out. The Geode-back then retracts its limb and siphons the resulting fluid, leaving a hollow, brittle husk that is quickly swept away by the chitinous tide.

But these are only the most visible components. Deeper, in the scalding heart of the geothermal vents where water boils instantly, live the **Glass-Spools**. They are not animal. They are slow-growing structures of silica, living crystals that feed directly on the raw thermal and chemical energy. They look like coils of spun glass, impossibly intricate and delicate, anchored to the slag floor. They glow with a faint, internal blue light. They are the ecosystem’s memory. The deep hum of the city vibrates through the slag, and the Glass-Spools resonate with it, their crystalline lattice recording the endless cycles of the machinery above as minute changes in their own structure. They are a geological record of the city's entire operational history, a silent, sentient library of vibrations.

And above it all, in the cool, sterile, silent upper level, live the **Janitors**.

They are never seen on the ground. To the living wilderness below, they are abstract gods, their existence proven only by the life-giving waste they provide. They occupy the black cubes and travel through the enclosed transitways on silent, gliding platforms.

A Janitor is a tall, unnervingly thin being, averaging three meters in height. Its body is not organic in a familiar sense. It is encased in a smooth, seamless suit of a ceramic-like material the colour of bone china, a material that never stains and is cool to the touch. This is not armor; it is their skin, their exoskeleton, grown and shaped with them from birth. They have no face. The head is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless save for a single, horizontal band of dark, translucent material where eyes might be. Behind this visor, a soft, constant amber light glows, the same amber as the weak lamps that illuminate the mire below. They do not seem to perceive the world through sight alone, but through a constant polling of the environment's data: temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, the resonant frequency of the continent-sized engines.

They do not eat. They do not breathe. They do not speak. Their limbs are long and delicate, their hands ending in three slender fingers that can interface directly with ports set into the walls of their habitat. When not moving, they stand for immense periods in silent alcoves, their amber glow dimming as they enter a low-power state, absorbing the trickle of energy fed to them by the city itself. Their purpose is Maintenance. They are a caste of biological servants, bred or designed for a single function: to ensure the Great Machine continues its endless, quiet hum.

Their "culture" is a set of rituals based on incomprehensibly ancient technical manuals. They do not understand the *why* of the machine—its original purpose is as lost to them as it is to the geology. They only understand the *how* of its continuation. They slide panels open on the humming cubes to monitor glowing fluid-filled conduits. They replace crystalline components the size of a human head, handling the ancient, still-functioning parts with a reverence born of pure, instinctual programming. Their movements are graceful, economical, and utterly without passion.

Their relationship to the world below is one of complete detachment. They know it exists. Their console readouts refer to it as the "Lower Level Thermal and Biomass Reclamation System." They monitor the nutrient output, the water cycle, and the overall biomass density as one more variable in the machine's perfect equilibrium. The idea that the seething carpet of life below is anything more than a waste-processing function is a thought they are incapable of forming. They are the brain stem of this world, performing the autonomic functions, while the vibrant, violent, teeming life of the basin is the guts, the blood, the skin. They are two parts of the same organism, utterly dependent on one another, but destined to never, ever meet.
