They are called the Weavers, though no one is there to name them. The name is a description of their form and function, an inference one might make after weeks of patient, ear-splitting observation. You will not see one on your first day, nor likely your second. The trilobite grazers are the obvious life, the slow-moving, solid punctuation in a landscape of music and moss. But the Weavers are the true inhabitants. They are the active principle within the song.

At first, you mistake them for a trick of the senses. In a place where sound is a physical force and the air is in constant motion, your eyes play games. You see a shimmer of heat-haze against a white Sonophore spire, but the air is bitingly cold. You see a strand of what looks like spider silk, impossibly thick, drifting in the wind, only for it to move with a purpose that defies the prevailing gust. You see a trickle of condensation on a porcelain tube suddenly reverse its course, flowing upwards for a moment before vanishing. These are your first sightings. They are the stirrings of a presence you cannot yet resolve.

To truly see a Weaver is to have your perception bend. They are creatures born of resonance and decay. They have no flesh, no blood, no solid core. A Weaver's body is a long, impossibly slender form, like a nerve fiber given independent life, perhaps seven or eight feet in length. It is woven from the finest white dust of eroded Sonophores, held together by dew and static charge, and animated by the sympathetic vibration of the city’s eternal music. They are translucent, the colour of moonlight through fog. When still, they are nearly invisible against the bone-white architecture they climb. When they move, they catch the light in a way that is liquid and crystalline at once, like a filament of spun glass melting.

They are beings of the vertical world. Their lives are spent clinging to the Sonophores, their forms draped across the fluted spires and porous galleries like ghostly ivy. They possess dozens of impossibly fine, thread-like limbs, some of which end in minute, hardened points like diamond styluses, others in soft, absorbent pads. They move with a slowness that makes the grazing trilobites seem hasty. A Weaver might spend an entire day traversing a few feet of a single sound-tube. Their movement is a kind of silent, fluid climbing, more like a physical process than the locomotion of an animal. They are the city's immune system, its librarians, its tuners.

The water that seeps through the basin is rich in minerals. Over decades and centuries, these minerals precipitate inside the delicate hollows of the Sonophores, clogging the channels, muting the notes. This is the city's natural aging process; it is how a Sonophore falls silent before it crumbles. The Weavers exist to resist this entropy. With their stylus-limbs, they tap and scrape, dislodging the mineral incrustations with surgical precision. The work is impossibly delicate. A wrong touch, a vibration applied with a fraction too much force, could fracture the porcelain structure. But they are born of the music; their every twitch is in harmony with the resonant frequency of the spire they tend. They feel the dullness of a clogged passage not as a blockage, but as a missing voice in the chorus they embody. Their soft, padded limbs absorb the dislodged grit and moisture, which they carry to the highest, wind-scoured spires to be cast away as fine powder.

You do not hear a Weaver work. The sound of their tiny scrapings is utterly lost in the oceanic roar of the wind-song. But you can, if you listen for long enough, hear the results of their labour. You can be standing in a plaza of sound, familiar with its particular chord after days of immersion, when suddenly a new note will pierce the air—a high, clear whistle that was absent before. It is the sound of a cleaned artery, a renewed voice rejoining the symphony. This is the only announcement of a Weaver's success. It is the only mark they leave.

They have no eyes, no ears you can discern. Their entire being is a receptive surface for vibration. The world, for them, is not a visual space but a complex orchestral score. They sense the grazers below as rhythmic, low-frequency pressure changes in the mossy ground—ignorable, like the passage of clouds. They sense the approaching rain as a shift in barometric pressure and a change in the tenor of the wind's hum. Their own life cycle is tied to the city's. When an old Sonophore finally succumbs and shatters, its dust settles on the damp moss. In the silent morning, when the first gusts of wind stir the basin and the music begins its daily swell, some particles of this dust, wetted by the dew, will begin to vibrate in sympathy with a particularly complex harmony. They will cling together, attracting more, coalescing. A new Weaver is born, a ghost assembled by music, rising to begin its slow, patient, unending work of keeping the song alive. They are not inhabitants *of* the city; they are the city’s song, made manifest and given a fleeting, custodial form. They are the music, tending to itself.
