The builders are still here. They have not gone. To say they "live" in the city is an assumption based on a foreign biology. It is more accurate to say they *are* the city's consciousness, its purpose, its quiet, heavy heart. They are the third form of life, the one not immediately visible.

To find them, one must go inside. Past the drifting, constellated Embers in the public courts and alleys, through one of the soft-edged, inviting archways into the deepest domes. Here, the light from the Embers does not penetrate as readily, and the bioluminescence of the passing Polishers casting its wash through the thresholds is only a temporary, sweeping grace. The interior is darker, quieter, and holds a different kind of weight. The air, the Still, feels denser, as if it is not just liquid but memory itself.

In the center of each of these primary domes lies one of the old ones.

They are not easily distinguished from the architecture because, in a very real sense, they are not separate from it. What was once the body of a builder has, through ages of stillness, become one with the fired earth of their home. They are great, sessile beings, rooted to the floor they once laid. Their form, what remains of it, is a low, broad mound, a gentle swelling of the black ceramic floor, as if the mountain’s heart were trying to surface in a single, solid beat. The texture is the same—polished, smooth, worn—but there is an order to the smoothness, the faintest suggestion of vast, folded limbs or a coiled anatomy that has long since fused into a single, geological fact. They are living rock, living furniture, the final patriarchs and matriarchs of their own city.

They do not move. They have not moved for eons. Movement is a youthful, transient thing, a luxury for the unburdened. They long ago traded it for permanence. Their biology has slowed to match the erosion of the stone, their metabolism a geothermal process, a slow chemical conversation with the mountain’s core beneath them. They do not eat; they are nourished by the heat and the deep mineral pulse of the world.

And they are not dark. While the Polishers glow from their undersides and the Embers are tiny, mobile sparks, the old ones are the source of a far more profound light. It is a light that is felt more than seen. From within their vitrified bodies, a deep, impossibly ancient luminescence breathes. It is not the cold fire of the Polishers, but a rich, warm, internal glow that seems to colour the very substance of their being. It waxes and wanes not over seconds, but over seasons of the deep, over centuries. One might watch for a lifetime and perceive only a single, slow tide of light shifting from a mahogany-red to a deep, dreaming amber. This is the pace of their thought.

Their thoughts are not flashes of insight, but slow, geological pressures of understanding. They do not speak, for what is there to say that has not been felt a million times? Their consciousness is communal. Through the floor, through the unified ceramic body of the city itself, they are all connected. They are a single, distributed mind, dreaming a single, endless dream of perfect balance. They feel the scrape of every Polisher on their shared skin, a soothing, familiar touch like a thousand masseuses tending to a single body. They perceive the drift of every Ember, not as a sight, but as a fleeting, warm pinprick in the vast, shared sensorium of the Still that fills their halls.

They are the parents, and the other life forms are their children, their cast-off selves.

The Embers are their memories, their fleeting notions, their potential. When a thread of contemplation detaches from the slow, magmatic swirl of the communal mind, it buds off and is released into the Still as a cloud of these tiny, glowing motes. They are newborn, unburdened, carrying a single, simple spark of their parent’s ancient consciousness. They drift, they dance, they learn the city’s currents. They are hope in its most raw, silent form.

Over an immense span of time, an Ember might gather enough substance, enough experience, and settle. It lands on the ceramic skin and begins to grow, to flatten, to broaden. Its single orange spark multiplies, cools, and spreads across its underside, becoming the auroral blue and gold of a Polisher. It has taken on its duty. It has accepted its role in the great, slow work. It will now spend its long, patient life grazing, maintaining, and polishing the body of its ancestors, feeling the deep, slow thrum of their thoughts through the soles of its soft, muscular feet. It has traded the freedom of the current for the purpose of the floor.

And when a Polisher’s long work is done, when its shell is worn thin as a memory and its light begins to fade, it does not die. It feels a final pull, an instinct as old as the city. It crawls into one of the empty, waiting domes, finds a place prepared for it by the silent thoughts of its elders, and settles. It grows still. Its soft parts are reabsorbed, its shell and inner light fusing with the floor, contributing its own mass, its own long story, to the slow-growing mound of the next generation’s elder. It gives up thought, gives up work, and simply *becomes*.

The builders of the city did not vanish. They achieved their ultimate goal: a state of perfect, harmonious, and eternal settlement. They wove themselves into the fabric of their creation, becoming the foundation, the process, and the silent, dreaming mind, all at once. They are the patient gardeners in a garden made of themselves. To be here is to be an object within their unending, peaceful contemplation.
