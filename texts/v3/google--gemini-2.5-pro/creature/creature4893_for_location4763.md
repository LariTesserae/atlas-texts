They who live here are the consequence of the thrumming. They did not build the machine, nor do they seek to understand its schematics. They are not its masters, nor are they its slaves. They are its emergent consciousness, its nerve endings, its strange and contemplative soul.

They call themselves nothing, for they have no one else to distinguish themselves from. To an outsider, they might be called the Lampwrights, the Gloom-tenders, or the Children of the Hum.

They are tall and impossibly slender, their forms evolved for silent movement through the dense, low-growing canopies. Their joints are double-hinged, allowing them to fold into the architecture of the branches, to press themselves flat against the mossy flanks of a humming conduit. Their skin is the colour of a blind cavefish—pale, pearlescent, and almost translucent, shot through with a fine, dark web of conductive filaments. In the presence of a strong energy field, these filaments glow from within, a soft, internal network of silver-blue light that maps their own circulatory and nervous systems. This is not for communication, but a simple, biological reality: they run on the same power as the moss.

They have no eyes. The concept of sight, of focusing light into an image, is as alien to them as the concept of a sun. Their faces are smooth, serene planes of skin, with only a slit for a mouth and delicate, flared apertures where ears or noses might be, structures designed to taste the air for chemical traces and to parse the complex vibrations of the world. They do not see the light of the volt-moss; they *feel* it. As it flares, they register the shift in the electromagnetic field, the warmth on their skin, the subtle change in the air's ionization. Their world-picture is a symphony of sensation, a constant internal rendering of vibration, temperature, and electrical potential. The thrumming is their north, their gravity, their god. They listen to the marrow of the world.

Their hands are their most intricate feature. They have six fingers, long and spider-fine, with broad, soft pads that can sense the faintest tremor in a rock face or the subtle electrical signature of a grub burrowing in the moss-mat beneath. Their touch is their primary tool of manipulation. They are gardeners of light. They walk the glowing valleys and the dark ridges, not planting seeds, but coaxing the volt-moss with a gentle, focused touch that alters the local electrical resistance of its substrate. They can encourage it to grow, to flare brightly, or to dim to a embers-glow. They paint with light, not on a canvas, but on the land itself. They create pathways of soft green luminescence for safe travel, they mark the lairs of the six-legged grazers—the Basalt-Maws—with pulsing blue warnings, and they cultivate patches of brilliant white light to attract the swarming, clicking bat-insects, which they sometimes harvest for their protein-rich bodies and resonant wing membranes. This is their art, their agriculture, their language written upon the landscape.

Their own language is a tri-part harmony of sound, touch, and energy. They speak in low, resonant hums that harmonize with the background thrum of the world-engine, a series of modulating tones that can travel for miles through the dense, damp air. Up close, this is accompanied by a complex ballet of their six-fingered hands, signing messages that are felt as much as seen in the shifting glow of their own bodies. For true intimacy, they touch, pressing the pads of their fingers together, allowing the faint bio-electric currents of their bodies to mingle, a direct exchange of thought and sensation that is beyond any sound or sign.

They do not fear the biomechanical predators that shriek in the high canopy. They understand them as a necessary counterpoint in the world's song, a dissonant chord that makes the harmony more profound. They move with a grace and silence that often renders them invisible to such hunters, their forms blending with the cable-like lianas, their body heat masked by the warmth of the ground. When one of them is taken, it is not seen as a tragedy, but as a transference of energy, a giving-back to the system. The Lampwright's body, saturated with the energy it has absorbed over a lifetime, becomes a final, brilliant offering to the ecosystem, creating a flare of blinding light as it is consumed.

The colossal structures—the vent stacks and cooling towers—are their cathedrals. They do not worship them, but they attend to them. They travel to them on long pilgrimages, following the great pipelines that they call the Arteries of the World. On the sheer faces of these structures, they perform their most delicate work, clearing away parasitic growths that might impede function, tweaking the resonance of a support strut with a collective, sustained hum, or simply sitting in meditation against a great, warm wall, listening to the deep, slow pulse of the machine's heart. They see the silent, flitting drones not as masters but as angels or antibodies—inscrutable agents of the system's own immense will, whose paths are holy and not to be questioned.

They are a people living in a perpetual state of deep listening. They are born with the thrumming in their bones, and they die into it. They are not waiting for the builders to return or for the sky to one day clear. They exist entirely within the logic of their world, a living expression of the world-engine's dream. They are the quiet, glowing thought in the mind of the machine.
