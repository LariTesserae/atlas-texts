They who live in Iskar are the Iskari, though they do not use this name for themselves. In their quiet, rasping tongue, they are simply the *Khatt-i-Ghor*, the People of the Stone and the Ghor. They are as much a feature of the geology as a people who inhabit it.

Their physique is a testament to their world. The Iskari are broad and compact, with dense bones and powerful, low-slung muscles developed from a lifetime of hauling stone and navigating vertical spaces. Their chests are deep and barrel-like, a biological adaptation to the thin, high-altitude air, allowing their large lungs to draw every possible molecule of oxygen. Their hearts beat with a slow, deliberate rhythm, conserving energy. Their hands are their most expressive feature: wide, calloused, with thick, strong fingers and knuckles like river stones. The lines on their palms are not just from fate but are etched deep with the grit of stone and the oil of treated hide. An Iskari's handshake is a geological event, a brief, firm pressure that speaks of immense potential force, held in check.

Their skin is pale, the color of wet slate or the underbelly of a mushroom, having known little direct sunlight in the deep shadows of the canyon. But their faces—the parts exposed above the wraps of hair and hide—are weathered by the wind and cold. Their cheeks and the bridges of their noses are often a raw, chapped red, and fine networks of broken capillaries map the hardship of the climate. Their eyes, set deep beneath heavy brows to shield against wind and the occasional blinding glare off a distant ice-field, are typically dark and still. They hold a profound, unnerving quietness, the stillness of a creature that knows how to wait. An Iskari does not fidget. They do not pace. They settle into a space, into a moment, with the weight and implacability of a boulder.

To live here is to master a different kind of perception. The Iskari have a "vertical sense" as innate as hearing or sight. From infancy, they learn the feel of balance on a narrow ledge, the subtle shift in weight that precedes a stone's slippage, the hum that travels through a sinew-bridge underfoot. They can read the structural integrity of a rock face with a glance, discerning the flaws and stresses in the stone as another might read a book. They have no concept of vertigo; for them, the sheer drop outside their window is not a threat but a simple, immutable law of their world, a neighbor they have learned to live beside with quiet respect. Falling is not a fear so much as a final, profound failure of attention.

Their culture is one of extreme patience and pragmatism. The unit of time is not a day or a year, but a generation. "Haste" is a word for a type of madness, a spiritual sickness that leads to cracked stones, broken bones, and wasted effort. A conversation with an Iskari can be an unnerving experience for an outsider; their pauses are long, filled with thought, and they speak with a slow, deliberate gravity, as if each word must be quarried and shaped before being offered. They see the world in terms of endurance. The highest praise one can give is to say something—a tool, a dwelling, a person, a bond—is *dûr*, "it endures."

Their society is structured around the Ledge-Family, a unit that is both kin and geographical reality. Your Ledge-Family consists of those whose dwellings are structurally connected to your own. Their stability is your stability. A crack in your neighbor's foundation is a crack in your own world. This fosters a deep, unspoken, and fiercely practical form of interdependence. Help is not asked for; it is seen, and it is given. A family struggling to secure a new slab will find their neighbors appearing silently at dawn, antler picks in hand, to share the labor. No thanks are expected. The work itself is the communication.

Spirituality for the Iskari is tangible. They do not worship distant sky-gods; their pantheon is the ground beneath their feet and the life that sustains them. The deepest, most profound entity is the mountain itself—the Stone-That-Is. It is neither benevolent nor malevolent; it simply *is*, and to live is to learn its ways, to listen to its deep, slow language of fissure and stress. The Ghor are sacred, seen as avatars of the mountain's slow, placid strength, a gift of motion and life from the unmoving stone. To waste any part of a Ghor is the gravest sin, an insult to the mountain, the animal's spirit, and the generations of ancestors who survived on its generosity.

Their art is inseparable from function. There is no painting on stretched hide, no sculpture for mere display. Art is the perfect, almost invisible joinery of two slate slabs. It is the intricate, geometric carving on the handle of a basalt maul, a pattern that not only tells the tool's lineage but also provides a flawless grip. It is the series of knots tied in a hide rope, a language of strength and purpose. The great halls, formed from the ribcages of long-dead Ghor, are their cathedrals, and the slow, rhythmic *chink* of a hammer on a chisel is their choir. The chants they sing while working are low and monotonous, less melody than a resonating hum designed to feel the vibrations in the stone around them.

The Iskari are a people of the deep, resonant quiet. They communicate as much with silence as with words. The raising of an eyebrow, a slight tilt of the head, the way one places a cup of lichen tea on a stone table—these are all part of a rich, subtle lexicon. They are wrapped in gray and brown layers of felt and hide, often wearing masks of polished Ghor bone over their mouths not just to warm the frigid air but as a mark of their culture's inwardness. To be unmasked is to be vulnerable, emotionally raw.

To see them at work is to see a form of worship. A stone mason will sit with a new slab for a day, tracing its grain with a bare hand, listening to the sounds it makes when tapped, seeking to understand its nature before striking the first blow. To see them at rest is to witness a profound settling. An elder, wrapped in a thick Ghor-hide blanket, will sit on their ledge for hours, watching the mist curl in the depths of the canyon below—The Great Below, their term for the unseen, unknowable abyss that is both their foundation and their final destination. They are not watching nothing; they are communing with the slow, immense, and patient reality of their world. They are another form of stone, one that breathes and dreams of stability.
