The inhabitants of this geode-city are the Chthonic Tenders. They are as much a part of the city's biological and mechanical cycle as the sweating pipes and the heat-drinking vines. To see one is to see a living component of the machine, as inseparable from its function as a gear or a valve.

Their skin is the first thing one would notice, for it is not skin in the way a surface-dweller knows it. It is a semi-permeable membrane, perpetually slick with a secretion that is part mineral, part oil. This glistening integument has the faint, opalescent sheen of the Substrate itself, and it serves multiple purposes. It allows them to grip the greasy, condensation-slick surfaces of the ironwork and crystal with preternatural surety. It protects them from the razor edges of the selenite and quartz, more prone to yielding and deforming than to tearing. Most importantly, it is an organ of respiration.

They do not so much breathe the thick, water-logged air as they absorb it. The atmosphere is too dense for conventional lungs to process efficiently; it would be like trying to breathe syrup. Instead, the Tenders osmotically filter the ambient vapor through their skin and through a complex, gill-like membrane that lines their throats and sinuses. When they open their mouths, which is rare, one can see this inner membrane gently undulating, a soft, grey, velvety curtain that sifts what they need from the oppressive heat. They are, in a very literal sense, drinking the air. This method of respiration is almost entirely silent, contributing to the profound, unnerving quiet of the city. Their own bodies make no sound of breath, only the faint, wet slap of their bare feet on the iron grating.

Physically, they are elongated and gracile, with limbs that seem too long for their torsos. This gives them an arachnidan grace as they move through the three-dimensional labyrinth of their home. Their musculature is not bulky but dense and fibrous, like woven cable, built for slow, constant, patient effort. Their hands and feet are nearly identical: long, elegant digits with broad, tough pads that can splay wide for grip or curl tight around a pipe or tool. They often move on all fours, their bodies low to the walkways, distributing their weight and maintaining a stable center of gravity on the treacherous surfaces. Their spines are remarkably flexible, allowing them to curl through tight spaces in the scaffolding or stretch to reach a distant valve.

Their faces are simple and placid. Their eyes are large, dark, and devoid of white, like pools of crude oil. They have no pupils to speak of, as the concept of "bright light" is utterly alien here; their entire world is lived in a perpetual twilight of bioluminescence. These eyes are not for sharp, detailed vision, but for perceiving shades of movement and subtle shifts in the low, pulsing glow of the mosses and fungi.

Their primary sense is not sight, but a form of vibratory tactility. They feel the city through the pads of their hands and feet. The deep, metabolic thrumming of the central engine is their constant companion, their baseline of "normal." From this baseline, they can sense the world. A subtle change in the frequency of a pipe's vibration tells them the Substrate is thickening. A faint, high-pitched tremor in a crystal wall, imperceptible to an outsider, warns of a potential fracture weeks before it might occur. They lay their long-fingered hands on the great ceramic vats and "listen" to the stewing of the fluid within, their expressions as rapt as a sommelier tasting a priceless vintage.

They are born of the process they serve. In certain secluded alcoves of the city, there are ceramic pods, much like their dwellings, but sealed. These are the crèches. A trickle of purified Substrate, diverted from the main flow, feeds these wombs. Within, a Tender gestates, growing slowly in the warm, nutrient-rich fluid. When they are developed, the pod cracks along a pre-fired seam, and the new Tender emerges, fully formed, slick with fluid, their instincts already imprinted with the rhythm of the hum. They do not have parents or families; their progenitor is the city itself.

Their "language" is as subtle as their senses. They rarely speak, and when they do, it is a series of low, guttural clicks and hums from the back of the throat, a sound that is easily lost in the ambient drone. Their primary communication is through gesture, a slow sign language of the hands, and more profoundly, through slight, controlled modulations of their skin's shimmering surface. A ripple of iridescence down an arm can be a warning; a uniform dulling of the sheen, a signal of contentment or that all is well.

When a Tender's life ends—not through violence, which is nonexistent, but through a simple, slow cessation of function—they are returned to the city. Their bodies are carried to the great, primary vats where the raw Substrate is brewed. They are committed to the fluid, their physical forms dissolving back into the chemical soup, their matter becoming part of the very substance they spent their lives refining. There is no ceremony, for it is not a tragedy. It is the completion of a cycle. They served the process in life, and they feed it in death.

They are not individuals in a human sense. They are the sentient ganglia of the city-machine, the slow, conscious part of the process. They don't have desires, ambitions, or fears beyond the continued, unbroken functioning of the great engine. They are the living embodiment of the city's single, slow thought: the process must not stop.
