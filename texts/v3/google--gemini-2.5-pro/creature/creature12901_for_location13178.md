They are the Carvers. But they do not call themselves this, for they have no names. They do not have a language of spoken words, for their entire world is a single, unending utterance. They are the motile consciousness of the Umbral Arboretum, its nerve endings and its gentle hands.

To encounter a Carver is first to notice a subtle deviation in the symphony of the world. Amidst the planetary groan and the granitic scrape, a new sound enters: a quiet, rhythmic *tock... tock... tock*. It is a sound too deliberate for geology, too soft for collapse. It is the sound of purpose. It is the footfall of a Carver, its feet of hardened, polished lignolith meeting the seamless floor of the root-plate.

Following the sound, you would perceive not a creature of flesh and blood, but a sculpture given the gift of slow, deliberate motion. A Carver stands roughly the height of a human, but it is impossibly slender, a being of elegant, dark lines against a background of utter black. Its body is made of a fibrous, charcoal-dark wood, more porous and less dense than the polished lignolith of the Arboretum itself. Its surface is not smooth, but covered in a million microscopic pits and channels, like a sponge or a piece of driftwood, designed to absorb and interpret vibration.

It has no face. The head is a smooth, ovoid shape, featureless save for the intricate grain of the wood it is made from. There are no eyes, for there has never been anything to see. There is no mouth, for it has no need of food or breath in the human sense. Its nourishment is absorbed through its feet, a trickle of the same piezogenic energy that feeds the colossal trees. Its limbs are long and thin, jointed with a ball-and-socket arrangement that allows for impossibly fluid, silent motion. Its hands are long-fingered and delicate, yet possessed of an unyielding strength.

The Carver is a living sensorium. Its entire body is an ear. The grand drone of the Arboretum is its daylight, its ambient reality. It navigates the vast, echoing halls not by sight, but by *acoustimancy*. It feels the shape of a cavern by the way the chord of the world reflects from its unseen vaults. It can distinguish a pillar from an arch by the unique frequency at which each one hums. They feel the stresses building within the wood a continent away as a tightening in their own fibrous skeletons, a sympathetic resonance that sings through them like a plucked string. This is their map, their language, their universe.

They communicate through this medium. A Carver needing assistance will not shout. It will climb partway up a pillar, brace itself, and tap a precise rhythm with its resonant hammer. The pulse, a pure and perfect musical note, will travel through the lignolithic structure, faster than sound in air, across the boulevards and plazas of the city. Another Carver miles away will stop its work, its porous head cocked as if listening, and feel the message vibrate up through the soles of its feet—a request, a warning, a discovery. A change in the hymn.

Their work is a liturgy. They are not merely maintaining the city; they are in constant dialogue with it. A Carver will spend a week—a month—a year—pressed against a single colossal buttress, its porous body absorbing the structure's song. It is listening for the subtle dissonance that precedes a stress fracture, the discordant hum of unbalanced growth. Only when it has fully understood the structure's need will it unlimber its tools.

The tools are part of them, often grown with them and shaped over centuries. When a Carver swings its adze, the motion is one of sublime grace, a dancer's arc in the echoing dark. The strike is not an act of violence, but of tuning. And in that moment of impact, a miracle occurs. The hyper-dense tool edge shears the lignolith, and for a fraction of a second, a spark of cold, blue-white light shrieks into existence.

In that flash, you see what the Carver truly is. The spark illuminates its dark, slender form, clinging to a wall of wood the size of a mountain range. You see the whorls and grain in its body, patterns of age and experience. For that instant, the Carver is not blind. It receives a single, perfect frame of visual data, a confirmation of the world it has already mapped with sound and touch. The spark shows it the precise grain it needs to follow, the hairline fissure its other senses detected. Then the darkness crashes back in, absolute and complete, and the Carver continues its work, guided now by a fleeting memory of light.

They have no culture of art, story, or song as we would know it. Their entire existence is the art. The slow, perfect shaping of a vault is their epic poem. The continued, stable harmony of the Arboretum's drone is their grand symphony. They do not have individual identities; they are nodes of a single, city-spanning consciousness. A Carver does not think "I am carving this arch." It feels, "The arch requires carving, and this limb of the Arboretum is here to perform it."

They are born from the city and they return to it. In certain deep, quiet places, where the resonance is purest, the Arboretum grows a 'bud,' a dense knot of the dark, porous wood. Over decades, it elongates, forms limbs, and one day, it detaches from the wall, a new Carver born with an innate understanding of its purpose. And when a Carver's long life ends—when its movements slow and the energy it absorbs is not enough to sustain it—it does not die. It finds a place where the city's structure is weak, a place that needs reinforcement. It braces itself, becomes still, and over the course of a century, its body hardens, petrifies, and merges seamlessly back into the lignolith. It becomes architecture, its final act a contribution to the chord, its own existence a grace note added to the unending hymn of the Umbral Arboretum.
