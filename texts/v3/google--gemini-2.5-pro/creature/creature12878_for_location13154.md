They are the Ghyrma. The name is not their own, for they have no names, but it is what the silence calls them. They are the thoughts of this place given slow, soft-bodied form.

To see one is to see the landscape in motion. One emerges from the grey veil of mist not as a distinct creature, but as a swell in the clay itself, a ripple of purpose on the primordial floor. It is the size of a sleeping child, a being of profound mass and deliberate presence. Its body is a single, locomotive muscle, a foot that is also a torso. As it contracts and expands, it processes across the mud with a sound like a heart breaking in slow motion, a wet, yielding tear followed by a heavy, sealing suck.

Its skin is its history. The underside, the vast plane that kisses the clay, is soft, porous, and pale, the color of new tissue. But its back is a different story. It is sheathed in overlapping, chitinous plates, like the armor of some colossal isopod. These plates are not grown; they are secreted. They are the same amber resin that forms the towers, but here they are personal, biographical. In the young, the plates are few, thin, and translucent, tinged with the green of the malachite veins they have crawled over. In the old, the carapace is a thick, dark cowl of umber and near-black, layered so densely that the creature's own form is obscured, becoming a mobile hillock, a fragment of architecture broken free. The iridescence common to the young—a fleeting shimmer of peacock-oil on their flanks—fades with age, replaced by the dull, profound luster of ancient amber.

They have no face. The front of a Ghyrma is a blunt, featureless prow. There are no eyes; in this world of diffuse, eternal twilight, sight is a forgotten indulgence. There is no mouth in the way we understand it. Instead, where a mouth would be, there is a complex, recessed orifice, a glandular pout that is usually withdrawn into the body's mass. This is their tool, their voice, their legacy. From this organ they drink the mineral-rich slurry of the ground, and from it they extrude the two substances that define their existence: the glistening slime that paves their way, a chemical map of intent and memory for others to follow; and the thick, honey-like resin that, over a lifetime, they will spin and pat into the walls of their home.

Their perception is a symphony of the other senses. Two feathery antennae, delicate and white like sterile fungi, are their primary organs. They do not just feel the air; they taste it, reading the subtle shifts in humidity, the particle-scent of a distant bladder-orchid, or the mineral signature of an untapped clay deposit. They sense the world through vibration, their entire bodies acting as a diaphragm pressed against the ground, feeling the distant, slow progress of their kin, the minute tremors of a geothermal vent sighing steam.

Consciousness here is not individual. The low, thrumming hum that emanates from the towers is the physical manifestation of their shared mind. It is a slow and silent consensus, a collective dream of form and function. There are no arguments, no leaders, no dissent. There is only the Plan, which is not a blueprint but a persistent, shared instinct—a feeling for where a new tower should rise to balance the emptiness, where a buttress is needed to support the weight of the water-logged vines. A young Ghyrma, beginning its first extrusion, is not taught. It simply listens to the hum and remembers a future history, adding its own thread of resin to a pattern that has existed for millennia.

They are gardeners as much as architects. You will see them paused for days beside the great vines, their antennae gently stroking the velvety leaves, their bodies pressed against the woody stems, perhaps monitoring the flow of nutrients. They cultivate the glowing moss, nudging it into crevices where its cool light is most needed. Their lives are impossibly long and placid, measured in the gradual thickening of a wall or the full maturation of an orchid.

The rest of life exists in their shadow, in the ecosystem they secrete. The gleaming Chitin-Breakers, beetles whose iridescent carapaces mimic the Ghyrma’s own youthful sheen, are their janitors. They scuttle in the foundations, consuming waste, hardened slime-spill, and any organic matter that begins to decay. They are, in turn, the prey for the Glass-Stalkers, the translucent, finger-long amphibians that haunt the wet resin walls. These hunters are nearly invisible, their bodies like living lenses, their movements so patient they are almost indistinguishable from the dripping condensation.

And above, drifting in the still, heavy air, are the Pollen-Ghosts. These are the moths, their wings not of membrane but of a fine, black, velvet dust that seems to drink the light. They are the city's couriers, flitting from the glowing stamens of one bladder-orchid to another, their silent passage essential for the propagation of the plants that serve as the city's reservoirs. Their flight is the only swift movement in this entire world, a flicker of silent urgency in a kingdom of geologic patience.

When a Ghyrma’s long life ends, there is no ceremony. Its movement slows over decades, its extrusions cease. It finds a place at the base of its tower, often in a hollow or alcove it prepared long ago. It settles. The hum of its life rejoins the great collective completely. Over years, its soft tissues dissolve, seeping back into the mineral paste of the floor, enriching it. Its carapace, the hard-won story of its life, remains, fusing with the resin of the tower itself. It becomes a foundation stone, a dark and permanent knot in the architecture it spent a lifetime building. In this city without graves, death is not an end, but an act of becoming. The builders do not simply live in the city; they become it.
