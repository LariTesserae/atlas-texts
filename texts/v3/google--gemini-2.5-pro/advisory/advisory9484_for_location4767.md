### **Traveler's Preparatory Briefing: The Sustaining Cathedral**

**An Introduction to Your Destination**

You are not going to a world. You are going *inside* a world-sized organism. The distinction is critical. Forget concepts like geology, weather, and distinct ecosystems. Here, there is only the organism, its architecture, its metabolism, and its symbiotes. It has been described by previous travelers as a city built by a single plant, a self-maintaining factory, and a cathedral whose god is the very stone it's made from. It is all of these things. Your primary goal is not exploration in the traditional sense, but observation and quiet passage. The Prime Directive here is absolute: **You are a ghost in the machine. You will leave no trace.**

### **I. Attire & Essential Gear: The Principle of Non-Contamination**

Your gear selection must be guided by two principles: personal comfort in a high-humidity environment and zero impact on the organism.

**1. Clothing: The Second Skin**

*   **Primary Layers:** Absolutely no cotton. The air is so saturated with moisture that cotton will become a heavy, cold, and permanently damp shroud within an hour. Opt for high-performance, moisture-wicking synthetic fabrics (polyester, nylon blends) or fine merino wool. These will pull moisture from your skin and allow it to evaporate, though evaporation will be slow. Multiple thin layers are superior to one thick one.
*   **Outer Layer:** A lightweight, highly breathable waterproof shell is essential. Not for rain, but for the constant, cool condensation that drips from the architecture above. A hardshell will also prevent your own skin's oils and microbes from being directly transferred should you accidentally brush against a surface.
*   **Footwear:** The living floor is described as resilient but can be slick where moisture beads on the silvery filaments or pools in the shallow undulations. You need boots with exceptional grip, akin to high-end canyoneering or wet-rock climbing shoes. They must be waterproof, not just for your comfort, but to prevent the water of the organism from soaking into your footwear and being carried away. Before departure, your boots must be sterilized to remove any foreign seeds, spores, or microbes.
*   **Headwear:** A wide-brimmed waterproof hat will keep the constant dripping off your face and neck, which can be a significant psychological irritant over time.

**2. Sustenance: A Closed System**

*   **Food:** **DO NOT CONSUME ANYTHING FROM THIS PLACE.** The fluids in the conduits, the moisture on the floor, the flesh of the symbiotes—all are part of a complex biological system. At best, they are non-nutritious. At worst, they are biochemically incompatible with human physiology or outright toxic. You must bring all your own food. Nutrient pastes, vacuum-sealed rations, and energy bars are ideal. No loose crumbs, no organic waste.
*   **Water:** The water here is not water as you know it. It is a biological solvent, a transport medium for nutrients and waste products within the organism. It is not potable. Bring your own water in sealed, durable containers. A high-grade filtration system is useless; you cannot filter out dissolved complex organic compounds. Calculate your needs generously. If you run out, you must leave.

**3. Observation & Navigation Gear**

*   **Light:** While daylight filters down, the immense halls can have deep shadows. A headlamp is essential, but use it sparingly and with a red-light function to minimize disturbance. The organism’s own bioluminescence is a key feature; your bright white light will wash it out.
*   **Optics:** Bring powerful but lightweight binoculars. The scale is cathedral-like. You will want to observe the mechanics of the vaulted ceilings, the unfurling of distant vents, and the shifting of the upper canopy without needing to traverse miles of the living floor. A macro lens for your recording device is also recommended for studying the small custodian-symbiotes without disturbing them.
*   **Navigation:** The environment can be disorienting and repetitive. A standard compass should function, but its utility will depend on whether this organism generates any significant magnetic fields. Far more reliable will be to create your own map. Use major pillars, conduit junctions, and unique bioluminescent patterns as landmarks. A wrist-mounted GPS will function for tracking your own path and ensuring you can retrace your steps, but it will not provide you with a pre-existing map. Tying markers is forbidden. You mark your path mentally and with your own data, not by scarring the environment.

### **II. Behavioral Protocols: The Art of Invisibility**

Your behavior is more critical than your gear. A mistake here could be catastrophic, not for you, but for the eons-old balance of the system.

**1. Movement and Interaction**

*   **Tread Softly:** Walk on the main, resilient floor. Do not attempt to climb the pillars or access the conduits. They are organs, not ladders. The fine silvery filaments are likely delicate sensory or transport structures; avoid stepping on them where possible.
*   **The No-Touch Rule:** Do not touch anything with your bare hands. This is for your protection and for the organism's. The surfaces may harbor defensive biochemicals, or you may introduce a fatal microbe. Do not lean against pillars. Do not sit directly on the floor without a groundsheet.
*   **Waste Management:** This is absolute. Pack it in, pack it out. This includes all food wrappers, depleted batteries, and especially human waste. A portable sanitary waste kit (often called a "WAG bag") is not optional; it is a mandatory piece of equipment. Leaving human waste would be like injecting a foreign, bacteria-laden substance into the organism's tissue.
*   **Sound Discipline:** The environment is defined by a deep, organic hum. This is its "sound of silence." Do not contribute to it. Speak in low voices. Avoid using any loud equipment. Let the thrum, the clicks, and the sighs be the only sounds you hear. It is the language of the place; listen to it.

**2. Interacting with "Fauna"**

The creatures here are not animals in the way you understand them. They are living components, biological maintenance drones. The crustacean-scrapers and ester-feeding moths are as vital to this place as its own vascular system.
*   **Observe from a distance.** Do not touch, capture, or startle them. Their work is part of the perfection of the system. Frightening a colony away from a pressure-vent could, in some small but cascading way, disrupt a process that has run smoothly for millennia.

### **III. The Psychological Element: Adjusting to the Pace**

Prepare for a profound sensory and temporal shift.

*   **Embrace the Slow:** This world operates on a biological timescale. Dramatic events unfold over hours or days. A vent unfurls with the patience of a blooming flower. Nutrient flows pulse with the rhythm of a resting heart. If you are accustomed to rapid gratification and constant stimuli, you will find this place maddeningly inert. You must slow yourself down. Meditate. Sit for an hour and simply watch one section of the ceiling. You are not here to *do* anything, but to *witness* everything.
*   **The Hum:** The pervasive, low-frequency hum will become the backdrop to your existence. For some, it is calming, a reassurance of the life all around them. For others, it can become an oppressive, unnerving presence. Earplugs can provide temporary relief, but a better strategy is to acclimate, to let it become part of your own sense of being.
*   **Solitude and Scale:** You will feel profoundly alone, even with companions. The sheer scale of the void-filled halls can induce agoraphobia in some, while the intricate, living detail can feel claustrophobic to others. You are a microscopic speck in a vast, living body. This can be a source of terror or of sublime, ego-dissolving awe. Know yourself, and be prepared for either reaction.

Go to observe, to be humbled, and to leave no trace. You are a thought passing through a mind made of wood and light, and when you are gone, it should not even register that you were ever there.
