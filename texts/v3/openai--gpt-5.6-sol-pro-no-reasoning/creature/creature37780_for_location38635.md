The garden is inhabited by the **Wrought**: beings who are made rather than born, though the difference means less to them than it would elsewhere.

At a distance they resemble people. They keep households, raise children, practice trades, quarrel over paths, remember their dead, and sit together in patterned shade. Up close, no two agree on what a body should be made from. One may have skin like fine, unglazed ceramic, another a hide of overlapping cloth strips stiffened with resin. Some are wicker beneath a soft coat of ash; some are assembled from lacquered wooden pieces joined by cords drawn through the joints. Teeth may be shell, fired clay, flint, or tiny tablets impressed with family marks. Their eyes are usually polished stones, beads, knots of colored glass, or apertures lined with dark felt.

They are warm because warmth moves through them. Beneath the outer covering lie cords, hinges, nested frames, tensioned loops, ceramic sockets, and packets of spice and red sealing powder. Their organs are not fixed from one generation to the next. An old anatomical panel might show a bell behind the ribs, three braided stomachs, and a thumb-sized kiln near the pelvis; a living patient may possess none of these and still be considered ordinary. What matters is not which parts they have, but whether those parts remain in agreement.

Each Wrought carries a **binding**, a system of internal tensions that makes all their materials insist on being one person. The binding is partly physical and partly social. Its major cords are tightened during childhood and adjusted at rites of age, marriage, grief, apprenticeship, illness, and return. The knots nearest the heart are said to hold the name. For this reason names are not merely spoken. They are bitten, tied, scarred, tapped out on pottery, and taught to beetles that walk their syllables.

Most Wrought have only one visible mouth, but many have additional mouths hidden in the body: a whispering seam under the arm, a small hard palate inside the wrist, a lipless opening behind the knee. These do not eat. They hold old names, unfinished apologies, instructions for repairing the body, and words that must not be exposed to shifting eastlight. During a loosening, people place their principal names in whichever mouth closes most securely.

Their children begin as **gatherings**. Families prepare a shallow heated cradle and place within it donated materials: a finger-length of cord, a pinch of ash, a bead, a sliver shaved from a household threshold, filings from an ancestor’s teeth, fragments of tools, and one small component whose origin is deliberately unknown. For many days the gathering remains only a careful arrangement. Then shadows start to collect around it. The materials draw closer when unwatched. A new sound appears in the room—the faint creak of tension taking hold.

Eventually the gathering acquires appetite, direction, and a refusal to come apart. Only then is it recognized as a child.

Parentage is therefore plural and imprecise. One person may have supplied the hinge of a jaw, another the fibers around the lungs, another the first remembered gesture. A lintel beast sometimes contributes a chip fallen from its sleeping face. Trees occasionally lower a new clay leaf into an infant cradle. Objects have been found in children that no craftsperson remembers adding: a future tooth, a sealed eye, a little door that opens inward onto warmth. Such parts are not removed unless they cause pain. They are treated as gifts from the completed garden.

Young Wrought are flexible in both body and category. Their limbs may change number during growth. A child who sleeps repeatedly in a narrow cupboard may become thin enough to pass beneath doors; one fostered near a kiln may develop brick-lined chambers in the torso and be apprenticed as a potter. Adults discourage premature certainty. A child asked what they will become is expected to name a material, a tool, and a direction rather than an occupation.

Death is called **coming unpersuaded**.

The binding slackens, and the body’s materials gradually stop accepting the argument that they belong together. It is rarely sudden. The dying may begin to appear in adjoining rooms, or their shadow may remain seated after they stand. Joints point toward remembered places. A familiar hand is found attached to an unfamiliar history. Families gather close and repeat the person’s construction: who tied them, who repaired them, which doorway first recognized them, what animal slept beside their cradle, and where east stood on the morning of their naming.

Afterward, the body is not buried. There is no soil to receive it. It is carefully unmade.

Useful pieces return to the household, but not necessarily for useful purposes. A grandmother’s rib may become part of a roof brace, then a child’s toy, then the handle of a ritual knife that never cuts. Teeth are kept longest because they resist revision. The red powder is fired into tablets. Name-knots are loosened over several years, one loop at a time. A deceased person may therefore persist diffusely through a district: in tools, walls, descendants, furniture, livestock harnesses, and the turn of a particular gate.

This is not considered survival. Survival requires tension. But neither is it absence.

Among the Wrought live other, less settled kinds of inhabitants.

The six-legged hares are thought to have been assembled before symmetry became fashionable. Their extra middle pair of legs allows them to remain stable when distance shifts under them. They eat wax leaves and shed warm felt in flat, seamless pieces, which tailors use to line sickrooms. Their ears are stiff vanes that turn toward structural strain. A room full of hares facing the same wall is evacuated without debate.

The ceramic beetles are not merely animals but minor surveyors. Each carries a simple geometry in its shell. One beetle knows straightness, another parallelism, another the repeat of a particular curve. Craftspeople place them on new work and watch the routes they take. If a straightedge beetle wanders, the line may be visually straight but false in some deeper way. Children train them with grease and warmth, though the beetles often correct their teachers.

The tiny lace-winged birds feed on unused sound. They nest where conversations nearly happened: inside sleeves, beneath thresholds, in the hollows of stopped chimes. At dawn they open their beaks and release no audible song, yet shadows quiver around them. It is said they keep the garden quiet by consuming noises before those noises are made. Large colonies near a house may indicate a coming argument, a future celebration, or a room that has forgotten an entire family.

The lintel beasts are older in memory than in material. Each seems freshly carved, its tool marks sharp, yet civic records attribute decisions to the same beasts across hundreds of years. Their heavy shoulders fit naturally over doorways, but no one has seen one climb into place. They move only after long deliberation. Where one chooses to lie becomes protected by custom, shade, and an indefinable structural confidence. Where one leaves is considered finished, condemned, or forgiven.

They may be guardians, but not of the people. Their attention appears fixed on entrances themselves. A lintel beast will ignore fire, sickness, and theft, then wake abruptly because someone has made a door where no passage is needed.

Spindle-vines may also count as inhabitants. Gardeners argue the point while cutting them back. The mouths carved into their beads whisper against posts, tasting the tension in structures and bodies. They seem drawn to sleepers because sleep loosens the distinction between person and place. Their stitched fruit contain black sand warmed from no apparent source. The named teeth inside are taken to the House of First Materials, where they are catalogued until their future owners appear. When such a person is finally born, the tooth is not implanted. It is shown to them at maturity, so they know the garden had a place prepared in which to bite their name.

Then there are the incomplete ones: feet without bodies, antlers without heads, the polishing purr beneath leaves. The Wrought do not assume these are fragments of absent wholes. A procession of feet may be complete as feet. Antlers may require no animal. To demand the rest would be considered crude, like insisting that every door must have a room.

Some incomplete beings become neighbors. Food is left for a warm hollow that passes through one lane each evening. A set of hands lives in a basket-makers’ quarter and joins in whenever difficult weaving is underway. An elderly household keeps a profile—forehead, nose, lips, and chin—which appears only along the edge of a particular curtain. They address it as an uncle. It remembers their childhoods more accurately than they do.

A few inhabitants are created by rearrangement rather than craft. After a loosening, a workshop may contain a woman everyone recognizes as its grandmother. She has old hands, strong opinions, and memories of teaching techniques invented only last year. She is neither impostor nor apparition. The relationships around her possess knots, scars, tooth wear, and habitual tenderness; those evidences are accepted. She takes her place at the bench.

Such arrivals are sometimes called **the Familiar**, though they are no single species. They come supplied with belonging.

The keeper of the empty jar is chosen from among people whose bindings are unusually tight. Keepers tend to be plain in construction, dense-jointed, with few hidden mouths and no appetite for revision. Over years of watching the liquid absence, however, they grow less definite. Color fades first from the eye that faces the jar. Later, tools forget their grip. Relatives may remember the keeper’s deeds but not their shape. No keeper serves until death. They are released when their shadow begins to pass behind objects without touching the floor.

Former keepers often become mapmakers, because they are able to perceive spaces the garden has declined to make.

Beneath all these lives the vast slow breather.

No one agrees whether it is an inhabitant of the garden or whether the garden inhabits it. Its exhalations warm every furnace root, every sleeping body, every stone cradle and animal burrow. Some say the layered floors are its skin: ceramics, mats, mosaics, bone-pale plates, each a scale or bandage or garment laid over the enormous form. Others believe it is a machine still being assembled, and that each generation unknowingly adds one part. Others say it is the first Wrought, so immense that its binding became geography.

During the inhalation, which few individuals live long enough to recognize, the garden cools by imperceptible degrees. Cords tighten. East wanders farther each morning. The spindle-vines draw inward. More children are born with doors in their bodies.

During the exhalation, the garden grows warm and accommodating. New rooms form. Old repairs find their wounds. Creatures arrive with their pens already waiting. People dream of being held in a maker’s hands the size of streets.

The Wrought have no certain account of what will happen when the breath finally ends. Perhaps the great being will wake. Perhaps it will speak. Perhaps the entire garden is only the object it has been slowly making in its sleep.

They do not devote much labor to the question. There are leaves to replace, knots to inspect, animals to feed, doors to turn toward the latest east. Existence here is not regarded as a mystery to solve, but as a structure requiring continual, affectionate maintenance.

So they mend one another.

They tighten the slack. They preserve useful incompleteness. They make vessels for organs that may someday need them. They leave the accidental rooms empty.

And whenever the whole garden warms around them, they pause briefly in their work—not in fear, but in recognition—placing their palms against the nearest crafted surface to feel the long breath passing through everything that has been made.
