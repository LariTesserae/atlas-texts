The people of the Husk call themselves the Resonant, though the word in their own speech means several things at once: one who strikes, one who answers, and the substance through which an answer travels.

They are slight, long-limbed beings, seldom heavy enough to strain the narrow bridges they cross. Their bodies feel warm only after labor. At rest they approach the temperature of the wall and can become difficult to distinguish from it, even by touch. Their skin is finely striated, with a grain that runs differently across each part of the body: lengthwise down the limbs, in whorls over the joints, and in close rings around the throat. Age adds new lines rather than wrinkles. Elders can be read with a fingertip.

No two have quite the same texture. Some are smooth as worn bark; others are fibrous, scaled, or furred with pale sensory filaments. These filaments are especially dense along the jaw, palms, soles, and ribs. They lift in response to distant vibration, allowing a resting citizen to feel the approach of work long before its sound arrives.

Their faces are narrow and variable. Features do not settle into perfect symmetry, as though each person was folded rather than assembled. The eyes are sealed beneath translucent plaques and have no known function. Some remove these coverings ceremonially and reveal shallow, dry cavities lined with reflective tissue. Others regard the plaques as listening organs and would no more cut them away than sever a hand.

The mouth is used for speech, feeding, and resonance. Teeth are not teeth in the animal sense, but rows of hard growths periodically shed and replaced. The tongue is dark, divided near the tip, and sensitive to airborne dust, heat, and electrical change. A citizen entering an unfamiliar chamber often tastes the air before speaking.

Inside, their anatomy resembles neither flesh nor timber entirely. Their limbs contain bundles of taut fibers around hollow channels. Their joints are layered with smooth resinous pads. Instead of a beating heart, they possess several contractile knots distributed through the torso and thighs. These tighten in sequence when the body moves, pushing warm vapor and nutrient dust through narrow passages. In silence the sequence slows. In prolonged silence it may stop.

For this reason they hum in their sleep.

A household at rest produces a low, irregular chord: throat notes, finger taps, heel movements against the floor. Solitary travelers carry small clappers tied to wrist or ankle. To fall utterly quiet in an acoustically dead chamber is dangerous. The body loses its internal measure. Thoughts become discontinuous; limbs stiffen; memory detaches from sequence. Rescue consists first of rhythm. A finder kneels nearby and knocks a simple pulse until the lost person’s contractile knots begin to answer.

The Resonant do not believe the individual ends at the skin. Breath enters the wall; voice travels through streets; warmth wakes edible growth; resin from an injury seals tools and doors. A person is therefore understood as a temporary concentration of exchanges. Identity lies in pattern, not matter.

Each citizen has a body-rhythm called a bearing. It is discovered during childhood by listeners who press their faces to the alcove where the infant has appeared. The bearing may be a double pulse followed by a drag, a tremor too rapid for the hand, or a long interval ending in a single sharp click. It becomes name, signature, legal testimony, and funeral mark.

Spoken names are used only at close range. A true introduction is sounded through a shared surface.

Their language has three intertwined forms. The voice carries immediate meaning. Hands and feet add grammatical rhythm. The surrounding structure supplies a third register through resonance. The same utterance spoken against bark, dead fiber, or living membrane may indicate certainty, memory, obligation, or doubt. Away from the wall, many statements are impossible to make precisely.

They dislike unsupported speech. Someone who makes promises in midair is considered evasive.

There are few children. When a warm alcove begins to fold inward, the nearest ward appoints a Vigil of Germination. For years, sometimes decades, attendants feed the site with warmth, breath, and carefully varied sound. They do not know whether their work creates the infant or merely permits it. The uncertainty is sacred.

The shape within first answers as a distortion in the wall’s echo. Later it develops a bearing. Only when the rhythm remains stable through an entire contraction cycle do the attendants open the alcove.

The new person emerges dry, curled, and nearly rigid. Fine root threads often join its back and heels to the chamber. These are not cut. The attendants sing and warm them until they release. A newly germinated child has no voice for some time, but taps constantly with fingers and tongue. It is raised by the ward rather than by any pair of adults, though the person whose bearing most closely resembles the child’s may become its echo-parent.

Kinship follows resemblance of rhythm. Two citizens may be siblings despite emerging centuries apart and in distant districts. Conversely, those found in neighboring alcoves may share no recognized relation.

Because births are so rare, no one is treated as replaceable. This does not make the Husk gentle. It makes punishment structural.

The worst sentence is not death but damping. A condemned person’s bearing is forbidden: it may not be sounded, answered, or incorporated into communal work. Thresholds bearing the rhythm are scraped smooth. Route tablets are altered. The person continues living, but the city ceases to resonate with them. Many eventually descend into the cold chambers and press themselves among the dead.

Ordinary disputes are settled through counter-rhythm. Opposing parties state their claims while striking the same resonator. If their patterns can be made to coexist without weakening the cultivated tissue, a settlement is possible. If the stalk produces destructive interference—sudden dulling, cracking, or loss of edible dust—the claims are judged incompatible and one must yield.

Their leaders are called Tuners. A Tuner possesses no palace and little power to command. Their work is to hear when districts have drifted out of relation. They travel constantly, carrying rods of different lengths and a cloak sewn with dry plaques that chatter softly as they walk. Most are elderly, with exceptional sensitivity to low frequencies. Some have had the bones of their feet deliberately thinned so they can feel vibrations others miss.

Below the Tuners are keepers of particular civic tissues: bridge coaxers, membrane singers, nodule gardeners, dust bakers, cold-chamber guides, and archivists of delayed sound. No occupation is entirely secular. To maintain the city is to maintain the organism that produces people; to maintain the organism is to negotiate with whatever intelligence it possesses.

Whether the seed is conscious remains the central disagreement of Husk philosophy.

The Tender doctrine holds that the city wall is a slow mind. Its thoughts require centuries to cross it. Growths are decisions; contractions are moods; germinations are acts of attention. Tenderers speak to their work surfaces and leave long pauses for replies.

The Instrumentalists believe the seed is alive but witless. Meaning comes only from the Resonant, who have taught ancient tissue to participate in civic life. To them, the Husk is not a parent but an instrument of almost unlimited depth.

The Enclosed teach that citizens and seed are one organism divided only by speed. The wall grows slowly, the people quickly; neither is complete without the other. They point to the dead absorbed into beams and ask where, exactly, a person ends.

A smaller and feared tradition claims the inhabitants are not the seed’s children but its disease: proliferating motile cells consuming reserves intended for an embryo that has not yet awakened.

Its adherents listen for a second bearing beneath the First Erosion’s note.

They are not always wrong.

The Resonant experience time through fatigue and return. There is no day, season, or year. Short intervals are measured by breath cycles, task rhythms, and the cooling of warmed tissue. Longer periods are counted by bridge retunings, migrations of avenues, and the rare contractions of the rind. History is arranged not by dates but by layers.

An event is said to be “three walls below” or “before the tightening of the Red Span.” Someone very old may be described as having crossed a street that no longer exists.

Memory is communal and physical. Important accounts are beaten into layered bark tablets, but the tablets do not encode words alone. Grain, thickness, cavity, and edge determine how each passage must be struck. A skilled archivist can perform a treaty, a migration, or the collapse of a district as an extended sequence of taps and abrasions.

Copies are never exact, so histories evolve. This is not considered corruption. A memory incapable of adapting to a new resonator is thought to have become dead matter.

Some archivists study echoes still circulating through the Great Interior. They work from isolated balconies and compare returning patterns against records of old ceremonies. Most returns are blurred beyond interpretation. Occasionally, however, an echo arrives with details absent from every archive: an extra strike, an unfamiliar bearing, a response where the original account records silence.

These additions are called unborn testimony.

No consensus exists about their source.

The Resonant wear little. Garments are wraps of bast fiber, layered for warmth and tied to leave hands, soles, and ribs exposed to vibration. Rank is marked by texture rather than color: polished cords for bridge workers, rough fringes for gardeners, thin rattling slats for Tuners. Those who tend the dead wear soft material that makes almost no sound.

Adornment consists of knots, seed-shell splinters, hardened resin, carved thorns, and tiny cold grains of metal. A person may carry pieces of former dwellings threaded around the neck. Lovers exchange plaques shaved from the insides of their own sleeping alcoves. These retain warmth and scent for many cycles.

Courtship is often conducted at a distance. Two people choose opposite ends of a bridge or resonator and exchange variations on one another’s bearings. They may continue for years before meeting. Affection is understood as accurate answer: not repetition, but a response that reveals something hidden in the first rhythm.

Physical intimacy involves long stillness pressed against a shared surface. Partners listen to how the other’s inner contractions enter the wall and return altered. Such unions have no known connection to germination, though traditions persist that a perfectly matched pair may persuade the Husk to form a child somewhere far away.

Food grows as dense nodules, brittle films, and dust-filled bladders induced in cultivated tissue. Gardeners do not sow. They establish patterns of pressure and sound, then wait. A mature nodule may take most of a gardener’s life to develop. It is shaved thinly so the base remains alive. Flavor depends on resonance: bass-fed tissue is bitter and sustaining; high, rapid cultivation produces pale, fragrant flakes eaten at ceremonies.

Communal loaves are not baked. Nutrient dust is compressed with resin and body warmth into porous cakes. Before eating, citizens breathe through them, loosening the particles. Meals are quiet by local standards, because wasting vibration during consumption is considered vulgar. The only sounds are breath, the click of replacement teeth, and a slow gratitude beat tapped into the floor.

They make art, though they have no visual image for it. Their greatest works are spaces engineered to transform sound: corridors that divide one footstep into a hundred; domes that return a whisper as a descending tone; membranes that hold a spoken word and release it only when warmed. Sculptors work in pressure, grain, hollowness, and delay.

One ancient artwork consists of an empty route requiring several days to traverse. Nothing stands along it. Each footstep sends a vibration forward through buried fibers, so that on arriving at the final chamber the traveler hears the accumulated journey strike all at once.

The Resonant call it the Person Made Audible.

They fear fire, moisture, severance, and silence. Fire is remembered without ever having been seen. It appears in doctrine as a devouring vibration that gives nothing back. Moisture is more obscure. Rare shell grains contain chemical traces of compounds no living citizen has encountered in bulk. From these, philosophers imagine a substance that flows without fibers, enters every hollow, softens the dead, and allows uncontrolled growth.

Some worship this imagined fluid. Others consider it a nightmare.

Severance is the practical horror: a bridge snapping, a district peeling from the rind, a membrane tearing in such a way that no vibration crosses the gap. The detached place becomes acoustically foreign before it falls. Citizens stranded there can still call across the cavity, but their voices arrive late and disembodied. Rescue may require generations of new growth.

Silence is feared most of all because it resembles nonexistence. Yet their deepest rites require it.

When the First Erosion sounds, every citizen tries to become still. For beings who must rhythmically sustain themselves, this is both worship and ordeal. Contractile knots slow. Hands flatten against the substrate. Voices cease. In those days of groaning, the boundary between body and city becomes difficult to maintain.

Some experience memories they never lived: abrasion against the outer shell, mineral pressure, a terrible distant heat, the sensation of falling through open air. Some feel themselves immense and dormant, with a whole city prickling like thought along the inside of their skin.

A few rise afterward with altered bearings.

These are called the Reanswered. They may know routes beneath sealed tissue or anticipate where a new alcove will form. They may also strike doors belonging to no district and address people not yet germinated. Their condition is honored but not trusted. Most become attendants at the First Erosion, where they spend their lives listening to the note from outside.

The oldest among them claim that the note is changing.

Not rising or falling, but developing a rhythm.

One interval repeats across contractions: a long pressure, two shorter abrasions, then a pause vast enough to contain generations. It resembles no civic pattern except, faintly, the bearing of an infant still enclosed somewhere in the wall.

So the inhabitants labor. They cultivate vibration, maintain bridges, coax rooms into being, and feed the tissue that feeds them. They cross empty districts guided by echoes of strangers. They press their dead into the cold ivory chambers and later walk across beams containing their ancestors’ hands. They answer delayed blows whose makers may have died centuries before.

They live carefully because nothing is plentiful except darkness and reverberation.

And beneath all their separate bearings, beneath the drums and clappers, the threshold names and bridge songs, another life may be proceeding—vast, patient, and not yet born.

The city believes it inhabits the seed.

The seed may believe the city is its dream of germination.
