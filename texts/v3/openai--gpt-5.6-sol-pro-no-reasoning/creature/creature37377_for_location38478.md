What lives there is difficult to divide into inhabitants and habitat. Each creature seems to be a temporary organ of the plateau, assembled for some task and eventually returned to roots, brine, and stone.

The most visible are the high walkers.

They stand on four or six narrow, many-jointed legs, with their torsos slung low between them like dark seedpods. From afar, a resting herd resembles a field of lichen-covered boulders. Their hides are not merely overgrown: they are layered ecosystems of insulating felt, mineral scales, symbiotic moss, and pressure-sensitive lichens. Grooves between these layers collect condensation and conduct it toward warm reservoirs inside the body.

Their heads are small and tucked beneath the forward torso. They have no obvious eyes. Instead, fans of metallic whiskers read electrical gradients, air pressure, and vibrations traveling through rock. They browse along condensation fronts, grazing filament colonies and the frozen skins of cushion plants without penetrating the warm interiors. When danger comes, valves open along their flanks. Stored gas escapes in controlled bursts, throwing them across the plateau in long, nearly silent bounds.

They migrate as part of the mountain’s circulation. Spores ripen on their backs. Metallic nodules accumulate in their gut chambers. Wherever they pause, they leave damp, chemically altered footprints in which brief gardens germinate. Thus a herd carries one ravine into another.

The cushion jungles contain their own populations.

Pale, soft-bodied grazers tunnel through the warm compost beneath the frozen outer leaves. They have dozens of short limbs and translucent skin through which dark digestive threads can be seen contracting. They prune dead tissue, turn the interior, and keep passages open for air and water. If a cushion splits, they gather along the wound and secrete a flexible black membrane over it. A colony can heal a plant the size of a hill.

Among them travel the pollinating mites: long-legged, glittering creatures scarcely heavier than frost. Their bodies collect conductive dust, so their processions shine with weak blue-green current. They descend into steaming fissures where the black flowers open, carrying pollen from one sheltered chamber to another. During electrical weather, thousands may lock legs and form living circuits across gaps in the vegetation.

Under leaves and overhangs cling glass-winged crawlers. Their wings are not used for powered flight. They are clear heat-exchange vanes ribbed with fine channels. By opening or folding them, the crawlers condense moisture, regulate their temperature, and sometimes sail a few meters on rising air. When threatened, they flatten until their bodies become indistinguishable from wet stone.

The cliff forests shelter the furred gliders.

They sleep in root chambers in dense, interlocked masses, sharing heat until the colony resembles a single warm-blooded organ. Each individual has a broad membrane stretched along the flanks, but steering is accomplished as much by temperature as by shape. Bands of tissue heat and cool in sequence, disturbing the thin air just enough to turn a fall into a controlled traverse. They emerge in darkness, drifting between ravines to feed on leaf films, mites, and mineral-rich secretions from the trees.

Their fur is pale where it faces the sky and black against the body. Fungal spores live within it. When a glider dies, its colony presses the corpse into a root wall, where fungus digests it and the tree incorporates its minerals. Old chambers contain no bones, only denser rings in the living wood.

The rivers beneath the ice hold blind fish, though “fish” describes only their shape. They are ribbon-bodied, nearly transparent, and lined with organs that sense pressure and dissolved charge. Some carry warm bacterial cultures in sacs beneath their throats. By releasing measured amounts of heat, they open temporary breathing holes beneath the ice or soften frozen sediment to expose food.

They seldom enter open pools. Those waters belong to slower things: mats that fold, unfold, and migrate along thermal gradients; shell-bearing siphoners fixed to submerged rock; and pale radial animals that surround vents like flowers. Several may be parts of one colonial life cycle. A drifting larva in one season becomes a rooted filter in the next, then fragments into blind swimmers when the pool’s chemistry changes.

The filament fields are inhabited at every scale, but it is unclear where the inhabitant ends and the field begins. Metal-eating bacteria line the hollow mineral stalks. Fungal threads bind their bases underground. Minute armored organisms climb the tubes and scrape away excess growth, preventing the colonies from sealing themselves shut. When wind charge accumulates, these organisms arrange their bodies at junctions and function as living switches.

Sometimes an entire hillside changes direction at once.

Current runs downslope, the stalks murmur, and buried nodules begin to warm. The organisms involved do not appear to command this event. They participate in it as blood cells participate in a heartbeat.

The predators are less easily named.

Beneath scree live broad, flattened hunters with no fixed outline. Their bodies consist of muscular lobes separated by insulating tissue, allowing them to spread through the spaces between stones. They sense footsteps as patterns of pressure and electrical leakage. Rather than pursuing prey, they alter the ground ahead of it—warming one route, charging another, or releasing attractive mineral odors from buried vents. A herd may bend its migration kilometers before reaching the hunter.

When one feeds, stones settle. Lichen dims. For days afterward, the ground retains a faint warmth.

Certain root systems are predatory in another manner. Most of the time they pump water and brace the cliff forests. Yet when sustained body heat enters an empty chamber, fine roots surround it without apparent haste. They do not crush. They seal exits, alter the air, and absorb what remains. Whether this is feeding, defense, or an automatic repair response cannot be distinguished.

The old pools may also hunt. Approached at the wrong electrical phase, they produce upward-flowing surface currents. Droplets rise and collect on the ceiling stone; breath and body heat follow. Small creatures become sluggish, then move toward the water’s edge. Nothing emerges to seize them. The pool simply changes them into chemistry.

There are scavengers adapted to these disappearances: jointed white ribbons that arrive through cracks, ceramic-shelled colonies that spread over remains, and mobile fungal knots that drag scraps into warmer ground. They leave little behind. Even bone is too useful to persist.

Yet the most consequential life may be entirely buried.

Far below the forests, roots, fungi, bacterial films, and brine-filled chambers form a network extending across watersheds. It has no single brain and perhaps no boundary. It stores information in mineral layers, adjusts flow by growing or dissolving membranes, and signals through heat, pressure, chemical pulses, and electrical potential. Some responses occur in seconds. Others unfold over generations.

The metallic knock from the depths may be one of its rhythms: a valve closing, a crystal plate flexing, a pressure chamber discharging. Or it may be communication between regions so distant that a single exchange takes years.

The black trees are its vents and pumps. The cushions are its warm reservoirs. The filament fields are its collectors. Herds are its dispersal bodies. Predators prevent electrical congestion. Rivers carry its soluble materials inward, while fumaroles extrude what it cannot presently use. Ceramic rings and crystal lattices may be waste, reproductive structures, temporary circuitry, or experiments repeated over geological time.

There are also brief beings that exist only during mist.

They assemble from organisms normally dispersed through soil, fur, and vegetation. As moisture advances, filaments extend from separate colonies and join across the wet stone. Translucent membranes inflate between them. For less than an hour, low branching forms rise from the ground and turn toward the pressure front. They have no durable tissues and leave no corpse. When the mist fails, they collapse into droplets, spores, and threads.

During their short existence, nearby systems synchronize. Leaves open in sequence. Herds alter course. Pool surfaces tilt. Electrical ignition moves from ridge to ridge. These mist-bodies may be sensory organs temporarily grown by the plateau—or merely opportunistic colonies exploiting the same conditions.

Nothing lives alone there. Every body is crowded with other bodies; every species is also infrastructure. Lichen insulates the walkers. Walkers transport the forest. Mites close circuits. Fish carry heat. Predators edit conductivity. Roots make weather inside the mountain, while microbes turn stone into organs.

So the country’s inhabitant may be the whole arrangement: not a hidden giant beneath the plateau, but the plateau’s capacity to gather itself repeatedly into walkers, forests, currents, hunters, blossoms, and dark pools.

It has no face because no one place needs to face the world.

When the nightly silence comes and every pulse stops together, the small lives wait within the larger one. Then mist crosses a saddle, a million valves open, and the mountain resumes being alive in all its separate forms.
