What lives here is difficult to separate from what merely persists.

The small forms beside the pools are the clearest candidates. They resemble low crustaceans assembled from milk-white glass: broad, segmented shells drawn close to the mineral crust, with six or eight jointed limbs tucked beneath. The number seems to vary according to the angle from which they are viewed. Their extremities taper into black conductive points embedded in the wet margin, some in water, some in stone. A fine oxide film passes over their translucent backs without adhering.

They have no visible mouths, eyes, or organs. Under certain light, however, dim structures can be seen suspended inside the shell—threads, chambers, tiny dark grains moving in closed circuits. None of these internal motions produces growth or waste. They appear less like anatomy than a miniature version of the mechanism beneath the basin.

Each is oriented along the same invisible axis, regardless of the shape of the pool. With every subterranean concussion, they flex by a fraction: plates compress, joints lock, and the black points sink imperceptibly deeper. Between impacts they release the strain. This may be breathing. It may be feeding. Pressure enters them through the ground and leaves as a minute electrical discharge into the metallic water. Sometimes a blue-white spark passes beneath the oxide skin, linking one animal to its reflection.

No individual has ever been seen to change position. Yet they cannot confidently be called immobile. Their posture contains a whole gait held within a single instant, as if each were continuously completing one step without advancing. At some pools they face the trench; at others they face away, but taken across the basin their arrangement forms long, precise curves. They may be sensors in a buried network, valves through which the land exchanges charge, or creatures that evolved until evolution itself became unnecessary.

There are no juveniles and no remains. Cracked shells are absent. So are cast-off plates, eggs, burrows, and trails. Every specimen appears equally ancient and equally unfinished. If one is touched, the shell is warm and faintly elastic. It yields to pressure, then returns the finger—not merely to its prior place, but to the exact configuration the contact interrupted. The sensation is of touching an event that refuses to become past.

The upright figures on the horizon may be another stage of the same organism.

Seen through the basin’s permanent shimmer, one stands as tall as a narrow tower. Four long limbs meet the ground widely; two more may fold against a central body, or may be shadows cast by the first four. Its upper portion is small and dark, without a recognizable head. At intervals corresponding to the deep impacts below, its silhouette appears to divide into several adjacent positions, then close again. It neither walks nor sways, but seems to contain walking: a sequence of strides compressed into one stable outline.

It may extend far underground. The visible legs could be cooling fins, antennae, or mineral extrusions from something immense buried beneath the flats. The buried rotors alter their pitch slightly around it, though whether it causes the change or merely marks where the machinery approaches the surface is impossible to know.

More ambiguous inhabitants reveal themselves only through sound. Inside the fused black ridges, the reciprocating vibration occasionally loses its mechanical regularity. A cluster of impacts passes from one outcrop to another, too rapid for echoes and too patterned for fracture. The sequence resembles signaling, though no answer ever follows; or perhaps the answer is the identical sequence continuing elsewhere. Pressing an ear to the warm stone exposes a dense interior of clicks, scrapes, circulating fluid, and slow stresses that nearly resemble enormous digestion.

Nothing emerges from the fissures except warm air.

The basin itself may be the principal organism. Its channels function as vessels while carrying no net quantity of water. The metallic pools hold differences of charge like organs maintaining potential. The slag ridges exchange heat and vibration across distances. The trench is a long resonant throat in which the fixed river gives voice to motion without consequence. Beneath all of it, rotations and strokes continue with the steadiness of metabolism freed from hunger.

If so, the pale pool-creatures are not residents but exposed tissues. The distant walkers are articulated structures raised above the crust. Even the oxide films may be membranes renewing themselves without addition or loss. The distinction between animal, machine, and geology fails because all three are local shapes taken by the same operation.

This life does not consume the basin. It does not reproduce across it, compete within it, or adapt to it. Those relationships would require one state to replace another. Instead it participates in maintenance. Every motion closes upon itself. Every intake is also an output. Every creature is exactly as old as the conditions allowing it to exist.

Nothing here waits, though everything appears to be waiting.

The pale bodies remain at the waterline. The tall forms hold their endless interrupted stride. The buried mass turns, strikes, compresses, and releases. Together they inhabit no succession of moments, only the depth of one moment repeated without error: a complete ecology whose sole surviving act is to remain complete.
