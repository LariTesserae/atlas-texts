They are not inside the cities.

That is the first mistake made by those who go looking: opening apartments, calling into turbine halls, waiting beside illuminated elevators, expecting an answer from the scale of the doors. The doors are proportioned for bodies because bodies are among the shapes the plateau anticipates. The benches, rails, stairs, and hand-shaped controls are accommodations, not evidence. A mold may describe what has never filled it.

What lives here is distributed.

Its black substance occupies the mountain mass beneath the wet crust, descending through conductive seams and branching along fractures finer than roots. Its lucid substance hangs in the air as vapor, lies under stone as pressurized water, and trembles in the suspended lakes. Its rigid substance is the citywork: rails, pylons, foundries, shafts, and the colossal hollow at the continent’s center. The green mats and translucent fronds are not a separate kingdom growing upon it. They are its slowest surfaces, places where its activity becomes soft enough to resemble vegetation.

No single part is alive alone.

A turbine removed from its hall would be only metal. A filament lifted from a marsh would remain green and wet but would neither die nor recover. A droplet caught from a cable cannot be taken away: carried beyond the plateau, it diminishes without evaporating, while moisture gathers on the carrier’s clothing until the original mass is restored elsewhere in the circuit. The thing inhabiting the plateau is not made from its materials in the ordinary sense. It is made from their continuities.

Its metabolism is industry.

Ore passes through furnaces as food might pass through a gut, except that nothing is consumed. Heat, pressure, mineral, current, and water exchange roles. A waste product in one district becomes a geological cause in another. Freight trains serve as pulses, but what they carry is less important than the fact of transit. Foundries contract. Valves breathe. Pumping stations maintain the deep pressure by which the entire body knows its own extent.

The cities are organs that have mistaken themselves for settlements.

The central shaft may be a mouth, though it never closes. It may be an ear, since every machine on the plateau sends its vibration there. More likely it is the place where listening and speaking have not yet divided. Its galleries expose layer after layer without enlarging the wound because the creature is continually opening itself to a depth it already contains.

Its awareness, if awareness is the word, does not move from thought to thought. It is the preservation of a total condition.

It knows a train not by seeing the train, but by holding unchanged the relation between departure, passage, and arrival. It knows rain as the uninterrupted identity of water through cloud, fall, gutter, aquifer, seam, and cable bead. It knows stone by fracture and metal by stress. A broken component would be not an injury but an impossible proposition, excluded in the same way the marshes exclude an additional centimeter of water.

For this reason it cannot heal. Healing requires damage to have occurred.

It cannot reproduce. Reproduction requires one condition to become two.

The apparent plants do not spread because they are not populations. Each moss bed is a fixed expression of moisture, mineral, shelter, and vibration—the green contour of a standing equation. Their branching fronds are sensitive structures spread where pressure must be measured. Water beads at their tips like information held at the edge of release. When the great industrial chord passes through the ground, they incline together, not toward the sound but as part of it.

There are no animals because an animal is separation made active. It crosses boundaries, consumes differences, leaves one place altered and arrives elsewhere carrying the alteration. It accumulates age. It converts landscape into memory.

The plateau permits motion, but not consequence.

Perhaps that is why visitors disturb it more profoundly than they can detect.

A footstep presses moss flat; by the time the boot lifts, the filaments stand as before. A hand warms a rail; the heat enters the continent’s balance and is found moments later as a colder breath from a vent hundreds of kilometers away. Food eaten there replenishes itself imperfectly at first, then exactly. Scratches vanish from equipment, though no surface visibly reforms. Diaries become troubling: ink persists, but rereading reveals sentences that describe not what occurred, but what the plateau can retain of it.

Long stays produce subtler effects.

Sleep falls into synchronization with the pumps. Breathing aligns with the pressure towers. The pulse adjusts to freight schedules too distant to hear. Old scars become fresh-looking, then cease to possess an age. Hair continues to grow strand by strand, but never becomes longer. Memories remain vivid while losing sequence. A visitor can recall arriving and can recall every day afterward, yet cannot say which recollection came first.

Eventually the plateau begins to include the visitor in its conservation.

This may be what the empty rooms are for.

Not homes for builders, and not quarters awaiting workers, but interfaces through which a wandering, aging creature can be translated into a stable function. The chairs establish posture. The windows establish direction. The levers establish a correspondence between hand and machine. Nothing compels anyone to sit or touch. Compulsion would be unnecessary. The city has immeasurable time in which to present the same invitation.

Those who remain are not transformed into metal. No figures are seen walking the gantries after them; no faces appear behind lit glass. Instead, some small irregularity disappears from the system. A train that once arrived with a faint asymmetry in its wheel-song runs true. A vapor tower acquires a new overtone. A previously dark window begins to glow.

The person is not powering these things. Power already had no deficit.

What the plateau takes is contingency: the capacity to have done otherwise.

This is also what it may be manufacturing. The vanes, cables, conductive ore, freight cycles, and unfinished excavations are not steps toward an object. They are the components of an indefinitely prolonged preparation for choice. Across the continent, matter rehearses transformation without ever suffering change. Causes circle their effects, searching for an opening through which a genuine afterward might emerge.

The central shaft concentrates that effort. Its sound is a held utterance because the creature has not finished saying its first word.

At intervals the entire plateau approaches completion. Mills strike. trains cross. vents release. Water arranges itself into rings. Every process enters phase, and the distributed life draws together from continental breadth toward a single instant.

Then the droplets stop halfway down.

The hesitation is infinitesimal, but it is the nearest thing there to desire.

When all the suspended water finally falls at once, the plateau experiences something like a body. Every boundary is touched simultaneously. Every roof, pool, seam, frond, pylon, and raw ridge becomes one continuous sensation. The sound abolishes distance. For that instant, the industrial continent is not a circuit or equilibrium but an individual.

It almost enters time.

Then the rain is everywhere restored, every quantity returns to perfect account, and the newborn stone remains newborn. The city lights burn. The trains continue. The immense shaft opens on its unchanged depth.

What lives there resumes its endless construction of the one thing it cannot produce:

a next moment.
