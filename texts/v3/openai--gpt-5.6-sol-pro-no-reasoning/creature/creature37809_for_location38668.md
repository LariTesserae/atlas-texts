What lives there is not a population so much as a set of tenancies.

First are the fixed inhabitants: the pumps, gates, governors, lifts, and switchboards. They occupy the drowned city with the assurance of organisms too old to distinguish function from instinct. Each has its interval and reflex. A piston wakes when pressure accumulates behind it. A relay closes because another relay, rooms away, has opened. Turbines feed on the harbor’s passing weight. The western gates keep their ninth-cycle observance. Together they form a dispersed creature whose organs are buildings and whose nervous system is copper, current, and vibration.

It has no single body. It has habits instead.

Its pulse is the hammering of pumps. Its breath is the surge and reverse flow. Heat gathers in its lower cavities like fever. Signals travel through walls faster than any swimmer could follow: three blows through brick, a tremor along a handrail, a bell sounding inside water. Damage does not kill it. Damage revises it. Broken machinery is incorporated into the next pattern, and collapsed shafts continue to receive elevators as if material obstruction were merely an administrative error.

Then there are the films.

They live nearest to warmth, current, and corrosion: gray skins around exhaust seams, black crescents beneath gasket lips, clear colonies suspended on mineral branches. They are the city’s true citizens, though no eye can easily separate one from its substrate. They eat iron, sulfur, stray electricity, rubber softened by decades, and whatever microscopic nourishment the harbor forces through the intakes.

A surge strips whole provinces of them away. Those that remain divide in scratches, bolt holes, and the sheltered backs of ceramic insulators. Those swept loose settle elsewhere. Thus their neighborhoods migrate while the architecture stays fixed.

The white lace on the control panels is partly theirs and partly not. Living colonies encourage crystals; crystals conduct leakage; leakage warms the water; warmth feeds the colonies. Their structures grow in repetitions—fans, forks, little radial cities laid over the dead diagrams of the larger one. When dissolved, they leave faint templates from which the next growth begins.

The shrimp live briefly by comparison. Blind and nearly transparent, they enter through pipes no wider than a wrist. They gather inside pressure gauges, relay cabinets, and the hollow floats of level indicators. During quiet intervals they drift out to graze the microbial varnish. At the first tremor of a coming surge, they fold themselves into crevices and become indistinguishable from scraps of pale wire.

Most are eventually taken by the machinery. Their shells collect in sump corners or pass through turbines as white dust. Yet more arrive. The city does not sustain a shrimp population so much as repeatedly borrow one from the harbor.

The silver fish are less suited to it. They enter as crowds and leave as fragments. Light confuses them: amber globes resemble openings, blue arcs resemble prey, and the white traffic arrow in the collapsed exchange draws them again and again toward brick. When the pressure gates move, a school becomes a single flashing shape stretched along the current. Survivors emerge days later from unrelated ducts, their scales dulled by metal.

The eel is different.

It is pale enough to seem unfinished, with small eyes under a translucent veil of skin. Its head is blunt and scarred; its mouth bears dark abrasions from feeding inside pipes. It favors the Boiler Ward, where the water passes through alternating bands of warmth and cold. There it lies in a split conduit above the old furnace floor, curving farther into the wall than the conduit’s dimensions should allow.

Those who have seen it disagree about nearly everything. Sometimes it is no longer than a forearm. Sometimes its body crosses an entire valve court and continues through two separate grilles. One account gives it a ring of scars behind the head. Another places the same scars near the tail. A third insists that the marks move while watched, opening and closing like gills.

It may be many eels inhabiting the same route. It may be one animal repeatedly injured and healed by the city. Or the sightings may concern only portions of something threaded through the pipe system, too long to enter any room completely.

It feeds when the reverse current begins. Shrimp vanish from housings. Fish scatter before it appears. In the sediment afterward are narrow trails, soot-dark mucus, and occasional metal filings arranged in crescents as if sorted by a tongue.

Something else uses the elevators.

It is never seen in a car or shaft. Its presence is inferred from changes after the ninth cycle. A descending elevator may leave empty and return with its inner gate buckled outward. Mud appears on the floor, though the shaft does not reach sediment. Handprints have been reported on the ceiling: not human prints, exactly, but five blunt radiating marks, repeated in pairs and pressed into rust.

Once, an elevator returned carrying a row of warm iron rivets. They had been recently manufactured. Their heads were clean, their shafts oily, and each bore an inspection stamp belonging to no foundry in the harbor records. Another time, the car came back full of cold water in a stairwell otherwise almost warm enough to touch like skin.

Whatever occupies the lower floors understands loads, doors, and schedules. It may not understand that the upper city has been drowned.

The bells appear to be for it.

They ring in flooded rooms where ordinary bells could produce only a dull concussion, yet their tones arrive clear and separate: one note for the closing gates, two for descent, then a lower sound after the indicators pass –19. At that last note, activity changes throughout the chamber. The pale eel abandons the Boiler Ward. Shrimp seal themselves deep inside instruments. The microbial lace dims as though electrical current has been diverted elsewhere.

Even the machinery hesitates—not stopping, but shifting its labor into a slower and heavier cadence.

Then a pressure moves upward from beneath the known foundations. It carries warmth, soot, stone odor, and a minute quantity of air. The bubbles are black until they reach a lamp. Some contain a pinprick of orange light. They rise to the trapped pockets under the platforms and join the hot metallic atmosphere there.

Those air pockets have their own inhabitants.

They are small, soft things that cling upside down to iron ceilings just above the waterline. They resemble colorless moths at first: folded membranes, narrow bodies, many jointed feet. They cannot survive immersion for long, yet no dry route connects one pocket to another. After a surge they disappear. Days later they are found beneath a different platform, arranged around rust nodules and drinking condensed water through hair-fine mouths.

Their membranes vibrate when the submerged bells ring.

Sometimes they vibrate beforehand.

They may feel changes in pressure long before the gates respond. They may communicate across the iron by drumming their feet. If disturbed, an entire cluster releases at once and drops onto the water, where the creatures run briefly across the surface film before sinking. In the warm dark below, they close their membranes around themselves. No one has observed what follows.

And throughout all of this lives the current—not biologically, perhaps, but locally, specifically, with a temperament shaped by the city’s ducts. It remembers routes. It hunts weakness. It circles certain courts and refuses others. It lifts doors that weigh tons while leaving flakes of rust balanced on ledges. It carries heat from the impossible lower floors, scatters mineral gardens, ushers animals in, and removes them.

The current is the nearest thing the city has to a crowd.

It fills every avenue at once. It presses through ticket windows, climbs staircases, waits behind gates, rushes along galleries, and gathers in empty halls. Through it, every inhabitant touches every other: eel, shrimp, bacterial film, blind mechanism, and whatever rides the elevators below the foundation.

None of them owns the drowned city.

They are impulses inside it, temporary concentrations in a much older circulation. The only permanent resident may be the system itself: the whole iron chamber continuing its obscure municipal life, accepting pressure as command and motion as obedience.

Above, people call the harbor restless.

Below, the city calls that restlessness food.
