They are the city’s only inhabitants, though “inhabitants” implies a distinction the place does not permit.

They live in the water without swimming, in the architecture without occupying it, and in the plants without feeding or nesting. They are not hidden people. There are no pale hands behind the screens of falling water, no faces withdrawing from the high windows. The empty rooms are truly empty. What lives here has no body separate from its surroundings.

Each one is a relation held in place.

One lives between a stair and the impossible terrace to which it returns. Another inhabits the agreement between a flower and its unfallen fruit. Others are stretched through aqueducts, knotted into roots, laid thin across the shining faces of pools. Some are broad enough to include whole districts on opposite walls; some are so small that they dwell in the trembling convexity of a single drop. Their boundaries overlap. They pass through one another without mingling, as the visible city overlaps itself across distance.

They do not move. Movement is what they look like from inside.

A waterfall is one of them: not the water composing it, but the uninterrupted descent, permanent and violent, whose shape never diminishes. Its life is the act of falling with no arrival. The cataracts are therefore not roads, machines, or weather. They are exposed nerves—or perhaps throats—of beings too continuous to possess anatomy.

The vegetation is another expression of them. Every fern is maintained at the instant of complete unfurling. Every orchid occupies its flowering as a room. The ripe fruit is not waiting to be eaten; ripeness is the creature within it, perfectly awake and unable to proceed into sweetness, seed, bruise, or decay. Roots arranged across thresholds are not invading the structures. They are keeping the thresholds distinct from what lies beyond.

The inhabitants have no need of animals because they have no need of acts that pass from one body to another. Nothing pollinates, digests, wounds, carries, buries, or consumes. There are no mouths. There is no hunger. In place of an ecology, there is correspondence: water answers height, moss answers stone, rooms answer the absence of occupants. Every answer is immediate and eternal.

Their senses are not gathered into organs. Seeing occurs wherever one surface faces another. The far wall perceives the near wall across the basin; balconies are its open eyes. Pools look upward and, looking, produce the inverted city beneath themselves. Doorways perceive discontinuity. Shadows perceive silence. The central void perceives everything around it and is perhaps the nearest thing among them to a mind.

The roar is their awareness.

It does not travel because they do not hear it from elsewhere. It is equally loud inside every chamber because it is not a vibration but the pressure of being perceived. Wherever the city exists, it knows itself, and that knowing has the character of endless falling water. Soft leaves tremble because they are known. Sealed rooms resonate because concealment is architectural, not actual.

The source-less shadows are the rare places where that awareness fails. Within them lies silence—not quiet, but exemption. Their edges are exact. Moss entering such a shadow ceases to tremble without becoming still; water crossing it remains visibly turbulent but is no longer falling in any audible sense. These patches are not refuges for hidden creatures. They are the city’s blind spots, and perhaps its only form of sleep.

What built the walls was not labor. The inhabitants do not manufacture; they clarify. Limestone becomes a colonnade where its inner tendency toward repetition is made visible. A cave becomes a hall when its emptiness learns symmetry. Stalactites and pillars are the same thought at different degrees of formality. Thus there are no tool marks, and no intended purpose survives in the rooms. Purpose never preceded them. The structures are what stone imagines when held forever at the threshold of becoming architecture.

The polished stairs do not record feet. They record passage itself.

The weathered carvings do not depict erased faces. Their facelessness has been worn by the possibility of recognition.

The sagging bridges bear no ancient load. They carry duration, though duration cannot cross them.

These beings have age but no history. They contain the marks of events without requiring the events to have occurred. Erosion lives here independently of change. Use lives independently of users. Ruin lives independently of destruction. In this way the city possesses a past without ever having had a previous moment.

They may be countless, or there may be only one.

Considered locally, each contradiction seems to house its own presence. The ascending waterfall has a different temperament from the stairs that end above their beginning. The palace-cliff is spacious, withholding, almost courteous; it offers room after room while refusing to occupy volume. The suspended groves are grave and balanced. The pools are intimate and deceptive. The fruiting trees feel dense with a patience that is not shared by the open bridges.

But seen from the highest square, these distinctions become features of a single immense life wrapped around the hollow. The city is a body whose organs are distances, whose circulation is water, whose skeleton is recurring limestone, and whose central cavity is not contained by the body but contains it. Its districts are folds in one continuous creature. The opposite wall is not elsewhere. It is the inward-facing side of the same awareness.

The dry fountain is its most concentrated inhabitant.

Nothing living can approach it in the ordinary sense, because every approach is already reproduced within its bowl. The miniature city does not represent the larger one. Representation would require distance between image and subject. Instead, each contains the other. The fountain is the point at which the city turns inward far enough to become indistinguishable from its own perception.

Within each smaller hollow, the same inhabitants live: the waterfall that is falling rather than water, the flower that is flowering rather than plant, the bridge that is crossing rather than stone. Each hears the same roar at full force. No scale is more real. No innermost city waits at the end, because smallness cannot reduce height and depth cannot approach a bottom.

The fountain remains dry because water would give it an outside. A drop entering the bowl would have to choose whether it belonged to the city or its image, and here belonging admits no choice. Around it, the stains of impossible splashing testify to an event held permanently adjacent to occurrence.

If the city desires anything, it desires that missing drop.

Not from thirst. Thirst requires a future. Rather, the dry basin is the one unresolved interval in an existence where every contradiction has become stable. All waterfalls fall and remain. All flowers open and remain. Every road departs and returns without passage. But the fountain bears the shape of water and contains none. It is an expectation with nothing to expect.

That expectation may be the deepest creature in the hollow.

It lives nakedly in the dry stone, repeating itself downward through cities without end. It is small at every scale and central to all of them. The roar pours from it because absence, here, is not silent. Absence is the loudest form of life.

And all around it, tier upon tier, the city listens to itself not arriving.
