Those who live in the summitless mountain do not form a population in the ordinary sense. They are remnants, custodians, opportunists, and perhaps symptoms of the city itself. No census has ever held true for long. Rooms known to be occupied stand empty when opened; figures seen crossing distant bridges cannot always be found at the other end. Still, several kinds of inhabitant are recognized.

The people call themselves **the Circuit**, though the term refers less to kinship than to the routes by which they survive. Each person belongs to a repeated passage through the city: cistern to control room, control room to cooling chamber, cooling chamber to the ceramic gardens where drinking salts are harvested, then back before the evening flood. To know someone is to know where their route intersects yours.

They are lean without appearing starved, their bodies shaped by heat and constant mineral exposure. Their skin is usually covered, but what shows has a faint sealed look, as though polished by steam. Hair is kept short or wrapped in absorbent wirecloth. The oldest have branching stains around their fingernails and eyelids—copper green, iron brown, occasionally the lightless violet of the weeping cliffs. These are not tattoos. They form gradually from years of handling valves, drinking condensed water, and sleeping near masonry that sweats in the dark.

Outside cooled rooms they wear broad reflective capes over close-fitting layers of porous ceramic fiber. Their masks are personal objects, repaired throughout a lifetime. A mask catches moisture from breath, filters airborne salts, and cools the blood at the cheeks and temples. Its interior gradually takes on the mineral signature of its wearer. After death, the mask is dismantled. Its metal enters the city’s repair stock; its condensation plates are hung among the dwelling chimes, where warm drafts cause them to sound faintly.

The Circuit do not claim to operate the city. They say the city is already operating and that interference must be made carefully.

Their most respected workers are listeners. These people sleep beside pipes, press their teeth to rail housings, and lower ceramic rods into flooded shafts to feel the tremors carried through them. They can distinguish a freight lift descending six wards away from the deeper motion that travels beneath the basin before storms. They keep records not in writing but in racks of tuned tiles. A tile is fired to reproduce the pitch of a particular pump, transformer, wall cavity, or subterranean engine. When a sound changes, the corresponding tile is shaved, weighted, or replaced.

Thus maintenance becomes a kind of music no one performs in full.

There are also **walkers**, specialists in crossing streets that lengthen in rain. They carry spools of braided mineral cord and drive temporary pins into the pavement as they travel. Counting steps is considered unreliable. So are clocks. A walker judges distance by tension, temperature, and the delay with which taps return through the cord. If a route begins to extend too quickly, the cord is cut. No one knows what happens to the severed length. Sometimes it is found months later protruding from a drain in another district, warm and pulled taut.

Children are few. They are raised in groups inside the coldest towers, where walls are lined with hollow ceramic blocks through which cistern water circulates. Before they can read, they learn the prohibitions of the basin:

Do not carry a bright flame beneath wet brick.

Do not answer an echo that uses your voice incorrectly.

Do not step into a dry channel during the hour before rain.

Do not enter a train merely because its doors have opened.

If a room has become cooler than the corridor, leave it open.

If the chimes sound while hanging still, wake everyone.

Most children eventually choose a route and become part of the Circuit. A few disappear into the machinery. This is not always spoken of as death. Years later, a missing person’s habitual knock may issue from inside a sealed conduit, or a lift may begin stopping at the floor where they once lived. Families place a new chime beside the door and listen for repetition.

The people share the basin with less comprehensible life.

In the warm galleries dwell the **ribbons**, nearly transparent animals that fold themselves through water without swimming. They have no apparent mouths or eyes. Fine metallic threads run the length of their bodies, branching into fans when electrical current is present. Around leaking junctions they gather in silent knots, each passing through the others like strips of wet glass.

They are harmless unless removed from water. In air, a ribbon contracts violently and releases its stored charge. For this reason the Circuit sometimes uses them to test submerged cables, but never keeps them. A ribbon placed in a closed vessel will always be gone by morning, even if the seal remains intact. Afterward the vessel’s inner surface bears a delicate conductive pattern resembling a drainage map.

The shelled creatures of the upper galleries are called **insulator saints**. Each is no larger than a thumbnail and appears at first to be a blister in glazed ceramic. Their shells accumulate in concentric layers of white, yellow, and smoke-gray salt. They move only when voltage changes, extending clusters of filament-thin legs and turning toward the stronger field.

A colony may spend decades crossing a single switchboard.

Because they consume deposits that would otherwise bridge electrical contacts, they are tolerated and occasionally transplanted. Old electricians carry them in little perforated boxes lined with charged foil. There is disagreement over whether the animals truly feed on static or merely use it to locate some subtler nourishment. When they die, nothing remains inside the shell—not tissue, not fluid, not even ash. Only a warm hollow that continues holding charge for several days.

Deeper in the drowned wards are the **postures**.

This is not an official name. Divers coined it after seeing pale configurations standing between submerged lamp posts: jointed forms too tall to be human, motionless in water hot enough to blister skin. Their limbs, if limbs they are, seem arranged according to the old architecture. One bends where a staircase used to turn. Another spans the gap between a balcony and a doorway. A third stands inverted beneath the ceiling of a flooded station.

They do not pursue divers. They are simply found closer on the return journey.

Images of them are unreliable. Cameras show only mineral veils, bent rails, or empty water. Sonar returns the outlines of rooms that no longer exist. Once, a diver surfaced carrying a white branching object believed to be part of one. Exposed to air, it softened and unfolded into a map of the lower ward, including passages unknown to the Circuit. Before the map could be copied, it heated to incandescence and fused with the table.

No dives have been authorized since, though authorization has little meaning in a city with so few authorities.

There are rumored inhabitants beyond these known forms. Workers crossing the industrial barrens report distant figures walking around the cooling towers, always keeping the tower between themselves and the observer. These figures seem broad and dark, perhaps because they wear old furnace gear. Their footprints, when found, consist of paired dry patches on wet concrete. They leave no route leading toward or away from the tower.

Others speak of residents behind sealed doors in the oldest administrative blocks. Food left outside is untouched, but the bowls return clean and hot. Ceramic chimes there ring in patterns too complicated for drafts. Sometimes a voice recites obsolete platform numbers through the wall. The Circuit neither opens these rooms nor builds over them.

The city itself produces things that behave almost like inhabitants.

Soot shelves beneath vents detach and migrate several centimeters each night, always toward active machinery. Mineral fronds on walls alter their branching when watched over long periods. Empty rail cars select routes no scheduler recognizes. Certain elevators refuse passengers but descend willingly when offered a sealed vessel or a block of translucent stone.

Among the listeners, there is a belief that these are not separate phenomena. The ribbons, the saints, the moving deposits, the postures, and perhaps even the Circuit are all local expressions of one buried process: the city distributing attention through whatever matter can endure its heat.

They do not call this process alive. The word is considered too small.

At the Receiving Field, inhabitants are least easily distinguished from machinery.

When storms gather, the Circuit retreats to designated galleries overlooking the glass. They extinguish all unnecessary lamps and wait behind mineral-darkened windows. Yet figures can sometimes be seen already standing beside the converging trains—long silhouettes in the rain, apparently unprotected from the heat. They never arrive by bridge or roadway. They are present only after the pumps have stilled the water.

As the reflected towers emerge, the figures turn toward them.

Some witnesses insist that they are people reflected upward from the plan beneath the glass. Others say they are passengers who came on an earlier occasion and have spent the intervening years waiting where ordinary time cannot quite reach them. The oldest listeners believe they are operators from another alignment of the city, offset by those few degrees visible under storm water.

When the darkness passes beneath the field, every figure raises one hand.

The trains open.

On rare occasions, after the water drains, one more person is found in the basin.

They are usually standing in a station, wearing clothing cut in an unfamiliar style but made from the same reflective fibers the Circuit uses. Their masks fit no known standard. They speak little at first. They recognize the layout of the city, but turn the wrong way at intersections, expecting doors where walls now stand. They ask after towers that were never completed and districts thirty meters underwater. They are unsurprised by the heat, the upward-creeping rain, or the sounds below the rock.

What unsettles them is the emptiness.

Each new arrival expects the basin to be crowded. They describe platforms full of workers, illuminated apartments, market bridges beneath a higher sky. They say the warehouses were built to hold something abundant. They say the trains once departed loaded.

But when asked what the city received, or what lives beneath the glass, their accounts fragment. Some remember a substance. Some remember a migration. Some remember the mountain having a summit.

One or two remember that the summit opened.

In time, these arrivals adopt routes of their own. They wear the city’s dim colors, add their masks to its racks, and learn to hear the evening water entering the streets. Their memories precipitate into stories, then prohibitions, then chimes. They become indistinguishable from those who claim their ancestors were always there.

So the basin remains inhabited, though never filled: by people who live as circuits through vacant architecture; by delicate organisms feeding on leakage and salt; by drowned arrangements wearing the shape of lost rooms; by movements in mineral, steam, and infrastructure; and by the uncertain citizens of a second city visible only when rain makes a mirror of the ground.

All of them wait, maintain, circulate, and listen.

Whether they are waiting for something to arrive, or keeping it from arriving again, no inhabitant can say.
