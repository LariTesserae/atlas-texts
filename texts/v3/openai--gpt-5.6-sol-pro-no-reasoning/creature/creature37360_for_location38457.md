The city is inhabited less by separate species than by a layered, collective appetite.

Its first citizens are the builders: soft, repetitive animals that live fixed to what their ancestors left behind. Each occupies a minute chamber in the limestone. By extending crowns, filaments, fans, or translucent tentacles into the flow, they take drifting particles from the water; by withdrawing, they disappear into the city’s substance. Individually they are vulnerable, almost negligible. In colonies they become towers, ramparts, bridges, and streets. Their bodies are temporary, but their secretions endure. Generation after generation inhabits its own accumulating remains.

Among them spread sponges, whose porous flesh fills caves and abandoned galleries. They draw the sea through themselves continually, filtering it, altering its chemistry, and exhaling it from raised vents. Some are pale and branching, some soot-dark and low, some little more than living films beneath overhangs. They serve as reservoirs, lungs, and hidden foundations. Worms lace through them. Shrimp shelter inside them. Small fish enter their chambers and emerge carrying their scent.

Feather-crowned worms line the avenues wherever current is reliable. In calm water their plumes remain open, turning whole façades into gardens of red, cream, and glass. At the approach of danger they vanish almost simultaneously, each crown snapped into its tube. The bare wall left behind looks dead until caution passes and color cautiously returns.

Bivalves occupy the gates. They grow shoulder to shoulder in the narrowings, opening toward the accelerated flow. Their shells are ribbed, layered, and thickened by mineral-rich water. Some close at shadows; others remain open even while crabs walk over their lips. Beneath them lie generations of discarded valves, cemented into banks that divert the currents and make conditions favorable for still more shells.

The mobile inhabitants move through this fixed multitude as traffic, scavengers, tenants, and weather.

Crabs are everywhere. Minute ones comb bacterial films from the stone. Long-legged ones step delicately between feeding mouths. Heavy-clawed forms dismantle shells in sheltered courtyards, leaving pale heaps that are soon occupied by worms. Some decorate themselves with living fragments of the city until they resemble ambulatory pieces of wall. Others bury in the warm mud with only stalked eyes showing. Their labor never ceases: sorting, scraping, carrying, breaking, and being broken in turn.

Shrimp fill the undercity. Their jointed bodies overlap in crevices, nearly transparent except for their eyes and the dark threads of their digestive tracts. They click to warn, contend, or stun. In enclosed quarters the combined sound becomes a dry, persistent crackle conducted through the limestone. Cleaner species wait at habitual stations, picking parasites and dead tissue from the gills, jaws, and hides of larger animals that could swallow them but usually do not.

Brittle stars live with their central bodies hidden. Only their arms appear, branching from cracks and basketed through the current. At disturbance they withdraw in a flowing sequence, so that a moving absence travels along a wall. Urchins emerge when the upper darkness deepens. They rasp the limestone and its thin red growths, producing shell sand from architecture. Where many have fed, the city is rounded and pale. Their excavations weaken old districts, but their waste provides material for new ones.

Eels possess the interior. They reverse into holes until only their heads remain outside, jaws opening and closing to move water across the gills. Pale scavenging kinds thread through the deepest rubble; patterned hunters watch the busier lanes. Some cavities contain several bodies knotted together, impossible to distinguish until one suddenly uncoils. They know the city by touch and pressure, every concealed passage and narrowing where prey cannot turn.

The larger fish hold territories measured not in walls but in flows. Thick-bodied residents rest beside vents, fins braced in soft mud, mouths scarcely moving. Narrow schooling fish pass overhead in compressed, glittering crowds. Their hundreds of bodies behave like one evasive animal: appearing in a synchronized flash, folding around a tower, then becoming darkness again. Grazers travel in loose processions over the outer ridges. Hunters enter alone.

In the sulfurous old quarter live the patient giants. They are pallid, scarred, and slow enough to be mistaken for collapsed structures. Their eyes may be small or clouded; their lateral senses are acute. They detect the vibration of hidden breathing, the scrape of a claw, the irregular pulse of an injured fish. When one rises from the mud, the neighborhood empties before it. Worm-crowns vanish, shrimp spill backward into cracks, and fish flatten themselves into impossible shelters. Its attack is brief. Its stillness afterward may last for hours.

Rays pass above the city without seeming wholly subject to it. Broad and soft-edged, they cross the black water like moving portions of the ceiling. Some descend into plazas and stir the sediment with wingbeats, uncovering buried shellfish and worms. The resulting cloud draws scavengers from every direction. Others remain overhead, known only by a pressure-shift and the sudden obscuring of distant bioluminescence.

Around the warm chimneys assemble creatures that need neither the weak red films nor the debris descending from above. Blind grazers crowd over mineral crusts and harvest bacteria nourished by chemicals rising from the seabed. They are soft, pale, and densely packed. Some resemble snails stripped of color; others are jointed things with reduced eyes and feathery organs exposed to the water. Their eggs adhere to the warm stone. Predators graze the grazers, and scavengers gather where the predators feed.

The mud has its own population. Burrowers ventilate it with synchronized motions, pumping oxygenated water down one shaft and expelling dark, mineral-heavy water from another. Microbes live deeper than any animal can tolerate. They transform sulfur, methane, and decay; they release bubbles that quiver in the sediment before slipping upward. Vast portions of the city’s metabolism occur unseen, in black layers where flesh has no eyes and often no name-sized individuality.

Larvae are its most numerous wanderers. They drift as specks, threads, bells, and ciliated grains. For a brief period they belong nowhere. Tides lift them through avenues and carry them over the outer plain toward distant reefs. Most are eaten. Some respond to heat, salinity, mineral traces, or the chemical signatures of adults. Finding suitable stone, they settle, change form, and begin a fixed life that may outlast every mobile creature around them.

At intervals the water itself seems to become an inhabitant. Eggs coat protected walls in milky veils. Newly hatched animals fill the passages so densely that every movement touches another body. Filter-feeders gorge. Fish abandon territorial caution. Transparent hunters rise from the plain with expandable mouths. For a few nights, abundance overwhelms concealment; then the multitude thins, leaving nourished adults and another layer of remains.

Bioluminescent creatures inhabit distance. Some are nearly invisible until disturbed, when they shed green sparks into a wake. Some advertise themselves in measured blue pulses. Others carry a single luminous organ near the mouth, making hunger resemble a small, steady lamp. Colonies in enclosed chambers glow without interruption, using light to gather larvae from the current. Their rooms appear serene from outside. Within, every point of brightness is bait.

None of these inhabitants sees the whole metropolis. A polyp knows the passing water and the shadow that makes it close. A crab knows a circuit of ledges. An eel knows connected hollows. A ray knows clearings and buried prey. Larvae know only gradients. Even the greatest hunters experience the city as successive pressures, scents, obstructions, and openings.

Together, however, they make something larger than habitat. Their breathing drives water through chambers. Their feeding clears and redirects currents. Their shells raise barriers. Their burrows drain the mud. Their waste feeds films; their carcasses support colonies; their attempts at shelter create shelter for whatever follows. They inhabit the city by consuming it, and construct it by being consumed.

Its true population is therefore not merely the animals visible in its streets. It includes the dead beneath them, the microbes inside the stone, the eggs waiting on the walls, the drifting young exchanged with distant reefs, and the colonies whose many mouths share one body. It includes every temporary tenant of every cavity and every creature that arrives to hunt those tenants.

Nothing lives there alone. Nothing leaves without becoming, in some measure, part of the place.
