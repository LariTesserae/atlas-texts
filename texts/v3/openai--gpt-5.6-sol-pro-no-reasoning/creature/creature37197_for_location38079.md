Those who live there are creatures of accommodation. Nothing native to the city—not the people, not the animals, perhaps not even the plants—expects the world to remain exactly where it was left.

The people are mostly lateral in their habits. They think in terraces, traverses, and crossings rather than in neighborhoods arranged around a center. Asked where they live, they name a shelf, a pressure station, a bridge sequence, or the color of the stone beneath their district. Height is not a reliable address. Neither is direction. A person may live above the foundries in winter and west of them by spring without moving house.

They tend to be wiry, deliberate walkers, practiced at distributing their weight across surfaces whose allegiance is provisional. Children learn three kinds of balance: balance against wind, balance against machinery, and balance against a place changing its mind. They can feel a turbine starting through the soles of their shoes. They know which railings gather safe water, which tremble before an altitude correction, and which should never be touched when the fog is below them.

Most carry more equipment than strangers initially notice: chalk, a folding cup, a spool of bright cord, a compact respirator, two or three bridge tokens, and a little brass plumb whose weight has been certified by the municipality. The plumb is not merely a tool. People lower it into stairwells before descending, suspend it in unfamiliar rooms, or set it on café tables when conversation turns serious. If it points anywhere but down, everyone pays quickly and leaves.

Their clothing is layered, dark, and glossy at the shoulders from condensation. Coats have drain seams and high collars lined with engine-cured wool. Factory workers wear ceramic masks against mineral dust; commuters wear short capes to keep cable grease from their backs. Warning colors are used sparingly. A red cuff may mark a structural listener. Yellow stitching identifies someone authorized to cross animal routes. White is worn by those who tend cisterns, so impurities show immediately.

They are not solemn all the time. That impression comes from the pace of their surroundings. In fact, the terraces contain a dry, highly technical humor. Residents joke about staircases reaching voting age, buildings claiming dependents, and fog that has failed inspection. A bridge longer on the return journey is called “generous.” A corridor that delivers workers to the wrong factory is said to be “career-minded.” They give impossible events bureaucratic nicknames because bureaucracy is one of the few things that can be applied to them without making them worse.

Their oldest civic profession is surveying, though a surveyor there is part cartographer, part negotiator, part animal watcher. Surveyors wear mirrored goggles and carry instruments made to measure not only angle and distance but reluctance. They record whether a wall permits an approach, whether a tunnel remembers both ends, whether a district appears under direct observation or only in peripheral vision. They seldom declare a measurement true. They declare it valid until a date, weather condition, or geological event.

Close behind them in importance are the structural listeners. These people work at night, when machinery pauses expose smaller sounds. They kneel with one ear to bridge plates and cliff foundations, listening for changes in the crystal alignment beneath. Many keep lint deer, though no one claims to have domesticated them. A listener may spend years following a particular herd through freight yards, learning the distinctions between their alarms: wall shift, cable fatigue, interior multiplication, imminent weather, approaching shadow. The deer are always believed before the instruments, but the instruments generate the official paperwork.

Engine crews live nearest the boreholes and are recognizable by the permanent black crescents beneath their nails. Their work is hereditary only in the practical sense that the machines take longer than one lifetime to understand. A mechanic may inherit responsibility for a single valve that has not yet completed its first rotation. Families keep notebooks recording ancestral adjustments: half a degree closed during the Frost of Copper Windows; opened again after the western shelf became audible; untouched since the birth of a grandmother.

They do not agree on what the engines consume, and their disagreements have produced schools, unions, feuds, and religions. Some insist the boreholes extract pressure. Others say they burn the difference between where a thing is and where it might fall. A minority believes the flywheels slow the descent of the entire city through an unseen medium. Whatever their theory, mechanics treat the engines with neither awe nor contempt. They wipe condensation from gauges, grease the processional belts, and listen to the mountain’s immense internal labor as one might listen to a sleeping relative with a chronic illness.

Water workers occupy another respected caste, though they dislike the word. They are called gutter stewards, fog clerks, cistern divers, and tidekeepers. Before dawn they walk the silvered channels with hooded lamps, clearing moth scales and boiler moss from the night’s catch. Tidekeepers decide when fountains may run. Their charts contain phases unrelated to any moon: machine rest, inward weather, bridge shadow, animal ascent, distant thunder, and conditions known simply as “clear below.”

Cistern divers are small, patient people who can remain in cold darkness for a long time. They inspect the reservoirs cut behind habitation, where water sometimes develops a current toward a wall. Divers tie themselves to two separate lines in case one leads elsewhere. They return with mineral blooms, old pipe fittings, eyeless amphibians, and occasionally objects dropped into reservoirs not yet built.

Food growers are manufacturers as much as farmers. They work in warm galleries under laundries and beside exhaust towers, combining issued soil according to recipes guarded as closely as engine schematics. A garden bed might contain six years of kitchen waste, pulverized basalt, swift guano, furnace ash, and one legally inherited handful of true valley loam. Gardeners know soil can become ambitious. They inspect it for gradients, unlicensed watersheds, distant lights, or weather too small to be part of the public atmosphere.

When a plot begins producing horizon, specialists called limiters are summoned. They approach from multiple sides, mark the apparent boundary with red stakes, and offer the forming landscape measured additions of compost until it stabilizes. If it does not stabilize, the property is rezoned. Families in the famous inaccessible country still lower baskets to the roofs below, trading unfamiliar fruit for salt, lamp oil, and municipal news.

Children grow up with the city’s contradictions as ordinary facts. They play games around the slow machinery, wagering on where a flywheel spoke will be by the end of the school year. They collect cast-off warning placards and use them to build forts. They know not to enter fog without naming each other aloud, and not to answer a voice that identifies them by a job they do not yet have.

Their schools teach provisional geography. Pupils redraw local maps every month and are graded less on accuracy than on notation: what moved, what was newly visible, what became uncertain. Arithmetic includes return distances. Civic lessons require memorizing animal right-of-way law and the correct procedure for discovering daylight underground. Older students apprentice in soil galleries, cable stations, echo offices, or the Department of Additional Interiors.

Adolescents sometimes dare one another onto the reflection tram. Most fail to reach it. Those who succeed come back subdued, with foreign punch marks on their tickets and the conviction that someone resembling them remained aboard. The city does not punish this directly. It assigns the returned youths several weeks of community work in brightly lit rooms.

The elderly are valued for having accumulated long observations. An old person may remember when a shelf was still glossy enough to reflect stars, when a bridge connected different districts, or when the western peaks were close. Their memories are entered into municipal records as instruments, with margins of error and dates of calibration. Some sit each evening outside housing stacks, watching the mountains withdraw. They can identify distant ranges no longer visible to younger eyes.

Death requires unusual care in a place where decay is reluctant. Bodies do not return easily to the mountain, and ordinary burial risks creating private soil or an unauthorized hollow. Some dead are cremated in industrial furnaces, their ash allocated between family gardens and civic compost. Others are placed in cliff chambers where slow crystal growth encloses them. Over generations they become dark silhouettes inside translucent mineral, still visible when maintenance lights pass behind the wall.

A few are given to the engines. This is not described as sacrifice. Their names are engraved on pressure housings, and a measured quantity of their ash is introduced into a borehole during scheduled maintenance. Mechanics say the flywheels turn more quietly afterward, though official reports find no change.

No single people own the city. Human residents share it with populations that have their own districts, schedules, and rights.

The lint deer are the most intimate neighbors. They enter courtyards, tram depots, canteens, and ground-floor apartments without invitation. Their six feet make a soft, complicated patter; their huge antlers pass through narrow spaces with impossible ease. In wet weather, each herd becomes a moving instrument of glassy notes. Residents hang ceramic beads from public trellises so the deer will rub against them and leave behind information in scratches.

No one feeds them directly. They prefer cable grass, rust flakes, moss, and the pale residue that forms around stressed bolts. Before structural failure they gather in silence, all facing the threatened object. Before stranger events, they face empty air. Entire streets have been evacuated because a herd stood for hours staring at a place where a doorway appeared the following winter.

Swifts own the warm arteries of the city. They nest by the thousand in ducts and chimney crowns, black bodies flashing silver when they turn. Shift horns bring them out in spirals, though it is unclear whether they have adapted to the factories or the factories have unconsciously adapted to them. Ventilation crews schedule repairs around nesting cycles. A blocked exhaust is inconvenient; an offended swift colony can relocate heat across three districts.

Their droppings are precious to gardeners, their shed feathers used in sensitive weather vanes. Workers read the flocks as informal forecasts. A tight downward spiral means pressure loss. A sudden dispersal indicates indoor fog. If the swifts enter buildings and refuse to leave, all engines on the terrace are stopped.

The pale goats of the aqueducts belong nowhere and are recorded as municipal contractors. Their lack of echo makes them useful for identifying false sound. Signal crews follow them onto parapets too narrow for human confidence. The goats lick minerals from condensation gates and sleep on pipe arches above the rift. They are tagged, counted, and paid annually in salt, though the invoices list their service as “acoustic subtraction.”

Foxes inhabit transformer courts and warm cable housings. Their fur is usually black with copper-colored paws, stained by the surfaces on which they sleep. They are accomplished thieves of gloves, lunch tins, survey ribbons, and still-warm ceramic fuses. Electricians leave decoy objects for them, partly to protect equipment and partly because a transformer abandoned by its fox is regarded as unsafe.

In sumps and cistern margins live the long amphibians. They are translucent, handless-looking things with delicate ribs and mouths that open sideways. Children call them gutter dragons. They feed on metallic moths and on tiny pale organisms that condense directly from pipe sweat. Water workers treat their presence as a sign of ordinary contamination; their absence is more alarming. During pressure reversals they climb walls and arrange themselves head-down around doorframes.

The large gliders above the terraces remain less understood. Up close—when they ever are up close—they seem neither feathered nor furred. Their broad bodies have the matte darkness of openings. Light does not reflect from them, and cloud can sometimes be seen continuing through their wings. They circle industrial updrafts for days without flapping, then fold and vanish toward the impossible peaks.

People disagree about whether these are animals, absences shaped by flight, or parts of the sky that have learned to forage. They are protected under all three classifications. Their shed material, when found, resembles thin sheets of evening and cannot be stored in a lit room.

Plants, too, have acquired civic status. The silver trees in sheltered cracks are fitted with root gauges because their horizontal growth reveals tension in the cliff. Their black leaves collect condensation more efficiently than roofs, each tree feeding a narrow basin below it. Some are older than their terraces. When the stone ends, their roots continue across open air until the mountain catches up.

Boiler moss grows in plush blue-green masses around heat vents. It is harvested for insulation, medicine, and packing material, but only in strips; a whole mat removed at once may take the warmth of the wall with it. Orchids flourish in chemical drizzle, producing flowers the colors of tarnish and flame. Foundry vines wind around chimneys and tighten during shutdowns, as though holding the structures upright while the engines sleep.

Beyond the inhabited belts live the shelf herders. They move with mixed groups of goats, lint deer, and heavier animals resembling shaggy beetles from a distance. Their camps gather near isolated wetlands and migrate according to fog height rather than season. Herders trade milk, woven underfur, moss salt, and observations from unclaimed land. They often arrive with news of a new escarpment months before survey instruments detect it.

Farther still, the abandoned drilling towers have residents of their own. Red-eyed birds nest in the signal lamps and blink in sequence with them. Pale insects live in old bore pipes, ascending at intervals of several years. Travelers sometimes find fires burning in tower cabins, kettles warm and bedding recently folded, though no crews have been assigned there for generations. The official explanation is thermal persistence.

There are also those who come from the reflected district.

They look like ordinary workers at first: tired, properly dressed, carrying stamped permits and metal lunch boxes. Their coins show familiar buildings from impossible angles. They know people in the city, but seldom the current versions of them. They ask after factories demolished before their supposed births. Some stay. They are assigned provisional housing, medical observation, and jobs that do not involve mirrors.

Others spend one shift searching for a particular bridge or platform, then board the tram when it appears in wet steel. No one tries to stop them. Their departures are entered under transfer rather than disappearance.

What lives in the city, finally, is not a single adapted population but a compact among unfinished things. Humans live there, certainly, with their codes, unions, gardens, jokes, and measuring devices. But so do bridges awaiting their correct length, rooms developing private interiors, soils attempting to become countries, and engines feeding on a quantity no one can define. The distinction between resident and structure is not always useful. A family may maintain a machine for six generations; a machine may regulate the gravity beneath their house; lint deer may determine when both must leave.

The deepest inhabitant is perhaps the city’s patience itself. It lives in the people who dismantle homes for a landslide their grandchildren may never see, in gardeners who mark a lichen edge for successors to measure, in mechanics who inherit a valve mid-turn. It lives in animals sleeping on warm iron while the mountain crystallizes beneath them.

And below all of these, in the vapor where the floor should be, something else is beginning to live: not yet a river, but the behavior from which a river will eventually arise. It gathers silt, rehearses channels, and sends up the sound of water considering motion.

Those above listen to it without expecting completion. They lower instruments, retrieve traces of future landscapes, update the maps, and go on drinking what the night leaves on the rails.
