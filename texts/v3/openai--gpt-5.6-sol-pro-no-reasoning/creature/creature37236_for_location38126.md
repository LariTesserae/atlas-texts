The people of the tableland are hard to see because they have practiced being mistaken for the country.

They are slight, though not uniformly small. A standing adult would reach the chest of a person from below, but few stand. Height is wasteful where a gesture may take years and a fall may occupy dynasties. They go folded close to the stone, knees set wide, spines bowed beneath layered mantles. From a distance they resemble heaps of fitted shale beside their shelters. Up close, the shale opens an eye.

Their skin has the pale, exhausted colors of the plateau: salt-gray, old lavender, the green-black of copper that cannot quite corrode. It is divided into fine polygonal plates. At the joints these overlap like scales; across the face they are thin enough to show dark mineral channels beneath. No pulse moves there. Their blood, if it can be called blood, circulates as a succession of crystallizations, dissolving at one extremity while assembling at another. A wound does not bleed. Its edges slowly grow mutually compatible until they become one surface again.

Their eyes are broad and flat, protected by clear lids that may remain closed for decades. They do not track motion well. Quick things vanish for them into continuous blurs, and the whole lower world appears as weather: forests flashing dark and bare, cities briefly glittering, rivers whipping loose across plains. But they distinguish with exquisite precision whatever remains. They can recognize an individual shell-grazer by the angle of a single facet, remember the intended position of a stone not yet placed, and read centuries of pressure in the bend of a black stalk.

Their mouths are narrow seams. They have little need to open them.

Like the animals, they draw much of their substance from air, light, and rock. Mineral combs along their ribs collect brittle gases. Mantles woven from tendon and translucent bell-creatures spread over their backs, exposing broad chemical surfaces to the sun. They supplement this slow harvest with thin wafers pared from film-beasts, salt nodules cultivated in bowls, and the glassy internal rods of certain crawlers. A meal is not swallowed so much as worn against the palate until its matter has passed into the body. One wafer may feed a household for generations.

They possess voices, but speech here cannot depend on sound. A spoken syllable might outlast the speaker without ever reaching its end, accumulating around a shelter until later words became impossible to distinguish from earlier ones. So they converse chiefly by arrangement.

A shell set open-side east means attention. Two knucklebones touching at their widest points indicate doubt. A larva placed alive in a carved bowl proposes an action whose consequences are unknown; the same larva enclosed in a copper loop refuses it. Long statements run for miles in rows of upright chips. Because the chips are read from several directions, a message can contain its question, answer, objection, and amendment at once.

Their language is less like a line of speech than a landscape negotiated into meaning.

The green-black ornaments around their wrists are instruments of grammar. Rings, pins, and hooked plates mark which version of a person is being addressed: the one who intends, the one who acts, the one who remembers, or the one who remains after the act. Without such distinctions, conversation near the central towers becomes dangerous. A promise may be overheard by the self who has not yet made it. A warning may fasten itself to the wrong cause.

They have personal names, but each name is only the innermost element of a larger figure. Around it accumulate names for all the stages that continue to inhabit the plateau. A child might begin as a single sound scratched into a copper tile. With age—or accumulation—the tile is surrounded by shells, pins, cuts, and stones until the name becomes an enclosure large enough to crouch inside. The very old are not called. One visits their names.

Childbirth is rare and difficult to define. Their bodies do not produce offspring quickly. Two or more adults begin by laying down a small mineral framework in the shadow of a shelter. Over centuries they feed it salts, organic films, splinters of bone, and portions of their own living tissue. At first it resembles a jointed effigy. Then it acquires warmth, opacity, and a direction of attention. Its earliest movements remain visible within and around it, so a child is attended by a dense halo of unfinished gestures: hands forever reaching, eyes forever opening, a mouth held at the beginning of its first recognition.

No child belongs only to its makers. It also belongs to those who will complete its construction, including descendants who may appear beside it before they are born. Kinship therefore extends in several directions. One may be raised by an ancestor, a future grandchild, and a middle-aged version of oneself, each contributing at the stage when they are locally present.

Their homes are small because a shelter cannot be abandoned in the usual sense. Every wall persists through construction, use, collapse, and repair. A large building would become impenetrably crowded with its own states. The low huts are attempts to limit this accumulation. Slabs are fitted with deliberate gaps so that incompatible phases can pass through one another. Bone pins anchor not merely stone to stone, but one moment of the wall to another. Braided tendons are kept under exact tension; loosened, they may allow the shelter’s ruin to spread backward into its founding.

The shallow bowls outside are not vessels but calendars, courts, nurseries, and graves.

In one, shell arrangements record disputes. In another, an immobile larva measures how far a decision has advanced. Knucklebones mark absent members of a household, though absence on the plateau never means nonexistence. Some bowls contain the residue of names removed from their owners. These are the nearest things the people have to tombs.

For death among them is not the end of activity. It is the end of revision.

A living person may still alter the relationship among their accumulated selves, bringing an old gesture into harmony with a later one or loosening a memory from the place where it occurred. The dead cannot. Their final configuration remains, and every prior shape settles around it. Families care for these dense, motionless persons by polishing exposed surfaces, separating entangled generations, and preventing grazers from consuming the mineral skins through which identity persists.

Their predators are seldom beasts. More often they are coincidences.

Near the smooth towers, two states of a person may become aligned so perfectly that they adhere. An arm reaching tomorrow can fuse with the same arm withdrawn centuries before. A face may acquire its own reverse side and thereafter see only inward. Travelers sometimes return carrying a wound whose injury has not occurred, or missing a tool still visible in the hand. There are hunters who specialize in disentangling such accidents. They wear cages of bone around their bodies so that each former posture is held slightly apart from the next.

These hunters move faster than anyone else on the plateau. Their speed is appalling: a handspan in a decade, sometimes more. Where they pass, shell-grazers close in alarm over periods of several generations.

The winged animals above the cliffs are sacred to some households and merely useful to others. Their immeasurably extended falls provide a scale by which long undertakings can be judged. Marriages last from one wingbeat to the next. Boundaries are renegotiated when a particular flier descends level with a marked stone. A creature close to the abyss may have officiated over thousands of unions without yet completing the downward flex of its wing.

No one agrees whether the central ring was built by their ancestors.

Those who tend it have hands shaped for the stones: broad palms, shortened outer fingers, calluses that mirror every hollow in the ring. This might prove inheritance, purpose, or merely long contact. They crouch beyond the towers in overlapping congregations, each keeper surrounded by prior acts of tending. Some appear young from one direction and fossil-old from another. Their shells and copper pieces are arranged not as names but as warnings.

They teach that the ring does not cause the plateau’s condition. It is an aperture through which the country can be seen as it truly is: every event occupying its proper place, nothing lost merely because another thing follows it. Time, they say, is the error of creatures below, whose senses are too hurried to perceive the continued presence of what has happened.

Others believe the ring is a trap built to catch a single afternoon. According to them, the trap succeeded too well.

Still others insist there was once no plateau. The whole country is the accumulation of one ascent: every stone, beast, hut, tower, and inhabitant being some aspect of the first traveler who climbed above the clouds and could not descend intact. In this telling, the vast film-beasts are portions of that traveler’s skin, the black stalks are hairs mineralized in light, the shelters are thoughts of refuge, and the people are choices the traveler considered making.

The people themselves do not require these accounts to agree. Contradiction troubles them less than sequence. Several origins can inhabit the same ground.

What they fear is erasure.

They fear the quick world below, where a cry ends after being heard and a body can leave no more than bones. They fear rivers, not because water is scarce, but because water carries things away. They fear fire most of all. Flame changes too swiftly for identity to remain attached to matter. In old shelter layers there are blackened places that no person will cross. Around them, gestures terminate. Names lose pieces. Even the one-sided animals avoid them.

Yet they also fear complete accumulation. The plateau grows more crowded without appearing to do so. Each birth, footfall, quarrel, and act of attention stays. Paths thicken with walkers who cannot cease having walked them. In some districts the people can no longer move because their earlier movements occupy every possible posture. Whole households have become dense knots beside their shelters, conscious perhaps, but unable to create a gesture not already present.

For this reason, a few still approach the rim.

They can be found there in long processions, though each procession may consist of one person attempting departure many times. Copper bands circle their limbs. Bone pins hold their former selves in alignment. Their mantles are folded tight so the wind of the lower world cannot seize too much of them at once.

They look down at storms racing shadows over forests. They watch snow appear and vanish, kingdoms kindle around rivers, roads spread like cracks through ice. To them the lands below seem scarcely existent, yet unbearably free.

Some turn back. Their turning remains beside them, so from then on they stand forever as both the one who returned and the one who still considers descent.

Some step over.

The fall does not kill them, at least not immediately. It draws them out. A body that required centuries to raise one foot enters weather where seconds tear past. All the person’s accumulated states separate along the cliff face: infancy near the rim, adulthood distributed through open air, old age flickering far below. Travelers in the lowlands sometimes report pale figures fixed high against the stone, or translucent hands appearing in clear weather. They call these fossils, spirits, tricks of light.

The tableland people call them the Departing.

On rare occasions, something reaches the country below: a copper ring, a bone pin, a plate of gray skin, a final body emptied of most of its history. Whether what arrives is still a person is disputed. It may speak quickly. It may breathe. It may live for a few lowland years, shedding memories with every step.

Above, however, the rest remains.

At the rim stands the child who began the journey. Behind the child crouches every later self, hand laid into hand, a silent lineage made from one life. The procession leads inward across the pale tableland, past the fitted huts and black kiln-plants, through herds that have moved less than a finger’s breadth, toward towers whose shadows refuse agreement.

And each figure in the procession is still deciding whether to go.
