Nothing lives in the high country except the high country itself.

It is difficult to decide whether this makes it one being or an immeasurable congregation. No stem is sovereign. No trunk contains a central mind. The plateau thinks, if thinking is the word, through changes in pressure: through water arriving at a valve, heat tightening a membrane, sugar accumulating in a root-knot, light striking one side of a tower. Its decisions are distributed across miles and may take an afternoon to become visible. By then no single structure can be said to have made them. The answer belongs to the whole arrangement.

Its nearest equivalent to flesh is the tiled ground: broad plates laid one over another, buoyant with warm sap, their seams continually tested and resewn by exploratory roots. These plates are not merely a surface upon which the organism rests. They are skin, raft, reservoir, and nursery. Beneath them extends no dead foundation, only denser levels of the same life—dark load-bearing braids, swollen conduits, pressure chambers, buried leaves folded into beams. Depth here is not a descent toward geology. It is a descent into older and slower vegetation.

Above this skin rise its temporary organs.

The narrow tower-trees are lungs and chimneys. They draw heated air through internal shafts, condense mist along ribbed walls, and spill the gathered water into neighboring canals. Their high flowers are sensory structures, opening toward changes in light, wind, and atmospheric charge. Their apparent balconies regulate shade. Their doorways breathe. Even their grandeur is functional: height creates pressure, catches cloud, throws seed, and widens the range across which pollen can travel.

The bridges are tendons. The terraces are spread leaves trained to bear water and tension. Colonnades are interchangeable supports, grown with deliberate gaps so that sun and weather can pass between them. The palace-like structures beside the lake are mutable organs of filtration, changing their anatomy from hour to hour: hall into reservoir, room into pith, parapet into gutter. Nothing in them is preserved for appearance, though appearance continually results.

The lake-leaf may be the creature’s broadest exposed heart. Its slow flexing redistributes heat and water through the central basin. Each wave it sends against the embankments is both pulse and measurement. Pressure returns along the shore through buried fibers, informing distant sluices of the basin’s weight. When the lake moves, structures miles away may narrow a channel, close a pore, or begin the dissolution of a wall that has become too costly to maintain.

There are no nerves, yet signals travel. Some are chemical and slow, passing root to root in exchanges of sap. Others are mechanical: a tightening vine pulls upon a bridge, the bridge tensions a tower, the tower compresses a cistern wall, and the message crosses a district as a sequence of minute distortions. Water carries still more instructions. Its mineral poverty, temperature, pollen load, and speed are read by countless petaled gates. Lightning can address the whole plateau at once.

What might be called memory resides in form.

A canal remembers recurrent rain by deepening its corrugations. A tower remembers prevailing wind by adding ribs to one face. A terrace repeatedly torn at the same seam eventually grows hinges there and incorporates rupture into its motion. No event is recorded as an image or thought. It persists as altered tissue, as a tendency to bend, as a valve that opens sooner than it once did. The country’s history is embodied in what its structures have become prepared to do.

Yet it is not a passive intelligence. It anticipates.

Before a pressure front arrives, leaves turn edgewise. Before a basin overfills, distant mats separate along pale seams prepared days earlier. Towers lean apart to clear a path for rising architecture. Seed pods aim themselves toward spaces that do not yet exist. This foresight may be no more mysterious than a flower opening before noon, but magnified across a landscape it resembles strategy. The plateau appears to know the shape it is about to assume.

That shape never lasts.

The being has no adult form toward which it matures. Its maturity is the capacity to remain unfinished. It inhabits itself by continually moving its necessities elsewhere: lifting reservoirs, rerouting sugars, abandoning shaded spans, opening corridors to wind. Structures that no longer exchange anything with the whole are digested without sentiment. Their substance is withdrawn, softened, and carried away. A tower may vanish not because it failed, but because it completed the condition for another tower to rise.

Its hungers are elemental and immense. It drinks rain, cloud, and condensation. It eats sunlight across acres of exposed membrane. It gathers heat in blackened bark and uses temperature differences to raise water into the upper shelves. It feeds upon its own obsolete anatomy. Even space is something it harvests. The wide courts between structures collect light and permit thermal currents to organize. Emptiness is one of its cultivated tissues.

Its reproduction is harder to distinguish from maintenance. Seeds are made and scattered, but where could an offspring begin when every reachable surface already belongs to living architecture? Perhaps the pressurized seeds are not children but mobile instructions, packets sent to repair or renegotiate distant districts. Perhaps the plateau is a colony of innumerable plants whose identities have become meaningless through grafting. Perhaps each tile, tower, and bridge remains genetically distinct while participating in a superorganism older than any of them.

Or perhaps the entire high country is young: one vast germinating thing whose ridges and cities are only its first leaves.

If it has an edge, that edge may be where true individuals occur. Along the lower margins, exploratory cords descend toward the lowlands, braided tightly enough to hold water within themselves. They touch unfamiliar ground, recoil from mineral surfaces, and divide along cracks where moisture gathers. Behind them, thin membranes spread, carrying the plateau’s conditions outward. A successful cord thickens into a pier. A pier suspends a leaf. A leaf catches rain. In this way the high country may advance not by walking, but by manufacturing beneath itself the living world it requires.

It possesses no face. Nevertheless, it has orientations. Sun-facing flowers act briefly as eyes without images. Cloud-catching spines read the air. Root tips taste pressure, moisture, and resistance. The broad exposed mats register impact so precisely that a heavy fall of rain becomes a moving texture across the organism’s awareness. There are no animals to tread upon it, but it can feel its own seeds landing. It can feel the weight of detached petals entering a canal, the recoil of a snapped vine, the first cold point of rain from an approaching storm.

Its emotional life, if one can speak of such a thing, would be almost unrecognizable. Alarm is a district tightening. Relief is water released downhill. Attention is the slow convergence of pores, roots, and flowers upon a change in light. Pain does not cry out; it seals, reroutes, and digests. Desire appears as architecture extended toward a resource. What looks like patience may simply be the timescale on which sap becomes decision.

Still, certain states seem to pass through it like moods.

During long heat, the pale structures curl inward and the plateau grows austere. Openings narrow. Water retreats into shaded channels. Towers cease their groaning, and the black pavements lie tense beneath the glare. In prolonged rain, the country becomes extravagant. Membranes unfold. Cisterns brim. Fresh buttresses push outward, yellow and soft. Every seam seems on the verge of becoming an avenue, every gutter a branch.

After lightning, it flowers.

Then the solitary spires, distant terraces, and widely spaced colonnades answer one another with pale reflective blooms. For a few hours the high country reveals how completely its scattered monuments are joined. The flowering does not fill the emptiness; it outlines it. Moonlight lies upon the petals, water moves through the white-laden canals, and electrical change passes downward through the conductive sapways into roots too deep to see.

By morning the display is dismantled and drunk.

The being does not mourn it. Beauty here is not preserved as evidence of value. It is spent in circulation. Petals become waterborne matter; water becomes pressure; pressure opens sluices; sluices feed the next construction. Everything magnificent is also provisional.

And so what lives there is neither forest nor city, though it repeatedly resembles both. It is a landscape that has absorbed the roles of organism, habitat, weatherwork, and ground. It is builder and building, inhabitant and inhabited place. Its body is measured in horizons, yet no portion is exempt from revision. It has no hands because it has made the distinction between hand and world unnecessary.

When the plateau lifts, it is not something beneath the land moving the land.

It is the land changing its posture.
