The people who live here are not numerous by the standards of larger settlements, though the town makes them seem so. Bodies appear everywhere: sidling through wet passages, crouched beneath stairs, leaning from roof to roof to pass a jar. Yet many of the apparent houses are drowned, abandoned, or occupied only in the dry part of the year. The living inhabit a thin, shifting layer near the town’s upper surface, while beneath them lie the rooms of earlier seasons.

They tend to be compact and sure-footed. Children learn early to place the whole sole down rather than trust a heel to a loose stone. Adults walk with one hand free for walls and hanging ropes, and people carrying burdens announce themselves softly before turning a blind corner. Broad baskets are uncommon. Loads are packed in tall narrow frames, carried against the chest or spine, so that their bearers can turn sideways through the lanes.

Their clothing is made to dry on the body: short wrapped garments, rush-fiber capes, close leggings in colder weather, and sandals with thick woven soles that can be untied quickly if caught in mud. Almost everyone carries a length of cord. Knots serve for measuring, fastening, tallying, rescuing dropped vessels, and testing suspicious floors. Chalk sits in small lidded tubes at the belt. A person may leave home without food, but rarely without cord and chalk.

Constant damp marks them. Their fingers are pale and ridged, their nails kept blunt. Lime and clay gather permanently in the creases of their hands. Many have scars on the shins and forearms from sharp new stone, but fewer deep wounds than visitors expect: carelessness is regarded less as courage than as theft from those who must repair the damage afterward. Hair is braided tightly, cropped short, or bound under fitted caps so it will not brush condensation from ceilings into food and open pots.

No household here is fully separate. One family may sleep above another family’s workshop, cook at an oven maintained by three lanes, draw from a well reached through someone else’s floor, and cross four roofs to enter its own rooms. Kinship matters, but access matters almost as much. People speak of being “held by” a stair, a cistern, an oven, or a beam. Those who share the maintenance of such a thing acquire obligations resembling family ties, sometimes stronger ones.

A child may therefore belong to several practical households. They sleep where space is dry, eat where an oven is hot, and are corrected by whoever sees them mishandling water. Their first serious task is usually tending jars beneath drips. Later they learn to clear a gutter without sending debris into a lower dwelling. Adolescence is marked not by age alone but by being trusted to descend into a submerged level during the dry interval and return with timber, sealed pottery, or news of a widening crack.

The most honored inhabitants are not rulers but measurers. They keep no single office and wear no special robes. One may be a stooped old woman with shell gauges hanging from her neck; another a quiet boy chosen for his ability to distinguish echoes; another a builder whose plumb lines are trusted because she records failures as carefully as successes. Their authority lasts only as long as their observations prove useful.

Every measurement is witnessed. At the central platform, one person lowers the cord, another watches the mark, and a third repeats the result aloud. Numbers are never recorded without the condition of the water, the direction of the wind, recent rainfall, and any unusual behavior among the snails. A depth is not simply “twelve lengths.” It is “twelve lengths after two cold nights, with the southern well rising and the square-shells climbing.”

They do not believe accuracy makes the basin obedient. Measurement is a form of courtesy: the attempt to notice what has changed before placing weight upon it.

Potters hold a different kind of authority. They make storage vessels, settling jars, drain tiles, whistles, roof cups, oven linings, and the memory pots in which the deep water leaves its pale designs. Their workshops occupy whatever mud shelves the retreating water exposes, so a potter’s workplace may exist for only a few days. Tools and prepared clay are kept in wall niches above the high-water marks, ready to be carried down as soon as the shelves emerge.

Reading the mineral skin inside a memory pot is not treated as prophecy in the dramatic sense. The reader turns the vessel slowly, comparing branching lines with known lanes, blocked drains, buried walls, and pressures inferred from recent settling. Some patterns are dismissed as flaws in the clay. Some are debated for days. When several pots show the same enclosed shape, builders may cut open an old wall and discover a chamber behind it. When a pattern shows an impossible stair or a lane passing through occupied rooms, the drawing is copied and stored. Years later, after enough floors have risen and enough walls have shifted, the impossible passage may become the only sensible one.

Because of this, the town’s plans are kept in vessels rather than on tablets. Old pots line protected rooms near the central platform, each marked with the place and night of its filling. Some have cracked and been sewn together with rush cord. Others contain tracings no one can identify. These are called unborn quarters. No one is permitted to build from a single such pattern, but neither are they discarded.

Builders work in small crews organized around compatible weight. Heavy-bodied workers remain where the paving is firm, splitting stone and setting lower courses. Lighter workers cross plank bridges and fit upper walls. Children carry handfuls of rush fiber and thumb wet clay into joints. Very old people prepare mortar, sort salvage, and listen for changes in the sound of scraping tools. A wall that begins to answer differently is abandoned at once, even if only one course remains unfinished.

They seldom build straight lines unless water insists upon them. Corners are rounded where bodies must squeeze past; foundations widen around active seams; doorframes are made as nested pieces so one side can be raised without dismantling the whole opening. Roofs are deliberately incomplete at their edges, ready to receive another lip, gutter, platform, or room. A finished structure would be considered poorly made because it would have no means of changing.

Ownership follows the same principle. People own what they maintain, but maintenance must be demonstrable. If a family neglects its section of a shared beam and another household caps, binds, and supports it through a wet season, the useful portion may pass to the second household. Disputes are brought not before a judge but before neighbors who depend on the object. They decide who has kept it sound, who has endangered it, and whose continued use puts the least strain on the whole.

There is little inherited land. People inherit duties, access routes, tool rights, dry niches, places in oven schedules, and portions of structures. A dying person might leave one child the third rung and upper landing of a ladder, another the right to collect condensation from the east side of a lintel, and a friend the obligation to renew the clay cap on a beam. Such inheritances sound trivial only to outsiders. In a place with almost no unused surface, a reliable rung may be wealth.

They eat from the basin’s narrow abundance. Rush shoots, moss scraped from designated stones, eels from peripheral wells, frogs’ eggs in rare seasons, and small pale fish trapped in channels after rain all supplement stores brought through the six cuts. Grain arrives in tightly sealed jars, as do salt, oil, dried fruit, charcoal, shell, and occasional timber. In exchange the town sends out pottery, water clocks, unusually fine drain fittings, mineral pigments, woven rush goods, and advice about foundations.

The deep water itself is not traded. Visitors often ask for it after seeing its dim glow, but inhabitants say water taken beyond the rim loses its memory. Small amounts may be given to the sick or to builders from neighboring settlements, always in unglazed pots and never for payment. Attempts to carry larger quantities away have ended with cracked vessels, lost pack animals, or water that arrived ordinary and warm.

Meals are communal chiefly because fuel demands it. Shared ovens are heated with rush charcoal, dried moss cakes, dung brought by traders, and small pieces of wood too damaged for building. Several dishes cook in the same retained heat. Food is thick, sour, and often served in handled cups rather than broad bowls. People eat standing or perched along drain-covered benches, lifting their feet whenever sluice water is released.

Evening is the nearest thing the town has to stillness. When the ovens are warm and the day’s channels have been cleared, people gather in low rooms whose walls sweat gently. Stories are told quietly, because loud voices return from drains in altered forms. The favorite tales concern not founders or conquerors but successful adjustments: the woman who saved three dwellings by cutting through her own floor; the child who noticed snails climbing an hour before a lower wall folded; the crew who spent a season building a staircase that proved to lead nowhere until the town sank enough to need it.

Songs are sparse and antiphonal. One singer offers a short phrase and others answer from neighboring rooms, sometimes through walls or pipes. The architecture delays and distorts the replies, so a song may seem to continue after everyone has stopped. During heavy rain, singers tune themselves to gutters and ceramic whistles. No performance is repeated exactly, because the town’s pitches change with water level and repair.

The inhabitants do not worship the pools as gods. They are wary of naming anything so unstable. Instead, they observe a collection of courtesies. No one spits into deep water. No cord used to sound a pool is later used to bind a person or animal. A pot that has shown an accurate future plan is broken only if the building it foresaw has itself failed. Before opening a roof gate, a person calls downward through the nearest drain so those below can move vessels and children.

Death is handled with particular care because there is almost nowhere to bury the dead. Bodies are wrapped in rush mats and carried through one of the six cuts to shallow clefts beyond the basin rim. There they are covered with loose stone. The finest personal vessels are not buried with them; useful things must remain in use. Instead, a small cup is filled from the deceased person’s household well, left overnight, and then sealed into a wall. Whatever pattern forms inside is not read aloud. It belongs to the structure.

There are also people who disappear into the lower layers. A floor fails, a channel surges, a ceiling gives way in darkness. Recovery is attempted only while it can be done without adding more dead. When it cannot, the chamber is closed and marked with a white circle crossed by a single line. These marks are not called graves. They mean: someone remains part of the weight here.

Children are taught to greet such places by touching the wall, never the floor.

The town’s healers understand damp ailments, crushed fingers, infected cuts, chilled lungs, and the peculiar exhaustion that follows days of listening for structural change. They keep heated stones wrapped in cloth, bitter moss pastes, rush splints, shell lancets, and precious copper blades reserved for work that stone cannot do cleanly. Illness is rarely isolated from architecture. A healer may prescribe sleep in a higher room, removal from a certain wall’s vibration, or a week of work near a warm oven. If several people dream of descending stairs, the room where they sleep is inspected for settling.

Dreams are not regarded as messages from elsewhere so much as sensations noticed too faintly while awake. A person may have heard water moving beneath a foundation without knowing it; in sleep the sound becomes a corridor, a black fish, a door opening underfoot. Skilled dream listeners ask practical questions: Was the stair wet? Which hand touched the wall? Did the echo arrive before or after the voice?

Their calendar has little use for fixed dates. Time is remembered by wettings, exposures, and shifts: the season the northern pool rose to the seventh stone; the dry interval when the red moss crossed the split lintel; the year three chalked springs died and one returned under the ovens. Ages are approximate. A person may be said to have lived through eleven lower-room openings, or to have been born when the western cut still admitted two people abreast.

Outsiders often mistake this flexibility for disorder. In fact, the people are governed by countless exact habits. Jars must be placed with their handles outward in flood-prone rooms. Plank paths are tested at dawn. No load may be rested against a wall marked with three vertical chalk lines. A sleeping mat must never cover a drainage seam entirely. Anyone who sees a square-shelled snail above head height must tell three households, whether or not they believe rain is coming.

The snails are treated with affectionate seriousness. Children give names to unusually large ones and move them from oven walls before firing. Potters place chips of damp clay beneath suspended vessels to encourage grazing. Their trails are read in lamplight, and sudden migrations can empty a lower lane faster than any shouted warning. Yet no one calls them sacred. In the town’s speech, sacred means something set apart, and almost nothing here can be spared from use.

The blind eels receive more fearful respect. When one appears, conversation stops. It is lifted in wet cloth, never by bare hands, and returned to the deepest available well. Then every household checks its lowest support. If several emerge at once, people sleep on roofs, doors are unpinned, jars are moved upward, and cords are stretched across widening lanes to catch anyone who slips. Sometimes nothing follows. This does not diminish the warning. A warning, they say, is not a promise of disaster; it is a chance to avoid one.

Visitors find the inhabitants reserved but not unfriendly. Hospitality begins with a pause at the basin cut, where a host wets the newcomer’s soles and asks them to stand still. Only after nearby channels remain calm are they led inward. Guests are given narrow sleeping spaces and warned not to brace themselves against walls without asking. They are often startled to find their conversations repeated faintly from distant openings. The townspeople consider it polite to ignore words carried accidentally through drains.

Privacy exists, but as a discipline rather than a property of walls. People lower their voices, turn jars over listening holes, and hang wet mats to absorb sound. To deliberately attend to a drain carrying another household’s speech is a serious offense. So is withholding news of a structural danger because of a feud. Personal quarrels must yield to water.

Their greatest fear is not drowning. Water usually announces itself. What they fear is false stillness: a pool whose level does not respond to rain, a wall that ceases creaking after months of movement, a spring that vanishes without leaving damp clay, a silent interval in which even the underground whisper stops. Motion can be answered. Stillness may mean pressure gathering beyond detection.

When such a silence comes, work pauses. People stand in lanes and on roofs, hands resting lightly on stone. The measurers lower cords. Potters fill rows of vessels. Children are brought from the lowest rooms. No prayers are spoken. The whole town listens.

Then, somewhere, a drop falls. A snail shifts. A beam complains. Water resumes beneath the paving, and hundreds of people breathe out almost together.

They do not imagine themselves masters of the basin, nor victims trapped within it. They think of themselves as its temporary inhabitants in the strictest sense: those who keep making a place habitable, and are made particular by the work. Their virtues are attentiveness, restraint, repair, and the willingness to surrender a room before it takes another life. Their deepest disgrace is to pretend that a failing thing is sound.

So they live upward, inward, and beside one another. They inherit no certainty except change. Each morning they step from their sleeping ledges, inspect the chalk marks, touch the walls for damp, study the snails, and ask what portion of the town still consents to bear them.

Then they begin again.
