The plateau is inhabited less by separate species than by tenants of other lives.

In the high crowns dwell slight, hook-footed creatures with dense, backward-lying fur. They travel beneath branches rather than above them, clinging belly-up to bark slick with condensation. Their limbs are long, their faces blunt and whiskered, their eyes fitted to green half-light. Whole generations may inhabit a single elder tree, drinking from branch pools, grazing its moss gardens, and nesting inside warm tangles of aerial roots without ever touching the buried ground. Seeds adhere to their coats and germinate there. Old individuals often carry small ferns along the spine and pale fungi behind the ears, until an animal at rest is nearly indistinguishable from a mossed limb.

The leaf cisterns belong to broad amphibians, soft-bodied and patient. Their backs host living patterns of algae that alter with the chemistry of each pool. Some resemble green stones, some rafts of duckweed, some drowned leaves filmed with silver. They feed on larvae and pollen until a reservoir begins to dry, then climb out beneath a cloak of wet vegetation and cross from cup to cup. Their eggs are laid in strings along leaf veins. The tadpoles graze the inner surfaces of their nursery, keeping it free of choking growth; the plant, in turn, holds water more reliably where they live.

Dark insects fill the mist. Their flights have no obvious center, appearing like soot suspended in water. At dusk they settle simultaneously on the lower faces of leaves, aligned head-down along the veins. Other insects live in flowers whose opening takes years. They hatch, mate, and die within a single petal chamber, never knowing the flower from outside. Their descendants depart only when the aging blossom begins to fold and its warmth declines.

The plateau’s birds are creatures of touch and vibration. Long-legged drummers walk on the upper root mats, placing each narrow foot with care. Their hollow bills strike sequences into the wood, and distant birds answer through trunks, buried vines, and roofed-over lakes. Their plumage is dark and matte, broken by pale bars visible only when the wings open. They seldom fly far. The living floor carries messages better than foggy air.

Smaller birds nest in resonant seed cases. They court by shaking the chambers, producing low rattles that travel down the supporting stems. Some have lost almost all voice. Others communicate by snapping specialized feathers or by tapping polished stones against hollow roots. Flocks can remain unseen while their presence moves beneath a traveler as a wandering pattern of knocks.

In the underland lakes live pale fish with reduced eyes and delicate fins like torn petals. Seeds sift down to them through cracks in the root ceiling, along with insects, pollen, and the occasional drowned fruit. The fish mouth the hanging root tips and gather wherever vibrations promise food. Their spawning movements tremble through the black water into the roof. Far above, certain trees release pollen after such tremors often enough that the relationship seems older than accident.

There are grazers on the open heaths: low, heavy animals with divided feet that spread their weight over the saturated felt. Lichens grow across their flanks. Their antlers, where they bear them, are smooth and many-pronged, shaped less for combat than for parting hanging vegetation. They follow inherited paths, pressing routes that are obscured almost as soon as they pass. At intervals they enter the tree chambers and leave shed antler, seeds caught in their coats, or one polished stone carried in the mouth.

No single creature can be confidently named as the maker of the chambers.

Warm-furred climbers sleep in them. Birds arrange their shells there. Heath grazers shelter newborns on the dry fiber floors. Broad-backed amphibians gather beneath the entrances during heavy condensation. Yet the chambers themselves grow from the trees: ribs arching without joints, walls thickening around remembered pressure, oval doors recurring at the height most useful to those who return. The trees appear to retain habits. A chamber repeatedly nested in becomes drier. A root often drummed upon grows more resonant. A hollow supplied with pale objects may bud shallow niches that keep them from rolling apart.

The occupants participate without planning. Their bodies press, fertilize, abrade, select, and return. The tree answers over decades. The next generation finds the answer and alters its behavior in turn. Thus the seven pale objects around one dark object may be ritual, instinct, horticulture, or merely the stable shape produced by animals and plants teaching one another where things belong.

The pillar rings are larger expressions of the same accord. Each ring is a single colonial organism whose upright fluted stems open after rain. Within the circle the ground stays comparatively firm, and animals congregate there during periods of deep saturation. Their passage brings mineral-rich clay from exposed margins. Their dung feeds the root crown. Birds sharpen resonant taps against the hollow columns; insects breed in their grooves; climbers sleep in the fans overhead. When a pillar dies, the living ring closes around the gap, but often preserves a narrow passage as though accommodating a remembered route.

The reddish walls nearby are not built in the ordinary sense. They are trained growths—or growths that have trained themselves around use. Their pores admit air while shedding water. Small mammals burrow into their warm interiors, and colonies of nearly transparent insects maintain the cavities by eating softened tissue. Whether some vanished intelligence began this relationship is impossible to determine. If there was once a gardener here, its intention has been distributed so completely among roots, animals, fungi, and repeated behavior that no one species possesses it now.

The closest thing the plateau has to a dominant inhabitant is the weave itself.

It is composed of many organisms, but distinctions blur with age. Creeping trunks graft where they touch. Fungal threads pass sugars and chemical warnings between unrelated groves. Root crowns fuse around buried wood and continue using its channels after the original tree has vanished. Vines as thick as rivers cross valleys beneath the humus, surfacing miles apart in different forms of leaf. A blossom in one region and a pillar ring in another may be organs of the same ancient body—or merely neighbors that have exchanged so much substance they no longer behave as strangers.

This body senses slowly. Pressure becomes growth. Repeated vibration changes the density of wood. Minerals released from antler influence the next generation of chambers. Fish stirring beneath a lake, insects hatching inside a flower, and herds crossing a root mat all enter the plateau as faint information. Its responses take seasons or centuries. No moment contains the whole exchange.

Even death lives here as a transitional creature. Silver fungi inhabit leaves for years before consuming them. Beetles tunnel softened trunks without causing collapse, making passages later occupied by roots. Mites graze the fungi; translucent worms graze the mites’ leavings. Fallen animals become brief clearings of concentrated fertility, then green mounds, then indistinguishable rises in the terrain. Bones soften in plant acids and are drawn upward as mineral veins in leaves and fruit.

The great fruits sustain the most crowded societies. When one falls, tearing through layer after layer, scavengers arrive by vibration before its rind has split. Insects enter first through bruises. Amphibians breed in rain-filled grooves. Fur-bearing climbers cut no openings but worry softened seams with their teeth. Fungi lace the flesh into chambers, each with a different temperature and sweetness. For decades the fruit is pond, shelter, pasture, nursery, and finally island. The seedlings growing inside inherit the assembled community and carry portions of it upward.

At the outer edge live bolder, lighter-bodied forms adapted to wind and rupture. They cling to the hanging root curtains and feed in the fine waterfalls. Some spread membranous limbs when sections of the mat tear free, gliding between the descending trees and ponds. Others seal themselves inside bark hollows and survive the fall. In this way an entire fragment of society reaches the lowlands together: plants, fungi, eggs, sleepers, pools, parasites, and seed stores. What appears below as a new forest begins not with a tree, but with a displaced neighborhood.

Far inward, inhabitants become difficult to separate from the place that bears them. Moss-coated grazers look like moving hummocks. Climbers carry gardens. Amphibians carry pools in the folds of their skin. Insects pollinate flowers that house their complete ancestry. Fish signal through roots. Birds speak in the wood’s own voice. Trees grow rooms because rooms have been used before.

Nothing here lives on the plateau.

Everything lives on something else, within something else, and eventually as part of something else. The plateau is not their setting but their accumulated arrangement: an ancient, dim society whose agreements are written in root direction, chamber shape, flowering time, inherited paths, and the patient placement of seven pale objects around one dark one.
