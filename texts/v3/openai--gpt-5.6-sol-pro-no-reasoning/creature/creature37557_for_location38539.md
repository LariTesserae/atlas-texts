The people of the hanging valley are called, in the nearest translation, the **Intervening**.

The name does not mean that they stand between one thing and another. It means that they inhabit the *between itself*: the accumulating distance, the new fraction of a room, the additional mile where no mile existed before. They regard stone, grove, shelter, and body as temporary shores around this continual arrival.

From afar, an Intervening person resembles a narrow figure wrapped in overlapping shale. Their plates are gray, brown-black, greenish, or faintly translucent according to the minerals available during growth. No two plates touch directly. Mineral-thread muscles span the fine gaps between them, tightening and loosening in coordinated waves. Thus they move without joints in the ordinary sense. A shoulder can bend along several lines; a hand can flatten into a wedge; the long cage of the torso twists almost completely around without injury.

They walk slowly even when afraid. Haste makes their plates resonate, and resonance can disclose an internal fault to the roar. An individual who runs may arrive with a finger elongated, a memory displaced, or a narrow band of additional body inserted between chest and back. Children are taught first to stop, then to listen, and only afterward to flee.

Their heads consist of nested cups surrounding a hollow chamber. They have no faces fixed to one side. Sensory seams open wherever attention gathers: pale slits for light, branching pits for chemical traces, taut membranes that detect local alterations in distance. When speaking to one another, they turn the broadest uninterrupted plate forward as a gesture of trust. To show fear, they reveal every seam.

They do not hear the roar. Hearing implies the possibility of silence. They experience it through the entire body as a constant pressure of existence, much as another creature might experience weight. What they notice are imperfections within it: a momentary smoothing, a metallic overtone, the faint internal doubling that precedes the birth of distance. Their personal names are taken from such deviations. A name might mean *Three Brief Hollows in the Lower Roar*, *Tone Beneath Feldspar*, or *The Quiet That Passed Through Copper*.

Ordinary speech is difficult. Voiced sound vanishes into the surrounding tumult, so the Intervening communicate by touching resonant rods to one another’s plates. Pulses travel through the body and are interpreted by timing, direction, and texture. From a distance they use broad movements of the arms or flash polished mineral scales. Their most private conversations occur forehead to forehead, hollow chambers aligned, while one person softly rearranges the plates of the other’s face.

A household usually contains one to four adults, several young in differing stages of assembly, and at least one dead counselor seated in a nearby grove. Kinship is not determined by birth alone. A person’s child is anyone to whom they have contributed an inner plate, a muscle filament, or a remembered route. Most children therefore have many parents scattered across several shelters.

The young begin as loose frameworks.

Adults gather warm gray powder shed by wounds, deaths, and deliberate scrapings of their own bodies. This material is placed in a shallow mold marked with a simplified map of the local terrain. For years the powder imitates the surrounding mountains: ridges rise across it, tiny flats spread, and minute black threads appear where the model contains strain. The household corrects this imitation patiently, pinching away false peaks and inserting slivers from their own plates. Eventually the model stops representing the landscape and curls upright into a small, many-limbed person.

A new child’s first independent act is to choose which limbs to keep.

Because they contain no liquid, the Intervening eat sparingly. They cut thin wafers from living pressure-growth, favoring the dark inner pleats where accumulated strain has been converted into dense tissue. Fresh growth produces alertness and flexibility. Violet bloom induces long, intricate dreams. White lace from a dead grove has no nourishment, but chewing it helps preserve memory; elders often keep a splinter in the mouth while reciting measurements.

They also breathe the reactive powder made where newborn space grazes older stone. Their breathing apertures line the torso and open in sequence. Near rapidly widening places they must cover these vents, since an excess of fresh dust causes uncontrolled growth. Victims develop additional plates, phantom hands, or repeating sections of spine. Such changes are not always fatal. Some surveyors deliberately expose themselves in small degrees, cultivating bodies capable of spanning cracks or sensing expansion earlier than instruments can.

The Intervening build no settlements because proximity is a promise the valley refuses to honor. Instead they form **chains of regard**. Every inhabited shelter maintains sight of, or a measured route toward, at least one other. The people tend cairns between them and polish each cairn’s seeing face. So long as the sequence remains mutually visible, households consider themselves neighbors, even if newly made country has placed months of travel between their doors.

A traveler’s most important duty is not to arrive but to preserve these lines.

Before moving a stone, they stand before it and touch their forehead to its uppermost edge. The greeting identifies the traveler, names the adjacent cairns, and asks which direction the stone presently regards as up. This is not ceremony alone. Stones that have been acknowledged are less likely to reveal an unexpected side when lifted.

Their tools are simple in shape but subtle in use. Copper wedges separate rock along stresses without striking it. Black-frond cords tighten when the space they cross increases, giving warning before a doorway stretches. Resonant rods are carried in bundles tuned to different flaws in matter. Surveyors tap them against cliffs and read the answering patterns through their palms. A rod that answers before it is struck is immediately broken, and its pieces are laid facing away from one another.

They make fire only for tasks that cannot be accomplished by pressure, abrasion, or patient cleavage. The person tending it wears a mask of dead white lace and never looks directly along the flame. A fire that becomes a line is not extinguished. Stone shutters are built around it, and the place is marked as a forbidden route. Several such lines are believed still to be burning through the mountains, perhaps illuminating the impossible scenes exposed at the far rim.

Most Intervening spend their lives measuring.

They measure shelters, paths, bodies, shadows, intervals between tones in the roar, angles of the dead within groves, and the number of steps required to return from familiar places. Their records are not numerical in the usual sense. Figures are untrustworthy where counting alters what is counted. Instead, measurements are preserved as comparisons:

*The east wall has gained the width of Mother’s third wrist plate.*

*The stair is farther from the cliff than a falling stone descends during one sleep.*

*The lower path now contains enough new country for a child to become old while crossing it.*

Records are scratched into floors because walls may acquire additional surface and separate the marks. The oldest shelters have floors covered in dense crossing lines, each generation cutting its account at a different angle. When a record becomes unreadable, the household does not copy it. They lie upon the floor and allow its accumulated measurements to impress shallow patterns into their back plates. History is carried away in relief.

No profession is more honored than that of the **Returner**.

Returners travel outward from a shelter and attempt to come back by precisely the same route. Since journeys home tend to lengthen, many spend most of their lives returning from expeditions that occupied only a few days in the outward direction. They arrive generations later, bearing obsolete messages for people now embedded in groves. Their bodies are patched, elongated, and crowded with carved maps. Some have crossed so much newborn distance that they no longer fit wholly within a room: one part enters before another part has left the horizon.

Returners are welcomed without grief. The household gives them flakes of violet growth and listens to their records through the night. Afterward, they are shown the dead of everyone they once knew. Many choose to join those groves immediately. Others turn around and begin a second return toward a place that may no longer be reachable.

The Intervening do not imagine death as departure. Departure is a spatial claim, and space cannot be trusted. They call death **the giving-up of held strain**.

While alive, every body contains tensions accumulated through movement, memory, obligation, and resisted change. In death these remain trapped in the plates. A mature pressure-grove can consume them slowly. The seated dead whiten as filaments pass through them, their posture subtly changing over decades. Fingers incline toward unstable paths. Heads turn toward mountains whose lit sides are about to fail. Ribs open toward unseen hollows.

The living consult these forms as maps, but not as infallible ones. A dead person indicates what the world was preparing to do when it passed through them. The world may since have chosen otherwise.

Some figures in the oldest groves are too large to have been ordinary inhabitants. Their pale branches extend through whole colonies, with limbs appearing from separate cliff faces miles apart. The Intervening disagree about them. They may be ancestors stretched by the valley’s growth. They may be maps that mistook themselves for bodies. They may belong to inhabitants of one of the alternatives visible at the raw frontier.

There are hermits who live close to those exposed faces. Their plates often take on foreign colors: ocean green, sun-white, ocher stained with the scent of iron. They watch possibilities advance by less than a nail’s width each century and record which one seems most likely to become real. Few hermits return. Those who do sometimes carry impossible residues—a pocket of dampness, a rounded pebble, a seed with no stone in it.

The Intervening fear water more than any predator they can imagine. A substance that enters every opening, gathers in depth, softens edges, and falls without needing a path seems to them the perfect catastrophe. Yet some become obsessed with the drowned green world visible in the rim. They stand before it for years, pressing dry palms to the unyielding cross-section, trying to understand abundance that does not feed on stress.

Their oldest disagreement concerns the roar.

One tradition holds that it is indeed the sound of distance being made, and that the valley is a cradle expanding around an occupant not yet born. Every added mile is room for a limb, an organ, or a thought. When enough space exists, the unborn one will become possible.

Another teaches that the roar comes from the opposite process: elsewhere, all distances are closing. The valley receives what other worlds can no longer contain. Lost roads, abandoned gaps, the space between estranged people, the narrowing room beneath oceans—all of it enters here.

A third claims there is no valley at all. The cliffs are the inner plates of a colossal Intervening body; the pressure-forests are its thoughts feeding on strain; the inhabitants are temporary arrangements in its wound-powder. The thirteenth mountain beneath the flats is said to be a tooth still forming in its lower jaw.

Most people do not choose among these explanations. Certainty is another way of forcing things into agreement, and the mountains dislike that.

They attend instead to the practical signs.

They keep the cairns looking at one another.

They loosen cords before night.

They abandon rooms that gain a pace.

They never count all the summits in one method.

They greet a stone before asking it to move.

And when the roar develops a new high tone, they leave their shelters and press themselves flat against the nearest cliff, beside the dark pressure-groves. There they wait, plates touching stone, while minute quantities of country enter between every existing thing.

Sometimes, during these events, an Intervening person feels another presence taking shape inside the added distance: not a body, not yet, but a tendency toward one. A posture without substance. A hand implied between separated hands.

They do not reach for it.

They mark the place, strengthen the nearest cairn, and make the long journey home to record that something has begun to have room.
