The people of the high country are called fitters, though few now know what the country was intended to fit.

They are slight, deep-chested people, adapted to air whose thinness depends less on height than on the mood of the ventilation works. Their fingers are long and heavily callused at the tips. Most are born with excellent balance; those who are not seldom travel beyond the enclosed districts. They walk with one hand free, even indoors, because any room may become a ledge without visibly changing.

Their clothing is layered felt, bright woven cloth, and pieces of obsolete safety equipment. Sleeves carry ceramic inserts against hot pipes. Boots have split, magnetic soles and soft grooves that grip rivet heads. Children wear bells until they learn the crossings, though the bells often ring several seconds before the child moves. Adults remove the clappers and keep them as navigational instruments.

Every fitter carries tools, but no two kits are alike. A typical belt holds pliers, chalk, insulated thread, a mirror faced with black glass, three or four unidentified keys, and a short hammer for speaking into the structure. The oldest tools are inherited with their errors intact. Families may preserve a wrench that tightens only reflections, a level whose bubble points toward safe shelter, or a screwdriver that turns whichever screw is loosest within earshot.

Their settlements are not named by location. They are named for tolerable conditions: Warm Return, Seven Dry Steps, Quiet Under Load, the Market Where Shadows Match. A town may migrate without moving when the machine revises the function of the spaces around it. If a housing begins to vibrate, it becomes a mill. If a condenser starts producing potable water, it becomes a square. If a corridor develops weather, families fit gutters and call it a street.

Homes tend to be tall and narrow, partitioned with curtains so that no occupant can see every corner at once. Beds are slung from load-bearing members and lowered only after the evening inspection. Thresholds are marked with ash, grease, or sleeping animals. A goat inside the doorway is considered a good sign. A goat looking inward from an empty room is not.

Households gather around functional rights rather than blood alone. The people warmed by one pipe may call themselves siblings. Those who share a cistern inherit one another’s debts. Marriage commonly joins incompatible utilities: a family with reliable heat to one with clean condensation, or mirror-watchers to keepers of a stable stair. Separation is performed by uncoupling two household sounds. For this reason, divorces may require engineers.

Children are taught maintenance before letters. They learn which hums indicate load, which indicate hunger, and which are merely the dead repeating old labor. They practice crossing open frames with their eyes half closed. They are forbidden to count the cooling towers, but many invent private names for them instead.

Their first formal task is usually to sit beside a dormant machine for a full day and report everything it did not do.

Their writing comes in several forms. Ordinary messages are painted on cloth because cloth changes less readily than walls. Contracts are scratched into replaceable plates. Warnings are sung into pipes, to be carried as vibration where rain and oxidation cannot alter them. The most serious records are entrusted to rust.

Rust-readers form one of the high country’s oldest professions. They travel after wet seasons, copying the colored blooms before anyone paints them over. They do not agree whether the markings are language, diagnosis, prophecy, or the involuntary thoughts of metal. Even so, their readings are difficult to dismiss. A violet spiral may precede the opening of a new door. Three orange bars often mean pressure is migrating nearby. Turquoise branching around a house can signify either safe water or an approaching duplication; context is everything.

Rust-readers stain their teeth with iron salts so they can test a mark by taste. Many eventually speak in repetitions. Some acquire handwriting identical to the structure’s.

There are also bolt-singers, who interpret messages struck through connected plates; lightwrights, who cut and brace the solid illumination found in certain basins; and inverse surveyors, who map where a place cannot be reached from. Door shepherds tend entrances that wander between walls. They tether them with chains of labeled keys and feed oil into their hinges. When a door grows restless, the shepherd sleeps across its threshold until it settles or opens elsewhere.

The most respected workers are the finishers.

Whenever a machine begins producing something, finishers are called to witness the process and ensure that no one interferes. Most products are harmless: blank discs, left-handed bolts, warm glass eggs, lengths of perforated ribbon. Some arrive over months or generations. Families inherit unfinished productions along with instructions never to stop them.

Living products are more troublesome.

A stamping hall may press a thin animal from layered foil, after which the creature unfolds, shivers, and seeks shade. Kilns sometimes hatch red ceramic birds that remain hot throughout their lives. One ancient extrusion machine has been making the same pale limb for eighty-six years. It has reached the wrist. The finishers have built a roof over it, posted guards, and forbidden speculation about the scale of the completed body.

They are less afraid of such things than outsiders might expect. Fear in the high country is reserved for incompletion, unlabeled change, and apparent simplicity.

The people distrust anything that has only one use. They assume a plain wall conceals an obligation. They prefer objects with seams, fasteners, inspection ports, and documented failure modes. A visibly damaged bridge may remain in service for centuries because everyone understands its damage, while a flawless new stair will be avoided until an animal has used it in both directions.

Animals live closely among them, though none are considered fully domesticated.

Cliff-goats serve as guides, load-bearers, and structural diagnosticians. Their herders rarely lead them. They watch where the animals refuse to step. A goat sleeping against a support indicates warmth, stability, or a fracture too subtle for human instruments; experienced herders distinguish among these by the position of the horns.

The markings stamped into mature horns are treated as both load ratings and biographies. Some correspond to measurable weights. Others resemble dates not yet reached. Once in several generations, a kid is born bearing the name of a bridge. The bridge is then closed until the animal dies.

Insulation mice are tolerated in walls because they gnaw around overheated wires before fires can start. Bright switch-box birds are watched for changes in reality: if their delayed wingbeats arrive before they take flight, the district is evacuated. Long foxes are never fed directly, but dishes of metallic dust are left where they patrol. Their tracks show which passages remained continuous during the night.

The translucent salamanders are kept in public cisterns. Physicians study the tiny mechanisms turning inside them and compare their speed to a patient’s pulse. When a part stops, the salamander often remains alive, but somewhere nearby a pump fails.

Illness is understood as a misalignment between body and district.

Common altitude sickness is treated with pressure hoods and bitter fern broth. More serious ailments include echo fever, in which a person’s actions recur hours later without them; directional numbness, the loss of sensation on whichever side faces north; and serial rash, where rows of tiny numbers rise on the skin. Those numbers are never read aloud.

A person who has spent too long near repetitive machinery may begin duplicating habits. They set down two cups, tie each knot twice, answer a question and then answer it again in exactly the same words. Early cases are treated by surrounding the patient with irregular objects. Advanced cases require a journey south, where distinctions are already weak and the excess repetition can sometimes drain into the landscape.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere that qualifies as ground.

Bodies are wrapped in conductive cloth and placed in decommissioned cabinets, empty ducts, or unused counterweights. The family keeps one original panel accessible, just as in a living house. For several weeks they return to check for heat, vibration, corrosion, or newly issued instructions. Most bodies remain quiet.

Some become part of the weather.

Clouds have emerged from mortuary vents carrying familiar scents and fragments of speech. Rain falling over a dead person’s district may leave fingerprints on metal. Rarely, a reflection continues aging after its owner has died. Such reflections are given tools and permitted to work, though they may not inherit property.

The people worship no single maker. Their beliefs cluster around acts of upkeep.

Some say the machine is unfinished and that all life exists to help complete it. Others say it was completed long ago and that maintenance only prevents it from revealing its purpose. A severe sect holds that the high country is not a machine at all, but the scale model of one, and that every act of repair makes the unseen original change.

Pilgrims visit the Slow Works: the town-sized engine whose casing shifts like winter ice. They press their foreheads to it and wait for one of its deep contractions. If it moves while they are touching it, they consider themselves assigned. What they are assigned to do may not become clear for years.

No one prays for the machinery to notice them.

Their festivals occur when long processes finish. A conveyor completing its circuit is greeted with banners, public meals, and the reading of names belonging to everyone who tended it but did not live to see its return. When a crane finally delivers an ancient load, the nearest town assumes responsibility for whatever hangs from the hook.

Once it was a sealed room containing summer weather.

Once it was a block of ice in which lamps were burning.

Once it was an empty cradle large enough for a house.

The current load has been crossing the western valley for four generations. From below, it appears to be a dark bell, though surveyors insist it has windows.

At dusk, the fitters emerge onto balconies, ladders, and warm platework. They collect condensation, call goats through the walls, tie back the cable-vines, and cover reflective surfaces before the older images appear. Children watch the first lights come on beneath the floors. Rust-readers unfold their papers. Bolt-singers place their cheeks against railings.

Then the instructions brighten across the ranges.

Most are too large to comprehend from any one settlement. One district sees a line; another, a branching angle; another, a pulse moving downward through levels no survey has connected. Communities preserve their portion and compare it with records carried from beyond the horizon. Across centuries, a figure has begun to emerge—not a sentence, exactly, but a diagram resembling both a body and a key.

The fitters do not know whether they are meant to build it, repair it, become it, or use it to open something.

For now, they keep every panel accessible.

They let the machines finish.

And when the whole high country knocks once in the dark, they answer.
