The hollow has no single master. What lives there survives by yielding: flattening itself into cracks, fastening itself to stone, or moving so slowly that the river’s endless trembling cannot dislodge it.

Closest to the entrance lives the moss, a dark pelt drawn over the limestone’s dampest shoulder. At the lip of daylight it is green and densely tufted. Beyond the first bend it thins, blackens, and enters a kind of half-life, sustained by moisture, minute stores of light, and perhaps what washes inward from the heath. Liverwort presses flatter still, occupying old water-grooves like a residue left by vanished streams. Neither plant ventures far across bare rock. They keep to seams and hollows, growing in separate patches with long intervals of naked stone between them.

The roots come from inhabitants that never enter. Heather, coarse grass, and low shrubs stand in wind above the chamber, unaware of its shape, while their finest roots probe through the ceiling. Below, they hang pale and leafless, solitary threads or little curtains, their wet tips shining in lamplight. They lengthen into darkness in search of water. Some end above dry clay. Others reach a basin and trail upon its surface, where they nourish green films and browsing larvae. They are the cave’s suspended pasture.

Tiny gnats live near the mouth during the short summer. They gather where outside dusk meets the cave’s colder breath, rising from the moss in weak, wavering clouds. They seldom pass the bend. The deeper vibration, darkness, and scarcity hold them close to the opening, and many end in the webs stretched among the first crevices.

The spiders that spin those webs are compact, dark, and patient. Their webs are unlike the broad, delicate wheels made in still air. They are thick structures laid close to the rock, braced at many points so the tremor of the river passes through rather than tearing them loose. A spider waits with its body pressed into a sheltered notch, reading disturbances against the permanent vibration. To live here, it must distinguish a trapped gnat from the motion of the earth itself.

In the water-filled basins there are smaller lives. Green films cling to submerged mineral rims, nourished by seepage and by traces carried down from the surface. In two deep pools these films persist where no obvious daylight reaches. They may descend from growths encouraged long ago by smoke and lamplight, or they may feed on what the water brings through hidden pores. They spread slowly, vanish when a basin dries, and return as a faint stain after rain.

With prolonged wet weather, translucent crustaceans appear in the deepest pool. They are nearly colorless, small enough to hide beneath a curl of calcite, with limbs always testing the water. No one sees them arrive. They rise with the basin as if the stone itself had released them, feeding on films, root fragments, and whatever minute matter has passed through the limestone. When the water sinks, they withdraw through apertures too fine to follow. Their true country lies beyond the chamber, in the flooded grain of the rock and perhaps along quiet margins of the inaccessible river.

The cave’s strangest permanent inhabitants are the white moths.

They occupy the complete darkness beyond the last reach of dusk, resting on pale walls where their folded wings make them difficult to distinguish from flakes of calcite. Their whiteness is not bright but unfinished: bone-colored, faintly translucent, without the markings that would matter under a sun they never encounter. Their eyes are reduced, or at least of little use. Their antennae are long and continually sensitive to air, moisture, stone, and tremor.

For days or weeks a moth may not move. It holds itself flat against the limestone while the hidden river shakes through its legs. When disturbed, it drops rather than rises, flying close to the floor in uneven turns. The roar swallows every beat of its wings. Within moments it is gone—not far away, perhaps only on the opposite wall, but returned to invisibility.

Its caterpillar is better suited to the place. Pale-bodied and soft, it grazes on root tips, liverwort remnants, fungal threads, and the thin growth beneath mineral drips. It anchors itself with silk before crossing exposed stone. Vibration has shaped its habits: it feeds in the lee of ribs, retreats before pools rise, and pupates in compact cases bound to cracks. Many perish when a root dries or a seep changes course. Enough endure for the moths to remain, scattered so sparsely that the chamber may seem lifeless even when several are present.

A stoat sometimes comes in from the heath. It is not truly of the hollow. It uses the collapsed enclosure as a temporary den, slipping among stones that have settled into stable angles. Its body fits the old gaps readily, and its winter coat can resemble a moving piece of mineral in lamplight. It leaves fine prints in softened clay, a little fur on a rough edge, the musky trace of a warm-blooded animal briefly imposed upon the cold. It may hunt gnats or spiders, but there is not enough prey for it to stay. More often it has come to shelter from wind, to hide food, or to sleep where larger animals will not follow.

Bats avoid the chamber. They may pass above the crease at dusk, but they do not hang from its ceiling. Here the air is too clamorous with reflected vibration; stone and water fill the world with an unceasing false signal. The cave belongs instead to creatures that touch and cling, not those that map emptiness by listening.

And then there are the former inhabitants, who remain more strongly than any passing animal.

They were human, though the hollow has reduced them to gestures. They came sideways through the narrow entrance carrying lamps, hides, baskets, antler tools, stone hammers, cord, and perhaps water vessels. They could not speak once inside. Their community therefore became a chain of touches and lights: a hand on a shoulder, a palm raised in warning, a covered flame uncovered twice, fingers closing around another wrist in the dark.

They learned the chamber through their bodies. They knew which rib of stone led back to the entrance, which clay patch became slick after rain, which ceiling root marked the turn toward the rear. They knew how the hidden river felt in the soles of the feet and against the breastbone. Its seasonal change was not merely heard; it pressed upon them. A sharper trembling meant thaw. Dust falling from a ledge meant greater water. The filling of the enclosed basin meant leave.

Perhaps they stored food there, protected from surface weather. Perhaps they sheltered during storms or pursuit. They may have stretched hides from the wooden pegs and slept above the cold floor, though sleep beside that concussion must have been peculiar—less an escape from the sound than surrender to it. The crescent wall could have broken the draft. The circular enclosure could have kept feet and silt from a pool whose water mattered.

But the placement of their structures suggests another need. All face the sealed wall.

They sat or crouched before the stone behind which the river passed. Firelight, if there was any, would have shown only limestone: no opening, no idol, no carved face. Yet hands repeatedly touched the same surface until it became dull and smooth. Whether they believed a being moved beyond it, sought omens in changes of pressure, or simply came to feel the force hidden under their country cannot now be separated. Practical use and reverence may have been the same act. The river warned them of flood, gave water to the basins, shook their stacked stones, and spoke with a voice too immense for reply.

The hollow still contains the habits they built against it. Pegs were wedged deeply because vibration worked them loose. Bowls were buried at the base because objects wandered. Walls were made low and broad, each stone laid on its widest face. Cord was stiffened by minerals where it remained tied. Anything not secured migrated, settled, broke, or disappeared into cracks.

No people live there now in the ordinary sense. Yet their absences occupy fixed places. The overturned bowl retains the reach of the hand that shaped it. The antler pick carries a polished grip. Soot marks the height at which lamps were raised. The enclosures preserve where bodies gathered and where feet were not permitted to tread. At the rear wall, the sheen left by generations of palms remains the most vivid habitation of all.

Thus the chamber’s inhabitants are divided between those above, those within, and those beyond.

Above are the shrubs sending down blind roots.

Within are moss, liverwort, spiders, gnats, green films, rare moths, and the occasional stoat—small lives fastened to temporary pockets of damp and shelter.

Beyond are the colorless crustaceans withdrawing into flooded cracks, and the river itself, unseen but more present than anything visible.

The river may not be alive, yet everything in the hollow behaves as though sharing a den with it. Stones turn under its influence. Droplets tremble before falling. Moths cling low. Spiders brace their webs. Human walls settle toward stability. Even the basins answer its distant rising in a secret sequence.

Whatever lives there does not conquer the emptiness. It marks it lightly: a thread of silk, a wet root, a white wing, a print in clay, a polished place where a hand once rested against the wall. Then darkness and noise close over it again.
