The people of the caldera are built, culturally and physically, around patience.

They are tall more often than not, with deep chests and narrow limbs suited to thin air and vertical travel. Their movements seem deliberate to visitors, even hesitant, until it becomes clear that hesitation is a form of awareness. Before crossing a room, they notice which wall carries its weight. Before touching a rail, they test it with the back of a finger. They pause at thresholds to feel for pressure, vibration, and heat. A native child learns to read a floor before learning to read text.

Most wear layered, close-fitting garments of ceramic fiber and metallic cloth, pale on exposed bridges and dark inside the heatworks. Soles are replaceable. Gloves have conductive fingertips for testing surfaces and insulated knuckles for tapping pipes. Their belts carry water seals, contact keys, chalk, cooling patches, and small wedges used to prevent a door from changing its behavior as the structure flexes. Jewelry is seldom merely ornamental. Bracelets darken in bad air; collar pins respond to electrical charge; beads in a worker’s hair click together at particular vibration frequencies.

They sweat little and conserve what they can. Their nostrils are narrow, their eyes protected by translucent inner lids or artificial membranes, and their skin tends to be dry, scarred by heat flecks and marked with the pale lines left by mineral dust. In the eastern condensation districts they often develop chronic staining at the hands and throat from dissolved metals. In the high western towers, static burns make branching figures across exposed skin.

No one is entirely adapted. Adaptation is treated less as a finished inheritance than as a civic project. Children receive pressure training. Adults undergo periodic alterations to the lungs, blood, and inner ear. Old people carry small assistive reservoirs beneath the ribs and can often predict a pressure shift before the instruments do.

They are not hurried people.

A promise may be made for a year when a foundation cools. A marriage may wait upon the completion of a kiln cycle. A public argument can remain formally unresolved for decades while strain readings accumulate. They consider this neither indecision nor delay. To decide before the structure answers is childish.

Their institutions reflect the same discipline. The most respected citizens are not rulers in the usual sense but readers: of heat, stress, resonance, moisture, and slow displacement. Anchor surveyors carry genealogies of buildings. Reservoir custodians memorize the taste and conductivity of each chamber. Bell listeners spend entire shifts with one hand on the cliff, distinguishing machinery from weather and weather from deeper movement.

The city’s laws are maintained by councils arranged according to systems rather than territories. Heat, load, water, transit, pressure, and void each have their own keepers. No council acts alone. A new dwelling might require consent from five authorities and testimony from a family whose ancestors watched the cliff into which it will be fixed. Government is therefore cumbersome, but the inhabitants regard speed in governance as they regard speed in engineering: a desire to convert uncertainty into casualties.

Status is measured partly by custodianship. A wealthy household may possess no large rooms, but control access to a cool wall. A prestigious family may be entrusted with a cistern, an anchor cluster, or a sequence of calibrated cracks. People inherit obligations more often than objects. One does not say, “This reservoir belongs to us,” but, “Our line answers when it changes.”

Water shapes their manners. Offering a drink is intimate; offering cold water is solemn. Guests are given a damp cloth before a cup. Pouring where another person can hear but not see is an insult, suggesting concealed waste. Tears are not shameful, though they draw attention. Mourners collect them on narrow strips of absorbent ceramic and seal the strips into memorial walls.

Their dead are not buried. There is little loose earth, and bodily water is too valuable to surrender. The dead are brought to reclamation chambers where moisture is recovered and returned to the household’s allotment or to a public cistern. Mineral remains are fired into porous tiles. These are installed near anchors, vents, and reservoirs associated with the dead person’s work. Thus the ancestors literally become parts of the city, though never load-bearing ones; using the dead as structural material is forbidden.

Their language contains many terms for kinds of silence. There is the silence after machinery stops correctly, the silence of a blocked transmission, the tense silence preceding rock failure, and the broad benign quiet of mechanisms too slow to hear. The silence after two contact-bell strokes has its own word, one children are discouraged from saying casually.

The poorly explained rule belongs to an old fear.

Twice, followed by nothing, is not an alarm transmitted by any recognized civic system. It has been recorded in districts whose bells were disconnected, in abandoned platforms below habitation, and once in a sealed reservoir where no moving mechanism remained. Engineers say that paired impacts can arise from stress redistribution: one fracture, then its echo through a neighboring layer. Bell listeners say an echo does not stop clocks, extinguish static, or cause gauges to descend against an updraft.

So when the bells ring twice and fall silent, everyone becomes still. Machinery capable of stopping is stopped. Lifts lock to their rails. No door is opened, no load shifted, no water drawn. For one hour, the city tries to become indistinguishable from the cliff.

No official doctrine explains what might notice movement.

Children are given several explanations depending on their age. The youngest are told the mountain is counting. Older children are told that motion can propagate damage after a deep slip. Apprentices hear about obsolete autonomous survey systems whose signals may persist in the rock. Bell listeners, when pressed, say only that the second knock is sometimes nearer than the first.

Among the city’s few animals, the translucent gauges are welcomed but not trusted. They appear after rain as clear, pulsing sacs threaded with silver or black filaments. At rest they cling to shaded pipes and resemble beads of spilled glass. When heat rises through them, the filaments shorten asymmetrically, drawing their bodies into the updraft. They steer by changing shape rather than by beating wings.

Children follow them through vent galleries, recording their height on walls. Engineers pretend to disapprove, then consult the marks. A sudden descent of gauges can empty a pressure hall faster than an official siren. Their bodies collapse into wet mineral lace within hours of death, leaving almost no organic residue. Whether they hatch, assemble, or precipitate remains unsettled.

The mineral membranes in the condensation tunnels occupy a similarly uncertain category. Technicians scrape them away in accordance with maintenance schedules, yet many crews first copy their branching forms. Certain patterns recur before seal failures and electrical reversals. Reservoir custodians cultivate small unauthorized patches behind removable tiles, feeding them weak currents and drops of conductive water.

Some people believe these membranes are alive. Others believe life is an unnecessarily narrow explanation for their usefulness.

There are specialists called tracers who study them without official recognition. Tracers compare the membranes’ growth to maps of pipework, fault lines, kinship, and transit. They claim the branching films do not predict individual failures so much as reveal where the city has misunderstood itself. A membrane may outline a forgotten conduit, an unrecorded structural dependency, or a route by which vibration crosses apparently separate districts.

The tracers’ most troubling samples produce shapes resembling six-limbed bodies.

Out on the ash slopes, the heavy walkers remain rarely seen. A few night crews describe low, broad animals with three pairs of jointed limbs and hides like quenched iron. They move without visible haste but cross great distances between observations. Their forelimbs test the ground. Their middle limbs bear weight. Their rear limbs erase or distort much of the trail, which may explain why tracks so often end abruptly on stone.

They have been seen drinking from no reservoir, eating no mineral, and entering no known access shaft. Yet their footprints sometimes hold clear water, cool enough to condense mist at the edges.

Officially, they are legacy machines.

This explanation comforts almost no one. Old machines have serial plates, maintenance geometries, and purposes. The walkers possess none that observers can identify. Their surfaces show seams, but the seams migrate between sightings. Damaged individuals—if the accounts refer to individuals—have leaked both oil and something that clotted.

Outer-slope crews leave shallow metal basins where tracks have been found. In the morning, the basins are sometimes overturned. Once or twice a year, one returns containing a rounded piece of warm glass, polished on every side. These objects are delivered to the survey halls, where they resist cutting and emit two faint knocks when allowed to cool.

The inhabitants themselves have no agriculture. Their food is manufactured in sealed pressure vats from imported carbon, recovered salts, cultured microbes, and chemical feedstocks. The cultures never touch open air. Nothing green is permitted even in images near a working reservoir, an old superstition now preserved as hygiene regulation.

Food comes in dense sheets, gels, mineral broths, and brittle aerated wafers. Texture is prized because flavor is expensive to produce. The eastern districts are known for layered salt gels that fracture differently according to temperature. The southern terraces make copper-colored protein threads smoked in ozone chambers. On ceremonial occasions, families serve “rain”: a clear broth cooled until condensation forms on the outside of its vessel. Guests drink the droplets first.

Children sometimes draw imaginary plants. Having never seen one, they give them the wrong logic: roots fastened to ceilings, leaves shaped like condenser fins, trunks divided into expansion joints. In their stories, forests migrate to escape hot ground and ring bells through their branches.

The city’s domestic life is compressed but not joyless. Homes occupy chambers fitted into whatever geometry the surrounding systems allow. A family may sleep along a curved refinery wall or eat in a room crossed by a sealed freight conduit. Furniture hooks into structural tracks and is moved as heat shifts through the year. Privacy is made with hanging insulation, vibration curtains, and zones of agreed silence rather than with solid walls.

People know their neighbors vertically. The household above may regulate one’s shade; the household below may hear one’s water line. Disputes concern vibration, heat leakage, anchor privilege, and unauthorized cooling. Affection is often expressed through maintenance. To descale someone’s condenser, replace their boot soles, or sit beside a suspicious crack through the night is more intimate than elaborate speech.

Music is conducted through matter. Instruments are pressed to walls, pipes, rails, and tension cables. A performance may be nearly inaudible in the chamber where it begins but emerge several districts away as a trembling handrail or a chord in a reservoir skin. Composers work with transmission delays measured in minutes or hours. Some pieces are written for buildings that are slowly moving out of tune and can be performed only once every few decades.

Their art favors evidence. Murals are made from heat-sensitive glazes that change with the wall. Public sculptures double as plumb lines or pressure registers. Personal portraits often depict not faces but systems of attachment: the rooms a person maintained, the routes they traveled, the pipes they repaired, the anchors for which they answered.

Still, not everything useful is admitted to be art, and not everything beautiful is safe.

Young people climb unfinished frameworks to watch lightning form around the aerials. They paint obsolete warning marks in inaccessible places. They race freight carriages whose official speeds are nearly walking pace, winning by choosing routes with favorable gradients rather than by making the machines faster. Some collect fragments of mineral membrane and wear them sealed beneath glass. Others descend illegally toward the basin to stand where the city can no longer be felt through their boots.

Those who return speak of the lower dark as if it were inhabited—not by creatures, necessarily, but by a vast process with attention-like rhythms. They describe level plains flexing upward, red lines closing when approached, and distant arrangements of lights that mirror the city’s districts without reproducing them exactly.

The oldest inhabitants rarely descend and seldom look directly into the basin. They watch walls, cables, gauges, and water instead. They understand that survival here depends on attention directed toward small, interpretable things.

Yet beneath every custom lies an unspoken possibility: that the city is not merely fastened to a young landscape, but has become one of the processes by which that landscape develops. Its pipes redistribute heat. Its reservoirs move water through the rock. Its anchors redirect strain. Its mineral films propagate electrical order. Its dead become ceramic surfaces on which condensation gathers.

Generation by generation, the city alters the caldera, and the caldera alters whoever lives there.

Perhaps this is why the citizens resist a strict distinction between organism and machine. They know structures that breathe, animals that may be instruments, instruments that appear to anticipate harm, and stone that changes too slowly to seem alive only because a human lifetime is brief.

When asked what lives in the caldera, they do not usually name themselves first.

They gesture to the inhabited wall: to the warm buildings braced against one another, the patient engines, the silver cistern roofs, the branching membranes, the gauges rising over vents, the unaccountable tracks on ash, and the bright diagnostic lines crossing stone.

“The city,” they say.

Then, after considering the question more carefully:

“For now, we live in it too.”
