They live in the masonry.

At first they are indistinguishable from its ordinary discolorations: pale seams in black pavement, branching stains beneath mineral glaze, small arrangements of silt caught where no current should have placed them. A traveler may cross an inhabited district and notice only that the joints there are unusually clean, or that the wall-plants grow in deliberate-looking fans.

Nothing moves quickly enough to betray itself.

Each inhabitant is a colony rather than a single body. Its greater part consists of fine translucent filaments threaded through mortar, sediment, dead roots, and porous clay. These filaments may occupy an entire chamber while remaining thinner than hair. They enter cracks too narrow for a frond, pass through old ceramic fill, and spread in flat branching sheets between courses of stone. Where two sheets meet, they fuse. Where conditions differ too sharply, they withdraw and seal themselves behind a film of glassy tissue.

Their visible organs are small.

Along walls they raise blunt, finger-length nodules resembling deposits of lime. On ceilings they form pendant cups that collect descending seepage one drop at a time. Across pavements they produce low radial rosettes, colorless except at their edges, where a dim green-gray line appears and fades over periods of months. These organs open, close, divide, and are abandoned according to changes in pressure and dissolved minerals. An observer returning after ten years might find that a cup has turned slightly toward a conduit, or that a row of nodules has become a continuous lip.

They have no mouths, limbs, eyes, or centralized organs. They do not swim. They do not graze the vegetation, though they live intimately among its roots and dead fibers. Their filaments separate useful salts from seepage and encourage certain pale plants to grow over them. In return, the plants trap sediment, moderate the water’s chemistry, and provide slow accumulations of carbon when old growth collapses. What looks like a meadow may therefore be the upper surface of a much larger resident spread through the plaza beneath it.

It is difficult to say where one ends and another begins.

In younger districts, colonies remain local: one around a sealed well, another beneath a bridge footing, a third occupying the sediment in a roofless court. In older districts, their networks meet through conduits and cracked foundations. They exchange living strands, mineral packets, and slow chemical pulses. Distinctions persist, but not as boundaries. They are more like differences of pressure within the same body.

They sense the world through strain.

A settling stone compresses their filaments. A distant gate changes the pressure in channels too small for visible water to enter. A new current alters temperature first along one wall, then another. These changes pass through a colony as waves of contraction and release. Their response may take seasons. Filaments thicken beneath a weakened joint. A plant bed is starved on one side and fed on the other. Sediment is bound into a dense felt where a slab has begun to tilt. Mineral secretions accumulate around a crack until it becomes a pale scar.

Thus they maintain the drowned construction, though not from reverence and perhaps not from understanding. Stable masonry gives them stable gradients: cold against warm, clay against glass, stagnant water against seepage. Collapse destroys these arrangements. They preserve arches for the same reason a rooted thing preserves the bank in which it grows.

Many of the world’s apparent repairs are theirs.

The white bands around columns are not original clamps. The smooth plugs sealing minor conduits were secreted grain by grain. Curtains of mineral-stiffened vegetation often conceal filament lattices bearing part of their weight. In places where roof stones have separated, colonies have drawn dead fronds into the opening and petrified them into a tough composite. Given centuries, they can close a breach broad enough for a person to pass through. Given millennia, they can redirect seepage through entire complexes.

They cannot lift blocks, operate levers, or reason in the manner of the builders. Yet they have learned the old mechanisms through repeated consequence. Colonies gather around counterweight wells because such wells create dependable pressure cycles. They cultivate dense mats near float chambers, adding or shedding growth until the balance changes. Some gates that appear to be acting from their makers’ ancient design are now being influenced by life spread through the housings.

Whether this influence is intentional depends on the scale at which intention is sought.

No individual filament anticipates a gate’s movement. But a network that has experienced the same forty-year pressure cycle hundreds of times begins to alter in advance of it. Cups close before cold water arrives. Roots loosen from channels that will soon reverse. Mineral tissue thickens around stones that will bear the changed load. The response passes from old growth to new through preserved layers, so that structures laid down centuries apart participate in one pattern.

Their memory is architectural.

They store it not in symbols but in hardened forms: rings around wells, laminated films inside pipes, branching ridges beneath sediment. Each former current leaves behind a layer that biases later growth. Each collapse becomes a knot of mineralized tissue from which new filaments radiate. To dissect an old colony would be to find a history of the district expressed as thickness, orientation, porosity, and salt.

Some histories are older than the present arrangement of basins.

Deep in buried galleries, filaments surround hearths and dry stairways. They pass through soot, suggesting ancestors that lived when air remained above the lower floods. Those earlier forms may have grown as damp films in cisterns, gutters, and cellar walls, retreating upward as water rose. Unlike the builders, they were not driven out. Every inundation enlarged their possible territory. The drowning that ended one world connected another.

There may once have been many kinds. Now the differences are subtle: colonies that favor copper salts and darken around ancient cramps; clear, brittle colonies of the cold northern galleries; broad soft networks under the western weed fields; deep forms that grow inside compressed ceramic and rarely produce visible organs. Where they meet, they sometimes merge. Elsewhere they form borders meters wide, marked by bare masonry on which neither will grow.

Their reproduction is almost impossible to witness. At intervals measured in centuries, a mature network develops sealed beads within the hollow stems of certain plants. When a pressure change tears those stems loose, they drift—not as animals do, directing themselves, but as scraps carried through the old hydrology. Most lodge in dead chambers and remain inert. A few enter fresh cracks rich in clay and metal. There the bead opens into a colorless stain. Generations later, the stain becomes a resident of halls.

They are not alone because they count one another. They are not social because there are no meetings, no signals separated cleanly from metabolism, no urgent exchange. Yet information moves among them. A pressure disturbance in one basin may produce altered growth far away after years of transmission through root beds and seepage networks. What one district has endured, another may slowly become prepared to endure.

To a faster creature, their lives would appear geological. To them, perhaps, the gate’s forty-year descent is a gesture, a roof collapse a sudden wound, and the migration of a cold current a passing season.

The oldest colonies may be continental. Their filaments have been found—or could be found, if anything remained to look—in the mortar beneath widely separated landmarks: the Field of Leaning Piers, the Hundred Sealed Wells, the Green Ceiling. Similar branching scars recur in each place, aligned not with the present currents but with some older circulation through buried levels. It is possible that these are separate growths repeating a successful form.

It is also possible that much of the constructed world contains one life.

If so, it has no chamber from which it rules itself. No core waits below the pavements. Its being is distributed across fractures, conduits, root mats, sealed cisterns, and the load-bearing seams of countless roofs. Parts of it die continuously and remain in place as structure. Other parts wake when water reaches them after ten thousand years. It cannot know the whole expanse at once. Its unity, if unity exists, is maintained by very slow consequences.

The makers may have encountered its ancestors as slime in their drains. They may have scraped it from tiles, burned it from reservoirs, packed joints against it. Near the upper drowned levels, shallow hollows in the walls resemble repeated hand marks around pale mineral patches. In some chambers, copper sheets were hammered over cracks where the filaments later emerged around every edge.

Elsewhere the relationship seems less hostile. Clay vessels were built into walls with perforated sides, each filled with layered salts and fibrous material. Channels guide seepage through them. The descendants grow densely there. Perhaps the builders cultivated them as sealant, filter, warning system, or food for some vanished domestic use. Perhaps, during the long flooding, people learned that inhabited walls endured where empty ones failed.

No people remain to continue that partnership. No animals remain to disturb it. The colonies have inherited all maintenance without inheriting its purpose.

In the quietest districts they lie nearly dormant, their surfaces furred with unbitten plants. Silt accumulates over them in upright ridges. Chemical pulses take decades to cross a plaza. A century may pass with no visible change except the paling of one rosette.

Then a counterweight settles by the width of a finger.

The groan enters the wall before it enters the open water. Compression travels through columns, pavement, buried domes, and the fine living threads between them. Across a region too broad to see, cups constrict. Root mats loosen. Pale seams brighten with a radiance weaker than the mineral tiles and lasting only a few years.

Long before the new current arrives, those who live there have begun to turn toward it.
