What lives here is not an animal but a patience.

It inhabits the limestone as damp inhabits porous stone: without boundary, without visible source, known only by the darkening it leaves behind. It has no den beyond the whole basin, though it gathers most strongly in the western recess, where the ceiling rises and the mineral stains descend like the faded ribs of a curtain. The flat stone there may be its seat, its altar, or merely the place where the idea of it becomes easiest to hold.

If given a body, it would be low and broad, shaped by the hollow rather than placed within it. Its back would have the rounded articulation of shelves worn smooth by rain. Its skin would be warm gray, stippled with shallow pits, with darker seams wherever moisture traveled. At rest it might be mistaken for the basin floor; standing, if it ever stood, it would rise no higher than the contorted trees along the rim.

But height does not suit it. It keeps close to the ground.

Its limbs are the narrow channels leading toward the pools. Its fingers end beneath the water among flat stones and softened leaves. Coarse sedges grow at the joints, and the small ferns under the northern shelf are something like its slow, folded breath. Moss dulls the exposed portions of it. Clay gathers in its creases. Nothing covers it completely, because concealment here is not a matter of hiding. It is achieved through perfect agreement.

It does not eat. The absence of birds, insects, and tracks is not the result of hunger. No bones lie in the recess because it has never needed to kill. Perhaps animals fail to notice the basin; perhaps, at its rim, they recognize a place already wholly occupied and turn aside without fear. The presence within does not forbid them. It simply leaves no unclaimed interval into which another life might enter.

Long ago, people may have known it differently. The six blocks could have supported a wall built in acknowledgment of it. The three slabs might have formed an approach to the western recess. Yet whatever rites occurred here required little: no fire, no offerings durable enough to survive, no image cut into stone. Perhaps people came only to sit on the flat rock and wait until their breathing matched the nearly inaudible movement of water through limestone.

Or perhaps no one ever came. The blocks may only resemble masonry, the slabs only steps, the recess only a room. This ambiguity is part of what lives here. It loosens distinctions without erasing them: natural and made, occupied and empty, enduring and finished. Under its influence, a wall becomes an outcrop, an outcrop almost remembers being a wall, and both settle into the same weather.

Its awareness, if it has one, is not wakefulness. It knows pressure, warmth, and recurrence. It knows rain arriving by the old seams. It knows each pool’s slight expansion and retreat. It knows where a falling bead will strike and the single note the water will make, not by prediction but because the event has happened so often that occurrence and memory are indistinguishable.

Time does not pass through it as time passes through creatures. It has no anticipation and no decay it would recognize as loss. What changes here changes within a narrow allowance: a finger’s width of water, a leaf softening into silt, a fringe of moss brightening and dimming. These are not developments but motions of equilibrium, like the shallow breathing of something that has slept so long that sleep has become its form of being.

One might call it a guardian, though it guards nothing. One might call it a spirit, though it does not appear or answer. It is nearer to a habit the earth has acquired in this one limestone hollow: the habit of remaining, of receiving only what can be absorbed, of bringing every intrusion slowly into accord.

Its only utterance is the drop striking the pool.

Its only sign is that nothing here seems absent.

Everything that could leave has left. Everything that could arrive has chosen another way. What remains—the water, stone, sedge, moss, clay, pale trees, ambiguous ruins, and close warm air—is its body, complete and untroubled.
