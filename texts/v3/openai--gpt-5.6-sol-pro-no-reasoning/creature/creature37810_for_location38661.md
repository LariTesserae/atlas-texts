The people of the Scatter are long-limbed, broad-footed, and pale—not colorless, but carrying the subdued colors of things repeatedly washed: clay-white, limestone cream, faint gray, and the muted rose of shell interiors. Their skin is thickened at the knees, palms, and soles, where generations of kneeling on stone, hauling sledges, and wading through mineral water have left smooth protective pads. Fine pale hair lies close to the body beneath layers of hide. Their eyes are narrow and deeply hooded, with silvery inner lids that draw sideways against glare, dust, and sudden rain.

They are neither blind nor creatures of darkness. Brightness is their weather.

They read its changes with extraordinary precision. A blue cast along a distant wall tells them that condensation is gathering there. A dimness underfoot may mean grazing animals have passed, the luminous film is failing, or water has begun moving beneath the floor. Children learn these distinctions before they learn property marks. Adults can identify a district by the quality of its reflected light and recognize kin at great distances by silhouette, gait, and the pattern of shadows around their feet.

Their ears are small and close-folded, protected from water and deceptive echoes. Hearing is valued, but never trusted alone. They speak softly and near one another, often touching brow, shoulder, or wrist to establish who is being addressed. Their language is full of spatial qualifications: *beside me*, *within sight*, *before the next wetness*, *where the wall was*. Names change according to circumstance. A person may have a household name, a work name, a name used during floods, and an old name retained only by those who knew them before their house last moved.

At a distance they communicate with their whole bodies. One raised forearm can halt a work crew. Fingers spread against the chest announce unstable footing. A flat hand rotated slowly means that the water has reversed. During rain, shutters on fat lamps send coded flashes through the mist, while high poles carry ceramic panels painted with bars, chevrons, circles, and divided squares. The panels indicate not only commands but conditions: safe passage, contested water, a wall awaiting dismantling, animals loose, an untested opening.

Loudness among them suggests either childhood, panic, or calculated aggression. A person who shouts without necessity is regarded much as a surface people might regard someone brandishing a weapon in a crowded room.

Their clothes are assembled rather than woven. Thin hides overlap like roof scales and are stitched with gut through holes punched by bone awls. White mineral paint keeps them visible in dim districts and prevents the leather from drinking too much moisture. Work garments are short, jointed, and easily removed when soaked. Ceremonial dress hangs in layered panels incised with maps of former streets, inherited cistern rights, and the migration histories of houses. Copper dust may be rubbed into a line to mark an office held during rain. Blue-gray lead is used for mourning. Bright metal is never worn casually; even a bead may represent generations of salvage.

They carry few permanent possessions. Most own a knife of chipped stone, several marked pebbles, cord, a drinking bladder, a hood for sudden condensation, and a personal tally made from notched bone or pierced ceramic. The wealthy are not burdened with objects. They possess reliable containers, obligations from skilled workers, access to clean cisterns, and space around their households. Their compounds seem almost austere: a handful of low buildings standing within a broad interval of swept ground.

That emptiness is guarded carefully. Trespass is not merely entering a house. It may mean building too near another family’s pause, diverting water across their interval, tethering animals where a wall might someday need to fall, or leaving heavy materials upon ground whose pressure they have the right to keep light. Property law therefore concerns possibilities more than fixed boundaries. A household can own the tendency of runoff, the likely future position of a channel, or first claim to slabs if an adjoining structure must be unbuilt.

Their families are likewise less rigid than their architecture suggests. A household is whoever maintains the same stones, cistern obligations, animals, and threshold bowl through successive moves. Birth kin matter, but so do work kin, rain kin, and those adopted during a relocation. A person may leave one household and enter another by carrying a numbered wall stone to its next site. Children often belong for a time to whole courts, circulating between sleepers, animal keepers, and craftspeople according to the work of the season.

They do not ask a child what they wish to become. They watch what the child notices.

One who detects dampness early may join a water crew. One who remembers routes despite shifting landmarks becomes a surveyor. A child who handles the grazing animals without startling them is given pen duties. Those who habitually take objects apart, sort the pieces, and reconstruct them elsewhere are apprenticed to unbuilders—the most honored and exacting of professions.

Unbuilders tend to be patient, thick-fingered people with scarred knuckles and exceptional sensitivity to vibration. They tap stone with small mauls of differing weights, then rest bare fingertips or the side of the jaw against a wall to feel the returning tremor. They smell mortar, taste runoff, inspect roof scales for minute changes of angle, and observe how dust has settled in joints. Their judgments can empty a whole court within an hour.

They do not speak of destruction. A failing structure is said to be *ready to travel*.

Before dismantling, they mark each stone with soot, clay color, cuts, or impressed tokens. Roof scales come down first, passed hand to hand in silence. Then ribs, lintels, and wall courses are removed in a sequence intended to release pressure gently. The last standing stone is rarely the oldest or largest. It is the one judged to contain the structure’s remaining balance. This is lifted ceremonially and carried by members of the household to wherever the house will pause next.

Some houses have migrated for centuries. Their stones no longer fit as they originally did. Each rebuilding introduces wedges, substitutions, reversed slabs, or altered proportions, so that a house becomes a physical genealogy of decisions. Families can point to a crooked doorway and name the flood that caused it, the unbuilder who preserved it, and the marriage for which it was widened.

Water crews form the city’s temporary government. In dry periods they inspect sluices, dredge channels, seal storage vessels, and argue over forecasts. When condensation begins, their authority becomes immediate. They can open private courts, requisition labor, dismantle walls, redirect animals, and suspend ordinary claims to space. Their commands are accepted because everyone understands that the city’s shape must briefly become communal.

Once the channels empty, that power dissolves. A chief diverter may remove their geometric command panel, wash the copper marks from their arms, and return to scraping luminous film or firing bowls. Someone who attempts to preserve wet-season authority in dry weather is mocked as a person “still standing in vanished water.”

The custodians of brightness hold a subtler office. They monitor the living mineral film, regulate grazing, and determine which radiant surfaces must remain untouched. Some are natural philosophers; others are effectively physicians, because health, food, and light all depend upon the same microscopic growth. They know the sequence of colors that appears after condensation, the smell of a poisoned bloom, and the dark traces left by sick herds. They grind samples between ceramic plates and classify them by sheen.

To the people of the Scatter, the glowing stone is not sacred in itself. It is closer to a vast, unreliable commons—part pasture, part lamp, part calendar. Yet old customs surround it. No one willingly spills blood on a newly brightened floor. The dead are not laid upon active radiance. Children are told not to scrape their names into living light, because the ground already carries too many older inscriptions.

Food comes largely from animals and the organisms those animals concentrate. The long-legged stone grazers provide meat, hide, fat, sinew, bone, and milk thick with mineral salts. They are elegant, cautious creatures with split, rasping mouthparts and narrow silver eyes. Their keepers guide them with reflective paddles rather than calls. Herds cannot be confined too densely; they exhaust the luminous film and sicken if made to cross their own dark feeding traces too often.

Cistern filterers are harvested in soft translucent folds and eaten fresh, smoked over low fat flames, or pressed into savory cakes. Inflating drifters are caught on suspended thread frames during wet periods. Their membranes are dried into fine, nearly weightless sheets used for lamp shutters and waterproof packets. Jointed wall runners are trapped for their oil. Flat scavengers are tolerated so long as they remain below a certain number; they clean waste from workshops, but when they overbreed they consume glue, bindings, and the softened edges of structural bone.

Children delight in them. Builders do not.

The sail-backed animals of the ceiling occupy another category altogether. No household owns them, no crew directs them, and no reliable method of hunting them exists. Their descents interrupt work throughout the city. People step out from courts and channels to watch immense pale bodies sink through luminous mist, their ribbed sails adjusting without apparent effort. Some pass so low that the wind of them stirs clothing and standing water. Their shadows—one of the few strong shadows in the cavern—move over entire districts.

Old people claim each sail-bearer follows a route inherited from the shape of the ceiling before it was worn smooth. Surveyors record their descents because they sometimes anticipate changes in groundwater. Priests interpret scars and membrane folds. Children simply run beneath them until adults pull them back from unstable streets.

No one agrees whether the animals are intelligent. They never answer signals, but they sometimes alter course above dismantling sites and have been seen circling places where ancient foundations emerge after floods.

Corridor beasts inspire less ambiguity.

They are slow, muscular ambush predators covered in mineralized plates that resemble fitted masonry. Their limbs fold beneath them; their mouths open along what appears to be a harmless seam. Some mimic low arches, others collapsed walls or convenient stepping stones. Young ones have been found imitating door jambs near abandoned roads, suggesting that they learn the forms of human settlement.

Every threshold custom in the Scatter descends from fear of them.

A visitor selects a marked pebble from the bowl outside a house, shows it to the resident, and tosses it through the doorway. Both watch for thirty counts. Only when the pebble remains undisturbed does the visitor enter. Intimates may count together with hand taps. Lovers sometimes carry one another’s threshold stones. Children receive their first personally marked pebble when they are old enough to walk unaccompanied.

A pebble that vanishes is not pursued.

The ritual has expanded far beyond practical use. Contracts are witnessed by passing a marked stone across a threshold. Newborns are touched with a household pebble. The dead have their marks ground from the communal stones so that no one accidentally calls them home. To accuse someone of entering without testing is to accuse them not only of recklessness but of treating every person behind the door as equally disposable.

Their religion, where it can be separated from engineering and law, is concerned with pressure, passage, and what lies below.

They do not imagine creation as a building. To build the world would imply it had once been finished. Instead, they say existence was relieved from compression: a space opened, water found it, and living things learned to pause inside. Their oldest divinities are verbs poorly translated as *to loosen*, *to bear*, *to seep*, *to make room*, and *to remain apart without breaking relation*.

Shrines are low arrangements of stones with deliberate gaps between them. Offerings consist of clean water poured where it will choose its own route, numbered fragments from houses that could not be rebuilt, or bowls cracked so neatly that both halves remain useful. There are no towering temples. Height belongs to the cavern, and joining too much stone in one place is considered theologically suspect as well as structurally foolish.

The priests who attend the ancient foundations are often surveyors. They measure impossible angles, compare maps after floods, and preserve contradictory records rather than correcting them. Their archives are sets of ceramic tablets stored in separate chambers so that no single collapse can erase a district’s history. Each tablet records relations: the number of paces between a hearth and a line of buried stone, the direction water turned around a vanished plaza, the order in which bowl towers bowed.

The priests say the buried cities are not dead. Death would be simpler. They are continuing to arrange themselves without regard for those above.

Surveyors are more cautious, but many privately believe something similar. They drive paired markers into the floor, stretch knotted cords between them, and return after every rain. When both markers have moved while the distance between them remains constant, they speak of rafts. When the distance changes, they speak of settling. When a line reappears where no continuous movement can account for it, they close their notebooks and request witnesses.

There are districts where the ancient geometry affects people’s sleep. Residents report dreams of corridors too broad to cross and doors laid flat beneath their beds. Animals refuse certain patches of ground. Water vanishes into seams and emerges days later carrying white grit, fragments of unfamiliar ceramic, or occasionally worked objects whose purpose cannot be guessed. Such finds are never kept in private homes. They are placed in isolated courts, each surrounded by a generous interval of empty pavement.

The bowl towers beyond the city are watched by small rotating communities of custodians. These people live in movable hide shelters and speak even less than citizens of the central districts. They record the towers’ slow turns with charcoal arcs on the ground. Condensation makes the sealed bowls grind against one another, producing vibrations too low to hear but perceptible through bare feet.

When thousands bow in sequence, the custodians lie flat to feel the wave pass.

They never follow its direction. Their duty is to return with the record, not the answer.

Death in the Scatter is another form of unbuilding. The body is washed with the household’s oldest stored water, then separated according to use and affinity. Hide may be rendered; bone may become tools, roof ribs, counters, or memorial pieces; mineral-rich remains are placed in inactive cisterns where filterers consume what can return safely to the city. This is not considered desecration. To trap a body intact beneath stone would be cruel—a forced permanence denied even to houses.

What remains personal is usually a small inner-ear bone or tooth marked with the deceased’s sign. It travels with the household until the mark becomes unreadable. Then it is carried beyond the inhabited districts and left inside a roofless doorway.

Their art favors arrangement over representation. They compose fields of pale stones that change meaning when wet, ceramic panels whose designs align only from particular streets, and performances in which groups cross an empty plaza along remembered routes of vanished walls. Their songs are quiet, built from breath, tongue clicks, palm taps, and tones hummed into clay vessels. Because echoes cannot be trusted, a song is considered complete only among those close enough to see one another breathing.

They possess images of the legendary surface plant: a vertical trunk dividing again and again into thinner arms. No two versions agree. Some resemble antlers planted in stone. Some look like river systems standing upright. Some are clearly derived from mineral veins or the branching channels of a flood. Scholars debate whether such beings were animals, architecture, or a metaphor for kinship.

The most unsettling versions show countless thin structures joined continuously overhead.

Many viewers find them oppressive.

The people of the Scatter are cautious, but not timid. Their civilization rests on acts of repeated courage: entering channels as water rises, dismantling a family home before certainty arrives, crossing luminous emptiness alone, trusting a map known to be temporary, accepting that authority must end when its circumstance does. They are suspicious of anything that claims to last unchanged—walls, rulers, boundaries, even explanations.

Their deepest moral failure is not destruction but refusal to yield. A person who lets a wall collapse because they cannot bear to move it has endangered everyone nearby. One who hoards water while a public channel runs dry has mistaken containment for possession. One who insists an old road remains where it was, despite evidence beneath their feet, is said to be “arguing with the floor.”

Their virtues are spacing, attentiveness, graceful relinquishment, and readiness to rebuild without pretending the rebuilt thing is new.

So they live widely scattered beneath the radiant stone: pale figures in white hides, surrounded by dismantled walls and patient animals, their civilization forever loosening itself before the cavern can do so violently. During dry intervals they move almost solitarily through vast squares, tending lamps that are scarcely brighter than the walls and speaking close enough to feel one another’s breath.

Then moisture gathers.

Silver lids blink across the city. Hands test the air. Grazers lift their rasping mouths from the luminous floor. Inflating creatures loosen themselves into the thickening currents. Far above, sails detach from the ceiling. Water crews raise their marked poles, and temporary authority passes through the Scatter faster than any shouted word.

Threshold pebbles are thrown. Roof scales come down. Cistern gates open.

Beneath everyone’s feet, the ancient lines prepare another imperceptible revision.

The people do not ask the ground to remain faithful. They ask only that it give them time to notice.

Then, with cords, bowls, sledges, animals, numbered stones, and thousands of quiet hands, they begin moving their city out of the way.
