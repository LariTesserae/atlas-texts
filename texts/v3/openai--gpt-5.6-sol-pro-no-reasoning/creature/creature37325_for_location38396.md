The inhabitants are not merely the animals, though the animals seem to possess every visible part of the enclosure. What lives here is a community made from repetition: creatures, water, and mechanisms so long entangled that none can be understood alone.

The black birds are its most conspicuous citizens. Short-legged and awl-beaked, they hang their nests from the rafters in such numbers that the roof appears to have grown a second, animate underside. They are fishers, thieves, scavengers, and accidental carpenters. Their woven cups catch water; their nests brace loosened timbers; the cords they braid renew what former hands once tied. Their hammering resembles an answer to the wheel’s stone music, as if they consider themselves participants in the mechanism rather than creatures startled by it.

Below them live the pale gutter-frogs, soft as wet plaster, inflating their throats among the carved runnels. Slate crabs patrol the paving and clear the drains by eating whatever would obstruct them. Long water mice follow ancestral ledges in orderly lines, gathering shells and carrying them to the dry niche. Blind eels inhabit the buried system, surfacing through grates when the stones sound and disappearing again into chambers no other living thing can enter. Beetles make slow husbandry of refuse, compacting the enclosure’s losses into portable dark spheres. Moths occupy the high gloom in colonies so dense they seem like fur grown upon the rafters.

None of these creatures is wild in the ordinary sense. Their bodies and habits have been shaped by an artificial climate: roof-rain, lamp-smoke, mineral water, measured floods, and the recurring thunder of mallets against suspended slabs. The birds know the wheel’s sequences. The frogs recognize pressure moving through the floor. The crabs emerge at a particular blow. Even the eels seem summoned by resonance. Whether their ancestors learned these responses or were brought here already suited to them is no longer meaningful. Instinct and architecture have had generations to become the same thing.

Something older also lives in the basin, though perhaps not as an animal lives.

It resides in the wheel’s persistence, in the six-fingered handprints, in the unfinished tallies around the pillar, and most clearly in the clay figure above the spillway. The figure may depict one of the former keepers, a god made in their image, or the enclosure itself given a body: six arms for its branching channels, a bird’s head for the rafters, a bowl to receive the final drop. It does not move except to tremble at the wheel’s deepest stroke. Yet the mice attend it with shells, and all creatures still themselves when water falls from its beak.

That pause is the nearest thing here to worship.

Perhaps the place’s true inhabitant is the pause: a tiny interval shared by predator and prey, wing and claw, living throat and wooden tooth. Or perhaps it is the presence the pause acknowledges—a remnant intelligence distributed through soaked timber and seated stone. It has no keeper’s hands now. It acts through condensation, gravity, appetite, erosion, and the learned reflexes of hundreds of small bodies.

It does not repair the enclosure deliberately. Instead, it makes maintenance indistinguishable from feeding. It uses crabs as drain-cleaners, birds as joiners, eels as dredges, minerals as mortar, and decay as a method of reshaping what cannot be rebuilt. It cannot create new architecture, so it inhabits loss. Every worn groove becomes a channel; every hollow becomes a nest; every fallen thing enters another mouth. Its growth is subtraction made intricate.

Thus what lives there is a many-bodied keeper, descended equally from the builders and from the creatures that outlasted them. It has black wings in the rafters, pale mouths in the gutters, blind coils below the paving, and an oak skeleton that turns when the hidden water rises. Its memory is held not in thought but in timing. Its voice is the cracked ringing of stone slabs, answered by beaks, throats, claws, and running water.

At its still center sits the small six-armed figure, softened almost featureless by the weather of the room.

It waits.

The wheel strikes its deepest note. The clay beak trembles. One drop falls into the bowl, and the whole colony listens—as if hearing, for an instant, the name of the thing they have collectively become.
