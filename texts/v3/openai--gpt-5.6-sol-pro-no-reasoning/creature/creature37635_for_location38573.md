The people who live here are neither resigned to instability nor convinced they can defeat it. They regard permanence as a specialized condition, expensive to maintain and faintly suspicious. A house that has not shifted in twenty years draws inspectors. A street that remains level too long acquires rumors. Stability is useful, certainly, but only in the way a locked joint is useful: for particular loads, for a limited interval, under observation.

Most inhabitants develop a characteristic stance—knees loose, weight carried slightly forward, one hand often near a railing, counter, or strap. Even on firm ground they seem prepared for the floor to reconsider itself. Visitors mistake this for fatigue or timidity. It is neither. Children learn it before they learn to read. They can run across rippling plaza plates without spilling soup and sleep through the slow inclination of a room, waking only when a familiar creak changes pitch.

Their clothing is layered, close-buttoned, and full of narrow pockets. Wool, waxed cloth, rubberized canvas, felt, and soft leather predominate. Hard-soled shoes are disliked because they obscure small vibrations. Work boots have thin pressure membranes under the arch and colored stitching that shows when the sole has twisted beyond tolerance. Nearly everyone carries chalk, cord, a folding rule, and at least one wedge. These are not badges of profession. They are ordinary necessities, like keys.

There are more keys than locks, however. Many open valves, inspection plates, communal drying cabinets, lift points, refuge cupboards, roof bridges, or the little street boxes containing braces and warning flags. Children exchange obsolete service keys as charms. Old people wear rings of them beneath their coats, identifying vanished doors by touch.

Faces tend to be pale from the overcast and flushed at the nose and cheekbones by cold. Hair is kept short or tightly bound in industrial districts, though greenhouse workers often grow theirs long, claiming it detects drafts before skin does. Fingernails are dark with graphite, clay, moss stain, or machine grease. Even clerks have the hands of people who expect at some point to move a cabinet, tighten a cable, or help lift a wall.

They speak quietly. This is partly custom and partly civic discipline, but over generations it has shaped the language itself. Meaning is carried through timing, gaze, and small motions of the fingers. A pause may indicate doubt, danger below, disagreement with a superior, or simply that a tram is passing underfoot. Public arguments can be fierce while barely rising above conversation. The gravest insult is not loudness but carelessness: to stamp, slam, hammer without testing, or shout where a subtler warning would suffice.

Names are often provisional. A child may receive a household name at birth, a street name upon beginning school, and a work name after apprenticeship. Some collect further names when their district is renumbered or their building migrates across a ward boundary. Official records accommodate this with expandable forms. A person is identified not only by parentage and date but by a sequence of locations: born above the former blue conduit, raised at Nine Steps, registered when the north room descended, presently attached to Willow Span Four.

Households are similarly flexible. A dwelling is less a private enclosure than a knot of obligations extending through adjacent walls, shared boilers, braces, gardens, and escape routes. The people next door may have legal access to your kitchen because their safest stair emerges behind your stove. Your bedroom ceiling may support another family’s greenhouse. A retaining cable may cross six properties, making every occupant responsible for its tension. Families therefore include blood relatives, apprentices, old tenants who cannot be safely moved, boiler partners, roof gardeners, and those whose rooms have temporarily become incompatible with load requirements.

No one asks how many people live in a house. They ask how many sleep there under ordinary conditions.

The oldest and most respected residents are often called listeners. Some are trained surveyors; others possess no certification at all. They sit in basements, tram shelters, courtyards, or boiler rooms with a palm against brick and distinguish the minute noises of change: clay compressing, a buried pipe losing its bed, roots tightening around a cage, masonry beginning to redistribute weight. A good listener can tell whether a building wants to lean, rise, narrow, or open. They rarely use such language in reports, but they use it among themselves.

Engineers pretend to disapprove.

The engineers are numerous enough to form something like a civic caste, though they come from every district. They wear dark coats reinforced at shoulder and hip, with detachable cuffs marked in measurement grids. Their authority is practical rather than grand. They arrive with tripods, plumb threads, moth boxes, and soft-headed hammers; they drink whatever is offered; they ask where the cups have been sliding. Before examining a crack, they listen to everyone who sleeps near it, including children.

They do not promise to make a structure safe forever. Such a promise would sound fraudulent. They state instead how it may be used, under which conditions, and for how long before the next reading.

Alongside them work the adjusters, who are less formally educated and more widely admired. Adjusters operate jacks, wedges, traction frames, cable teams, and the compact machines that lift occupied buildings a finger’s width at a time. Their craft depends on patience. A celebrated adjuster may spend an entire day moving one corner of a bakery by the thickness of a coin while bread continues to bake inside. They cultivate steady breathing and dislike applause. Sudden celebration near a loaded brace is considered idiotic.

Ground crews follow unsolicited developments. They wear pale waterproof cloaks and carry sampling augers, cage lamps, tags, and portable fences. When a new rib of mineral appears or a courtyard begins filling with clean clay, the crews do not rush in. First they watch what arrives to inhabit it. Gulls, gauge moths, blind fry, certain molds, and the long-bodied voles all provide clues. A surface attractive to nothing is treated with particular caution.

Gardeners are everywhere and hold more influence than outsiders expect. They know which roots tolerate shifting salt layers, which cabbages draw metal from soil, which vines reinforce a courtyard without prying apart the brick, and which mosses indicate warmth rather than leakage. Roof gardeners can identify neighborhoods by condensation taste. Root wardens negotiate between trees and utilities, pruning underground with hooked blades and threading young roots into retaining cages. They speak of hinge willows as coworkers, though not as friends. A willow that adjusts too readily may transfer strain into the structure beside it.

Foundry workers tend furnaces, but they also tend lichen. Their live housings are mapped by color and brightness, and experienced technicians read the woolly orange growth more quickly than instruments. They trim it with ceramic scissors, transplant pieces to new lines, and carry dormant tufts in matchboxes close to the body. Some claim each electrical system produces a subtly different shade. The municipal board denies this while quietly employing people who can tell.

Canal folk remain, though the river does not. They live along separated reaches, lock pits, sumps, pipe junctions, and damp linear depressions still called banks. Their boats are short, flat, and narrow enough to be craned over obstructions. Many spend more time on rails than water, moving vessels between disconnected basins on tram bogies. They preserve a vocabulary for currents that now describes seepage, pressure gradients, vapor drift, and bureaucratic influence.

Market sellers occupy the Assembly Ground with practiced balance. Their stalls hang from overhead frames rather than resting wholly on the plaza. Fruit and root vegetables sit in wire cradles. Liquids are sold in broad-bottomed vessels with internal baffles. Prices change not only by supply but by stability: eggs cost more on ripple days, while onions and turnips become cheaper because they roll into the catches and sort themselves by weight.

Among them move municipal clerks in gray-green capes, transferring offices as the square rearranges. Their desks have locking drawers on every side because no one knows which face may become uppermost after relocation. They classify events, issue temporary addresses, revise maps, and stamp the pruning tags found on trees nobody planted. They are mocked for their language—“localized upward revision,” “unassigned biological arrival,” “temporary directional disagreement”—yet the city depends on their refusal to panic in writing.

Children inhabit the layers adults no longer fit into. They travel through root galleries, redundant conduits, wall spaces, tank interiors, and the low triangular voids formed when floors and foundations part company. They know which warm pipes are safe to sit on and which sleepers beneath the old rail lines still carry faint current. They keep gauge moths beside their beds, feed them mineral dust, and compare their behavior at school.

Their games imitate civic work. They build small districts from hinged tiles and try to keep them standing while another child pushes clay upward from below. They race along adjustment marks. They play “Unsolicited Development,” in which one child emerges under a blanket and the others must decide whether to incorporate, relocate, monitor, or fence them. The child designated municipal office usually refuses every proposal until bribed with preserved fruit.

Schooling emphasizes orientation over certainty. Pupils learn to draw maps with dates, confidence levels, and multiple possible routes. They memorize the tactile alarm pulses transmitted through floors. They practice dressing in darkness, shutting valves, releasing door catches under warped frames, and moving in silence to reinforced thresholds. Alongside these come botany, mechanics, civic history, pipe law, weather, arithmetic, and the ethics of shared load.

The central moral principle is simple: nothing bears weight alone.

This appears on schools, workshops, marriage contracts, retaining walls, and the tags attached to communal tools. It refers equally to engineering and society. To overload a neighbor—by structure, debt, noise, heat, drainage, or grief—is shameful. To accept help before collapse is considered a form of competence.

Their ceremonies reflect this. Couples do not exchange rings so much as calibrated links, fitted into a short chain held by both families. New households are blessed by testing every door and locating the strongest interior corner. When a child is born, someone records the level of the floor beside the bed, and each birthday the measurement is repeated. The resulting marks chart the movement of both child and room.

The dead are rarely buried. The ground is too active and burial too uncertain. Bodies are usually given to municipal heat houses, where warmth is recovered and ash mixed into ceramic markers, insulating brick, or greenhouse beds according to prior wishes. Memorials are kept in use: a handrail, a roof brace, a bench that warms over a steam main. To be remembered by something that continues bearing weight is esteemed.

Some choose another custom called dispersal. Their ashes are divided among several districts where they lived or worked, placed beneath hinge willows, mixed into mortar, or sealed within the counterweights of movable bridges. The city dislikes single points of failure, even in mourning.

Faith, where it exists, has little interest in distant heavens. Its sacred places are thresholds, foundations, junction chambers, pump houses, and rooms that have remained habitable through many revisions. Devotees leave offerings of clean wedges, oil, seeds, and folded maps. One tradition teaches that creation is not past but ongoing beneath every footstep, and that the ethical task is neither to halt it nor surrender to it, but to give each new form a fair test.

Another warns that the ground does not create. It remembers alternatives.

Most inhabitants reserve judgment.

They are wary of certainty but not incurious. When blind fish appear in a rainless pond, people bring buckets and lenses. When fossil markings surface in fresh stone, school groups visit under supervision. When a shaft bends sideways and opens into a cupboard, the owner is interviewed, the cupboard measured, and the event entered into district records before anyone decides whether it is miraculous.

Superstition follows practical lines. Never sleep with both boots out of reach. Never remove a moth from a wall if all the others face east. Never build a nursery directly above a capped pipe, regardless of what the plans say. If a room gains an extra corner, place a chair there for three nights before using it. If clay appears indoors, do not step into it barefoot. If an unknown staircase descends from your cellar, wait until the handrail warms before reporting it.

There are people who live outside the most crowded districts: salvage families in the thinning edge, line keepers among the frost meadows, marsh crews quartered in rail huts, and module workers assembling future streets beneath vast sheds. They are less buffered from the plain’s revisions. Their homes are low, tethered, and designed to be separated into pieces. They cultivate dense windbreaks of hinge willow and lay white cords between doors when frost erases the paths.

These outlanders come into the city carrying bent rails, mineral ribs, old signs, seed stock, moth eggs, machine parts, and stories of developments too distant for official attention. They say whole fields sometimes rise one shallow step during the night. They speak of pylons whose buried cables lead farther down than their maps allow, and warm patches where grass grows in perfect circles around no visible outlet.

City people call these exaggerations, then send surveyors.

There are also those who refuse registration: tunnel dwellers, abandoned-line mechanics, greenhouse drifters, families whose addresses have changed too often to remain useful. They inhabit forgotten layers but are not entirely hidden. They maintain unofficial pumps, mark unsafe walls, rescue lost children, and trade access for food, cloth, batteries, and medicine. The municipality periodically attempts to count them. The resulting totals are always lower than the number of blankets distributed.

No one knows the city’s population. At each census, rooms appear or become inaccessible; districts are reassigned; temporary crews establish permanent kitchens; old addresses return attached to new structures. The official figure is published as a range.

Even the people themselves sometimes seem adapted beyond custom. Some can feel pressure changes in their teeth. Some wake moments before buried alarms activate. Certain old adjusters possess thickened joints that ache not with weather but with ground movement. Children born near condenser works may have exceptionally fine color vision in dim green light. Whether these are inherited traits, occupational effects, or stories shaped by pride is debated without urgency.

What is certain is that the city has made them attentive.

They notice a cup’s slow drift across a table, the altered shine on a wall, a moth changing direction, a willow branch closing its leaves out of sequence. They notice when steam smells faintly metallic, when a neighbor’s door needs lifting, when an alley has acquired a slope overnight. Their competence is composed of such small observations shared quickly and without drama.

They do not imagine themselves heroic. Heroism implies an event with a beginning and end. Their lives are maintenance: continuous, collective, insufficient, and necessary.

In the evening, lamps come on behind wet windows. Roof greenhouses glow above factory sheds. Workers return with folded measures in their pockets and clay on their cuffs. Families eat at tables with adjustable legs while pipes murmur inside the walls. Someone checks the moth box. Someone marks a doorframe. Someone goes down to tighten a brace shared by four houses and a tram platform.

The floor may lift slightly during supper. Bowls settle toward one edge. Hands reach out—not in alarm, but together—to steady them.

Then the meal continues.
