The ones who lived here were small people—not necessarily in body, but in ambition, in the scale of what they asked from the world.

They came in a handful, perhaps one household at first and later two or three: enough to occupy the eastern shelters, few enough that every person could know every channel and ledge. The basin would not have supported a true village. It offered no pasture, no soil, no fuel, and little food. Its gift was water: constant, clean, already collected and naturally protected. Those who stayed therefore lived partly elsewhere. They climbed to the plain to gather, hunt, tend plots, or trade, and descended carrying baskets, cordwood, clay, hides, and whatever else the bare hollow could not provide.

Their bodies learned the basin’s cramped grammar. They moved sideways between pillars, ducked without thinking beneath shelves, and placed their feet on particular dry knobs in the flooded passages. They knew which stones rocked, which channels became swift after rain, and which pale crusts would break under a hand. The hollowed steps and polished grooves preserve this knowledge better than any image of their faces. Children likely learned the routes by following adults, then made games of passages that ended blindly in wet stone. Older inhabitants would have crossed by memory even in darkness.

They slept on raised ledges to escape the wet floor, wrapped in woven mats or skins. Their fires were small and difficult to keep. Fuel had to be brought down from beyond the rim and guarded from damp, perhaps on the missing wall rack. Smoke gathered beneath the roofs before slipping through joints in the stacked stone. The blackened hearth was not a place of roaring flames, but of embers nursed carefully: enough to warm food, dry cord, harden a wooden point, or lend a little human light to an evening of dripping water.

The jars suggest that they valued separation even amid abundance. Water was everywhere, yet they collected it where it fell clearest. One vessel may have been reserved for drinking, another for soaking grain or roots, another for clay, pigment, or washing. The limestone bowl and scraper belong to repetitive work: preparing hides, crushing food, smoothing fibers, scraping mineral scale from a useful surface. Their possessions were few because the basin allowed no clutter that was not already stone.

They altered the place without trying to master it. They deepened channels rather than damming them. They raised a causeway only a hand’s breadth above the wet ground. They fitted shelters into recesses instead of clearing space for freestanding houses. Each improvement accepted the hollow’s existing shape. Work here meant persuasion: turning a trickle, flattening an edge, wedging a slab, cutting one more secure foothold.

Perhaps their occupation was seasonal. The unwavering pool may have drawn them during dry months, when water on the plain became uncertain. Families might have returned year after year until their shelters seemed permanent, though no single stay was long. That would explain the enduring black hearth without abundant refuse, the jars left in floor sockets, and the absence of graves, middens, or heavy construction. They did not abandon a rich settlement in catastrophe. They simply ceased returning—or returned less often, until memory of the place belonged to no living person.

If anyone inhabits the basin now, it is the pale crab.

It lives where the human channels meet natural cracks, in water scarcely deep enough to cover its back. Its shell carries the colors of the hollow: old ivory at the joints, gray-brown along the carapace, translucent at the narrow legs. It feeds on what the clear water brings—minute organic fragments, drowned midges, films too slight for a human eye to distinguish from bare mineral. When footsteps or shadows cross above, it does not flee dramatically. It settles into the silt or becomes another angular detail of the stone.

The frog is less a resident than a puzzled visitor. It descends after rain, rests beside the channels, and waits in a place that offers little. Midges persist under overhangs where the surfaces are merely damp instead of flowing. Tiny colorless organisms inhabit the pool and the wet pores of the limestone, sustaining a life too sparse to soften the basin’s barrenness.

But the most complete inhabitant is water itself. It occupies the jars left for it, the beds cut to direct it, the hearth it has failed to erase, and every seam the builders could not seal. It takes the shape of all former purposes without remembering them. It fills a drinking vessel, crosses a causeway, beads above a sleeping ledge, and passes beneath the floor.

The people once learned the narrow ways of water. Now water alone keeps using theirs.
