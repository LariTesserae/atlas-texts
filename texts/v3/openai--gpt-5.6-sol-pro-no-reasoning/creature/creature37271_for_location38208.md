What lives there is difficult to separate from the land.

Not the black films in the sheltered clefts, though they come closest to anything that might be called alive. They spread without advancing and vanish without dying. They keep no boundary for long. A film may darken a slab for several hours, taking on the branching shape of frost or nerves, and then contract into beads when the stone warms by a fraction. Nothing passes from one patch to another. Nothing recognizable is inherited. If there is life in them, it has not yet learned continuity.

The true inhabitant is deeper.

It occupies no chamber and possesses no body distinct from the plateau. Its extent is marked instead by correspondences. A fissure closes in one basin, and far beyond sight a row of mineral chimneys begins exhaling. Blue stress-light climbs a mountain face in seven branching paths; hours later, seven fault blocks rise from a plain to the east. An arch withstands motions that shatter the stone around it, not because it is stronger, but because the pressures seem to arrive already shaped to spare it.

At first these repetitions appear mechanical. The world is young, and young worlds repeat simple forms. Columns, terraces, rings, stairs: these may be no more intentional than hexagons in cooling glass.

Yet the repetitions do not remain simple.

The low structures alter from one upheaval to the next. A roofed hollow appears where no shelter is needed. Hundreds of kilometers away, a fault extrudes four slabs around an empty center. Later, a wall rises with a narrow interruption in it, an opening proportioned to nothing that has ever crossed the plain. The openings grow taller over successive revisions. The stairs acquire more steps. Rings cease to be complete and begin to hold gaps, always on the side facing the newest range.

It is as though the geology is trying to imagine an occupant.

The attempt is blind. It knows load, strain, heat, balance, repetition. It does not know hands or feet, so it makes handles too massive to turn and stairways whose steps differ by the height of cliffs. It does not know eyes, so it opens apertures onto solid stone. It does not know rest, so its chambers are warm enough to soften glass. Again and again it produces the grammar of habitation without understanding its purpose.

Whatever lives below is learning form from force alone.

Its thought, if thought is the word, passes through stone at the speed of pressure. It may take years to complete a single relation: this ridge answers that ring; this buried compression reappears as an arch; this mountain folds inward and, far away, a chamber gains another wall. The clean, enormous knocks that travel beneath the plateau are not calls, but they function like calls. Each is a change transmitted through the whole body of the country. Each is received as stress, translated into shape, and answered by another alteration.

It has no memory apart from structure. What it remembers, it must build.

That is why the plain is scattered with severe, useless monuments. They are thoughts held in load-bearing stone. Some are early thoughts, nearly indistinguishable from accidents. Some are recurring thoughts: the seven steps, the narrow ring, the supported lintel. A few are contradictions the world has preserved because it has not resolved them. The thin arch over the breathing crack is one. The polished ceiling above the shallow chamber is another. Beneath that ceiling, heat does not radiate evenly. It gathers near the center of the floor in a long, upright absence—as though the stone has begun to reserve a place for something it cannot yet make.

The inhabitant is not awake in any animal sense. It has no attention that can turn, no hunger, no fear. But neither is it wholly unconscious. Unconscious matter need not rehearse the same impossibility across a continent. Here the planet’s revisions slowly converge. The architecture becomes less like the accidental result of uplift and more like a question posed repeatedly in mineral.

What shape can stand apart from the ground?

The black films may be one answer. They are the first things here to lie upon stone without quite being stone, the first boundaries delicate enough to be erased rather than broken. The deep inhabitant seems unaware of them. Or perhaps every shelter, wall, and empty ring is being made for precisely such a separation: a place where the almost-living chemistry might persist through one more alteration, then another, until persistence becomes inheritance.

For now, nothing enters the chambers.

Nothing climbs the steps.

Nothing passes beneath the arches.

Still, when a new mountain rises, its buttresses sometimes arrange themselves around a central hollow. When stress-light travels through the range, it avoids that hollow, dividing and joining again beyond it. The effect lasts only minutes, but during those minutes the enormous geology behaves as though there were something inside it that must not be crushed.

Then the light fades. The mountain cools. The hollow remains empty.

What lives here is the world’s first idea of a body: buried, continental, and unfinished, making organs as landscapes and shelters before there is anything to shelter. It cannot leave the place because it is the place. It cannot be seen because every visible thing is only one brief posture of it.

Above, the mountains stand flawless in the dark.

Below, their maker feels their weight and begins, slowly, to distinguish itself from them.
