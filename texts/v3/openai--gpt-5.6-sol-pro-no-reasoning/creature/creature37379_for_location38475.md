The people who live there are compact, deliberate beings, though whether the city made them so or was made by ancestors already suited to it is not an answer they consider useful.

They are not fish. Their faces remain unmistakably human at close range: brow, nose, mouth, the minute asymmetries by which kin recognize kin. But water has edited the old pattern. Their chests are deep and flexible, their ribs joined by pale seams of cartilage that allow the body to endure gradual changes in pressure. Small breathing folds lie beneath the jaw and along the collarbones, protected by lattices of scar-like tissue. These can draw enough oxygen from the water for ordinary life, provided the gardens and pumps remain healthy. For hard work, illness, or travel into stagnant districts, they wear ceramic respirators fed by hoses or by air cells clipped along the spine.

Their eyes are broad-pupiled and dark. A translucent inner lid passes over them when silt thickens or a current carries metal dust. Color matters greatly to them, yet their colors are not surface colors. They distinguish the bruised violet of a foundry leak from the harmless violet of ripe moss, the green of a lived-in room from the green of a failing lamp, the blue-white shimmer of stressed ceramic from the same material at rest.

Many are nearly deaf in air and acutely sensitive in water. They hear through the jaw, sternum, fingertips, and the soles of their feet. A hand resting on a pipe can identify a pump several districts away. Children learn the city first as rhythm: the deep beat of the Pressure House, the slower tremor of flywheels, the feathery agitation of filter combs, the thin irregular ticking of shell colonies. Only later are they taught names.

Their hair is usually cropped close or braided flat against the skull. Loose hair catches valves, gathers silt, and reveals one’s current to anyone watching. Eyebrows and lashes are often thickened with grease against irritation. Skin is rubbed with waxes rendered from algae and shell tissue, leaving a muted sheen. The oldest residents become marked by mineral deposits in creases and scars. Pale lines trace knuckles, eyelids, and the corners of mouths; some elders seem gradually to be turning into masonry.

Clothing is close-fitting, layered, and full of attachment points. Woven kelp fiber forms the inner garment. Over it lie panels of treated hide-like membrane, ceramic scales, tool loops, seed pockets, ballast beads, and small bladders for adjusting buoyancy. No one dresses without considering where they intend to go. A person bound for the upper gardens wears weights at hip and ankle. A worker descending toward the Silt Parliament carries buoyancy cells that can be broken open in an emergency. Infants are tethered not by cords but by broad woven sleeves joining them to a parent’s belt, so that no loop can tighten around the throat.

Adornment is structural whenever possible. A bracelet may also be a hatch key. Earrings hold current needles. Marriage clasps double as pipe gauges. Mourning beads are made from the glass inclusions reserved for family mortar, worn until the dead are added to a wall. Purely decorative objects exist, but they are tiny and intimate: a polished bolt that turns out to have no thread, a bead containing a trapped silver bubble, a fragment of blue glass from a window no one remembers.

They do not swim as visitors imagine swimming. In tight quarters, broad strokes would strike masonry, uproot seedlings, or send dangerous clouds into the water. Instead they pull themselves along fixed lines, push from doorframes, angle their bodies into familiar currents, or make small finning motions with hands and feet. Their toes are long and dexterous. Most can hold a rail with one foot while working with both hands. In crowded passages, people fold their elbows in and rotate past one another with the practiced courtesy of dancers.

To be graceful is to disturb little.

Children are clumsy by local standards. They burst upward unexpectedly, kick sediment from ledges, laugh bubbles into inspection apertures, and hide inside kelp ceilings until gardeners prod them out with hooked poles. Their first serious lesson is stillness. During red-lamp drills, they are required to release every handhold and remain suspended without turning. Adults watch for the twitch of a foot, the drift of a sleeve, an escaping bubble. Those who master it receive a first current weight engraved with their household mark.

Households are not defined solely by blood. They are pressure groups: those who share air allotments, sleep chambers, garden duties, emergency seals, and responsibility for a section of structure. A household may occupy six rooms on four different levels, connected by pipes and maintenance shafts rather than doors. Its members might include siblings, former spouses, apprentices, an elderly valve keeper, three children of uncertain parentage, and a gardener adopted because her root beds hold up the rooms in which they all live.

Privacy exists, but not as separation. Walls conduct vibration; currents carry scent and heat; pipes pass through sleeping places. Privacy is created by convention. A blue thread tied across an alcove means the space is closed. A person who turns their lamp inward is treated as absent. Touching someone’s wall to listen through it without cause is considered a violation more intimate than entering their room.

Speech is used mostly at close range. People bring foreheads or helmet shells together, making a small chamber in which words become distinct. Their spoken language is soft, rich in clicks, hums, and pressure-shaped consonants. At a distance, they sign. The city’s common hand-language is precise enough for engineering and law, and expressive enough that songs can be performed entirely in gesture.

A crowd conversing in a public chamber appears to be a field of hands opening and closing among slowly turning bodies.

Names have several forms. There is a touch-name, tapped into the wrist or shoulder; a lamp-name, made from a short sequence of colors; a household name; and sometimes a spoken name used only in air. The old engraved names on the Pressure House are difficult to render in any current system. Scholars reproduce them by laying fingers against the cuts and tracing their depth.

No single ruler governs the city. Authority belongs to those whose work gives them the right to interrupt other work.

A current marshal may close a transit lane but cannot order a gardener to cut a load-bearing root. A gardener may condemn a wall but cannot alter the air allotment of those living behind it. Pump keepers can requisition power from factories. Clinicians can countermand pump keepers during an outbreak. Mortar families decide where human remains are incorporated, even when engineers object. Disputes are settled by layered consent, which can take months and often becomes obsolete before agreement is reached.

This is not considered failure. Hasty decisions are associated with rupture.

The closest thing to civic sovereignty belongs to the Keepers of Interval, who tend the Pressure House and record the length, force, and after-vibration of every central pulse. They wear no special uniform, only a black ring around one wrist and a row of tiny ceramic counters braided into their clothing. Each counter marks a missed or anomalous beat witnessed during their lifetime. Some keepers have none. Some carry dozens.

They do not claim to understand the House. Understanding is not required for service.

Their discipline consists of comparison. They sleep against its walls. They taste condensation from its seams. They memorize which weeds lean before a piston engages and which shell colonies stop clicking when a deep valve opens. The oldest among them can detect a change too slight for instruments by placing both palms on the central cylinder and allowing the pulse to pass through the bones of the arms into the chest.

They are respected, pitied, and watched carefully. Too many years beside the House can make a person interpret all events as parts of its cycle. Such keepers begin measuring arguments, births, crop failures, and dreams by intervals between pulses. When this happens, their household quietly removes their black ring and assigns them to outer maintenance.

Gardeners form another broad caste, though they deny being one. They know every useful species and many that have no official existence. They graft filter weeds onto cracked brick, persuade root mats to grow around rather than through ceramic, and harvest kelp without changing the buoyancy of a district too quickly. Their arms are crosshatched by small cuts. Their hooked shears are inherited, rehandled, and sharpened to different curves for different stems.

They can also enforce quarantine.

A white line of luminous bacteria painted around a hatch is a warning. Two lines mean no entry. A closed loop means the room within has been given over to another ecology and may never be reopened. Whether that ecology is disease, poison, invasive growth, or something the gardeners refuse to name is rarely specified.

Industry is maintained by patient guilds. Cable drawers develop powerful hands and narrow shoulders from decades of controlled pulling. Platers have fingernails stained in permanent bands by metals. Kiln tenders bear pale heat scars despite the water. Divers who enter sealed machinery are often very small adults or adolescents chosen for calmness rather than strength. They travel through pipes that admit no room to turn, dragging bundles of tools behind them.

The most valued craft is diagnosis. Making a new component is admirable; knowing which old component must not be replaced is wisdom.

There are residents who spend entire lives listening for faults. They press cheek and sternum to masonry and map irregularities in charcoal on ceramic slates. Their records resemble anatomical drawings more than plans: chambers as organs, pipes as vessels, cracks as lesions, gardens as layers of tissue. A neighborhood is said to be fevered when its cooling stream warms, breathless when algae output drops, dizzy when conflicting currents make navigation uncertain.

The city is not metaphorically alive to them. It is alive in the practical sense that matters: it consumes, excretes, heals, grows, retains injury, and may die.

Religion, insofar as it can be separated from maintenance, gathers around exchange. Air becomes breath; breath becomes garden food; flesh becomes mineral; mineral becomes wall; wall shelters air. Nothing should be removed from the cycle without cause. Waste is both sin and engineering error.

Their chapels are not open sanctuaries but narrow hollows beside important conduits. Worshippers insert one arm into a warm current and the other into a cool one, holding themselves between flows. Prayers are made by releasing measured breaths into small inverted cups. The bubbles remain there for a time, joining those of earlier petitioners, until a valve opens and draws them into the communal stores.

The dead are kept briefly in cool water while their household decides where they are most needed. A beloved cook may enter the mortar of a kitchen hearth. A current marshal may be set into a bridge pier. Children are often placed in window frames so passing light will catch their glass markers. Those who served the Pressure House are mixed into repairs near its outer braces, never into its ancient core. No one alters the core.

There are exceptions. Some bodies do not mineralize correctly. Some resist the lime baths or produce dark branching stains in the mortar. Such remains are sealed in ceramic figures and lowered to the Silt Parliament. The figures stand for a few days before the sediment takes them. Families return later, hoping to recognize a new mound.

Food comes mostly from the trained growths: kelp flesh, tubers, soft algae pressed into sheets, moss oils, fungal threads cultivated inside porous stone, and the filter tissue of shell colonies. Eating is done in air pockets or through mouth valves in shared dining masks. Meals are slow and communal because every pocket of air is accounted for. A table may be fixed upside down beneath a vaulted ceiling, with diners floating below it and securing themselves by the knees.

Flavor is intense. Salt is abundant; texture is prized. Crisp foods are a luxury because nearly everything tends toward softness. Children receive toasted mineral wafers on festival days and make them last for hours, listening to the forbidden loudness of each bite inside the skull.

Festivals mark maintenance achievements rather than seasons. The Cleaning of the Western Combs fills canals with drifting scraps of blue algae and brings weeks of brighter light. When a new air-cell tier is completed, households contribute one breath each before it is sealed. The replacement of a major pipe is celebrated by decorating the obsolete section and leaving it in place if possible. Old pipes become corridors, shrines, nursery rooms, or gardens.

The longest observance occurs every nine days, when the brass shutters in the Pressure House open onto stone.

No one knows why they open. The gap is too narrow to enter and reveals only a smooth limestone face. Yet residents gather in the surrounding chambers and wait through the motion. Some lay hands on the shutters. Some offer tools, seedlings, written requests, or fragments of broken machinery. Children peer into the seam, hoping the rock will peer back.

For a few moments, water from inside the mechanism flows across the stone. It is warmer than the city water and carries a metallic sweetness. Gardeners collect samples. Keepers record the pulse. Then the shutters close, crushing any offering left between them.

Stories claim the opening once led somewhere.

Not outside, exactly. Inward in a deeper sense: into an earlier city, into a dry chamber beneath the aquifer, into the body of a buried machine, into the place where pressure originates. Skeptics point out that rock is rock. The faithful reply that the shutters continue to open, and purpose outlives obstruction.

Beyond settled districts live the Uncounted, though “beyond” may mean nothing more than behind a disused cistern or inside walls omitted from current surveys. They avoid lamps and communal allotments. They cultivate unauthorized growths in warm leak chambers and tap air cells without registering a household mark. Officially they are dangerous because their consumption cannot be measured. Unofficially they perform much of the city’s most perilous salvage.

They return with objects from sealed rooms: unfamiliar valves, old script plates, bones enclosed in transparent mineral, tools made for hands with too many joints. They know passages that close when shown on maps. Some have spent so long in narrow spaces that their bodies are permanently bowed. Others are said to have clearer inner eyelids, stronger throat folds, and no discomfort in the empty water above the grille.

The city distrusts them but needs them. When a conduit vanishes into an inaccessible wall, an offering is left near the opening: food paste, lamp cells, a coil of fine cable. Often the repair is completed in darkness. No one asks by whom.

Children tell stories of people who have adapted too fully.

These wall-dwellers flatten themselves into cracks and breathe through moss. They have lost spoken names and communicate by changing the color of bacterial lines. They repair masonry from within, mixing their own mineralizing skin into the stone. A knocking heard from a sealed wall is therefore answered politely. Adults insist this is superstition, yet most answer too.

Visitors from elsewhere are rare enough that the city has no single custom for receiving them. Those who descend from above are regarded with concern. They move too quickly, waste motion, depend on carried air, and stare into open water without fear. Their bodies are buoyant in the wrong ways. They speak aloud across rooms, making walls tremble. They ask where the streets are, which building is oldest, who is in charge, and how to reach the center—as if these questions should have singular answers.

Citizens assigned to guide them are chosen for patience. They teach the rules in order: touch twice, cut nothing, yield to black, stop at red, follow warmth in darkness.

The final rule is often misunderstood. Visitors assume it is only emergency instruction. To the inhabitants it is also a statement about belonging.

Home is not the surface, nor the ancient chambers, nor any fixed room. Home is the direction in which systems become denser, warmer, more entangled, and more dependent on one another. It is where a wall contains ancestors, where a pipe carries three purposes, where a garden holds up a stair, where every current bears evidence of other lives.

Inward is where one becomes impossible to separate.

At rest, the inhabitants hook feet beneath rails or settle into woven kelp cradles. Lamps turn low. Inner eyelids close. The city’s machinery enters their bones: shell-click, pump-draw, turbine-turn, root-creak, distant flow.

Then the deep pulse passes through them.

Sleeping hands tighten around cords. Infants startle against their parents. Keepers count the fading vibrations. Somewhere a worker pauses halfway through a gesture, waiting to feel whether the beat was true.

The silence after it is not empty. It is inhabited by attention.

Only when the next pulse comes do thousands of bodies loosen at once, almost imperceptibly, and continue living.
