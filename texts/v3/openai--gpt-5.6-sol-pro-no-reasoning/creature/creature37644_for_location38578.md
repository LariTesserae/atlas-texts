No single people live in the valley. The valley’s inhabitants are a people only in aggregate: an innumerable commonwealth of bodies, each species knowing a few passages, a few obligations, a few appetites, while together they inhabit and perpetuate a city none of them can comprehend.

On the broad roads move the largest citizens. Great herbivores, dark-backed with moisture, travel in habitual files between grazing terraces and mineral pools. Their shoulders brush the vegetation into walls; their feet compress loose rubble and polish ramps that would otherwise vanish beneath moss. Some carry whole mobile gardens in their coats—ferns sprouting from matted hollows, snails grazing their flanks, bright birds picking ticks from the warm skin around their ears. They appear slow until alarm passes through the valley. Then their herds become blunt rivers of flesh, and the masonry trembles under them.

Smaller hoofed animals occupy the steeper ways. Their split feet know ledges invisible in the gloom, and generations of their passage have cut narrow switchbacks across the old retaining faces. They maintain territorial middens at junctions, leaving pungent notices legible long after darkness or rain has erased every visual mark. Some sleep standing in crowded courts, horn against flank, each animal supported by the pressure of its neighbors.

The plazas belong to tortoises. They gather wherever black paving stores the valley’s weak warmth, their shells slick with condensation and marked by pale scratches from low doorways. Moss, seeds, and small flowering plants travel upon their backs. Their routes are patient but authoritative. Saplings that rise across them are crushed; loose stones are shouldered outward; shallow channels are deepened by centuries of dragging plastrons. Around the oldest individuals, smaller creatures arrange themselves as if around moving hearths.

In the upper crowns live monkeys adapted less to open forest than to architecture overgrown. Their hands read the valley’s joints. They know which vine bridges will bear weight, which hollow blocks contain water, which parapets conceal snakes. Troops inherit routes from their elders and defend particular fruit courts, sleeping galleries, and sun shafts. Their barks are both warning and address, flung across basins where faces cannot be seen. They move seeds, break dead branches, raid nests, and pry grubs from mortar, continually damaging and maintaining the city in the same gestures.

The birds are beyond counting. Cliff cavities hold rookeries layered hundreds of nests deep. Swallows occupy softened reliefs; parrots enlarge old drain mouths with their beaks; fruit-eaters keep ancestral perches above garden courts, sowing groves in dependable patterns. Broad-winged scavengers wait on the highest ridges for rising air. Tiny birds live almost permanently on the herbivores, sleeping in their fur and lifting only when a host enters water. Others rarely leave the galleries, navigating by calls so sharp that the architecture answers them.

At dusk, the underside of the city detaches itself.

Bats unfold from bridges, vaults, root arches, and abandoned nests in such numbers that their departure seems to darken an already dark sky. Different kinds occupy different depths: fruit bats among the crowns, insect hunters over cisterns, pale cave forms in the warm lower conduits. Their droppings feed fungal beds and blind colonies beneath the floors. In some galleries they hang in several living layers, the upper animals clinging to stone, the lower ones clinging to one another.

Rodents inhabit the walls as blood inhabits a body. They pass through drainage galleries and ventilation gaps, keeping narrow conduits open with their traffic. Some cache seeds in dry pockets and forget enough of them to plant the terraces. Some cut fibrous vegetation and pack it into leaks. Others undermine walls while making warm communal chambers, forcing roots, water, and larger animals to perform the repairs. To the creatures outside, their routes are merely scratches and rustlings. Within the masonry lies another complete valley of chambers, latrines, nurseries, granaries, and hunted corridors.

Burrowing mammals, insects, worms, and crustaceans occupy the garden beds. They turn compost, carry clay, sort grains by size, and ventilate soil that would otherwise sour in the wet. Termites raise warm mounds on older mounds. Ants establish roads no wider than stems but more persistent than the hoofways. Beetles roll dung down ramps and into cracks, transporting fertility from plazas to root courts. Every large nest contains smaller tenants; every smaller tenant carries parasites of its own.

Water has its citizens too. Frogs pack the cistern edges until the stone seems to pulse. Tadpoles blacken seasonal pools. Fish survive the interrupted river by passing through flooded gravel or remaining sealed in deep basins through dry intervals. Eels and pale amphibians inhabit the galleries between reservoirs. Crabs widen seepage holes, dragging leaf litter into the walls. Snails graze the wet masonry, leaving silver maps that other animals smell and follow. Mosquitoes and midges rise wherever water pauses, and their abundance feeds nearly everything with wings.

The predators do not rule this population, though each briefly reorganizes it.

Cats move along parapets with their bellies near the stone, patterned coats dissolving into leaf shadow and lichen. Long-bodied hunters enter conduits after rodents. Snakes wait in fruiting trees, warm drains, and bird-thick cavities. Raptors drop through rare openings in the canopy. In the lower marshes, heavy-jawed reptiles lie among floating debris like pieces of the architecture. None can empty the valley. A kill closes one path for moments; then scavengers gather, insects arrive, bones are carried into nests, and the interruption is absorbed.

The most consequential inhabitants are often nearly invisible. Fungi web the buried timber and old bedding. Mites prune fungal growth in beetle chambers. Lice and ticks regulate where animals sleep. Bacteria heat the compost courts and sour stagnant pools. Lichens digest exposed stone grain by grain, while moss traps what they loosen. Roots probe every joint, but around them cluster gnawers, grazers, borers, and mold-eaters, so that even decay is crowded and contested.

No creature possesses a plan of the whole place. Knowledge exists as thousands of overlapping customs.

A herd remembers a ramp. A monkey troop remembers a sequence of crowns. A tortoise remembers warmth beneath a particular wall. Swallows return to one niche; frogs to one basin; bats to one ceiling; ants to one seam of stone. These memories cross and reinforce one another until they become avenues, gardens, reservoirs, and halls. The city’s design is stored not in minds but in repeated movement.

Its inhabitants are therefore neither builders nor occupants in the ordinary sense. They are loads borne by the structure, weather acting upon it, and the means by which it repairs itself. Their nests brace failing cavities. Their dung makes soil. Their feet seal roads. Their hunger trims corridors. Their deaths fill hollows and support roots. They inherit works made by species long vanished and pass them, altered by use, to species not yet present.

Nothing here lives alone, and almost nothing lives only where it appears to. Every animal is a habitat, every nest a district, every district part of some larger migration. The valley’s true resident is this entanglement: warm, damp, blind in places, deafening everywhere—a many-bodied creature distributed across an artificial mountain range, forever eating its home and, by eating, making it endure.
