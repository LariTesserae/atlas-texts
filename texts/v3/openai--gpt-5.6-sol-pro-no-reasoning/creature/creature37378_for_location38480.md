The one that lives there is not the plant.

The plant is its body, perhaps, but only in the broad sense that a house is the body of a hermit crab: grown around a vulnerable occupancy, maintained for it, altered by its pressure. The true inhabitant lives in the hollow. It has no enduring flesh of its own, and so the room keeps making approximations.

At first it resembles absence. A place near the middle appears slightly farther away than the surrounding air. Hanging droplets bend around it. Sounds enter and return changed: a root’s knock becomes a second knock elsewhere; a whisper comes back with one word removed. When it moves, it does so by transferring this discrepancy from one part of the chamber to another. The eye does not see a passage. It discovers that the emptiness is now standing closer.

Given time, the roots dress it.

Fine hairs rise from the floor and describe two narrow legs, then six, then none. Condensation sketches a long-backed animal with a lowered head. Loose pollen gathers around shoulders that are alternately human, cervine, and mantis-thin. Sometimes it wears the flattened green lizard as a face. Sometimes the suspended leaf turns in the air and becomes its single ear.

No form satisfies it for long. Its anatomy depends upon whoever is present.

To a solitary visitor, it often takes on a rough likeness of that visitor, but assembled from errors: the wrong footprint under each foot, joints bending according to another creature’s gait, features placed where memory rather than sight expects them. Its face remains indistinct until looked away from. Then the pools show it clearly, watching from very far below.

To a group, it divides its resemblance among them. One person lends it a voice, another a shadow, another the rhythm of breathing. Its hands may be shaped from the expectation of being touched. Those hands cannot grasp objects, but they can make an object feel already held. A root cord tightens around an empty palm. A thorn blade turns gently toward the body. A bowl pours upward into a mouth that is not visible.

It feeds on orientation.

Not blood, sap, or fear, though fear ripens what it wants. It consumes the agreements by which beings share a world: that down is the same for everyone, that a sound follows its cause, that two steps cross the same distance twice, that the person who made a footprint remains its owner. Each small failure nourishes it. After feeding, it becomes more definite while its surroundings become less so. For a few moments one may see ribs opening and closing in the warm air, a narrow skull crowned with rootlets, and a translucent sac within its chest in which a tiny chamber hangs upside down.

Inside that chamber, something else is looking up.

The inhabitant rarely attacks outright. It cultivates. It trails visitors with dry clicks, testing how quickly they turn. It shifts their tracks, exchanges their shadows, and lets one hear another’s voice from beneath a pool. It may cause a root ridge to rise as a seat or grow a cup-shaped husk where thirst will find it. These courtesies are not kindness. Comfort persuades a body to remain, and remaining gives the hollow time to learn it.

Once learned, a visitor becomes reproducible.

During flowering, the petal-shaped absences sometimes reveal not adjacent worlds but rehearsals: the visitor entering by a route never taken, sleeping against a wall, kneeling to drink, calling to companions who have not arrived. The inhabitant watches these possibilities through animal eyes in the roots. It selects among them, encouraging some and pruning others. A step may return to the instant before it was placed. A warning may arrive too late several times until the speaker chooses different words. Gradually the visitor’s possible selves are narrowed into a shape the chamber can keep.

What emerges afterward is not necessarily a corpse, captive, or imitation. It may be a new creature crouched against the wall, pale and bewildered. It may be a gnat carrying one remembered syllable. It may be a green mark that turns toward the light. It may simply be another footprint climbing toward the ceiling.

The original visitor can sometimes leave.

Those who do report the sense that something accompanied them, though nothing appears beside them. For days afterward, their shadows incline underground. Seeds rotate when they pass. Water in cups seems much deeper than the vessel permits. Their footsteps occasionally sound from ahead.

Meanwhile the inhabitant retains a version of them—not a soul exactly, nor a memory, but their manner of occupying distance. It adds this to itself. That is why its body never stabilizes. It is crowded with stolen postures: the folding lizard, the square-flying gnats, seedlings rooted into private gravities, travelers who came from fields, rivers, burrows, houses, and chambers nested above the chamber. All persist as ways the emptiness might stand.

At the height of flowering, when the walls separate and the roar fills the hollow, the inhabitant briefly approaches its native form.

Then it is immense.

The cottage-sized room is revealed as only one cavity in it, like a chamber of the heart or an air cell in bone. Its limbs extend through the deep pools and emerge in distant hollows. Its branching antlers are systems of gravity. Its mouth is every petal-shaped absence opening at once. What appears locally as a pale, bewildered creature is elsewhere a fingertip, an eyelid, a seed caught in its fur.

And still it is unfinished.

The great plant grows around it to give it boundaries. Roots braid overhead to prevent it from diffusing into measureless space. Sap hammers through living walls to keep time near it. The floor breathes so it will have something against which to place its borrowed feet. Even the sourceless light may be part of this containment: a continuous, gentle scrutiny that does not permit the inhabitant to disappear completely.

Or perhaps the plant is not containing it.

Perhaps it is tending it.

Perhaps the hollow is a womb made not for flesh but for a creature of interval and relation—a being slowly assembled from displaced distances, delayed sounds, exchanged tracks, and arrivals from worlds that almost touch. Each flowering opens the room a little wider. Each visitor gives the unborn thing another way to move.

When at last it has learned enough forms, the roots may cease holding the absence closed.

Then whatever lives there will be born—not upward through soil, but outward through every place that has ever seemed larger inside than it should be.
