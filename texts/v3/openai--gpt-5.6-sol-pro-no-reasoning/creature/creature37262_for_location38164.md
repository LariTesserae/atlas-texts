The people who live there are quiet not by law or solemn custom, but because nothing around them has ever taught them to compete with noise.

Their voices are clear and measured. Across a courtyard, a person calls once and waits. At a work site, instructions pass in brief phrases accompanied by gestures: two fingers lifted for the next stone, a palm tilted to show a slope, a cord drawn taut between wet stakes. Children make more noise than adults, but even their play unfolds in a world without squealing animals, wingbeats, or insect hum. Their laughter rises strangely naked over the marsh and often ends as everyone pauses to hear how far it travels.

They are the only animate life they know.

Whether they understand this as privilege, burden, or merely the nature of existence depends on the district and household. They do not generally speak of an “absence of animals,” any more than they speak of the absence of a second sun. There are old speculative tales about movement that does not belong to people: reeds that uproot themselves, knots of leaves that crawl from shade to shade, pale swimmers imagined in deep cisterns. Such beings appear in children’s stories and on carved lintels as arrangements of human traits—hands without arms, faces borne on stalks, bodies made of braided legs. No artist has ever observed another kind of eye.

Their language distinguishes finely among human motion. There are separate words for walking on dry stone, wading through planted shallows, balancing on a narrow bank, carrying weight in pairs, and testing unseen ground with a staff. Many people can identify a distant neighbor by gait alone. Movement is personality made visible.

They tend to be long-limbed and sure-footed, with strong shoulders and hands roughened by cord, clay, stone, and reeds. Their feet are broad from a lifetime on uneven paving. Indoors and in gardens they often go barefoot; on causeways and quarry paths they wear sandals woven from split reed and tough plant fiber, with replaceable soles. Their clothing is layered rather than heavy: short wrapped garments for work, longer mantles against rain, broad shoulder capes stiffened with waxy plant oils, and hoods shaped to shed water without obscuring peripheral sight. Colors come from earth and vegetation—smoke black, silt brown, muted yellow, moss red, mineral blue. Brilliant white belongs mostly to fresh plaster and ceremonial cloth.

Hair is cut, bound, or braided close during labor. Pins are precious, usually stone, fired clay, polished wood, or occasionally metal. Because no wool, silk, fur, hide, shell, bone, horn, or sinew exists, everything worn must come from plants, earth, or the human body. Cloth is woven from bast fiber, marsh flax, and softened leaf strands. Cordage is everywhere and made in many grades, from delicate thread to thick structural rope. People judge workmanship by knots as readily as by masonry.

Their settlements are organized less as continuous neighborhoods than as linked households of responsibility. A cluster on one rise may be known for maintaining three cisterns; another for keeping the causeway culverts clear; another for firing roof tile, dredging black silt, storing grain, or watching the mountain springs. Residence and duty overlap. To belong to a place is to know where its water enters, where it rests, and where it must be allowed to leave.

No one owns flowing water outright. Families may hold garden plots, houses, fruit trees, reed-cutting reaches, and storage rooms, but channels are governed collectively. A person who blocks a runnel for private benefit commits one of the city’s gravest offenses—not because water is scarce, but because its balance is shared. Deliberate flooding is treated as violence. Neglecting a culvert is regarded much as another culture might regard abandoning a dependent.

Their officials are therefore observers before they are rulers. Each district appoints level-keepers, people trained to read gauges, settlement cracks, stains, moss lines, soil softness, and the trembling surface of water in shallow bowls. They carry cords, marked rods, plumb weights of fired clay, and notebooks of thin wooden leaves coated with pale slip. Their records are repetitive by design. A hundred unchanged measurements are not wasted effort; they are evidence that the city remains properly fitted to the world.

Public decisions take time. Before a new terrace, hall, or garden wall is approved, stakes are set and left through many rains. Water bowls are placed uphill and down. Neighbors watch where condensation gathers and whether reeds lean differently near the proposed work. Meetings occur under long shelters open on all sides, while rain threads from the eaves. The most respected speaker is often the one who can say exactly what remains unknown.

This patience enters their character. They distrust urgency unless someone is hurt. A person who acts before observing is considered immature, however old they may be. Their common moral vocabulary draws on drainage and weight: a good person “gives passage,” “rests level,” “does not gather what must flow,” or “bears without sinking.” Someone obstinate is said to be “laid across the channel.” Someone unreliable is “a stone seated in moss.”

Yet they are not passive. Their labor is constant. Every useful surface has been lifted, aligned, packed, woven, dredged, planted, or cleared by hand. With no draft animals and no wheel transport, large work depends upon coordinated bodies. Stones travel on sledges over wetted tracks, on timber rollers used sparingly, or suspended from carrying frames borne by teams. Songs accompany such work, but they are low, rhythmic sequences built for breath and timing rather than performance. The city’s deepest music may be the sound of forty people exhaling together as a lintel settles into place.

Food is almost entirely cultivated, gathered, or processed from plants. Morning meals are soft grain, steamed tubers, pickled greens, and warm infusions of bitter leaves. Gourds become vessels as well as food. Beans and reed grain are pounded into pastes, cakes, and dense loaves baked on clay plates. Tree fruits are eaten fresh, dried under sheltered heat, fermented, or cooked down to sour preserves. Oils come from crushed seeds and oily kernels. Salt and mineral flavors are highly valued, as are smoke, ash, acidity, and texture.

Without animals, eating carries no tradition of flesh, milk, eggs, or honey. There are no butchers, herders, hunters, fishers, tanners, shepherds, or stable keepers. Instead, specialized food workers cultivate molds and ferments, skim nutritious films from controlled vats, and tend beds of edible fungi in warm masonry chambers. These cultures are among the few living things understood as neither plant nor person, though people debate what kind of life they possess. Fermentation houses are watched carefully, their inherited starters guarded like household ancestors.

Agriculture requires intimate human intervention. Certain plants scatter pollen in the wind; others fertilize themselves. For treasured garden varieties, people pollinate flowers by hand with fiber brushes. Children often learn this work because their fingers are small and their eyesight close. At flowering time, whole families move quietly among climbing vines, touching one blossom after another. It is ordinary labor, but also one of their central images of care: life continuing because someone noticed what had to meet.

Birth occupies a similarly powerful place in their understanding. Every person has come from another person in a world where no other creature is born. Pregnant people are not isolated, but their work shifts toward seed sorting, cord making, record keeping, cooking, and garden tending. Midwives hold great practical and ceremonial authority. They are among the few who routinely encounter blood, pain, unpredictability, and sudden change in a landscape otherwise governed by constancy.

The dead cannot be returned to an animal cycle of scavenging and rapid consumption. Bodies decay slowly in the wet ground, so funerary customs are deliberate. Some districts cremate their dead on roofed platforms using reeds, dry timber, pressed fuel cakes, and oils; others place them in ventilated stone chambers until only the most enduring remains are left. Ash is sometimes mixed into memorial plaster or buried beneath a fruit tree, though never spread in food gardens without consent given before death. Names are cut into clay tablets and set behind running water, where they can be seen wavering through the clear flow.

Their religion, where it can be called religion, is concerned less with commanding gods than with continuities: water emerging, seed becoming plant, breath becoming speech, body becoming soil. Springs are honored but not personified uniformly. One district may call a spring an opening in the mountain; another, the mountain’s memory; another, simply the First Measure. Priests are uncommon. Keepers, witnesses, reciters, and midwives perform most rites.

Their deepest mystery is change.

They can see smoke disperse, plants grow, food rot, wounds close, and people age. Yet the larger world barely alters. Mountains remain sharp. Channels hold their depth. Shorelines keep their positions. The city’s oldest people remember nearly the same water marks as the youngest. This creates no single philosophy. Some believe the world is complete and human beings are its only unfinished part. Some believe the land is waiting for people to bring motion into it. Others fear that all accumulated change has merely been delayed and will arrive at once.

Survey towers are staffed partly for this reason. Their watchers record clouds, rain density, spring flow, visibility, condensation, and the rare fall of loose stone. Most days yield nothing unusual. The act of watching is still honored. A tower keeper may spend decades documenting a mountain face that never visibly changes. Such a life is not considered wasted; steadfast attention is itself a civic service.

Children are drawn to the towers. They search the still basins for impossible ripples and the empty sky for movement. Many go through a stage of inventing animals. They fold reeds into hopping shapes, give clay jars rows of legs, or make whistles that imitate calls no one has heard. Adults preserve some of these objects. The oldest public halls contain shelves of imagined beings: flyers with human fingers for feathers, many-footed marsh walkers, stone-eaters with grinding mouths, little companions designed to sleep against the body and make a comforting sound.

These inventions reveal a longing the people do not always confess. Humans can be surrounded by other humans and still feel the singularity of their kind. A solitary worker in a reed bed knows that every intentional movement on the entire horizon must come from someone like them. There is no harmless life that will approach without speaking, no wild gaze, no creature indifferent to civic obligation. Their stories therefore return again and again to the idea of companionship without language.

Social life is close despite the city’s spaciousness. Kitchens are commonly shared by several households. Work courts remain open, and covered walks provide places to sit during rain. Privacy exists, but prolonged isolation attracts concern. People announce journeys, even short ones beyond the outer gardens, by hanging a marked cord at the local hall. On returning, they untie it. An unexplained cord left hanging overnight can mobilize an entire district.

Rescue is one of the city’s most practiced collective arts. Marsh ground can give way, paving can become slick, and a person alone may be unable to lift a fallen stone or escape deep mud. Long poles, woven slings, floating reed bundles, and carrying litters stand at intervals along the causeways. Since there are no animals to threaten anyone, danger comes from terrain, injury, illness, fire, and other people. Their laws place severe judgment on anyone who refuses reasonable aid.

Conflict exists. So do envy, cruelty, ambition, theft, and resentment. The calm of the world has not made its inhabitants uniformly gentle. Water access can be manipulated. Measurements can be falsified. A household may hoard seed stock or conceal rot in a communal store. Lovers betray one another; builders take pride beyond their skill; surveyors defend old records against inconvenient evidence. Violence is especially shocking because its sounds have no natural counterpart. A shout or blow can stop an entire courtyard.

Punishments tend toward restitution, supervised labor, loss of custodial authority, or exclusion from particular civic decisions. Permanent banishment is rare and regarded as perilously close to execution. Imprisonment is used sparingly, since maintaining an idle body burdens everyone. For the worst offenses, a person may become “untrusted at water”: forbidden to work alone near gates, cisterns, channels, or measurements. This is both practical restriction and profound disgrace.

Their art favors pattern, interval, and surface. Murals depict water lines, mountain fractures, cords, hands, terraces, leaves, human faces, and crowds seen from above. Sculptors work in clay, wood, soft stone, and packed fiber. Bodies are represented frequently, not from vanity alone but because the human form is the world’s only known animal form. Dancers study posture and movement with extraordinary precision. A bent wrist, shifted heel, or turn of the head can carry meanings that elsewhere might be assigned to masks of beasts or birds.

Music uses voices, drums of stretched woven membrane rather than hide, slit wood, stone bars, clay resonators, seed rattles, reed pipes, and water-filled vessels. Instruments are soft-toned. Performances take place under rain shelters, where dripping water joins without rhythm. Silence is treated as part of composition, sometimes for longer than visitors would expect. Listeners know not to assume the piece has ended.

Time is counted through human and cultivated cycles more than seasonal ones. Plantings, fermentations, pregnancies, apprenticeships, plaster curing, and the filling of storage houses form the calendar. Rain provides little distinction. The passage of years is marked by named cohorts, repaired roofs, and generations of measured sameness. People celebrate first tasks: a child’s first carried bowl, first crossed causeway alone, first straight cord laid, first successful graft, first night spent keeping a reservoir gauge.

Names often derive from places and acts rather than animals or weather events. A person might be called for a particular spring, a clay color, an ancestral tool, a terrace, a quality of light, or the manner in which water passed on the day of their birth. Adults sometimes acquire a second name after completing an apprenticeship or taking responsibility for a public work.

They value maintenance more than novelty, but invention occurs everywhere in small, legible forms: a stronger weave, a cleaner-burning fuel cake, a better joint between tile and plaster, a new arrangement of carrying poles, a bean trained to climb a wet wall. Inventors are expected to demonstrate not only that something works, but what it changes around itself. The highest praise is not “new.” It is “well-entered”—something introduced into the existing balance without hidden harm.

From afar, these people may seem as fixed as their city. Up close, they are not. They gossip while cutting reeds. They decorate the undersides of roof beams where rain cannot wash the pigment away. They compete over whose garden produces the straightest grain stalks. They fall in love on long surveying walks. They complain about damp bedding, bland meals, careless apprentices, and committees that watch a proposed wall for ten years before allowing three courses to be built. They make jokes about tower keepers who record the same cloud twice.

Their children leap channels adults cross by bridge. Their adolescents explore abandoned paths and return covered in black mud. Their elders sit beneath eaves, polishing old wooden tools and noticing minute shifts no one else believes are real.

They are a people for whom humanity is not one living presence among many. It is the only presence that answers back. This makes them careful with one another, though not always kind; communal, though not without loneliness; conservative in construction, yet extravagant in imagination. They inhabit the plateau as both citizens and witnesses, carrying all known restlessness within their own bodies.

Across the broad wet shelf, every lifted basket, every planted seed, every argument, birth, footstep, and song belongs to them. Against the untouched mountains and the unchanging water, they are the world’s principal motion—and they know, at least dimly, that if they fail to keep moving, almost nothing else will.
