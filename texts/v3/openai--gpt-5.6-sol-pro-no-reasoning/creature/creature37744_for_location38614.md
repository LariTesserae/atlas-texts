The people who live beneath the salt call themselves, in most translations, **the Maintainers of the Interval**.

The name is sometimes mistaken for grandeur. It is closer to a job description.

They believe a human being is not defined by speech, tools, memory, or ancestry. Many beasts possess one or more of those. A person is the creature that can keep a small space empty on purpose.

Every morning they prove it.

Before eating, before greeting visitors, often before fully waking, each household walks the perimeter of its dwelling with hooked knives and bowls of hot ash. Overnight, exploratory threads have entered the seams. Pale feelers test the roof lashings. Buds no larger than fingernails press from beneath the bitter-bark floor. These are cut, cauterized, scraped away, and carried outside in lidded vessels. If thrown down carelessly, the clippings root.

Thus their villages are not built once. They are continuously subtracted from the world.

They are small, dense-bodied people, though no two communities look quite alike. Their ribs are broad and their necks heavily muscled from a lifetime of receiving speech through the forehead and jaw. The smallest bones of the ear are often damaged in childhood, and profound deafness is common by middle age. It is not considered a great disability. Hearing, in their understanding, includes vibration through feet, teeth, fingers, tools, and architecture. A deaf elder resting one palm against a root bridge may perceive an approaching herd sooner than a child with perfect ears.

Their feet are wide and unusually dexterous. Most can grip a narrow stem between the first two toes, and hardened pads extend along the outer edges for climbing tilted surfaces. Infants cling before they walk. Children learn not “up” and “down” but **toward the load**, **away from the load**, **with the grain**, and **across the living grain**. A wall is merely a road whose habits differ.

They wear close-fitted layers of cured membrane, shell scales, and braided hair. Loose garments are dangerous: seeds lodge in folds, barbs catch them, and small imitators enter them seeking warmth. Travelers shave much of their bodies and paint exposed skin with pungent mineral grease. They leave the crown of the head covered by a stiff crest or hood, partly to deflect falling husks and partly to spoil the silhouette sought by overhead predators.

Nothing worn is purely decorative. Beads rattle in patterns identifying family and direction of travel. Shell plates change pitch when cracked. Neck rings carry emergency resin, salt, and tinder. Funeral cords double as climbing lines. Beauty lies in making necessities answer several needs at once.

Their settlements seldom contain more than a few hundred people. Larger populations generate too much heat, refuse, breath, and rhythmic disturbance; the surrounding life begins to converge upon them. A prosperous village therefore divides. Half its households take an ember from the common scorch-pit, a length of the settlement rhythm, and one knot from the communal map, then travel until they find a scar that can be enlarged and defended.

Villages are temporary, but lineage is not.

Genealogies are kept as braided cords whose materials encode marriages, migrations, collapses, and divisions. A length of grazers’ hair means descent through a migration year. A glassy seed-edge records a birth during fan-grove detonation. Black knots mark settlements lost so completely that no body could be recovered. Such cords may be centuries old, although no original fiber remains. They are repaired strand by strand, and the continuity lies in the pattern.

People inherit obligations through these cords: a duty to exchange resin with one settlement, to shelter the descendants of another, to avoid hunting a particular herd, or to maintain a soundbreak along a path their ancestors opened. Kinship is consequently geographical without being fixed. One may be “a cousin by the swallowed bridge” to someone living many leagues away.

Speech has two forms. The intimate language is pressed through bone: foreheads touching, fingers laid along the speaker’s throat or jaw. It is rich in fine distinctions and difficult to conceal. Emotion enters directly through tremor, tension, pauses, swallowed sounds. Lovers may converse beneath the surrounding roar without making an audible noise. So may conspirators.

Public language is percussion.

Drum phrases identify settlements, announce safe passage, warn of collapse, claim carcasses, call lost travelers, and negotiate with animals. A person’s public name is a short rhythm, chosen during adolescence after teachers determine which beats local predators ignore. Names may become unusable when a vine population begins growing toward them or a mimic learns to reproduce them. Renaming is therefore ordinary and carries no shame.

Silence is different. Silence is never merely an absence of sound.

A child who suddenly ceases making contact-noise in the forest is presumed taken, trapped, or watched. Travelers tap their teeth, click tongue against palate, or keep a seed rattle moving so companions know where they are. Predators create quiet; people create pattern. To stop without warning is a grave breach of trust.

Yet silence is also sacred because the whole country sometimes enters it together.

Those rare intervals are called **the Held Breath**. During one, nobody strikes a tool, speaks through bone, or moves unless falling. Children are trained for years to master the terror of stillness. Afterward, witnesses describe the direction in which the animals faced, the pressure felt in their teeth, and any change in the nearest walls. These accounts are preserved more carefully than genealogies.

The people disagree about what opens the untouched stone.

Some say the buried country is one organism with countless temporary bodies, and the openings are acts of reproduction. Some say two living worlds are eating toward each other through the planet. Some believe the far side is the future, sending backward whatever can survive. Others hold that the apparent wall does not conceal another country at all—that attention itself becomes fertile when enough life turns toward one place.

The oldest doctrine is simpler:

**Stone is what life has not yet persuaded.**

Priests, where they exist, are called Listeners, but their function is practical as much as spiritual. They inspect strain groans, interpret chorus changes, judge whether unfamiliar rhythms are animal, structural, or deliberate, and decide when a settlement must move. A good Listener can distinguish a roof preparing to settle from a pillar merely thickening. A bad one may kill a village.

They carry staffs containing several live fibers under restraint. The fibers twist toward heat, fractures, particular mineral salts, or concentrated breath. Reading them is less like consulting a compass than managing a handful of impatient snakes.

Leadership belongs neither wholly to priests nor hunters. Most settlements are governed by three offices:

- the **Keeper of Ash**, who maintains dead zones, tools, fires, and food stores;
- the **Reader of Strain**, who oversees paths, supports, and evacuation;
- the **Beat-Holder**, who controls public signals and relations with neighboring settlements.

No one person may hold two offices. The danger is not tyranny in the surface sense, but resonance: one mistaken judgment repeated across every part of communal life. Their politics distrust anything too coordinated.

The same distrust shapes their art. Perfect repetition is considered crude and menacing. Songs change one beat each cycle. Patterns in woven walls contain deliberate faults. Carvings fork before resolving. A skilled artist establishes expectation, lets it begin to recruit the listener’s body, then diverts it before the surrounding plants can learn.

Music is never wholly under human control. Resonant vines answer horns. Bladder-plants take up drum pulses and return them hours later, slowed and deepened. Fliers add shrieks at regular intervals. A performance may migrate out of its makers’ possession and travel for leagues through responsive vegetation. Famous compositions are known not by authorship but by where they were last heard alive.

Their food is mostly dry, oily, resinous, and intensely flavored. They roast mineral bulbs in sealed pits, grind shells and seed glass into bitter meal, eat sheets of symbiotic growth peeled from grazers, and collect fat from animals that store hydrocarbons in bladders along the spine. Drinking is infrequent. The body obtains much of its necessary fluid from waxy fruits, rendered tissues, and metabolic pastes. Open water, when found trapped in stone or old organs, is handled with alarm. It spoils materials, awakens dormant growth, and encourages unfamiliar decay.

To offer someone liquid water is either extraordinary hospitality or a threat.

Fire is equally ambiguous. It creates the dead material on which settlements depend, but it consumes the same air people and plants require and can trigger explosive resin vents. Flame is kept small, hot, and enclosed. Hearths are ceramic-lined organs made from fired resin and shell. Children are taught to tend an ember before they are trusted with a blade.

Ash is wealth. It preserves boundaries, discourages grafting, reveals footprints, and keeps certain parasites from recognizing warm flesh. Every household maintains an ash genealogy alongside its human one: labels indicating which plants, bones, and structures were burned to produce a particular batch, since different ashes repel different growths.

Death requires speed.

A corpse cannot be left unattended. Within minutes, pollen threads gather in the mouth and nostrils. Scavenging blossoms attach at the joints. Rootlets enter old scars. The dead are taken to a scorch-pit and reduced as completely as fuel allows. Close kin inhale a little smoke through filtering cloth, accepting the last dangerous trace of the person into living bodies. The remaining ash is used to mark thresholds.

Those who die far from settlement are not always recovered. Their companions may instead plant a rhythm: a mechanical clapper assembled from nearby stems and tuned to the dead person’s name. It sounds until the forest absorbs it. Years later, travelers sometimes hear altered fragments of these memorial beats emerging from distant bladder-vines.

The people do not agree whether this means remembrance or digestion.

Their children grow up amid constant hazard, but not constant fear. They play at identifying creatures hidden as fruit, race along low rootways, and dare one another to touch harmless feeding fans before they close. They keep shell-gnawers as pets, paint patterns on their backs, and wager on which will cross a heat-blackened circle first. They build tiny villages and allow vines to destroy them, awarding points for how long the clearings remain open.

The first adult ordeal is called **Making Room**.

An adolescent receives an unworked patch of dense undergrowth and must create a space large enough to lie down in, then keep it clear through one full local chorus. Cutting alone is insufficient. They must redirect growth, bargain with useful species, establish repellent borders, vent heat, avoid attracting grazers, and sleep within the clearing without being enclosed. At the end, elders inspect not the emptiness but the living edges.

A cleanly murdered perimeter is judged immature. It wastes fuel and provokes invasion.

The ideal interval is held by understanding: one vine shading another, a scent that turns gnawers aside, a trained root carrying strain around the hollow, a clatter that discourages feeding mats. Adulthood means learning that space is not won by defeating life, but by setting its hungers against one another.

This is also how they make roads.

Path crews travel ahead with hooked poles, ash blowers, bait animals, tuned clappers, and rolls of membrane. They do not clear straight routes. They find tensions already present and widen them: the dry gap between rival colonies, the wake of a grazing herd, the unrooted underside of a migrating forest. A path is considered a temporary alliance among appetites. It may last an hour or three generations.

Guides are valued above warriors. They memorize not locations but tendencies: which bridge is tightening, which wall has begun to taste iron, which canopy brightens before buckling, which herd is likely to turn when the western booming changes. They read woven maps by stretching them over their knees and altering the tension according to recent reports.

The best guides are called **Those Who Return Elsewhere**, because a successful journey rarely ends at the place from which it began.

War exists, though armies in the surface sense do not. Large gatherings attract too much attention, and a battlefield would become impassable under the biological response to heat, blood, vibration, and damaged vegetation. Conflict is conducted through ecology.

Enemies alter migratory beats, seed roads with adhesive blossoms, attract mineral-borers beneath foundations, or introduce growth into an opponent’s stored dead stalks. They may teach a mimic a rival village’s name-rhythm so that false signals spread through the vaults. The most feared act is to break a soundbreak at night, exposing sleepers to the full disorienting roar before sending predators through the clearing.

For this reason, the gravest crimes are called **misdirections**. Murder destroys a person. A misdirection may cause a settlement to destroy itself.

Their warriors are usually counter-listeners: people trained to detect false rhythms and ecological manipulation. They wear mismatched armor so no repeated outline attracts coordinated attack. Their weapons include horn hooks, resin darts, vibrating saws, and seed cases selected to explode at different pitches. Few weapons kill cleanly. Most alter footing, sever support, lure growth, or make a route suddenly desirable to something larger.

They do not hate the forest.

Hatred would imply opposition, and opposition would imply that humans stand outside it. They know they are made from the same mineral oils, salts, waxes, and borrowed carbon as everything around them. Their homes are scars, but scars are living structures too. Even ash eventually enters roots.

What distinguishes them is neither dominion nor exile. It is maintenance: the daily, exhausting, ingenious insistence that not every possibility must be filled at once.

This belief draws some of them toward the unfinished depths.

These explorers are regarded with a mixture of reverence and suspicion. They cut their public name-rhythms short before departure, leaving the final beat in the care of kin. If they return, they complete the rhythm. If not, the unfinished name is never sounded again.

They travel lightly, carrying deformable maps, ash in wax bladders, strain fibers, shell blades, and little cages containing organisms from the settled vaults. The cages are not provisions. They are witnesses. Explorers expose them to new air and watch how they turn, molt, sing, root, or die.

Those who return from the lowest chambers are often changed in ways that are difficult to name. Some develop aversions to familiar chorus patterns. Some insist that the light below anticipated their movements. One woman returned with a pale shoot growing from beneath her sternum, its leaves opening whenever anyone lied within reach. She lived for many years and became a judge. After her death, the shoot rooted in the scorch-pit and could not be burned.

Another expedition claimed to have crossed through one of the inward-growing walls during a Held Breath.

Only three members returned. They disagreed about what lay beyond.

The first said there was another living country, equally vast, whose organisms were built around hollowness rather than substance. Its forests were networks of empty tubes, and its animals moved as cavities through flesh.

The second said they had entered the same country from the far side and watched their own departure happening at a great distance.

The third would communicate only by pressing her forehead to the ground. She maintained that there was no other side. There was merely a place where the world had not yet decided which organism was perceiving which.

All three agreed on one detail.

Something had noticed the cleared spaces around human settlements.

Since then, the walls have begun producing imitations.

Far from any village, travelers find circles in which growth has withdrawn from the center. Around them stand low bulges shaped like huts. Fibers resembling woven maps hang inside. Root clusters knock out almost-correct settlement rhythms. Figures made of bark and pale membrane sit forehead to forehead beneath false soundbreaks, trembling as if in conversation.

These places are never identical to human homes. The errors are subtle. Doorways close when approached. Floors breathe in unison. The ash is not ash but dormant dust. The figures have too many joints, or none beneath their clothing. Their throat vibrations contain no words—only exquisitely reproduced hesitation.

The Maintainers burn such places when they can.

But some Listeners oppose this. They say imitation is the beginning of negotiation. If the buried country is learning to make emptiness, then humanity may no longer be alone in its defining labor.

Others answer that it is learning to make bait.

Meanwhile the settlements continue their work. Roofs are cut back. Paths are recut. Maps are loosened and retied. Children master their first warning beats. Craftspeople scorch disobedient handles and coax blades from living seed glass. Guides lean against bridges and feel tomorrow’s migrations gathering in the grain.

Around them, the immeasurable country feeds, excavates, flashes, hunts, grafts, and sings.

And within that abundance the people preserve their little clearings—not truly empty, never safe, and never permanent, but open for one more breath.
