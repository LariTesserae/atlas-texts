The people who live there are compact, deliberate people—not necessarily small in body, but practiced in taking up little warmth, little water, little unnecessary space. They sit close without apology. They sleep several to a room, work shoulder to shoulder in low chambers, and pass one another sideways in passages worn smooth by generations. Solitude is known mainly to herders, messengers, channel walkers, and the very old. Privacy is architectural rather than absolute: a curtain of felt, a turn in a stair, a sleeping niche whose opening can be covered by a hide.

Their bodies bear the basin plainly. They are broad-chested from the altitude, strong-legged from stairs and tilted roads, often slightly stooped from roofed passages and low doors. Their hands are fissured by cold, stone dust, alkali water, and grease. Fine scars mark the knuckles and palms of almost everyone who works outdoors. Faces weather early on the exposed shelves; cheeks darken under hard sun and wind, while the skin around the eyes stays pale from habitual squinting. Many have a slow, deep way of breathing that visitors mistake for calm.

Their clothing makes them appear heavier than they are. Layers of felted hair, woven wool, hide, and quilted scraps overlap like masonry. Garments are rarely cut away when worn; they are patched, lined, turned inside out, divided among children, and finally shredded for mortar fiber, bedding, or lamp wicks. Rank is visible less in brilliance than in fit: a prosperous person owns clothing shaped closely enough not to drag, catch, or waste warmth. Water workers wear waxed shoulder capes. Masons bind their forearms. Messengers favor short layered coats and high gaiters. Herders carry the strong animal smell of smoke, milk, lanolin, and dung fuel.

Color survives in narrow places. Cuffs may be dyed rust-red, mineral yellow, lichen green, or the deep blue obtained through costly trade. Braids carry beads of horn, carved stone, shell from lands many months away, and small clay seals identifying a household or water association. Because heavy outer clothes conceal the body, identity gathers around the head and hands: hats, wrapping styles, hair bindings, rings, tattoos at the wrist, scars deliberately whitened with ash.

They do not think of themselves as inhabitants of one city. A person belongs first to a particular stair, court, cistern, channel branch, herd circuit, shrine wall, or roof cluster. Asked where she comes from, she may name the hour at which her neighborhood receives water. Asked where he lives, he may answer, “Below the red spillway,” or “Three roofs above the pigeon bridge.” Their geography is relational. So is their identity.

Children learn vertical language before distant directions. They know upstream and downstream, sunward and shadowward, windward and sheltered, upper door and lower door. They are taught which walls may be touched, which gutters must never be stepped across, which cistern lids can bear weight, and where to crouch when the ground begins to move. A child who wastes food is corrected; one who wastes water is frightened with stories. Pouring clean water onto bare ground is used in curses and funerals, never play.

Their toys imitate maintenance. They build tiny retaining walls from chips of slate, cut miniature channels in dust, and barter pebbles as water tokens. They herd beetles through model gates. After rain, when adults are occupied with roofs and tanks, children launch reed slivers into the gutters and run through covered lanes to see where they emerge. Many acquire an intuitive understanding of gradients before they can count.

Most people work at several trades because life cannot depend on one skill. A mason may also keep pigeons, repair cistern seals, and own grazing rights in three scattered places. A household known for weaving may send two members each year with the herds. Water stewards farm terraces; shrine keepers record debts; messengers carry medicines and gossip as well as decisions. Specialization exists, but no one is permitted the illusion that their specialty stands alone.

Masons enjoy a grave, practical respect. They are trained to listen as much as to look. By striking a wall with wood, horn, or stone, they hear loose faces, hidden cavities, trapped water, and fractures traveling deep into old construction. The finest among them can read generations in a cross-section: fire-blackened domestic stone, a sealed doorway, a former gutter, the bones of an animal placed beneath a threshold. They seldom demolish. They unmake carefully, numbering usable stones in chalk or clay, then rebuild with the old wall’s intentions in mind.

Water workers possess a different authority. They walk the channels before dawn, breaking skin ice, clearing grit, plugging losses with clay and fiber. Their occupation is both technical and judicial. They must know slopes almost too slight to see, the thirst of each district, the language of agreements, and the long genealogy of every disputed gate. They carry calibrated vessels nested one inside another, wax tablets, cords marked with knots, and small hammers for sounding cistern levels. Some shave their fingertips or keep one nail long to test the speed and depth of flow.

They are watched carefully. A water steward may receive gifts, but never casually. Meals offered during an inspection are witnessed. Tokens pass in public. A steward whose measurements are repeatedly questioned can become socially untouchable long before formal judgment. Conversely, those known for fairness may be asked to settle marriages, inheritance disputes, and grazing conflicts, on the principle that anyone who can divide a trickle without bloodshed understands justice.

Herders live both within and beyond the districts. They depart through low gates with animals packed close around them and return months later carrying news from half the basin. Their knowledge is expansive where urban knowledge is dense. They recognize distant weather in the behavior of carrion birds, know which temporary pools have bitter water, and remember slopes by taste and wind. Many maintain sleeping rights in dozens of refuge houses, each containing some tiny contribution from earlier journeys: a twist of fuel, a repaired latch, a scratched warning about wolves.

Their animals are not pets, though affection exists everywhere. A favorite milk-beast may be sung to and then slaughtered when age makes winter impossible. Horns are carved with ownership patterns. Newborns sleep indoors during severe cold, sometimes beside children. Herding families know individual temperaments within masses that outsiders perceive as indistinguishable. They breed for footing, endurance, useful hair, and the memory of routes. An animal that reliably leads others home may be worth more than one that produces twice the milk.

The dead remain within the architecture. Soil is too precious and workable ground too scarce for broad cemeteries. Bodies may be exposed at high stone platforms to birds and cold, then the cleaned bones gathered into wall chambers, foundation niches, or communal ossuaries. Some districts grind a little ancestral bone into threshold plaster; others consider this abhorrent and preserve every fragment. Names are recited when major repairs expose an old burial cavity. Thus a house is not merely inherited from ancestors. It is, in places, built around them.

Funerals are restrained in flame and abundant in water imagery. An empty cup may be passed from hand to hand. A channel gate may remain closed for the span of a prayer, allowing water to pool momentarily behind it before being released. The dead are described as having gone “below hearing,” “beyond the last marker,” or “into the stone’s keeping.” Herders sometimes say they have gone above the snow instead.

Religion belongs to acts of continuation. Shrines are found at springs, channel divisions, dangerous stairs, fault lines, warm vents, and places where travelers first see a distant ice range. Offerings are small because waste is not holy: a twist of clean hair, a bead, a spoonful of milk, a polished pebble carried from another watershed. Water itself is rarely offered. To spill it merely to demonstrate devotion would offend many local gods.

There is no single theology across the basin. One district treats springs as the mouths of sleeping beings; another says water is ancestral breath; another rejects personification and venerates the agreements that keep the channel open. Shrine keepers argue without expecting uniformity. What they share is suspicion of anything claiming sole ownership over a source. Water may be held, timed, inherited, and guarded, but absolute possession is thought both false and dangerous.

Their law is spoken, walked, and engineered. Boundaries are demonstrated by following them. Claims are proved through maintenance: who cleared the upper tunnel, who repaired the snow wall, whose grandparents replaced the aqueduct pier after the great shaking. Neglect weakens ownership. A family that fails to contribute labor may retain a ceremonial claim but lose its useful share. This makes labor a form of memory and memory a form of title.

Councils meet in covered courts where everyone can hear water, animals, or wind nearby. Speakers are expected to state from what level they speak: household, stair, district, channel, herd association, or shrine. A person may represent one interest in the morning and oppose it in the afternoon under another obligation. Outsiders find this duplicitous. The inhabitants regard it as accurate. No one is only one thing.

Arguments are quiet until they are not. Public shouting is associated with panic, drunkenness, or inability to marshal witnesses. Skilled disputants recite measures, precedents, kinship links, and old maintenance duties in flat voices. Insults often accuse someone of faulty gradients: “He pours uphill,” “Her gate opens only inward,” “They build on frost.” The worst condemnation is that a person consumes what others maintained without adding to it.

Wealth is similarly distributed through rights and reliabilities. A rich household may own few decorative objects but possess shares in several cisterns, winter access to a warm spring, trusted animals, roof space facing south, and obligations owed along distant roads. Stores matter: grain, salt, grease, dried curd, fiber, dung cakes, seed. Yet stores can rot, burn, freeze, or be stolen. The deepest wealth is being remembered favorably in many places.

Messengers embody this wealth. They are lean, weather-literate, and socially ambiguous, carrying sealed decisions through jurisdictions that recognize no common ruler. They memorize alternate routes for every season and may maintain several names for use in different regions. Their arrival can fill a court. They bring marriages, deaths, channel failures, new songs, judgments, disease warnings, and requests made so long ago that the requester has died.

Because written materials are scarce, memory is cultivated deliberately. Records are scratched into stone, pressed into clay, tied in cords, cut as notches into horn, and encoded in recitations. Important agreements exist in several forms at once. A channel treaty may be a tablet stored in a shrine, a row of marks on the gate itself, and a song whose pauses indicate durations. No version is considered sufficient alone.

Songs travel better than laws. The basin’s music is adapted to stone chambers and thin air: short phrases, narrow ranges, voices that interlock rather than soar. Long sustained notes are admired precisely because breath is costly. Work songs coordinate dragging, tamping, and lifting. Water songs count intervals. Children sing echo games beneath arches. Herders use piercing calls that seem small at the mouth and enormous when returned by cliffs.

Instruments are compact: horn flutes, skin drums, clappers of slate, strings stretched over shallow bowls, bone whistles whose notes carry through snow. Large drums are rare and politically charged; their sound travels into neighboring districts and may be interpreted as alarm or summons. Bells belong mostly to animals. People know the condition of an approaching herd by its uneven metal and bone music.

Meals are hot when possible, though fires are small. Barley appears as porridge, flat cakes cooked against heated stone, fermented drink, and roasted grain carried on journeys. Peas and roots thicken stews. Bitter greens are valued after winter with an intensity visitors find hard to understand. Milk is soured, dried, churned, and stored in forms graded by age and salt. Meat is less a daily luxury than an episodic abundance organized around slaughter, preservation, and distribution.

Steam is cherished. People cup bowls beneath their faces before drinking. Communal kitchens are among the city’s most social rooms, warm with bodies and animal heat even when the fire is little more than embers. Gossip gathers there, as do courtships, apprenticeships, and quiet bargains. A household unable to contribute fuel may bring labor, news, dung, or water-carrying rights. No one eats for free, but payment can take years to complete.

Hospitality begins with shelter, not food. A guest is shown where drafts enter, where to place wet clothing, which floor opening carries warmth from animals below, and which vessel may be used. Water is offered in measured quantity without apology. To give too much would suggest either ostentation or a desire to incur debt. A truly honored guest may receive a basin in which to wash both hands at once.

Beauty is associated with precision under constraint. A perfectly fitted lintel, a gutter that releases water in an unbroken thread, a repaired garment whose seams align, a herd turning together on a steep slope—these draw admiration. Ornament often emphasizes joints, edges, and transitions. Carvings surround cistern mouths and thresholds. Patterns mark where one labor meets another.

Their humor is dry, patient, and fond of impossible extravagance. Stories feature fools who heat whole rooms, build level roads, trust maps viewed from above, or marry into families upstream without asking the hour of release. People joke during hardship not by denying it but by making scarcity absurdly larger. A generous host may apologize for serving “the basin’s last pea.” A child carrying a small jar is hailed as keeper of the inland sea.

Love is shaped by proximity and obligation. Courtship may begin through shared night watches at a channel, seasonal work in a garden chamber, or repeated meetings along a messenger route. Lovers exchange useful objects: repaired gloves, seed packets, sharpened tools, a route description copied accurately. Poetry compares the beloved not to flowers, which are brief and uncommon, but to sheltered walls, returning animals, clear channels, and sun reaching a winter room.

Marriage can join water rights, labor duties, sleeping space, and herd access, so it is rarely private even when affection is strong. People may belong to multiple households over a lifetime through fostering, widowhood, apprenticeship, debt, or migration. Children are often raised by a court or stair more than by two parents alone. The question “Whose child?” may have several correct answers.

Old people are repositories, but not automatically authorities. Memory must remain usable. Elders who can recite obsolete boundaries yet refuse to learn a shifted route are teased as “maps of buried doors.” Those who remember not only what was agreed but why are prized. In winter they teach inside, repairing tools while telling layered histories in which the building itself serves as mnemonic: this crack marks an earthquake; that sealed arch, an epidemic; those dark stones, a fire; this widened stair, a reconciliation.

Illness exposes the basin’s crowded interdependence. Respiratory sickness moves swiftly through covered districts. Quarantine gates can divide passages, though compliance depends on stored food and trusted messengers. Healers use bitter herbs, warmed stones, fermented preparations, splints, pressure, and careful drainage. They understand frost injury, altitude sickness, infected cuts, animal-borne disease, and the dangers of bad air in sealed rooms. Some treatments are effective, some ceremonial, and most are both.

Birth takes place in the warmest available chamber, often near animals or a spring-fed floor. Newborns are rubbed with grease, wrapped tightly, and introduced to the household by touching their feet to a threshold stone. Naming may wait until the child has survived a set number of nights. Names refer to ancestors, weather, springs, routes, birds, repairs, or events noticed at birth. A child might be called First Melt, Wall-Listener, Swift-Shadow, or Returned Gate without anyone finding the name poetic.

Outsiders do come: salt traders, lowland farmers, metalworkers, pilgrims, fugitives, and envoys from polities that insist on imagining the basin as one kingdom. They are struck by the scale of habitation and frustrated by the absence of a central authority to bribe, threaten, or persuade. They ask who rules the city and receive different answers: the channels; winter; the councils; no one; everyone upstream.

Some outsiders stay. The basin has ways of absorbing them. First they learn not to block passages. Then they learn to ask before touching water. Eventually they acquire obligations, and obligations make them local. Accent may persist for generations, but belonging is measured in maintenance. A person born elsewhere who has cleared the same channel for thirty years is less foreign than a native-born heir who returns only to claim a share.

At the settlement edges live those whose belonging is uncertain: seasonal laborers, dispossessed families, itinerant craftspeople, escaped debtors, shepherds between herds, and people expelled from more protected districts. They occupy windier structures near roads and slaughter grounds, where walls are younger and rights less secure. These communities are often the first to trade with strangers and the first blamed for theft or contamination. Yet they also repair routes, carry warnings, and build new districts when old ones grow too dense.

There are thieves, smugglers, and water cutters. Theft tends toward the small and essential: fuel, grain, waxed leather, stored seed, a few minutes of illicit flow. Water theft is feared because it can be hidden by distance. A channel may be tapped far upstream, leaving victims to suspect leakage, weather, or one another. Investigators read damp seams, mineral deposits, displaced markers, and the growth of moss. The most accomplished thieves understand hydraulics as well as stewards do.

Violence occurs, but it is constrained by the danger of damaging shared systems. Feuds may be fierce yet carefully bounded: a person attacked away from a channel, animals seized but not scattered, doors marked rather than burned. Fire-setting is treated as monstrous. Deliberately poisoning a cistern can unite enemies across whole watersheds. The worst criminals may be denied refuge houses and road fuel, a sentence that makes the landscape itself the executioner.

No army could easily hold the basin. Each shelf contains choke points, hidden stairs, sealed stores, and inhabitants who know how to redirect water or open winter routes. Armed groups exist—district guards, herd escorts, pass wardens—but warfare is more often a struggle over movement and supply than open battle. A conqueror might take a square and discover that its water arrives from a tunnel controlled four districts away, whose gate depends on labor from a village beyond another range.

The people therefore admire endurance more than victory. Their heroes finish things: a channel after fifty years, a peace after seven generations, a staircase begun by someone whose name is lost. Legends concern those who found routes, kept gates open during freezing nights, led herds home through dust, or held a cracked wall long enough for sleeping families to escape. The miraculous usually takes the form of successful maintenance under impossible conditions.

And around them lives the more-than-human city.

Pigeons inhabit cornices with the confidence of hereditary citizens. Swifts turn courtyards into wells of motion. Owls peer from marked cavities, tolerated as quiet workers. Beetles, mice, silverfish, moths, and grain larvae accompany every storehouse despite seals and cats. Children know the tiny clicking sounds of insects inside walls. Cats move between households according to hunting rather than ownership and may be fed by an entire passage.

The great grazing animals are the city’s circulating body. They turn sparse grass into milk, fiber, heat, and fuel, then carry human claims across the empty reaches between districts. Their dung warms rooms and feeds gardens; their hair becomes roofs and clothes; their horn becomes tools that move the stones used to shelter them. They are wealth, labor, food, weather sense, and memory on narrow feet.

The wild cliff animals remain a troublesome parallel people. They leap walls no mason intended as paths, invade terraces, and stand on high roofs staring down into courtyards. Foxes haunt the margins and learn slaughter schedules. Wolves follow at a greater distance, appearing in stories as embodiments of hunger without obligation. Carrion birds are both cleaners and funerary witnesses, unwelcome over lambing grounds but sacred near exposure platforms.

Even stone is not understood as inert. It shifts, rings, sweats, fractures, stores heat, catches frost, and remembers pressure. Builders speak of cooperative and stubborn blocks. Channels grow inward with minerals as though healing themselves shut. Old walls lean together. Floors settle around generations of feet. The people do not worship all stone, but they live too intimately with it to call it dead.

What lives there, then, is not merely a vast population distributed across a cold basin. It is a mesh of dependencies dense enough to function as an organism: human breath, animal heat, bird colonies, seeds, dung, frost, stone, and narrow water moving by fractions of a degree.

Its inhabitants survive not because they master the high country, but because they rarely ask it for anything all at once. They take warmth by layers, water by measures, food by seasons, shelter by accumulated walls. They inherit incomplete work and expect to leave it incomplete. Each generation adds a course of stone, clears a length of channel, repairs a route, teaches a child where the water goes.

Their deepest virtue is continuance.

Their deepest fear is interruption.
