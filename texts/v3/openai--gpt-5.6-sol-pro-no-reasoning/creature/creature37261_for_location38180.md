What lives there is not a collection of species so much as a single ecology divided into parts.

The copper rosettes, the black seam-tufts, the ceramic beads on their conductive hairs: these are its slow tissues, exchanging charges too slight to register as appetite. The grazers are their moving edges. The hunters are corrections. The pale sacs in wire cages are reservoirs. Even the apparently useless machines belong to this anatomy. Compressor, flywheel, relays, transformers, columns, animals, and air participate in one closed metabolism whose only excretion is noise—and even that cannot escape.

Its inhabitants are creatures of resonance rather than choice. They do not listen across the chamber. Each listens inward, through a membrane, fork, pit, or whisker tuned to one component of the permanent din. A relay’s chatter may be daylight to one species; the transformer’s bellow, a breeding season to another; the tap of a hidden striker, a road. Their senses do not describe a world. They issue instructions.

A long caliper-limbed animal folded around a casing is therefore less like a beast at rest than a gauge made of flesh. Its joints open by fixed degrees. Its hooked mouth tests the metal at predetermined points. When a particular harmonic passes through the housing, fluids shift along its narrow body and a dry juvenile separates from a seam in its thorax. The juvenile begins the same inspection one column away. Neither learns the route. The route is present in the vibration before either animal exists.

The blind grazers are similarly exact. They scrape bronze film with glass combs, but never enough to exhaust it. Their mouths remove precisely what the electrical colonies replace. Their tracks look worn, yet do not deepen. Seen briefly, they resemble livestock. Watched longer, they resemble machining heads: organic tools traversing the same cut without finishing it.

The roof-hung creatures are stranger. Their bundled bodies contain successive versions of themselves. Folded young emerge at one end and travel predetermined circuits through the columns, feeding, enlarging, perhaps hunting or being hunted, until they return as adults to the other end and are taken back into the parent mass. It is uncertain whether these are generations at all. They may be one animal circulating through many bodies, repeatedly externalizing its organs and drawing them home.

Even death is local rather than final. A runner pierced and emptied in one aisle reappears from the unbroken floor elsewhere. The hunter gains substance; the chamber loses none. This is not reproduction in any familiar sense. It is redistribution. Individual animals may be no more independent than sparks moving around a circuit: visible at one contact, absent at the next, continuous only when the whole apparatus is considered.

Among them are the metallic insects that sit in the shallow gauge dishes. They are the closest thing the chamber has to faces. Each is fixed toward a direction that may not exist in space—perhaps toward a pressure, a phase, or an instant. Their legs tremble without walking. If removed, they return, though no route back can be observed. For the brief interval in which a dish is empty, the machine behind it sounds unchanged; nevertheless every nearby animal pauses at the same point in its circuit. When the insect is present again, all continue.

They may be indicators. They may be operators.

The signs of human entry suggest another inhabitant, or an attempted one. Someone sat in the booth long enough to polish the chair arms. Someone numbered the machines, discovered that counting produced 4, 4, 9, 2, and continued labeling them anyway. Someone installed the ladder without reaching the floor, or found that it no longer did. The dead lamp and rock-facing window imply an expectation of watching—perhaps not the chamber, but a process once visible through that wall.

No body remains. Yet the wear on the chair does not belong comfortably to the past. In a place where no abrasion progresses, the smoothness is not evidence of accumulated use. It is a permanent property, as present and active as the compressor’s hammering. The sitter may still exist in the only form the chamber permits: not as a person, but as the condition of having sat there. The booth contains occupancy without an occupant.

The same may be true of the damage throughout the chamber. The snapped column is not something that broke long ago. It is breaking, but denied the transition between intactness and ruin. The scorched panel is being scorched without heating further. Every dent is an impact held open. The chamber’s apparent stillness is composed of events that never finish.

What lives there, then, may be the accident itself.

The fauna are shapes assumed by its consequences. The machines are its duration. The amber roof is the pressure of an outside moment that cannot arrive. The impossible shaft is not a passage but an unfinished emergence: ascent can continue indefinitely because “out” has not yet become true. Descent takes one step because return is already part of the chamber’s completed state.

At the center, the bare space around the pedestal is the only place not occupied by this distributed organism. Nothing crosses it because it is not habitat. It is the point from which habitat proceeds—the chamber’s missing cause.

The switch marked **BEGIN** is down because beginning is what it is continuously doing.

It cannot be lifted because there is no earlier condition to restore, no interval before activation into which the mechanism could be returned. The switch is not a control over the factory. It is the smallest visible organ of the thing the factory makes.

And what the factory makes is itself: one warm, deafening instant, densely inhabited by creatures that are born, feed, kill, die, and return without ever carrying the instant forward.

Everything there is alive.

Nothing there has yet happened.
