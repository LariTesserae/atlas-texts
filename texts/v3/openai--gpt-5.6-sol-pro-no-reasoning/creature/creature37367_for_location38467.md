The hollow is inhabited by a community so slow that, at first, it resembles scenery.

Its most conspicuous residents are the low plants: creeping vines with thick, contracting tendrils; pale reeds whose discarded leaves behave like sluice gates; isolated ferns; mosses darkened by perpetual saturation; and broad-leaved growth gathered into loose islands. A few smooth-barked trees stand above them, slight and widely spaced, bending toward shade as other trees might bend toward sun. They do not compete upward. They spread, divert, retain, and wait.

These plants are not merely rooted in the hollow. Together they maintain it. Vines draw pebbles into crescents around pools, creating tiny retaining walls. Fallen reed blades catch in runnels and steer seepage toward thirsty moss. Roots knit the yielding floor and shift stones by increments too small to witness. There is no planner among them, but their growth has become a kind of distributed craft: a technology expressed through tension, shedding, swelling, and decay.

Fungi and colorless microbial films occupy everything beneath that visible garden. They soften fallen leaves without the help of worms or scavengers, reducing them gradually into black soil and dissolved nourishment. They glaze submerged stones, line hidden channels, and perhaps help lay down the cream-colored mineral rims. These are the hollow’s smallest and busiest lives, though even their work proceeds without haste.

There are no animals. The absence appears complete, extending from insects and mites to birds passing through the sky above. Nothing here possesses the quick appetites of muscle, flight, pursuit, or fear. Flowers have adapted to open fruitlessly; reproduction belongs to heat, moisture, fragmentation, and drifting dust. The plants do not solicit visitors. They propagate through the hollow’s own circulation.

Yet the truest inhabitant may be harder to separate from the place itself.

It lives through shallow water and porous stone. The sweating walls, the misplaced reflections, the uphill-running overflow, and the droplets that refuse to disturb the central pool all suggest one continuous process using the basin as its body. The pools may be its eyes, though they do not show what is directly above them. They seem to look elsewhere—or at other moments of the same sky. The runnels are its slow circulation. Moss, roots, and mineral crusts are tissues grown around that circulation, cooperating with it because they have never known another environment.

It does not act in any animal sense. It does not choose, pursue, or react. Its smallest gesture may take a season; its larger adjustments, centuries. Dry weather fills its central pool. Rain empties it. Water climbs where gravity says it should descend. Trees lean into darkness. These reversals are not violations within the hollow but signs of a local order, stable enough for life to have organized itself around them.

Its strangest product is the dark oval stone.

Each begins as a hanging droplet, clear and heavy beneath a leaf, containing an inverted image of the entire basin. Through the night, some cross an imperceptible boundary from water into stone. By dawn they have fallen—or completed themselves—without splash or fracture. Roots then draw them inward. Seepage rolls and polishes them. Decades carry them toward pools; centuries may lodge them in the regular alcoves of the northern wall.

There they rest like seeds that do not germinate, eggs that do not hatch, or records that cannot be read. The niches are natural, but the stones seem fitted to them because the hollow has had ages to perform the fitting. Perhaps they are condensations not only of mineral water but of place: each one the hardened remnant of a night’s reflection, preserving some minute state of the basin. Perhaps the alcoves are where the hollow stores what it has seen. Or perhaps stone is simply another phase in its water cycle, and in sufficient time each dark oval will dissolve, rise through the rock, gather beneath a leaf, and become transparent again.

Whatever lives here has no face and no singular boundary. It is a composite life: plants arranging stone, microbes digesting silence, mineral water remembering shapes, and the basin’s old geology guiding all of them. Its purpose, if purpose can be assigned, is not expansion. It does not seek to escape the rim or spread down the mountain. It sustains a sparse, exact completeness.

The hollow is its creature, and the creature is the hollow—warm, wet, patient, and awake only at the speed of stone.
