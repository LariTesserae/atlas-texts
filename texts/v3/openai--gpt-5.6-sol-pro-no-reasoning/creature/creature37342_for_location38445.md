The people of the canopy are long-limbed and spare, shaped by thin air, broad distances, and a world in which weight is less dangerous than attachment. They walk with a slight outward turn of the feet, testing every surface before trusting it. Even in familiar rooms they pause at thresholds. They have learned that a floor may soften, a corridor may begin circulating sap, and a doorway used carelessly can acquire conviction and become the only entrance to somewhere.

They call themselves citizens more often than they call themselves human.

This is partly civic pride and partly caution. Humanity is considered an inherited condition, while citizenship must be continually maintained. A person is entered in the municipal ledgers at birth, measured at intervals, assigned a sequence of names, and fitted with a small brass identity stud worn against the sternum. The stud is stamped with fixed facts: date, district, parentage, height at maturity, number of fingers, expected direction of aging. These details are recertified each year. Without such measures, a resident who works too long near an old pool or sleeps in an unlicensed room may return subtly altered—older in one hand, possessing memories of an unbuilt neighborhood, or legally identical to someone who died three generations earlier.

Their skin tends toward weathered browns, grays, and olive undertones, often stained at the fingertips by resin, chlorophyll, ceramic dust, or the blue-black grease reserved for metal bearings. Hair is worn short, braided tightly, or wrapped beneath waxed fiber caps. Loose hair catches condensation and roots readily in cracked bark. Among older workers, green threads sometimes appear in the scalp. These are trimmed without ceremony unless they flower.

Clothing is layered and close-fitting: felted moss undergarments, woven stem-fiber coats, bark-laminate aprons, high boots with ceramic cleats. Every garment has brass eyelets from which it can be hung clear of floors at night. Belts carry pruning knives, chalk, twine, measuring forks, whistles, and small sealed mirrors. Bright dyes are uncommon except in municipal uniforms. Color has practical meaning: ocher for pressure crews, white for water keepers, blue-gray for route engineers, red seams for those licensed to work near unstable distances. Surveyors wear black coats crosshatched with thin pale lines, making their bodies resemble portable coordinate systems.

Metal jewelry is rare. A plain iron ring might pass through six generations, thinning under successive inscriptions. Brass is more common but seldom ornamental. People wear washers, numbered tags, fragments of gauges, and clipped sections of old rail as signs of office, marriage, mourning, or successful return.

They are not a populous people. Children are deliberately spaced, not because food is scarce, but because rooms are. Every birth requires the licensing of new domestic volume, and domestic growth is difficult to control. A household that encourages one additional chamber may wake to seven, or to a nursery whose interior is larger than its exterior and subject to another district’s weather. Families therefore form around stable space rather than blood alone. Whoever sleeps under the same load-bearing branch, shares the same cistern allotment, and answers the same midnight root alarms is kin.

Children learn measurement before letters.

Their first games involve straightedges, knotted cords, bells, and cups filled precisely to marked lines. They race along growth rings, count doors, and practice describing objects without metaphor. This is believed to strengthen the world. A child who says, “The moon is a white seed,” will be corrected gently but at once: the moon is the moon, a seed is a seed, and the western freight depot has enough trouble as it is.

Only later are they taught figurative speech, under supervision.

They grow up keenly aware that language does not merely describe. Naming can shorten a street. Repetition can stabilize a machine. An inaccurate comparison may divert a train. The most highly trained speakers are not poets but dispatchers, contract witnesses, route callers, and civic declarants. Their public announcements are delivered in a flat, careful cadence:

The bridge is present.  
The bridge crosses the fissure.  
Those who enter from the east will leave by the west.  
The number of travelers will remain unchanged.

Passengers repeat the final line.

Despite this discipline, canopy citizens are not humorless. Their jokes are exact, elaborate, and often concern impossible things treated as bureaucratic inconveniences. A common story tells of a man who arrived at work before his own birth and was fined for employing a minor. Another concerns a surveyor who found a perfectly straight road and reported it as an obvious forgery. They distrust puns in transit stations but adore them at weddings.

Most citizens work with the plant in some fashion, though they rarely speak of serving it. The accepted phrase is negotiating growth.

Pruners are the most visible. They travel in suspended crews along towers, roofs, bridge roots, and civic conduits, removing unwanted buds before they become architecture. Their cuts must be decisive. A half-severed branch may interpret hesitation as consent. Veteran pruners are scarred across their arms by lashing tendrils and carry cautery irons in ceramic cases. They are admired, well paid, and thought to be unlucky in love: they notice new attachments too early.

Pressure workers tend the pistons that drive sap through industrial wards. They sleep in shifts determined by vascular tides rather than daylight. Years beside the engines broaden their hands and damage their hearing, though many insist they can eventually perceive the infrasonic complaint below ordinary sound. They describe it not as pain but as argument. Some become unable to sleep in quiet districts and retire beside pump houses, where they sit with tea balanced on trembling saucers.

Water keepers are both engineers and clergy, though they reject the latter title. They maintain cistern skins, condensation nets, shutters, gutters, and absorption barriers. They test pools with black glass, lengths of numbered chain, clocks, and sacrificial reflections. Before approaching questionable water, a keeper records who is standing nearby and how old each person is. If the reflected number of observers differs from the actual number, the pool is hooded.

They never look directly into water while grieving.

The surveyors have greater authority than their modest equipment suggests. They can condemn a stairway, close a day of the week within a particular building, or order an entire neighborhood to stop using its name. They settle boundary disputes by driving brass studs into surfaces and returning over successive mornings to see which studs remain mutually consistent. Their maps are stitched rather than printed so that obsolete streets can be cut out without tearing neighboring reality.

There are also graftwrights, fermentation tenders, seed archivists, shadow sanitation crews, frost wardens, route musicians, membrane glaziers, lower-shaft riggers, and clerks who inspect official documents for signs of germination. The least envied occupation is cabinet opener. After fog, no enclosed space containing an unexplained reflection may be opened by its owner.

Meals are plain, dense, and mildly bitter. The city eats what can be taken without provoking uncontrolled growth: pressed seed cakes, pale fungal curds, sap reduced to dark syrup, sheets of fermented leaf, smoked root nodules, mineral lichens, and occasional gnat paste after rain. Fresh fruit is dangerous luxury. Fruit is the canopy imagining departure, and its interiors do not always respect local scale. A plum-sized pod can contain enough pulp for a ward—or an orchard with its own municipal history.

Cooking is done in ceramic vessels over resin flames. Metal pots are heirlooms. Houses maintain small fermentation niches whose breathing holes must be cleaned daily. Each district has a characteristic yeast and accuses neighboring districts of allowing theirs to become sentimental.

Citizens drink hot infusions constantly. Warmth is comfort, hydration, and proof of sequence: the water was cold, then heated, then poured, and remains in the cup. Tea is therefore served at contracts, departures, reconciliations, and interrogations. When someone returns by an uncertain route, no questions are asked until they have finished a full cup without the liquid cooling backward.

Religion, where it exists, is practical and divided.

Some believe the canopy is one being and the city an irritation it has learned to tolerate. Others regard it as countless plants held in a union older and more successful than any human government. A smaller sect claims that the plant is not alive at all, but is the petrified route of something moving so slowly that birth, growth, and decay are artifacts of its passage.

No doctrine grants the canopy kindness.

Offerings are made, but as fees rather than worship: heat applied to a reluctant joint, sugar returned to exhausted cambium, blood smeared on a graft, songs beaten through root bridges to keep their tension even. The plant responds to all of these sometimes, which has prevented theology from becoming settled.

Death presents special problems. Bodies cannot be buried in the usual sense, and cremation consumes too much resin. The dead are dried in warm pressure halls, wrapped in salt fiber, and placed in mortuary forks at the edge of inhabited districts. There the canopy is permitted to take them gradually. Families visit to trim unauthorized shoots and read changes in the bark. Within a decade, a body may become a dark seam, a cluster of leaves, or a hard knot preserving the face in distorted relief.

If a corpse roots too quickly, it is considered evidence that death was incomplete. Surveyors are summoned before physicians.

Names of the dead are not spoken on shortening streets.

The rare animals occupy a disproportionate share of the civic imagination. Children collect abandoned gnat cases. Factories keep illustrated root-mouse procedures posted beside boiler controls. People who have seen a pollinator describe it for the rest of their lives, each account growing more precise and less believable.

Bird passage brings the city closest to panic.

At the first siren, work stops. Mirrors are covered with cloth. Pools are screened. Citizens stand beneath overhangs, eyes lowered, hands visible. No one points because pointing establishes a route, and a bird’s extraneous shadows are eager to be told where to go. Shadow crews wait with chalk hoops and potted cuttings, watching for darkness that remains after the wings have passed.

Some residents secretly collect bird shadows.

They keep them folded in sealed drawers or pinned beneath furniture, where they rustle on clear nights. Such collectors are blamed for trees that appear inside locked rooms, for staircases that lead briefly into open air, and for the sensation of being watched from above even when indoors. The offense is punishable by loss of windows.

The inhabitants fear water, but they fear drought more. They distrust growth, but all shelter and livelihood depend on it. They resist the canopy’s transformations while asking it daily for rooms, bridges, fuel, cloth, sweetness, warmth. Their civilization is built from managed surrender.

This contradiction shapes their manners. Gratitude is expressed cautiously, apology quickly. Ownership is considered temporary and faintly embarrassing. A house belongs to its occupants only in the sense that a hand belongs to the ring it wears. Tools are never left unattended, not simply because they may be stolen, but because they may decide to remain where they are.

Nothing made of plant matter is considered entirely inanimate. A wooden chair is furniture while occupied, vegetation while empty, and potential infrastructure if forgotten.

At night, households fasten shutters and suspend their clothing from ceiling hooks. Doors are counted. Cups are inverted. Written records are locked in dry ceramic boxes to prevent revision. Someone marks the floor beside each bed so sleepers can determine whether the room has shifted around them.

Then the root traffic begins.

The citizens lie awake feeling enormous pressures travel beneath their backs: migrations, reallocations, reflexes, or messages too large to be understood. Infants often sleep best during these movements. Adults dream of roads bending without breaking, of stations inside trunks, of descending so far they emerge above themselves.

And at the western limit, a small hereditary staff tends the arrival station.

They are called receivers, though nothing has ever been received.

Their uniforms preserve an antique cut no longer seen elsewhere: long coats, high collars, brass buttons bearing a route number absent from every current map. They wind the clocks each evening and test the bells in the legally prescribed sequence. They sweep pollen from the grooves and polish the dark reused rails. The timetable is copied daily, even though the times listed do not correspond to local hours.

Generation after generation, receivers record the silhouettes crossing the horizontal roots in the distant sky. They note apparent speed, number of cars, intervals, weather, direction of shadow, and whether the far-off windows seem illuminated. Each observation is signed by three witnesses. The accumulated registers fill a grown archive whose rooms spiral around a sapwell and whose oldest pages have begun putting out silver leaves.

Many citizens mock the office. Many more visit it before marriages, departures, or dangerous surgery. They sit on the empty benches and watch the uphill beads of water creep along the rails.

The receivers insist the station is not maintained because a train is expected.

Expectation is unstable, they say. It attracts possibilities indiscriminately.

The station is maintained because arrival must have somewhere to occur.

So the lamps are lit. The clocks are wound. The grooves are cleared. Far beyond the city, small shapes continue along the roots stretched into cloud, and everyone goes on living as if punctuality, properly observed, might one evening become stronger than distance.
