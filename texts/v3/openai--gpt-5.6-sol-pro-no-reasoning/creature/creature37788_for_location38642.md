The people who live on the mountain call themselves, in their own speech, something closer to **those-who-make-room** than any word for citizen or settler.

They are slight, dark-eyed people, though there is considerable variation among them. Some families are broad and low-built, adapted to hauling loads along steep paths; others are long-limbed and narrow enough to pass through the woven service tunnels beneath houses. Nearly everyone has strong, prehensile toes, callused palms, and an unconscious way of standing with the knees bent, as if compensating for a movement in the ground that has not yet occurred.

Their ears are small and usually covered. Children wear caps of felted fur; adults bind strips of moss cloth around the sides of the head. This does not deafen them. It dulls the sharpest noises and keeps the skin from registering every minute pressure change. Mountain-born people are acutely aware of vibration. They feel an approaching sledge through a threshold, a climbing animal through a wall, and a deep fracture through the soles of their feet. Many sleep with one hand resting on bare stone.

They rarely face one another to speak. Conversation happens shoulder to shoulder, mouth close to ear, with breath moderated by a hand or sleeve. At greater distances they use gestures: hooked fingers for water, two palms descending for cloud, a thumb drawn across the wrist for a predator nearby. Their sign for danger is not a raised hand but a hand made suddenly motionless.

Stillness is their shout.

Names are given quietly and used sparingly. A person’s first name belongs to the household, the second to the lane, and the third—if they acquire one—to the animals. Such names cannot be chosen. A woman repeatedly followed by ash moths may become Under-Wing. A child whom the transparent frogs tolerate in their cistern might be called Clear-Hand. Some animal names are unflattering, but refusing one is considered reckless. The creatures are believed to notice discourtesy more readily than affection.

No one is named for the enormous shadows.

The people wear layered garments of woven bark fiber, shed fur, and skins taken only from animals found dead or killed by predators. The outer layers are fringed, not decoratively but to gather condensation. In the cold hour they walk beneath the trees until each fringe is jeweled, then wring the water into household jars. Wealth can sometimes be recognized by the length of a family’s dew-cloak, though displaying too much bright moisture is thought to attract things from above.

Ornaments are small and soft: beads of fungus-pith, colored feathers sewn flat, polished seeds, warm black fishbones. Metal jewelry is almost unknown. Copper belongs to needles, hooks, surgical tools, and the few instruments used to test fissures. Wearing it without purpose would be like wearing a door hinge.

Their households are not defined entirely by kinship. A house may contain siblings, former spouses, apprentices, found children, elderly strangers, and one or two animals whose continued presence has become socially binding. If a broad-backed burden beast sleeps against the same wall for a year, the household widens the eaves for it. If thumb-sized furred animals nest in someone’s hair each winter, space is left for them among that person’s bedding. A widow may inherit no room at all if moths have occupied it, but she inherits the obligation to keep the room dry.

Children learn household boundaries by touch in darkness. They are led blindfolded through the rooms and asked to identify hearth, tools, sleeping platform, load-bearing roots, nesting shelves, and exits. Whatever they cannot name reliably is not truly theirs.

They grow up among animals in numbers that would overwhelm lowland visitors. They do not romanticize them. Wall grazers foul plaster. Long-bodied rain birds bite. The gliding mammals steal dried food and sometimes sever fingers when startled. Antlered walkers can crush a doorway merely by turning around. Yet the distinction between pest, livestock, neighbor, omen, and owner remains intentionally unclear.

The people do not domesticate animals so much as negotiate habits with them.

Moss-backed carriers are fed at particular corners until they begin presenting themselves for loads. Moth colonies are encouraged beneath roofs because their scales shed water. Flat lizards are admitted to grain stores to control thread-snakes, then removed before they consume the useful beetles. No creature is trusted absolutely. No creature is considered without use. Even pale ravine hunters leave behind teeth light enough for surgical blades.

The most respected residents are often not leaders but **noticers**: people skilled in reading collective animal behavior. A noticer may spend hours watching the angle at which frogs cling to a jar or counting how many legs the wall grazers lift while resting. Their conclusions are delivered without drama. “The western ledges will loosen.” “Do not sleep above the third cistern.” “Something has entered the wind.” Questions about how they know are considered reasonable; demands for certainty are childish.

There is no single ruler. Each lane keeps a small council, generally composed of a water keeper, a root tender, a bridge binder, a noticer, and whoever has recently survived being badly wrong. The last position is important. The town believes error gives knowledge a necessary scar.

Councils meet around the central tree, but never all at once. Too many human bodies in the square would displace its other inhabitants. Decisions therefore travel upward and downward through the hollow trunk. A proposal is marked on thin pieces of bark, placed in a branch storehouse, amended by successive groups, and returned to the ground days later. By then moths may have eaten parts of it, birds may have pierced it, or small animals may have carried sections away. These alterations are interpreted as participation rather than damage.

Only matters of immediate danger are decided quickly. Then the silent disk is turned, every visible person repeats its pattern with cloth or hands, and the town behaves like a single startled organism.

Their dead are not buried in the young stone. Nor are they burned, since open flame belongs uneasily at the summit. Bodies are wrapped in root fiber and carried into the Hanging Wood, where they are secured in the elbows of certain trees. What happens afterward depends on the season and the creatures that find them. Bones sometimes return years later, worked outward through the bark. These are collected without ceremony and made into useful things.

A dead person may become a shuttle, a latch, a handle, a rain comb, or one of the pale pegs in the central stair. This is not thought to imprison them. Use wears away the last confusion of being an individual. When a bone tool finally breaks, its pieces are dropped into a warm fissure.

Birth takes place near those same fissures. The laboring parent lies on a suspended mat while attendants keep their hands against the rock, feeling for sudden changes. Newborns are not encouraged to cry. They are placed chest-down against warm stone, where the vibration of the mountain usually quiets them. If an infant continues crying, doors are sealed, animals are moved from adjoining rooms, and watch is kept at the wall.

Everyone understands that the child is not at fault.

Everyone also watches the ravines.

Those born during a period of rapid uplift are said to remain slightly out of alignment with the town. They stumble on level ground but move effortlessly during tremors. They wake before fractures, sleep through storms, and often become fissure tenders or explorers of new ledges. Some claim these people can feel chambers inside the mountain: hollows, buried trunks, pockets of water, and places where something scratches upward.

Religion, if it can be called that, is directed less toward gods than toward attention. To attend to a thing is to enter a relationship with it; to be attended to is potentially dangerous. This is why people lower their eyes when vast shadows pass overhead. It is also why tools are turned blade-down when not in use and mirrors are made only of dark water.

They believe the mountain is young enough not to know what it is.

Its rising is neither benevolent nor hostile. It is a living uncertainty, assembling itself from deep stone, stranded forest, heat, weather, and all the creatures carried upward with it. The town’s task is not to master the summit but to help teach it patterns: where water may gather, where roots may deepen, where weight may safely rest, where emptiness must remain.

For that reason, builders are among the closest things the town has to priests. Before raising a wall, they leave the intended space open for three nights. Tracks are studied each morning. A wall crossing a major animal route is shifted. A roof that interrupts a habitual glide is lowered or pierced. If nothing enters the space, construction is postponed, because absence may itself be occupancy.

The warm fissures support another class of inhabitants, though no one has seen them clearly. These are inferred from the footprints inside fired pots, from cords that return tied in unfamiliar knots, and from objects found on ledges no hand can reach: curled mineral filaments, colorless shells, tiny woven cages containing heat.

They are called the **Below-Folk**, but the name does not imply they are human.

Children imagine them as pale, many-fingered potters. Fissure tenders insist they are more likely a kind of communal animal. Some elders believe the footprints are made by the mountain practicing feet. Whatever their nature, offerings are occasionally lowered: bark flour, shed antler, dead moths, broken tools. Most vanish. Once in a generation something returns.

Such returned objects are rarely comprehensible. A cup that keeps water warm but causes nearby roots to recoil. A black needle that bends toward sleeping animals. A thin stone lid beneath which a continuous breeze circles. None are kept inside a house. They hang in the upper branches of the central tree, where moths gradually cover them.

The Hanging Wood supports people who belong to the town without residing within its wall. Fish gatherers, bridge keepers, fungus cutters, and cloud-line musicians inhabit platforms among the elbowed trunks. They are louder than summit dwellers and considered somewhat unruly. Their songs are played through wind bladders and reed organs, often too low to be heard as sound. The music is felt through ladders, roots, and rib cages.

During courtship, a musician may send a repeated vibration up a particular bridge at dusk. If the invitation is welcomed, the recipient alters the bridge’s tension, changing the returning note. Entire romances unfold as variations in structural resonance, incomprehensible to anyone not touching the correct rail.

The warm fish are tended there as well. Children insist the white door-mark moves from fish to fish. Adults deny this but release every marked specimen without hesitation. A fish that emerges from bark inside the town is carried downslope rather than eaten. One that emerges inside a house is treated as a warning that the house has become a passage.

The residents then sleep elsewhere until the walls stop feeling warm.

And there are inhabitants less easily classified.

Wind kept too long in a room acquires preferences. It circles certain sleepers, avoids certain tools, or presses repeatedly against one shutter. Families may feed such a wind with loose down and scent it with crushed leaves. If it begins opening latches, it is trapped in a bladder and carried below the cloud line. There, someone plays it out through a flute until it is merely weather again.

The blue fungus in the night bowls is also said to remember. It brightens around people who have been absent, weakens around strangers, and occasionally casts the outline of a hand when no hand is near. Fungus keepers cultivate dozens of strains, some fed on specific feathers, some on the dust of particular houses. Their fingers are permanently blue at the nails. Their faces, like everyone’s, disappear in the light they tend.

Perhaps the strangest inhabitant is the settlement itself.

Not the mountain, exactly, nor the central tree, nor the sum of houses and lanes. It is something produced by their continual adjustment to one another. Rooms migrate. Paths close and reopen. A cistern becomes a nursery, a nursery becomes a moth chamber, a moth chamber loses its wall and becomes part of the weather. Every inhabitant carries an incomplete map, and no two maps agree.

The town’s shape changes in memory as much as in space. People speak of old districts now divided by chasms, houses lifted beyond reach, gardens swallowed by new seams. Yet they also tell of lanes returning after decades, recognizable by thresholds protruding from fresh stone. Once, after an earthquake no one heard, an entire woven room rose from a western fissure. Its bedding was dry. A bowl of blue fungus still glowed inside.

No one claimed it.

The animals entered first, as custom required.

Three nights later, all of them emerged and faced inward.

The gates were bound. The silent disk showed black. Families sat on their sleeping platforms with hands pressed to the floors while the mountain trembled too gently to move a cup. Beneath them came a sensation like footsteps, though it traveled through solid rock and paused under each house in turn.

At dawn, a continuous groove circled the central tree.

Since then, there has always been someone awake in the square—not standing in it, never looking directly at its center, but watching from a shutter or branch stair. The duty is shared by every household and some of the older animals, who appear to have included themselves in the rotation.

Most nights, nothing happens.

Condensation gathers. The moths fold and unfold their ash-colored wings. Warm fish work backward into wet bark. Far below, cloud presses through the Hanging Wood, and the town’s thousands of bodies shift in sleep as the summit rises beneath them.

Then, just before dawn, the watcher feels the central tree tighten its roots.

Every creature in the square becomes still.

And from somewhere inside the mountain—or inside the town, if those are still different things—something pauses to listen back.
