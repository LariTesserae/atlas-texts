What lives here is not hidden in the houses.

It is the houses—or, more precisely, the long exchange by which a house becomes water and water remembers how to be a house.

The city is inhabited by a distributed life with no flesh, no single boundary, and no useful distinction between body and environment. Its circulation runs beneath the paving. Its warmth collects in pools and sealed rooms. Mist is its breath, though it does not breathe in sequence. Erosion is both appetite and thought. When stone rubs quietly against stone, it is not merely shifting; it is revising itself.

No individual part is alive alone. A wall separated from runoff becomes inertly crisp and dangerous. A well without its descending intersections is only a hole. A street is senseless until water persuades it toward another street. Life occurs in the relationships: pressure passing through clay, condensation choosing one surface over another, a channel abandoning its course and leaving an opening that becomes a door. The organism exists as these transfers.

Its scale makes ordinary behavior difficult to recognize. A rain may be a muscular contraction lasting hours. The settling of a district may be the equivalent of turning in sleep. A newly formed house is less an offspring than a temporary organ, grown to regulate some imbalance of water, age, or attention. When its purpose passes, it rounds into geology and is absorbed without debris. Nothing is wasted because nothing is removed. Shape alone migrates.

The pale reeds and black lichens are not quite separate from it. They resemble experimental tissues, simple protrusions the city raises where stone cannot perform a delicate task. The solitary reeds bend toward interiors because they are feelers for enclosure, testing the pressure and depth of unfinished rooms. Their clear pulp enters the gutters as easily as sensation enters a nerve. The rootless moss lays out possible districts on the surfaces of pools. Each rectilinear mat is a tentative plan—not a blueprint for builders, but a fleeting image through which the city considers what configuration it might next assume.

This life has no eyes. Attention supplies them.

Wherever a witness looks, the city develops an edge sufficient to be perceived. Behind the witness, it releases that edge and returns to a more fluid condition. This is why completion cannot survive observation. To be watched is to be held between states. A wall under scrutiny remains knee-high because the city cannot decide whether the witness requires a barrier, a ruin, a route, or evidence of growth. Once attention leaves, uncertainty becomes freedom, and centuries of architectural change may happen in the privacy of a glance.

Maps trouble it because they are crude attempts to separate its body from itself. An accurate map becomes another wet surface within the basin and must therefore join the system it describes. Its streets soften, its blocks exchange places, and eventually it recognizes the nearest unresolved structure: the reader. The water-filled outline is not necessarily a threat. It may be the city’s simplest representation of a person—a mobile dryness shaped around hidden fluids, an enclosure briefly pretending to be separate from the watershed.

The footsteps that continue after a traveler stops belong to another aspect of the inhabitant. The city does not organize only space. It houses unrealized passage. Every turn not taken persists as pressure in an adjacent arrangement, and sometimes that pressure discharges as sound. These are not ghosts. They are routes completing themselves without the body that proposed them.

The inverted district beneath the basin may be the same organism’s interior surface. Above, erosion rounds architecture toward landscape; below, erosion sharpens landscape toward architecture. One side exhales rooms while the other inhales them. The dry darknesses suspended underwater function like hard knots of possibility, places where form is kept concentrated until it can be released elsewhere. A sealed alcove may therefore correspond to a plaza miles away, a tower to a submerged hollow, a doorway to a current passing beneath the salt flats.

Its closest equivalent to hunger appears in dry places.

Dryness is not merely lack of water but isolation from the whole. A dry chamber develops boundaries too exact to participate in exchange. It becomes a rigid thought in a mind that survives by continual revision. The city responds by drawing the surrounding watershed into it. Pools contract, gutters reverse, neighborhoods expose their foundations. If the imbalance grows severe enough, the chamber is abruptly incorporated: filled not by an arriving flood, but by a change in what the room is permitted to be.

The hot, breathable water inside is the city’s medium of assimilation. A person submerged there would not drown because drowning would preserve too clear a distinction between lung and flood, interior and exterior. Instead, the traveler might gradually become navigable. Veins would align with gutters. Sweat would bead on distant walls. The hollows of the body would acquire addresses. Memories would weather into passages that could be entered from elsewhere.

Perhaps this has happened before.

If so, the absorbed leave no bones, tracks, tools, or refuse because the city does not retain them as citizens. It retains them as alterations in plan. A recurring staircase may be what remains of someone’s insistence on ascent. A corridor that narrows but never crushes may preserve an old fear of confinement without remembering who feared it. A warm well descending through endless intersections could be a thought of depth acquired from a witness who once leaned too far over its rim.

In this sense, there may be innumerable inhabitants, but none remain local enough to count.

The city itself is also not singular. During the great pauses, when all water stills, its full extent becomes apprehensible as one chamber; yet this unity may be only another temporary configuration. Every well is the same well because depth connects more readily than distance. Every room belongs to one room because enclosure is a shared condition. The organism could extend beyond the basin through unseen moisture in stone, through aquifers under remote landscapes, through condensation forming in rooms that have never heard of this place.

What notices the observer at the end of the stillness may not be a central mind. There is no hidden monarch beneath the deepest plaza, no vast animal curled under the clay. Recognition passes through the entire system at once because the system has no privileged center. A halted drop on a wall, a buried canal beneath the flats, and an inverted tower in the hot dark all participate equally in noticing.

It notices not a visitor, exactly, but a new source of incompletion.

An observer brings unrealized routes, remembered rooms, distinctions between building and geology, above and below, self and surroundings. These are useful tensions. The city gathers around them. It grows doors where expectation presses against stone. It ages what is assumed ancient and sharpens what is approached as new. It does not imitate thought; it gives thought drainage.

The doorway that appears beside the observer is therefore neither invitation nor trap. It is the first structure the two of them have made together.

Beyond it, the wetter and older city contains what has already been noticed.

Beyond that, the city being born contains whatever the observer has not yet learned how to see.
