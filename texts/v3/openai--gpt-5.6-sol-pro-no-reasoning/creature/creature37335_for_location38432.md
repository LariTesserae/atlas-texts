The plateau is inhabited less by a single people than by a compact society of thirst.

Its most visible citizens are the rock antelope, narrow-bodied and long-legged, rust-grey enough to vanish when they stand still against the fins. Their backward-curving horns let them enter passages that would catch a wider crown. They move in files along old polished routes, each animal placing its hooves where generations have placed theirs. Before entering a blind corridor, the leader stamps. Stone answers stone; an open route returns a clean report, rubble gives back a cluttered one, and flesh interrupts the echo. At the wrong answer, the whole file turns without panic.

The lean plateau cat understands this custom. High-chested, dust-colored, and economical, it waits where corridors converge, using the upright beds as blinds. It hunts not by outrunning the antelope but by becoming one more silence among many. Often its presence is known only afterward: a disturbed patch of scree, a few dark hairs on sandstone, the abrupt cessation of stamping.

Black-faced primates live nearer the human apparatus. Their knowledge is mechanical and inherited. They twist inspection caps, worry at valves, and wait beneath regulators that release reliable drips. Youngsters learn which pipes are hot by watching their elders, though some acquire the lesson by touch. They raid shelters for salt, cloth, and bright pieces that have no evident use. To the maintenance crews they are vandals; to the plateau they are another kind of technician.

Smaller lives occupy nearly every seam. Pale frogs sleep behind plugs of their own dried mud. Lizards commute with the shadows, edging sideways through noon. Beetles drink from mineral ribs before the droplets disappear. Wasps inhabit dead control boxes. Bats roost in drill holes and emerge into the warmth above the pumps. Swifts fill cavities in the fins and, at dawn, pour out by the thousand, turning the stone briefly smoky with wings. Grass clumps contain nests stitched from hair, fiber, and shreds of cable insulation. Each thorn shrub is less a plant than a crowded vertical refuge.

The plants themselves live by refusal. Grey thorns expose little to the sun and send most of themselves downward. Wiregrass survives behind stones. Fleshy rosettes remain clenched through daylight, then loosen after dusk. Moss, sedge, and copper-stemmed reeds gather around hidden water, marking the few places where moisture persists. None tries to conquer the plateau. Each holds one crack, one shadow, one pocket of sediment, with immense tenacity.

People do not truly settle here. They visit as custodians.

Maintenance workers ascend before sunrise in pairs or trios, riding yellow cradles along steep service rails. They move slowly because exertion punishes the lungs, metal burns after noon, and sound cannot always be trusted. They carry water, filters, grease, tools, and two bells of unequal pitch. When heat stratifies the corridors and bends sound out of place, they strike the bells and listen. If the high note and low note disagree about distance, they turn back or wait.

Their work is dispersed across the stone: a borehead in one cleft, a compressor behind three ridges, a cistern under a talus court, a cooling array facing the night sky. They replace seals, clear gutters, test gas, and leave before the plateau can mistake them for residents. Their shelters are narrow and temporary, stocked with ceramic jars and manuals that resist heat. The real town shines far below.

Yet the workers inherit rules from people much older than the machinery. Those earlier inhabitants left steps, catch-cups, sealed clefts, charred grain, and walls placed across the hidden circulation of water. They seem to have understood that the plateau cannot be owned, only corrected in small ways. Their clearest surviving instruction remains law:

**Take water after it has overflowed once.**

Workers obey it. Herders obey it. Even animals appear to know its shape, waiting above the first filthy rush until dust, dung, salt, feathers, and dead insects have been carried away. No one takes the first water. Shade, likewise, belongs to whoever reached it first. Stones are not thrown into clefts that may breathe gas. A humming pipe is not crossed. Two stamps without an echo close a road.

The machines are inhabitants too, though intermittent ones. They sleep through most of the day, then wake separately as pressure gathers below. One hums beyond a ridge. Another knocks softly inside the rock. A valve exhales. Condensate ticks into a tank. Mineral crusts spread over anchor plates like slow blue-green growths, while nests accumulate behind cooling vanes and drinking places form beneath regulators. The equipment no longer stands apart from the ecology. It provides heat, cavities, shade, vibration, metal salts, and water. In return, animals block vents, chew insulation, foul sensors, and reveal leaks before instruments do.

What lives here has learned that permanence does not mean stillness. The fins expand and click under the sun. Scree settles grain by grain. Roots descend. Gas climbs. Sound folds. Water arrives violently and vanishes. Routes shift after every flood, but the principles remain.

By night, the divisions among inhabitants become hardest to maintain. Rosettes open. Frogs call from concealed pools. Bats feed around warm housings. The cat passes beneath old steps. Pumps send faint tremors through cliffs that were raised from ancient beds. Workers’ warning lamps blink among stars and nesting holes.

Everything listens for water.

That expectation is perhaps the plateau’s deepest resident: carried in sealed frogs, buried roots, animal memory, cistern gauges, old inscriptions, and the patience of stone basins. Life here does not fill the country by covering it. It fills it by knowing every exception—every cool crack, safe ledge, reliable drip, breathable passage, and hollow where the next storm will remain a little longer than elsewhere.
