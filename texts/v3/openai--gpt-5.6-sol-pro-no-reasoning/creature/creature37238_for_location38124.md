The people of the stone bell are spare, powerful in the back and thighs, and seldom entirely clean of red dust. Their bodies have been shaped less by hunger than by carrying: jars against the hip, ceramic cages over the shoulder, sledges checked by rope, children bound close during the violent turns of the air. Even the elderly retain broad, callused palms. The city leaves a permanent rust-colored line in the creases of every hand.

They are not numerous. Seen from above, a neighborhood may appear deserted until one notices six figures moving in deliberate sequence beneath an overhang, or a face behind a shutter waiting for the pressure to settle. Families occupy structures made for many more. A household seals three rooms and lets ten stand empty, opening them only when masonry must be inspected or a vent changes course. No one finds this strange. Absence is treated as one of the city’s native materials, like basalt, salt, heat, and breath.

Their skin is usually covered. They wear overlapping lengths of woven vine fiber, waxed at the shoulders and hems, with loose inner layers to trap cooler air. Veils are practical rather than ceremonial, though ceremony has accumulated around them. A child receives a first dust-cloth when old enough to recognize the warning tones. Adults carry several: a coarse one for mineral storms, a pale one for poisonous garden vapors, and often a finely dyed cloth for mourning. During sporefall, only the eyes show. Lashes and brows are greased so the red nap will not collect there.

Hair is cut short or braided tight to the scalp. Long hair belongs chiefly to images of the dead, who are depicted with streaming locks like rope-vines in an intake wind. Teeth are often stained amber by wax-sap syrup. Old gardeners develop darkened fingertips from testing residues, while kiln workers bear pale, smooth scars where heat has sealed the skin.

They speak softly when the cavern allows it. Loud voices are wasteful in a place that will soon drown every word. Their language favors compact phrases and precise gestures. During the bell’s strongest sounding, whole conversations continue by hand: fingers marking quantities against the forearm, a flat palm indicating unsafe pressure, two knuckles touched to the brow for *I understand, though I cannot hear you*. Lovers, siblings, and work crews develop private signs. Some marriages are said to fail when two people discover that they cannot understand each other in weather.

Children learn the city first as a pattern of tones. Before they can name districts, they know which groan means a shutter has come loose, which tremor belongs to a kiln draft, and which descending note warns of a fresh crack. They are taught never to invent alarms in play. Their games instead use distance and echo: hiding beyond empty foundations, rolling ceramic disks down abandoned roads, or standing on opposite sides of a bare interval and exchanging signs through bronze dust.

They are watched closely near the Quiet Floor. Every child attempts, at least once, to test the stories about it. Adults do not respond with panic, which might draw a child farther inward, but sit at the rim and call them back by reciting ordinary things: the rooms of the child’s home, the names of relatives, the sequence of meals, the crack beside the sleeping shelf. Familiar order is believed to give distance its proper shape again.

No one crosses the Quiet Floor willingly. Yet certain people linger at its edge: mourners, mathematicians, those troubled by visions, and the listeners appointed by the city. The listeners sit with one hand on the polished stone and report knocks from below. They disagree about whether these are impacts, words, or merely flaws in the bell cooling far beneath them. Their records fill ceramic tablets stored in padded racks. Many sequences recur centuries apart.

The dead are not buried. There is no soil, and no body may be given to a garden; the people regard the mixing of human flesh with crop tissue as a profound contamination. Bodies are dried in warm chambers, wrapped in absorbent ash-root, and rendered in sealed kilns. Oil and salts are destroyed separately. The remaining mineral fragments are ground into pale powder and fired into small hollow tiles.

One such tile is set into the family dwelling, usually near a doorway or ventilation slit. Thus the dead become part of the surfaces that divide safe air from dangerous air. When a household abandons a home, it removes these tiles first. The poorest families carry generations of them in padded baskets. A house without ancestor tiles feels temporary, however ancient its stone.

They have no inherited metaphors of herds, hunting, flight, or birdsong. Nothing is “skittish as a deer,” nothing “swarming,” no thought “takes wing.” A coward is said to shutter before the note changes. A stubborn person is a cold hinge. A crowd spreads like spores or locks like ash-root. Desire is an intake: a pull felt before its source is known. Anger is an exhalation, dangerous because it makes every loose thing speak.

Nor do they divide the living world into plants and animals, because there are no animals by which to establish the contrast. The cavern growths are simply the many forms of life. People are the walking kind.

Some theologians dispute even that distinction. They point to gardens that inflate, veins that transport oil, skins that cauterize when cut, and colonies that answer pressure without thought. They argue that people are merely warm gardens with memory, uprooted and briefly mobile. Their opponents reply that only people make choices against the breath. The argument is old and has never been resolved.

Breath itself is not worshiped, exactly. It is too immediate for worship. It is schedule, hazard, climate, and the nearest thing the city has to a universal authority. Promises are made for a stated number of cycles rather than days. Age is reckoned by great intakes, especially those strong enough to open the daylight chimneys. A person born during such an event is thought likely to become restless, curious about upper passages, and dissatisfied with inherited rooms.

Timekeepers maintain strips of pressure-sensitive bladder skin stretched across ceramic frames. Their swelling and relaxation move fine styluses through mineral dust. From these traces they forecast the next reversal and identify long rhythms invisible within a single lifetime. Their halls contain generations of fired records: red waves, black notches, interruptions marking collapses and famines. Political authority belongs partly to whoever can read these rhythms well enough to order shutters closed before others sense the change.

There are councils, but no single enduring ruler. Each inhabited cluster sends a keeper of ropes, gardens, walls, or heat, depending on what it contributes to the city. Their meetings occur around the Quiet Floor, where no district can claim precedence and raised voices sound oddly small. Disputes are settled through obligations of labor. Theft of food may be repaid in kiln watches; damage to a pressure channel may bind an entire household to masonry work for years. Exile is rare. Beyond the city lies either the fatal salt waste or the equally fatal depths. Instead, the severest punishment is unhousing: the condemned remain fed and sheltered but may not place ancestor tiles, claim a sleeping niche, or speak for a district. To be made socially placeless in a city of deliberate emptiness is considered terrible.

Their wealth is measured in controlled transitions. A rich household owns no great hoard of metal or water, but warm fissures, reliable shade, access to descending air, kiln time, lengths of sound rope, and rights to particular garden strains. Recipes are inheritance. Families guard methods for neutralizing bitter ash-root, clarifying wax-sap, or firing clay that will flex without cracking. A marriage may join a vent garden to a kiln draft more consequentially than it joins two lineages.

Meals are dense and small. Roasted food-bulbs are split from their ceramic skins and eaten with fatty ash-root paste, salt flakes, and blister-spice. Chalk stems are ground, fermented by heat-loving growths, and baked into thin gray sheets. Dark syrup is diluted with rendered plant oil and served warm. They do not drink in the ordinary sense. Moisture is scarce within their bodies and rarer outside them; fluids are taken as thick gels, oils, and sealed broths that do not evaporate readily. To spill a bowl is shameful less for the lost food than for the heedlessness.

Their physicians are gardeners of human tissue. They seal wounds with waxes, draw poisons using porous stone cups, brace broken limbs in layered clay, and monitor illness through breath, skin salts, and changes in body heat. Infection is understood by analogy with pale threads invading weakened garden beds. Sickrooms are marked with ash ridges, and tools are heated until their mineral glazes change color.

They know suffocation, burns, dust-lung, vent poisoning, and the slow deformities of heavy labor. They also know a disorder called false weather, in which a person feels the pressure reverse when it has not. Those afflicted gasp during calm intervals, hear warning notes inside ordinary speech, and seal doors against harmless air. Treatment places them in one of the city’s most acoustically stable chambers, attended by someone who taps the true rhythm against their wrist.

A few people climb to the salt waste. These chimney crews are regarded with a mixture of admiration and unease. They wear rigid veils, reflective clay plates, and many layers of waxed cloth. They emerge only during suitable intakes and never travel far from the openings. Above them is a brightness without walls, a white expanse so broad there is no opposite side visible. Many cannot endure it. They crawl, vomit gel, or become convinced they are falling upward.

Those who tolerate the surface bring back salt crust, glassy splinters, unfamiliar dust, and descriptions no one entirely trusts. They say the sky has no vault. They say light fills everything instead of traveling in blades. They say the wind does not reverse on schedule and contains no organ note by which intention can be judged.

The most daring surface workers hammer short stone stakes around chimney mouths and tie themselves to the city below. Not because the wind could truly carry them away, but because a body without enclosure feels unmoored. Every climber returns with the same instinct: to touch the inner wall of the bell and assure themselves that the world once again has an edge.

Artists work principally in sound, glaze, and shadow. Sculptures erode too quickly unless kept indoors, and pigment is forever covered by dust. Composers alter grooves in sacrificial walls or suspend arrays of ceramic tongues where the weather plays them. A composition may take years to hear in full because its highest passage occurs only under rare pressure. Some works outlive their makers by centuries and are still revised by masons who do not know the original names.

Silence is also an art. Wealthy districts construct cancellation chambers modeled imperfectly on the Quiet Floor, arranging ducts so that the city’s notes meet and diminish. People visit them to hear cloth move over skin, fingertips touch pottery, oil thicken in a cooling dish. Many find the experience intimate to the point of embarrassment. In those rooms, one can hear another person breathing.

Their festivals accompany events that cannot be commanded: a powerful daylight shaft, a mass rupture of garden bladders, the reopening of an old chimney, the first stable note from a repaired tower. During the great sporefalls, households extinguish lamps and paint their veils with oil. Spores adhere in shifting patterns, so people emerge bearing constellations of bronze-red light. They circle the Quiet Floor while the city roars too loudly for music. Instead, thousands pull braided cords in rhythm, and the tension passes from hand to hand around the oval.

No animals appear in their oldest images. There are human figures, pressure fans, vents, roots, geometric breaths, and shapes presumed to represent the exterior sun. In later centuries, surface workers began reporting marks in the salt too irregular to be wind. Some claimed to see distant moving specks. These accounts produced a minor school of speculative art: impossible creatures assembled from known parts—walking rope-vines, bladder growths with human eyes, ceramic shells full of teeth.

Most people consider such inventions grotesque.

A smaller number are fascinated. They ask whether life elsewhere might move without speaking, consume without cultivation, or possess intention without hands. They wonder what it would mean for the world to contain footsteps not made by people.

For now, every unexplained sound is investigated with that old certainty: if a pebble moved, a person moved it—or the bell itself did.

And when the deepest note begins, all distinctions briefly narrow. Masons brace their walls. Gardeners open channels. Kiln workers seal their masks. Children press palms to the floor. Ancestor tiles murmur in their sockets. Across the uninhabited spans, the walking life and the rooted life lean under the same pressure.

Then the whole city breathes out together.
