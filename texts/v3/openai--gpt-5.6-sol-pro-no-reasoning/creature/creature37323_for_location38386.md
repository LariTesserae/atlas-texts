The one who lives most fully in the Drowned Summit has no name of its own. *Custodian* is a title inherited from diagrams, stamped beside a part number in records that no longer agree on whether the word referred to one machine, a class of machines, or the work they performed.

It lives in the annular passage surrounding the summit chamber.

At a distance it resembles an unfinished mechanism being carried away by something translucent. Brass rings turn slowly within its flesh. Cables emerge from muscle, pass through ceramic eyelets, and enter again elsewhere. Its long body has no fixed boundary: pale tissue streams behind the rigid frame in veils and contracts around it when the current strengthens. Some parts are plainly manufactured—hinges, brush heads, inspection lamps, toothed collars polished nearly smooth. Others heal when torn.

No single end of it can confidently be called the head.

The cluster of black lenses with which it studies the western window is mounted near what seems, from the arrangement of its limbs, to be a shoulder. Its mouth is a ribbed aperture farther down the body, used mainly to rasp mineral scale from pipes. Along its underside, rows of amber organs shine behind thick transparent membranes. A few are bulbs. A few have pupils. It is difficult to tell which became like the other.

The custodian does not swim so much as fasten and release. Three or four limbs hold the mountain while the rest reach ahead, testing seams, clearing grilles, tightening old collars by minute repeated pressure. Its progress is deliberate enough that algae grows on the trailing sections of its frame. Fish graze there. Tiny crabs occupy two unused sockets beneath its forward hoops and defend them as if they were caves. Pale fry sleep between its ribs, sheltered from the Lung’s violent exhalations.

A circuit takes most of a day because the custodian attends to everything it passes.

It scrapes heat exchangers until water can move through their fins. It plucks ribbon weed from turbine mouths but leaves the roots intact. It probes cracks with flexible wires that may be whiskers or instruments. Where ceramic has fractured, it presses a secretion into the break: a milky cement seeded with calcifying organisms. Months later the patch is indistinguishable from the mountain’s oldest skin.

It eats what it removes. Rust, algae, shell, cable insulation, dead fish, flakes of mineral glaze—all enter the rasping aperture. Useful metals migrate through its tissues and appear later as bright deposits around worn joints. Organic matter feeds the translucent muscle. Waste leaves as fine fertile haze, so every act of cleaning also sows another generation of growth.

Thus it maintains the mountain by nourishing the things that obstruct it.

This contradiction does not trouble it. Its care is cyclical rather than corrective. It does not seek a finished state. It preserves flow: enough growth to bind the surfaces, enough open space for water, enough animals to graze the growth, enough accumulation to feed itself when it returns.

The Drowned Summit is not merely where the custodian lives. It is the organ by which the custodian measures time.

The Lung divides the day into breaths. The changing pitch of the old bearing marks the tide. Noon is the blue interval when light reaches the outer passage, and night is the amber succession of service lamps waking behind the walls. The custodian knows neither dates nor seasons, but it remembers disruptions. Its oldest tissues carry bands of altered mineral, dark rings laid down during poisoned currents, compressor failures, thermal inversions, and distant collapses.

Parts of it may be older than its living flesh. Parts may be older than the sea above.

When a hoop corrodes through, the custodian abandons it in a recess and grows around another piece scavenged from the mountain. When a limb is crushed, cables remain and become tendons for the replacement. Nothing proves that the being circling now is the same one first placed there. Nothing proves that it is not. Continuity in the summit belongs to labor, not material.

At the western window it always slows.

The lenses gather, dilate, and orient toward the Lung. Sometimes the custodian remains there through an entire breathing cycle, holding itself against the outer wall while bubbles fill the chamber with white violence. It watches the branches tremble, the gardens flatten, the bell moths tumble from their air pocket. When the water clears, the lenses follow individual creatures as they right themselves.

Whether this is inspection, curiosity, or companionship cannot be determined.

It does react to irregularities. If the Lung misses its breath, the custodian stops wherever it is. Lamp-organs brighten one after another along its body. It waits through the interval in which the chamber’s vines begin to close, then climbs to a particular plate outside the northern arc and strikes.

The blow is not random. Its heavy limb finds the same buried reinforcement each time.

Once.

Then again.

The sound passes through brass, iron, ceramic, water, root, shell, and bone. Fish vanish into the grilles. Shrimp cease ticking. Even the old bearing’s note seems briefly submerged beneath it. Most often the compressors answer, far below, and the custodian resumes its circuit without celebration.

But the threshold on the northern wall causes it to behave differently.

It never brushes the corresponding portion of the outer shell. Mineral buildup there is thicker than anywhere else, yet a narrow margin remains perfectly clean around the seam, as though the wall itself rejects attachment. When the custodian approaches, its forward limbs shorten their reach. The fish leave its ribs. Its lights dim behind their membranes.

It crosses that section without fastening to it.

For those few moments, the custodian truly swims. Its whole body contracts in a wave, pushing it through the annular passage while every mechanical limb folds inward. The movement is awkward and urgent. Its lens cluster turns away from the wall and toward the flooded chamber, as if the thin interior room offers greater safety than whatever lies behind the sealed outline.

When the cold water comes, at intervals too long for smaller animals to remember, the custodian knows first.

Hours beforehand it abandons its ordinary route and coils around the summit’s western braces. Clamps emerge from folds in its tissue and lock onto ancient anchor points. The creatures living in its frame are expelled by pulses of light. Its brushes retract. Brass hoops draw close until the loose body becomes compact and armored.

Then the northern seam exhales.

Cold spreads across the chamber and through the walls. The custodian’s translucent tissues cloud. Frostlike crystals bloom inside its lamp-organs. From beyond the threshold comes the vibration of machinery starting somewhere that should not exist: first one deep rotation, then another, then the layered tremor of an immense system accepting load.

During this sound, the custodian’s lenses close.

Not all at once. They shut in sequence from the outermost inward, like ports being sealed through a sinking vessel.

The mountain answers. The Lung discharges out of rhythm. Ballast moves in the depths. The artificial peak leans by a fraction against an invisible force, and the custodian bears the change through its locked limbs. Cables draw taut inside its body. Old metal groans. Sometimes a hoop bends and does not straighten. Sometimes pale tissue tears and drifts from it like shed cloth.

Still it holds.

When warmth returns, the custodian remains anchored for a long while. Fish reappear before it moves. Shrimp enter the folds of its damaged tissue to feed and clean. Bladder-vine seeds catch on its brushes. By the time it releases the braces, life has already begun repairing what the correction cost.

It unfolds slowly and resumes its circuit at the exact point where it abandoned it.

In the summit chamber, the silver fish return first. The bell moths rise. Leaves open. The Lung settles into its eleven-minute measure. Through the western window, much later, the custodian passes again: a dim procession of brass, cartilage, cable, and light.

It slows.

Its surviving lenses turn toward the room.

Then it goes on, carrying the summit’s smallest inhabitants within its ribs, tending the immense machine that contains them all, preserving a purpose it may no longer understand against a danger it has never forgotten.
