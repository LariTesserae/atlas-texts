The High Orchard is inhabited by the Orchard itself.

Its “plants” are not plants in the wet, cellular sense, but a mineral ecology suspended at perfect equilibrium. Pale scale-fronds, black filament cushions, silver lattice-fans, and finger-high ceramic trunks root into quartz seams with copper-colored tendrils. They feed on strain made electrical: the mountain’s minute compression passing through their crystalline tissues as charge. Carbon, silicon, salt, and mineral vapor have assembled into something that resembles vegetation because vegetation is the nearest available word for such dense, branching occupation.

They are alive only at the threshold where life becomes indistinguishable from machinery. Nothing grows, yet every structure remains metabolically active. Nothing withers, because no process gains enough advantage to carry change forward. A broken frond contains nested glass capillaries, clean and hollow, without sap or moisture. Its fracture does not kill it. Given time—or merely averted attention—it persists both broken and unbroken, each state ancient, stable, and overgrown.

Among this vegetation lives a second order of forms: factories without workers.

Needle smokestacks rise between silver fans. Seed-sized ore carts wait on rails along precipices that fit beneath a hand. Derricks, bridges, stairways, roofs, conduits, cabinets, and immense-seeming industrial districts fill the dust valleys. They may be machinery, mineral mimicry, or settlements belonging to a scale at which the chamber is a continent. Their apparent size depends less on measurement than on attention. Touched, they are tiny. Watched, they withdraw into distance.

No builders remain. Perhaps none ever existed.

The machines nonetheless produce the Orchard’s perpetual roar. Dead flywheels contribute turbine thunder. Slack belts grind like mills. Motionless transformers hum. Every factory, from the three engines near the pressure door to the remotest works beneath the central tree, sounds simultaneously. They do not transmit noise through space; they participate in a single noise already present everywhere. A struck railing joins the chord from every depth at once.

The Orchard also harbors instruments—drills, lamps, winches, frames, stakes, and lengths of cable—all aged beyond use and swallowed by crystalline growth. Some are versions of tools that have not yet been carried inside. These are the place’s nearest equivalent to offspring. Intervention breeds no lasting alteration, only additional histories: the cut railing and the uncut one, the deployed instrument and the instrument still outside, layered together until exploration itself becomes another immobile industrial stratum.

Nothing animal lives there. There are no mites in the crevices, no spores in the air, no microbes upon the rails. No mouth has grazed the fronds. No body has decomposed among the roots. The emptiness is exact. Forest and factory alike retain the shapes that elsewhere would summon birds, insects, laborers, vermin, and decay, but every niche remains unclaimed.

At the Orchard’s center stands the thing to which all the rest may belong.

It is a pale ceramic tree upon an anvil-shaped peak, at once near enough to touch in detail and remote enough to have cities weathering around its roots. Its crown occupies the chamber’s remaining volume without quite touching itself. Its black roots bind salt terraces, quartz veins, conduits, and machine housings into one system, then descend through every available scale toward a bottom that cannot be reached.

The tree is not monarch, organism, or engine alone. It may be the mature form of the entire mineral ecology. It may be the factory that manufactures the factories, or the fixed pattern from which the lesser growths precipitate. It may be a physical expression of the mountain’s stored strain—a standing circuit vast in perceived extent but folded into a cupboard-sized fault.

Within its hollow trunk resides the Orchard’s deepest inhabitant: an unwavering resonance.

It has no heartbeat and no rhythm. It does not call, communicate, or vary. It is simply present, an immense operating note with no beginning, louder than any movement and produced by none. The crystalline forest conducts it. The dead machinery divides it into mechanical harmonics. The cliffs carry it. The suspended dust marks its invisible architecture.

If the High Orchard possesses anything like a mind, that mind is not an individual hidden somewhere inside. It is the equilibrium of the whole place: every charge entering as another leaves, every fall opposed, every distance expanding to contain observation, every injury retained without becoming consequence. It neither sleeps nor wakes because those would be changes. It neither wants nor refuses.

It persists.

And because nothing within it can finish happening, everything that has entered the Orchard may be said to live there still—not as ghosts or captives, but as possibilities packed among the branches, ancient from the instant they appear, contributing forever to the sound of an engine that has no need to turn.
