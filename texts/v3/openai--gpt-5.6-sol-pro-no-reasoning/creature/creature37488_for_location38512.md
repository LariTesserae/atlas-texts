The people who live there call themselves the Unfinished, though the word in their own speech also means *those who leave room*.

There are fewer than a hundred of them. No one agrees on the count, because they do not count people directly. To name a total is to suggest that the settlement has reached its proper number, and proper numbers belong only to the dead. Instead they count occupied hearths, working hands, nursing children, and voices able to carry a building song. One census might therefore say twenty-three fires, fifty-six hammers, nine milk-mouths, and seventy-one voices.

They are compact, strong-backed people, shaped by climbing wet stairs and moving stone in cold air. Most have broad hands, scarred palms, and one shoulder thicker than the other from years of hammering. Their feet splay from gripping slick ground through soft hide shoes. Even the elders walk with a testing gait, placing each step as though listening through the sole.

Their clothing is wool, felt, hide, and coarse plant fiber, layered against damp rather than severe cold. Cloaks are short so they do not drag through channels. Their hems carry stitched patterns indicating household and trade, but these designs are always interrupted before they close. Rings remain open. Spirals stop half a turn early. Braids end in loose strands. A perfectly bordered garment would be considered both vain and unlucky.

Hair is usually bound close to the head with cords, copper wire if a household possesses it, or strips of dyed wool. Builders often rub pale stone dust into their braids without meaning to. Water keepers stain their fingers green from clearing moss and medicinal growths. Echo-mappers wear small clay tabs threaded at the throat, each shaped like one of the signs fixed to walls: spiral, bar, or empty circle.

Their city exceeds them so greatly that they do not experience solitude as people elsewhere might. Empty buildings are not abandoned buildings. A vacant house may already belong to a child still unborn, or to a family expected in some distant generation. People greet certain doorways as they pass. They leave tools in workshops whose eventual artisans have no names. A person may repair the roof of a future descendant’s home with greater care than their own.

Children are not assigned houses at birth. Instead, each child is associated with an unfinished place: a stair, cistern, bridge, terrace, gate chamber, or length of wall. They are expected to tend it as they grow and alter it at least once. A child whose place is a dry basin may spend a lifetime waiting for the choosing to bring water there. Another may inherit a stair leading into a cliff and add three steps every decade, though no destination is planned.

No one is expected to complete what they inherit.

The highest-status inhabitants are not rulers but master listeners. Age matters less than discernment. A listener must distinguish a present footfall from one delayed in stone, rain under paving from water inside a wall, and an old voice from a living person calling through fog. They know which chambers can safely hold spoken plans and which may release them at the wrong season. Before a dispute, birth naming, marriage promise, or change to the city’s channels, a listener selects the place where the words should be said.

Some locations return speech almost immediately and are used for agreements, because all parties hear their own statements repeated. Some retain sound for years and are reserved for instruction. A few swallow every noise. These are avoided by most, but tended by silent keepers who clear moss, inspect cracks, and enter only in pairs tied together by cord.

The silent keepers are considered necessary and slightly apart. They communicate in taps, gestures, and pressure on a shared rope while at work. People say they become difficult to overhear even outside the silent zones. Dogs fail to wake when they pass. Children dare one another to follow them, but rarely do.

Builders form the greater part of the population. Their work is divided not by structure but by action: cutters, bruisers, lifters, setters, level-finders, seam-readers, and singers. A person may remain a bruiser for forty years, striking the same class of blow, and be honored for knowing exactly how long stone can endure force before it must rest.

They believe stone tires.

A newly worked block is left untouched for days while its internal echoes settle. A lintel that sounds too bright under tapping is shaded with wet cloth. A dull one is warmed by small fires. Before lifting, workers press their cheeks to the rock and hum. If the returned vibration is uneven, the block is turned or abandoned where it lies. Such rejected stones are not waste. They become seats, markers, garden edges, or simply named presences beside the road.

Their tools pass through households, but major works belong to the city. Hammers often have genealogies longer than families. Handles are replaced repeatedly, heads reshaped until they fit no standard form. A famous hammer may have only a thumb-sized fragment of its first stone remaining. It is still considered the same tool because its working rhythm has been preserved.

Meals are modest. Grain and dried roots come from lower slopes by paths hidden beyond the cliff gaps, though journeys down are infrequent and treated almost as departures into another world. Within the bowl they grow beans, sorrel, bitter leaves, bulb plants, and medicinal herbs. Floating turf is valuable enough to be given as a marriage gift. Families exchange squares of living soil, towing them through flooded lanes on woven slings.

Meat is rare. Snails are gathered after rain and cooked with herbs. The tiny wall-climbing amphibians are never eaten. They are called the First Strikers because their brief chorus announces rain before it hits stone. Children imitate them by rattling pebbles in cups, but adults forbid this under clear skies, lest the mountain confuse imitation with forecast.

The crows belong to no one, although several households insist that particular birds recognize them. Bright scraps are left on the ring cliffs: fishhooks too bent to mend, flakes of mica, twists of copper, white snail shells. If a crow takes an offering, its giver watches where it flies, believing the path may reveal a new crack, spring, or sound chamber.

Death does not remove a person from the daily life of the city as cleanly as elsewhere. A voice may remain in the mountain, sometimes for seasons, sometimes for generations. Therefore the dead are not represented by statues or carved names. Their surviving sounds are enough.

Funerals are held in places that return little echo. The dead person’s tools are struck once, their household sings an incomplete version of their work rhythm, and their body is placed in a shallow stone chamber left open along one side. No chamber is sealed. Over time, moss, water, and root mats enter. If the dead person’s voice later emerges from a well or seam, no one calls it a spirit. Yet relatives may answer all the same.

Marriage is marked by opening rather than joining. The two households remove a stone from each of their walls and exchange them. Each stone is fitted loosely into the other house, leaving a visible seam. Couples often live apart for part of the year according to work, spring behavior, or the location of the structures under their care. Kinship is understood as a network of maintained openings, not a closed descent line.

Arguments are common but rarely shouted. Anger spoken carelessly may return long after its cause is gone. Children are taught that every word has three lives: the one in the mouth, the one in the listener, and the one in the stone. Apologies must therefore be made in at least two places—where the offense was heard and where its echo is likely to travel.

Their language favors verbs of emergence, leaning, seeping, loosening, and waiting. It has many terms for wetness: water newly condensed, water uncertain of its course, water returning to an old channel, water that appears where speech was stored. There is no ordinary verb meaning “to finish.” The nearest expression means “to close against change,” and is usually an accusation.

Their personal names are provisional. A child receives a short name after birth, generally based on weather, sound, or some event in the stone. Later, another may be added after a person demonstrates a craft or survives a choosing. Names are not erased; they accumulate. An elder may possess seven or eight, sung in sequence on formal occasions. The final note is always left unresolved.

Visitors from below are rare. Those who arrive often find the inhabitants evasive, for questions such as “Who built this?” or “When will it be done?” have no sensible answers here. The city was built by hands still working and by hands not yet born. Completion is not expected. Expansion is not conquest but hospitality toward the future.

The Unfinished do not see themselves as few. They regard the empty streets as densely inhabited by intention. A vacant chamber contains a task. An unused stair contains a direction. A dry channel contains possible water. The plazas are not too large; the assemblies are merely late.

Every morning, before work begins, one person from each hearth walks the cliffs. Children often go too, solemn at first and then distracted by snails, puddles, or crows. The listeners press ears to the pale stone. Builders hold hammers loosely so the heads do not knock against their legs. No one speaks until the circuit is complete.

Most mornings, the mountain has made only small noises: cooling ticks, trapped water, the soft internal slip of mineral sheets. These are noted and the day proceeds.

Sometimes, however, the stone returns a sound no one remembers giving it—a tool of unfamiliar pitch, a word in no known voice, the rhythm of many feet crossing a plaza that stands empty.

When that happens, the people do not ask whether it comes from the past.

They mark the place, clear the waiting streets, and make more room.
