They are called the Inquilines, though they have no name for themselves.

At first they are easy to mistake for some underlayer of the settlement: pale knots in root hollows, folded membranes beneath shelf-fungi, tassels of lichen hanging where walls meet floors. They possess neither the clean symmetry of animals nor the rooted certainty of plants. Their bodies are soft, narrow, and jointed only where habit has taught them to bend. Fine filaments cover them, pearl-gray in dry cold and darkening to green when wet. If one remains motionless against a living wall, moss spores settle and germinate on it. After a season, even another Inquiline may fail to see it until it moves.

They are small enough to pass through lanes that would stop a mountain bird, yet large enough to disturb the city by doing so. Most stand no higher than the columnar shrubs’ lower forks. Their limbs are long and compressible, with many-fingered ends that close like root tips around stems. They have no hard feet. Instead, the undersides of their hands and lower limbs are covered in thousands of backward-facing fibers, allowing them to cling to wet bark, crawl beneath bridges, or sleep upright against a wall while snow loads the roof around them.

Their faces are simple and difficult to read. Two dark, lidless patches register brightness and motion but little detail. Between them lies a vertical seam fringed with sensory hairs. Through this seam they taste resin, water, bruised leaves, fungal breath, approaching frost, and the faint mineral signatures of the springs below. Their mouths are hidden in a soft pleat under the head. They eat by pressing this pleat against ripe fruit, fungal flesh, pollen slurry, or leaves already beginning to dissolve. Nothing living and whole is bitten. Whether this is restraint, incapacity, or law cannot be distinguished.

They do not build the settlement. They persuade it.

An Inquiline wishing to widen a passage will sleep there for weeks, its warmth and breath altering the growth of the adjoining walls. To preserve a doorway, several may return each night and lean against the same young stems until those stems harden into an arch. They redirect runners with loops of their own shed fiber, never pulling hard enough to break them. They carry pollen between districts on their damp bodies. They empty an overfull cistern flower by lowering a porous tongue into it and letting the water travel down their limbs into a neighboring gutter.

These acts are slight, patient, and never fully obedient in their results. A wall encouraged outward may thicken instead. A roof opened for light may answer with a mass of violet leaves. The Inquilines accept such refusals. They seem to understand that force creates wounds, and wounds in this city are answered unpredictably.

Their dwellings are not private chambers but favored intervals in the growth. One may inhabit the warm underside of a root vault until a black pool rises there, then move upward into a dry fork. Several may gather inside a hollow formed by joined shrub trunks, pressing together without ceremony while sleet moves across the roof. They leave few possessions. Strands of shed filament are twisted into absorbent pads; empty berry skins serve briefly as cups; hard fungal scales may be tucked beneath a bridge to mark a route. All are eventually swallowed by growth.

They communicate mostly through pressure. A hand laid upon a wall sends a tremor along connected stems. Slow pulses mean water, damage, open passage, falling ice, or the presence of a bird. More intricate messages are tapped into taut leaves, though these vary as the leaf ages and tightens. In the dripping season the entire settlement becomes noisy with false signals, and the Inquilines rely instead on scent: bitter resin rubbed onto a dangerous stair, sweet fungal paste near shelter, crushed silver lichen to indicate a newly opened way.

Speech, if they possess it, is reserved for darkness. From the lowest chambers sometimes comes a sound like wet fingertips moving around the rim of a glass. One voice begins; others answer from root vaults, hanging walks, and enclosed galleries. The tones pass through the living frame more clearly than through air. Leaves tremble without wind. Cistern surfaces dimple. The song may continue for hours, changing whenever water shifts beneath the mat.

No individual seems to direct it. Nor is it clear whether the song belongs to the Inquilines alone. Certain flowers open during it. Root bridges tighten. Pale fungal fans brighten fractionally, and droplets leave the undersides of roofs all at once. The singers may be addressing the city, listening to it, or merely joining a process already underway.

Their lives are measured less by years than by migrations through the vertical districts. The youngest inhabit the dim, warm chambers near the buried water, where their bodies are almost colorless and their filaments trail like root hairs. As they grow, they climb into the crowded middle levels and become green with moss, spores, and minute plants. Older ones favor the exposed galleries. Their coverings silver in frost; lichens spread over their backs; their movements slow until they may spend entire months attached to one spire, combing cloud moisture with outstretched limbs.

At some point an old Inquiline ceases to descend.

It finds a place where the city is thin—a torn roof, a failing bridge, the raw edge above the basin—and fastens itself there. Its limbs stiffen around neighboring stems. The soft body flattens and becomes fibrous. Moss seals the joints; fungi enter without visible injury; roots pass through it. By the following thaw, shoots emerge from hollows along its spine. Within a few seasons there is no clear boundary between former inhabitant and architecture.

Such places are treated with attention but not grief. Passing Inquilines touch them. They sleep against them. They bring pollen from distant levels and press it into their damp seams. Many of the oldest bridges have pale cores, and some towers bend in shapes that resemble clustered bodies leaning into weather.

Whether the Inquilines arose independently or are another phase of the settlement is impossible to determine. Their young are never visibly born. After deep winter, when inner chambers have remained sealed for weeks, small pale figures sometimes appear among newly opened root folds. They carry the scent of mineral water and have soft green threads entering the skin along their backs. The threads wither only after the figures begin to move.

They are not numerous. A traveler might cross the entire saddle and see none, though signs of their passage would be everywhere: a line of pollen lifted above a flooded gutter, a dangerous lane marked bitter, a cistern quietly draining, a doorway held open by the warmth of a sleeping body. If startled, they do not flee at once. They still themselves and press into the nearest wall, becoming another wet fold among wet folds. Only their dark sensory patches remain visible, watching without expression.

The mountain bird is the one creature they avoid completely. At the first vibration of its landing, every Inquiline vanishes beneath roofs, into cistern shadows, behind translucent leaf-skins. The city appears empty. The bird drinks among the flowers while hidden hands grip stems all around it. Only after it has crossed the spires and disappeared into cloud do the walls loosen and the pale inhabitants emerge.

Then they resume their quiet negotiations: leaning, tapping, carrying, diverting, waiting. They belong to no chamber because chambers close, and keep no map because streets migrate. What they preserve is not the city’s shape but its continuance—the passage of water, the sharing of weight, the possibility of movement through growth.

They live there as nerves might live within a body: hidden, distributed, unable to command the whole, yet sensitive to every strain. And the city, in turn, lives through them, slowly incorporating their choices until habit becomes structure and structure becomes green law.
