The valley’s most characteristic inhabitants are the **fumarole shepherds**, though they neither tend the herds nor command the mountain. They are simply so closely fitted to its unfinished workings that, from a distance, they can be mistaken for part of them.

They are tall, four-legged animals, narrow through the chest and high at the shoulder, with the wary proportions of creatures accustomed to seeing danger across open ground. Their coats are charcoal gray to black, coarse along the back but dense and felted beneath. Moisture gathers continually on the guard hairs. When they emerge from cloud, they shine like wet basalt; when they stand beside a vent, condensed steam whitens them with rime until they resemble frost-armored stones.

Their feet are the feature by which their presence is easiest to read. Each hoof divides into four blunt, independently flexing pads that spread over loose scree and close tightly on slabs. The pads are pale, almost translucent at the edges, and insensitive to both freezing water and the mineral-warm mud around springs. A shepherd can cross crusted snow, slick rock, and newly deposited carbonate without noticeably changing its stride. Behind it remains an irregular chain of marks that often ends abruptly where heat, frost, or seepage erases the trail.

The head is carried low. Broad nostrils warm the thin air and detect sulfur, salts, predators, and approaching weather. The eyes are dark and protected by a second clear lid against steam and blowing grit. Both sexes bear backward-sweeping horns, not branching but layered. Mineral water collects in fine grooves on their surfaces; over years it leaves pale and rust-red bands. Old animals carry a visible record of the springs they have visited.

Fumarole shepherds feed sparsely and travel constantly. They crop pressed sedges, tear low shrubs from sheltered gravel, and scrape moss from seep margins with hard lower incisors. More important are the mineral films. They lick iron crusts, carbonate rims, and salt efflorescences, following changes in the valley’s chemistry with remarkable precision. A newly opened spring may attract them within a day, even from another drainage. When a vent cools or its water turns toxic, they leave before the change is obvious to anything else.

A herd is seldom compact. It may consist of hundreds of animals scattered across several kilometers, each maintaining sight of only two or three others through the shifting mist. They communicate with low chest-borne pulses, more felt through the ground than heard in the air. In calm conditions these calls pass along rock benches and frozen watercourses. A herd can therefore turn almost as one body while appearing to be no body at all: isolated dark figures changing direction one after another until the whole mountainside is moving.

They do not defend permanent territory because permanence is useless here. Instead, older females remember sequences—where warmth followed the failure of a spring, which snow hollow survived a dry year, which shallow seam became a torrent after thaw. Their knowledge is not a map of places but a map of transformations. The young learn to read fresh fractures, altered steam, hollow sounds beneath ice, and the faint tremor that precedes a settling slope.

Calves are born near warm wetlands at the beginning of the melt. They have unusually pale coats, the color of weathered stone, and remain motionless among mineral crusts while adults forage. Within days they can follow the herd across rubble. Many do not survive the valley’s rearrangements: pools drain, ledges collapse, warm ground becomes suddenly cold. The survivors become extraordinarily attentive animals. Even at rest, one foot often remains lifted, testing vibration through the other three.

The smaller residents live partly by following them.

A dark, round-bodied burrower—the **seam mouse**—colonizes the fine debris exposed where shepherds break mineral crusts. It has strong forelimbs, reduced ears, and incisors stained orange by iron. Its tunnels are shallow and disposable. Rather than maintaining one durable colony, a family digs many temporary chambers beneath stones warmed by groundwater. When frost tilts the stones or floods the passages, the mice move overnight. Their abandoned tunnels admit meltwater and air, hastening the breakdown of the valley floor.

Steam wetlands support clouds of minute, dull-winged midges. Their larvae graze microbial films in water barely above freezing, especially where hot seepage mixes with snowmelt. Adult midges cannot fly far in the wind. They cling beneath moss and to the lee sides of animals, rising in brief swarms whenever the air stills. Migratory birds learn these moments. Small gray finches descend in sudden sheets, feed for minutes, and vanish when the gale resumes.

Pale foxes shadow the scattered herds, though they rarely attack healthy adults. Their prey is whatever the shepherds disturb: seam mice driven from collapsing burrows, birds feeding at churned pools, larvae uncovered by hooves, or a calf separated in cloud. The foxes are long-legged and nearly colorless except for black ears and feet. Mineral stains often mark their muzzles yellow or red. They sleep near vents but never directly over them; individuals that cannot distinguish safe warmth from unstable ground do not live long.

Above them hunt the **ledge cats**, low, heavy-shouldered predators whose coats mimic wet talus. They wait rather than pursue. A cat may occupy the same shelf for half a day, gathering droplets until rivulets run from its whiskers. Its tail is short and thick, used as a brace on steep stone. It attacks from above, choosing narrow passages where loose footing prevents a herd animal from turning. The shepherds detect cats less by sight than by interruption: a place where finches will not land, where seam mice have gone silent, where the ordinary drip of water strikes fur instead of rock.

The great scavengers are always nearest the cloud. They have broad, flexible wings and bare, heat-sensitive faces. Updrafts along the scarps carry them with almost no effort. When visibility fails, they navigate by ridgelines of warm rising air, circling vents, sun-touched cliffs, and recent landslides. They descend quickly after a fall or avalanche, sometimes before the sound has finished crossing the saddle. Their nesting ledges are whitened not only with droppings but with carbonate dust carried on damp feathers.

The strangest inhabitants are under the water and stone. Colorless crustaceans crowd the warm margins of springs, feeding on rust-red bacterial mats. Pale worms occupy fractures through which mineral water rises. Deeper still, beyond light and ordinary seasons, microbial colonies live on chemical gradients created where heated fluids meet cold groundwater. They lay down dark skins and orange deposits grain by grain. Much of what appears to be geology is partly their work.

No single creature rules the valley. The shepherds are its most visible expression, but even they are temporary—a moving response to moving warmth. The true community is an arrangement of arrivals: ungulates following salt, birds following insects, foxes following burrowers, scavengers following falls, microbes following fractures.

When cloud fills the saddle, almost all of them disappear. What remains visible are steam columns, shifting patches of dark water, and occasional lines of tracks crossing raw gravel. Then a horned head emerges from the mist, wet and rimed; gray birds flicker around its feet; a fox pauses behind it; something vast turns once above the cloud base. For a moment the apparently empty valley reveals itself as inhabited from the heated rock upward.

Then the mist closes, and the mountain resumes making both the land and the lives fitted to it.
