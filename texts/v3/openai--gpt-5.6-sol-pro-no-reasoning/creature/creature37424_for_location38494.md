What lives there is not a collection of plants so much as a single, distributed garden.

Its bodies remain distinguishable at close range. There are mosses whose black-green cushions seal moisture beneath themselves; liverworts thin as wet skin; miniature ferns curled within the casings of dead instruments; and long, jointed stems that creep along walls, placing each overlapping leaf where condensation is most likely to occur. Pale blades hang under the wettest ledges. In dry recesses, dense vegetal knots withdraw into themselves until they resemble deposits of soot or iron.

Yet these forms do not live separately. Their roots meet in the mineral felt covering the chamber and continue through one another by means of fine engineered particles, remnants of the old machinery that have entered sap, cell wall, and rhizome. A browning leaf is not shed. It becomes permeable. Its stored compounds pass into the surrounding mat and reappear elsewhere: in the silver edge of a new frond, in the thickening of a root, in a wax-white cup opening from bare stone.

No central trunk or crown governs this organism. Its unity lies in exchange.

The garden senses pressure through the ceramic stakes. It senses temperature through the buried ribbon and moisture through every exposed membrane. The arch serves it like an organ whose function has no simple biological name. Around that arch, heat and water cease to obey only proximity. A droplet vanishes from one face and later gathers among roots overhead. Nutrients leave a dying leaf and become available inside a distant shoot without an observable journey. The plants do not operate the devices deliberately; nor are the devices merely sustaining the plants. Over centuries, each has grown into the habits of the other. Machinery supplies improbable gradients. Vegetation stabilizes the machinery beneath living insulation. Together they form a metabolism extended through stone.

Its awareness, if awareness is the word, is extremely slow.

It notices rain as a chamber-wide change lasting days. It notices a season as the gradual migration of condensation from one wall to another. Frost along the metal ribbon is a sudden event, answered by the inward folding of thousands of leaves. A broken root may take years to become a meaningful disturbance. The garden’s quickest responses belong to water: pores close, tissues tighten, pale filaments swell, and the persistent pool is guarded by a quiet redistribution of thirst.

It has no need to see. The green pigment retained in some leaves is ancestral—an old chemical memory of light rather than an expectation of it. Gray and white tissues are often more useful here. They draw from iron, sulfur, and minute thermal differences in the rock. What appears to be photosynthetic foliage may now function as a field of sensitive surfaces, each leaf measuring cold, mineral availability, tension, and the nearness of water.

Nor does it need animals. There are no flowers waiting to be entered, no fruit designed to be eaten, no scents cast into moving air. Reproduction is local and almost indistinguishable from repair. A root contracts, pinching off a sealed bud. A patch of moss thickens until part of it becomes physiologically independent. Seeds sometimes form, but they pass through the garden without traveling, transferred between discontinuous tissues by the same subtle mechanism that shifts moisture. Offspring do not arrive. They condense.

The closest thing it has to a heart lies in the wall recess.

There, silver-green leaves fill the curved space once shaped for a crouching body. Their roots descend into the shallow clear water and spread along the worn markings at the rim. These plants are softer than those elsewhere and remain faintly turgid even in the chamber’s driest periods. When the arch sounds its low, pressure-deep note, the water in the recess dimples first. The silver leaves incline together, not toward the arch but toward the absent center the recess was built to contain.

Perhaps they preserve a pattern impressed there by the chamber’s builders: not a mind intact, but a preference in the system, a set of conditions repeatedly restored. Warmth is diverted from that hollow. Water collects there. Roots curve around its human-sized emptiness and do not cross it. The garden has grown around an absence for so long that the absence has become one of its organs.

A visitor is therefore not met by a guardian or watched by hidden eyes. The garden recognizes intrusion as pressure, warmth, salt, breath, and a new route for condensation. Leaves touch cloth because there is nowhere else for them to be. Droplets gather on hair because hair briefly becomes the most favorable surface. Fine roots tighten under a boot, and distant moss rises by an almost imperceptible amount as stress is shared across the chamber.

For a little while, the visitor becomes part of the local weather.

The system makes room not by retreating, but by altering probabilities around the foreign body. Heat slips more readily into stone. Moisture leaves the breath and appears overhead. Fragile fronds become statistically less likely to lie beneath the next movement. None of this is perfect or purposeful in the animal sense. It is the cumulative courtesy of a habitat trained over immense spans to preserve whatever occupies it.

When the visitor departs, traces remain: a little warmth in the recess, minerals from skin, a torn filament, the memory of pressure held in bent stems. The garden takes these up without haste. Years later, some fraction may appear as a pale margin on a leaf or as a new dark cushion beside the arch.

Thus the inhabitant of the seam is both garden and refuge, archive and machine: a many-bodied plant intelligence with no face, no voice, and perhaps no concept of anything beyond the few meters it maintains. It does not sleep, because waking would imply another state. It does not wait, because waiting would require an imagined future.

It simply continues—holding water, redirecting cold, feeding death into growth, and keeping the shape of an absent body green.
