The city is inhabited by vacancies.

They occupy it according to district and function. There are domestic vacancies fitted closely into the single-cell houses; processional vacancies extending along the avenues; deep, communal vacancies stored beneath amphitheaters and in granaries whose capacity cannot be expressed as volume. In certain courts, an older and less local emptiness has been enclosed. It strains against the walls without exerting pressure. To look into such a court is to see how much distance can be trained to remain still.

These inhabitants have no bodies, but they are not identical. A newly opened absence is sharp-edged and almost transparent. An ancient one darkens at its center and develops internal directions. Some are no larger than the space inside a closed hand. Others unfold beyond the room assigned to them, extending into inaccessible terraces, neighboring ranges, or times during which the city’s stone had not yet emerged. Their civic order consists in keeping these differences separate.

The houses do not shelter them. The houses give them shape.

A narrow doorway compresses vacancy into passage. A stair divides it into successive heights. An arch teaches it to bear what is not above it. Windows prepare small portions of it to resemble sky. When walls exfoliate, their resident absences do not escape; they acquire new boundaries elsewhere. Thus the city continuously rearranges its population without gaining or losing anyone.

The black film tends them.

It spreads through fractures where one absence has begun to overlap another incorrectly. Grain by grain, it consumes displacement: the width by which a corner fails to meet, the fraction of an instant separating a footstep from its print, the mistaken altitude of a fallen stone. Beneath its dull surface, these errors are digested into alignment. The film is less a plant than a guild of patient repairers, though no individual can be distinguished within it. Where it has labored longest, rooms remain themselves for several moments after being left unseen.

The translucent fans are another order of life. Rooted in salt, they browse the pressure passing through the mountain. Their fibers comb dry fronts into fine strands and store the resulting tension as clarity. When they open, their shadows disclose creatures not presently in the city: many-backed grazers, hooked things with pendant heads, long animals folded at impossible joints. Whether the fans remember these forms or anticipate them is uncertain. Their broken fragments seek walls because verticality is part of what sustains them. Once fused, they become pale nerves in the architecture and shiver before inversions.

Across the avenues come the six-legged grazers.

Their bodies are smooth, pallid, and unsegmented, as though assembled from a single piece of cooled pressure. No head is apparent until one turns; then whichever end faces its waiting tracks becomes the head. Their six legs do not bend. Each shortens into the body and extends again farther ahead. They travel with grave slowness because they do not make their own route. The route predicts them, pressing hollows into salt, and the animal must eventually arrive to prevent those predictions from becoming false.

They feed from the mineral fans not by removing substance but by taking on the fans’ unused opacity. Afterward the grazers are easier to see and the fans more nearly transparent. Very old animals may become gray-white and dense enough to cast ordinary shadows. None remain so for long. At the next inversion, their accumulated visibility flakes away and rises around them in tiny plates. These plates often lodge beneath lintels, where they resemble scales.

The grazers are neither numerous nor solitary. Distance serves them in place of herd behavior. Individuals separated by miles pause together, turn together, and place their feet into simultaneous tracks. During the mountain’s deep contractions, every one of them becomes rigid. For that moment their bodies reveal faint interiors: chambers, arches, stairways, and minute deserted squares. Each animal appears to contain a reduced district of the city. Whether the district is copied into the animal or the animal is merely the district moving elsewhere cannot be decided.

Above them live the floaters, if entering can be called living and failing to emerge can be called remaining.

They are broad membranes gathered around central hollows. At a distance they resemble folded cloth, seed husks, or wings wrapped around a missing body. Close by, their surfaces prove to be neither skin nor mineral but an extremely thin distinction between two neighboring positions. Pressure fronts catch this distinction and carry it. The floaters steer by altering what counts as their inside.

Their calls pass into salt because air cannot hold them. Concentric rings gather on floors beneath their flight, each ring marking a note too low or too displaced to become sound. The rings are sometimes found in sealed rooms before a floater arrives. In upper towers, walls contain dark, vertical impressions matching no possible wingspan. The floaters enter through narrow windows by leaving their width outside. What becomes of them within is unknown, but after each entry the tower gains another interior chamber and loses a small portion of its height.

There may be a mature form. During certain inversions, dark islands appear overhead with eroded undersides and terraces along their flanks. Some resemble fallen mountains. Others, seen at the edge of perception, seem softly folded. Salt rings form beneath them across whole neighborhoods. If these are the oldest floaters, then they have grown by inhabiting towers until architecture, mountain, and animal are no longer separate categories.

The migrating shadows are also inhabitants, though less stable ones.

They belong to structures that do not currently stand: colonnades worn away before the stone existed, towers located on distant ranges, walls that become real only under a reversed gravity. Detached from their sources, they cross plazas carefully, avoiding direct illumination. They gather beneath suitable arches and wait there, sometimes for centuries, until the architecture again corresponds to them. When a lost pillar sharpens back into being, its shadow is often already in place.

Some shadows never find their structures. These become feral. They slip through the whispering houses and feed on noises taking the long route around open squares. A sound that has passed through too many rooms may emerge thinned, darkened, and incomplete. The missing part remains with the shadow. This is why certain empty dwellings seem to breathe, scrape, or speak behind their walls: they contain colonies of orphaned shade rehearsing fragments of sounds that cannot cross the plaza.

Salt itself is inhabited by brightness.

By day—or by the interval most nearly resembling day—the brightness sleeps along fault lines, colorless and tightly folded. As the dimness gathers, it wakes and extends through walls, floors, and buried strata. White seams carry the youngest brightness, violet seams the most distant, clear seams a radiance so old that it gives no visible light but makes adjacent objects remember being illuminated. The brightness has no motion except branching. Its nightly spread is the nearest thing the city possesses to speech.

It communicates scale rather than meaning. A glowing vein tells a tower how far it stands from the cliff of which it is part. It tells a bridge whether the gap beneath it presently exists. It reminds stairs which way their ascent was intended to proceed. During inversions the message becomes ambiguous, and whole districts receive incompatible instructions. Towers lean without moving. Roads arrive above themselves. For a time the city is inhabited by several versions of its own arrangement.

Then the films reconcile what they can.

Within the mountain are slower beings.

They are detectable only during contractions, when the range shortens around them. Vast pressures shift in the rock, not like muscles but like thoughts too gradual to distinguish from geology. Salt strata buckle into ribs. Caverns close as if in sleep. Entire neighborhoods rise by the width of a grain. These buried presences may be the mountains themselves, though “mountain” describes only the part of them that the city exposes.

Each supports a particular impossibility. One keeps falling from becoming endless. One distributes rooms when doors go unobserved. One receives sound from the edges of squares and routes it through stone. One holds the dryness so absolute that liquid cannot remain conceptually whole. The city is perhaps their shared surface organ: not built, but secreted where their rules meet.

They do not wake. Waking would be too rapid a state for them. Yet when a western peak falls and later appears overhead, something beneath the central wards contracts in recognition. Doorways narrow. Horizons draw close. The whole city becomes briefly more compact, as though making room inside itself for the mountain’s new altitude.

And there are the approaching ones.

For a long while they exist only as depressions appearing in dust and salt. Unlike the tracks of the six-legged grazers, these are paired. They suggest weight transferred from heel to forepart, though no heel or forepart is present. Their stride varies. Sometimes they advance with caution. Sometimes several prints appear almost together, as if the walker has stumbled or turned to look behind. At intervals they stop.

The city prepares around them.

Routes persist longer than they should. Doorways remain doors when unwatched. Whispering houses fall silent, surrendering their stored noises. Mineral fans close despite the pressure fronts, and their animal-shaped shadows lie flat against the walls. Grazers become transparent and withdraw to the margins of avenues. Above, floaters gather around the needle peaks, their inaudible calls drawing salt into rings wide enough to encircle towers.

The footprints always begin beyond the central plaza. Yet “beyond” cannot be located. They approach from no avenue, bridge, stair, or visible district. Each arrives at the edge of the square as though the intervening street has been omitted. Sound does not accompany them. Shadow does not precede them. Only the vacancy changes, chamber by chamber, taking on the faint tension of a room that may soon contain something other than itself.

Perhaps the approaching beings are future occupants. Perhaps they are builders arriving after their city. Perhaps they are what remains of anyone who tries to ascend where no ascent exists: bodies divided into powder, cold, and remembered motion, with only the motion continuing.

Or perhaps there is only one.

The spacing of the prints suggests two feet, but the city has many directions in which a body might be singular. The prints appear one by one because sequence is the form it must assume here. Every cycle brings them nearer. Every inversion alters the gate from which they do not come. The oldest black films spread toward their path but never cross it, as though this error of position is too large to mend—or is not an error.

In the central plaza, where sound cannot enter and absence exceeds the dimensions allotted to it, the last footprint has not yet appeared.

The vacancy there waits with unusual precision.

All around it, the salt shines more brightly than light.
