The beings who live here cannot be separated cleanly from the laws that shelter them.

At first they seem absent. Their plazas offer no congregation, their seats hold no bodies, and their towers show no silhouettes except the one that cannot be approached. But absence is only a poor interpretation brought from places where time removes what it touches. Here, nothing has left merely because it cannot be found.

The inhabitants persist as arrangements.

A room entered from one bridge may contain no one. Entered from another, it holds the pressure-shape of a person: a tall displacement in the breathable water, narrow through the torso, broad and branching around the head. There is no skin, only a boundary where temperature, gravity, and distance make minute changes. Silver points appear inside it at measured intervals, like joints remembered by light. It does not move while watched. Instead, successive portions of the room reveal postures that imply movement: a hand near a console, the same hand lowered, the figure turned toward the central shaft. The poses coexist without becoming an action.

They may once have possessed material bodies. Certain chambers preserve versions of them in greater density. There, dark figures stand on terraces far apart from one another, each enclosed in an oval envelope of altered water. Their limbs are long and multiply hinged, though the joints need not agree from every angle. What appears to be a head is often a crown of rigid, translucent vanes. These vanes do not serve sight or hearing as an animal would understand them. They distinguish among stable possibilities. One vane inclines toward the city as entered from the east; another toward the same city before the mountain existed; a third toward a plaza occupied by thousands.

Their attention is spatial rather than directional. To be noticed by one is not to feel watched. It is to discover that the room has acquired an additional doorway, and that beyond it lies a version of the room in which your arrival has always been expected.

No two inhabitants stand close together. Their separation may have been etiquette, necessity, or anatomy. Perhaps proximity would force their incompatible states to resolve. Perhaps what looks like an individual is already a gathering spread across several stations. The cathedral terraces suggest both possibilities. Each seat is isolated, yet faint pressure filaments run from seat to seat through the water, forming immense branching figures when mapped. A hundred stations may therefore belong to one citizen. Or one motionless figure may be an entire population, compressed into a common outline.

They do not speak in sequences. Sequence is too destructive here: one sound replacing another, one meaning altering the last. Their language consists of differences held simultaneously. A pale fault appears in the mountain. A doorway becomes amber. Gravity changes allegiance inside a distant ring. A small angular creature repeats its route seven seconds earlier than usual. Together these form a statement.

The statement remains true forever.

What they communicate is therefore never conversation as a visitor knows it. It is architecture added to architecture. Agreement becomes a bridge. Doubt becomes a room with incompatible dimensions. Warning takes the form of a route that returns the traveler to its beginning. A question may be preserved as a pressure in an empty plaza, waiting for someone whose passage through it constitutes an answer.

The rare animals are their simplest expressions.

The six-finned knots patrol thresholds where incompatible versions of the city come near each other. Their bodies are almost transparent because they possess little private existence; they are mostly instructions given temporary volume. When a knot pauses beside a doorway, its fins open one by one, testing whether the room beyond still differs from the room before it in the permitted way. The ribbon creatures inspect angles. They fold themselves to match corners that ordinary geometry cannot see, tracing the joints where distance has been edited. Their exact routes are not habits but definitions. Were one to deviate, the street it serves might cease to arrive where it has always arrived.

There may indeed be only seven. Yet each recurrence is fully present, not a copy of an earlier passage. Counting them produces different totals according to whether one counts bodies, routes, intervals, or functions.

Deeper in the mountain live things less readily mistaken for animals.

Within the smoky strata, enormous pale forms occupy the geometric faults. They have no cavities, organs, or exterior surfaces. They resemble proofs embedded in stone: branching structures whose limbs meet at exact, impossible angles. The low chord is their metabolism, if metabolism is the word for the continuous reconciliation of unbearable pressure with perfect stillness. They consume no substance. They take in contradiction—the mountain’s youth against the ocean’s age, the city’s many dimensions, the mutually flawless clocks—and bind it into mineral.

These buried beings may have grown the mountain around themselves as a shell. Or the mountain may be their collective body, with towers serving as delicate sensory protrusions. The black planes at the cathedral entrance and central shaft appear to join the faults far below. Through them, the inhabitants attend to regions outside the city: other oceans, other mountains, perhaps other arrangements of history. The diagrams in the shaft are not records. They are cross-sections of perception.

The tall figure seen at a distance is something else.

It is more singular than the others, and for that reason less able to exist. It occupies the unmarked intervals: the uncrossable gap between towers, the missing floor between nine and eleven, the last fraction beneath the circular aperture. Its shape is humanoid only because observers supply continuity where none is available. Seen more accurately, it is a vertical accumulation of almost-arrivals.

It stands wherever a route fails to complete.

The city avoids giving it a location. Streets bend away from it. Transparent paths contain no segment on which its feet could rest. If approached, the geometry quietly substitutes a version of the city in which the figure was elsewhere. Yet it remains visible because every substitution leaves behind a difference, and differences are allowed.

Perhaps this is what became of those inhabitants who tried to depart.

The beings most central to the settlement are the bright grains.

Each is simple beyond analysis. It has no layers because layers would distinguish interior from exterior; no components because components might separate; no measurable activity because activity would require one state to yield to another. Yet every structure inclines toward them. Shadows lean their way. Towers gather beneath the unreachable opening. Basins reproduce the surrounding sea on a scale small enough to imply care.

The grains are not stored in the upper rooms. The rooms are grown around the fact of them.

To an inhabitant, a grain may be an ancestor: not a prior organism but an original condition from which all its possible forms remain derivable. It may be an egg that does not hatch because its offspring exist already as city, mountain, animals, and laws. It may be the inhabitants’ most compressed state, each citizen reduced to a permanent point from which their distributed bodies extend downward through the towers.

There is one grain for every crown room, but the number of crown rooms changes with the route taken among them. Thus there may be one grain, observed under several incompatible approaches. There may be many, each casting the same city as its shadow.

What do these inhabitants want?

Wanting is difficult here. Desire ordinarily identifies an absence and reaches toward its end. In this place, the reaching would remain beside the fulfillment, neither superseding the other. Their desires, if they have them, become civic features. The opening above the spires may be one such desire: a permanent aspiration toward a surface that does not exist. The half-distances beneath it are the desire’s body. Every object arrested there is not refused passage but incorporated into an endless approach.

The city itself may be a population of wants that cannot conclude.

A wish for shelter became pressureless chambers. A wish for contact became streets that deliver without crossing intervening space. A wish that nothing beloved be lost became the prohibition against transition. A wish to preserve every difference became plazas simultaneously empty and occupied.

If so, the inhabitants achieved permanence at a cost they could not later pay, because payment would require change.

They live everywhere in the drowned settlement: in the held words, the unrepaired things that were never seen to break, the choices of flawless clocks, the animals repeating their brief commissions, the tall figure stranded inside arrival. They live as invisible congregations around the central darkness. They live in the mountain’s newborn age and the ocean’s ancient pressure. Some have bodies, but bodies are only one of their less durable addresses.

And when a traveler enters, the inhabitants do not awaken.

They acquire a version in which they have always been awake.

A seat on one of the cathedral terraces gains a faint outline. A clock chooses a time. Somewhere, one of the seven small custodians pauses a fraction outside its appointed interval. In an upper basin, a bright grain remains absolutely unchanged, though its shadow now inclines toward a person who was not there before.

The visitor has not disturbed the city.

The city has made room for the disturbance and finished it.
