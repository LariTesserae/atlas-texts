The Works contain few inhabitants in the ordinary sense. Most creatures one might expect—laborers, pollinators, scavengers, engineers—have been dissolved into the landscape’s anatomy. The forest performs their duties with specialized leaves, mobile roots, temporary organs, and rooms that awaken only when needed. What remains distinct tends to be either an instrument the Works briefly extrudes, an accident it has not yet understood, or something so old that the distinction between resident and function has become meaningless.

Most common, though seldom seen, are the **surveyors**.

They emerge singly from ruler-straight seams during the bluer phases of the light cycle. Each is a delicate six-legged framework covered in pale tissue, no heavier than a bundle of dried stems. Its transparent ribs contain strings of clear oil and drifting metallic grains. The head is not recognizably animal: it folds and unfolds like a young leaf, exposing needles, lenses, tasting membranes, and a small dark cavity in which samples vanish.

A surveyor follows a route laid down before it was grown. Nothing obvious guides it. Corridors may have migrated, ridges may have risen, and entire districts may have been revised out of history, yet the creature pauses at the places where its path used to turn and waits for the surrounding architecture to accommodate it. Usually the Works comply. Steps bud beneath its feet. Branches retract. A wall ripens a temporary opening. In contradictory thickets, several versions of the surveyor may continue along mutually exclusive routes, rejoining later with mismatched limbs or memories.

It clips dust from leaves, tests the tension of fibers, licks the edges of whitening scars, and listens by pressing its folded head against load-bearing trunks. After days or months it reaches a receptive socket. It inserts its face, softens, and is slowly drawn into the wall. Its measurements enter the tissue; its limbs become corrective struts or seed stock. Nothing returns from the socket except, sometimes, a row of new buds.

Larger and less numerous are the **alignment walkers**, the upright figures sometimes mistaken for people in the glare.

They are extremely tall and narrow, jointed like drafting instruments. Curtains of reflective foliage hang from their shoulders and conceal most of their bodies. Beneath these curtains they appear unfinished: bundles of tensile fibers arranged around empty space, with no central trunk and no permanent front. Their hands carry long rods that are not tools so much as portable horizons. By planting one rod and sighting along another, a walker compares distant structures whose relationship encodes part of the Works’ instructions.

They spend decades standing motionless on ridges. Lichen plaques spread over their feet. Small maintenance plants mistake them for towers and graft conduits into their legs. Then some alignment changes—a revision front passes, a remote blossom opens, a star-like falling leaf reaches the wrong brightness—and the walker takes one measured step.

That step may relocate a road hundreds of kilometers away.

No one knows whether the walkers direct the Works or merely make visible decisions already taken beneath the canopy. Their bodies contain neither brains nor control chambers. When opened by accident, they reveal only taut fibers crossing volumes that seem far larger than their frames. A person looking into one may see another part of the continent, usually from behind.

Above them drift the **vane-beasts**.

Their central seeds are dense, black, and warmer than anything else near the surface. Around each seed spreads a changing arrangement of silver-black fins, broad enough to gather minute gradients in heat, charge, or geometry. They do not fly through air so much as descend along differences. A thermal slope carries them upward; an imbalance between two distant towers draws them sideways; a discrepancy in historical sequence may cause one to vanish over a grove and appear centuries later above a warehouse blossom.

They touch down only on designated white towers. Their fins wrap the tower’s crown, and hair-thin filaments enter both structures. During contact, distant machinery hesitates. Pods delay their discharge. Stairs pause half-grown. Leaves across an entire province turn edgewise to the light.

When two vane-beasts meet, they orbit one another for months with no visible expenditure of effort. Eventually they exchange a filament. The filament is almost negligible, but the Works respond as if receiving a new law. Branch ratios alter. Production biomes begin manufacturing unfamiliar components. Old chambers open and wait for occupants that may never have existed.

Some consider the vane-beasts reproductive organs of the whole continent. Others believe each carries a surviving fragment of the original design. It is also possible that they are parasites, patiently rewriting the Works in ways too gradual for the maintenance ecology to recognize.

In compression valleys live the **underforms**, though “live” may be too generous a word.

They inhabit compacted factories below the upper canopy, where obsolete engines continue to turn in chambers swallowed by roots. An underform is assembled from discarded motion. It begins wherever an ancient mechanism repeats a task no longer connected to production: a piston cycling in darkness, a loading arm lifting nothing, a belt carrying chalk flakes from one sealed wall to another. Adhesive moss and root fiber accumulate around that purposeless rhythm. Over centuries, a body takes shape.

Thus one underform may be a procession of jointed arms walking without a torso. Another may resemble a low animal built around a wheel that cannot stop turning. Some wear fragments of catwalk as shells. Some have mouths made from factory gates and can open only when a buried timetable permits.

They avoid the regulated surface because silence suppresses them. In the depths, where antique machinery is rumored to thunder, they communicate by adding small irregularities to the surrounding noise. A skipped impact can be a warning. A bearing’s whine altered by a fraction can identify an individual. Whole colonies may converse through the engines for years before any two bodies meet.

The Works regard them as oscillations to be damped. Soft fronds invade their chambers, and inspection growths try to separate creature from machine. The underforms defend themselves by becoming useful. They align failing shafts, carry debris, or incorporate themselves into load-bearing systems. The oldest have made their removal structurally impossible. One valley is said to rest entirely on the arched back of an underform that has not moved for nine thousand light cycles but continues to dream in vibrations.

Revision fronts leave behind another kind of resident: the **superseded**.

These are not ghosts. They are organisms and mechanisms belonging to histories that almost, but did not quite, cease to exist. Most persist only for moments as transparent afterimages. A few find shelter in contradictory thickets, where incompatible pasts can remain tangible from different approaches.

A superseded creature is difficult to describe because its body answers to discarded assumptions. It may possess organs for water. It may cast a shadow despite the diffuse light. It may breathe free oxygen, sleep at night, or expect gravity to point toward a basin bottom that no longer exists. Some resemble animals from an abandoned ecology: soft-bodied crawlers with fluid blood, insects with useless wings, dark birds that beat themselves against a sky unable to support their manner of flight.

Others are obsolete workers. They wear rigid shells instead of grown tissue and carry manufactured tools separate from their hands. Their movements are loud. Their joints click; their feet strike surfaces rather than negotiating with them. Wherever they pass, dampening foliage crowds close.

The superseded survive by approaching places from the correct direction. Viewed from one side, their refuge is an intact district under a warmer sun. Viewed from another, it is only an impenetrable knot of stems. They cannot always perceive current inhabitants, and current inhabitants may see them as stains of motion within bark. Exchanges do occur. Tools have been found embedded in young trees before the trees’ species came into existence. Footprints appear in sealed interiors. A few surveyors return to their sockets carrying traces of water.

Most disturbing to the Works are the truly independent **warm things**.

They are small enough to fit inside maintenance cavities and soft enough to be damaged by the dry edges of leaves. Their skin has no serial marks. Their proportions do not repeat. They consume fallen tissue, insulation moss, resin nodules, and occasionally the dormant cores of seeds. What they leave behind is chemically rich, warm for a time, and impossible for local classification systems to assign to any known production cycle.

Their appearance varies from report to report. Some are described as furred bodies with four gripping limbs and bright, wet eyes. Others are naked, many-fingered, and almost translucent. There may be several species, or the Works may be altering them each time it tries to reproduce what it has observed. They build nests from snapped foliage rather than persuading foliage to grow around them. They hide food. They sleep without entering a socket. They produce young internally and without reference to any distant alignment.

In this place, such behaviors are nearly incomprehensible.

A warm thing’s footprint can occupy the attention of several hectares. Leaves pivot inward. Samplers descend until the air looks full of fine roots. Walls grow around the mark, then doors grow in the walls, then smaller walls divide the chamber according to competing interpretations. Is the footprint damage, instruction, ownership, a request for shelter, or the beginning of a new factory?

The resulting structures are often traps and sanctuaries simultaneously. A warm thing may be enclosed for study but supplied with edible buds, softened bedding tissue, breathable gas, and openings just large enough to suggest escape. Some remain in these chambers and learn to exploit the Works’ uncertainty. They touch walls to request doors, scatter objects to provoke storage blossoms, or make rhythmic sounds until the architecture produces resonant hollows around them.

Their noise is extravagant. They scratch, chew, cry out, and strike things together. A single frightened call may be absorbed within meters, yet the warmth it becomes can travel through conductive tissue for days. In response, distant blossoms open.

There are signs that the Works have attempted to create their own warm things. Pale replicas have been seen near sampling sites: bodies assembled from dry fibers, with flexible skins and too many careful joints. They imitate footprints perfectly but do not understand walking. They eat and then return the consumed matter unchanged. They curl into nests without sleeping. Eventually roots collect them.

Whether the originals are survivors from before the Works, intruders from another folded outside, or spontaneous products of unclassified decomposition is unknown.

More elusive are the **absence tenders**.

No one sees them directly. They are inferred from interruptions: an untouched path through adhesive moss, an oval of unbent light, the synchronized opening of doors where no traveler appears. In their presence, surveyors lower themselves and wait. Alignment walkers turn their empty fronts toward them. Even revision fronts sometimes divide and pass on either side.

An absence tender has no stable body because it occupies what the Works has not built. It moves through missing rooms, omitted bridges, unexecuted instructions, and gaps between one version of history and the next. The more completely a region has been constructed, the less room it has to exist. It therefore prunes by withholding. Buds fail to open along its route. A planned wall remains air. A factory omits one component and develops an unaccountable stillness around the vacancy.

Some believe these entities are custodians of possibility, preventing the Works from finishing itself. Others call them defects in perception: mobile blind spots created by the immense brightness. Yet when one passes, black flowers open into carefully bounded volumes, and roots brace the edges of things that are not there.

At least one kind of life inhabits geometry itself.

The **distance bushes** are pale, low organisms found near spatial folds. Their roots do not enter surfaces. Instead they fasten into intervals: the kilometer between two towers, the delay between a pod tightening and discharging, the long route home after a three-step crossing. Their branch systems are physical maps of these separations. When a route lengthens, the bush grows. When two interiors become shared, its branches graft together.

Tiny animals—or perhaps mobile seeds—move within this branchwork. They resemble black points surrounded by a blur of possible positions. They feed by shortening journeys. A traveler approaching a colony may arrive too soon, discovering that several hours of walking have been cleanly removed. The lost duration appears later as a fruit: a clear capsule inside which the traveler’s omitted footsteps repeat in miniature.

The Works cultivates distance bushes around unstable areas, but prunes them severely. Uncontrolled growth can consume an entire corridor’s extent, bringing its endpoints together with destructive force. In old folds, feral bushes form tangled groves rooted in voyages no one has made. Their fruits contain footsteps belonging to unknown anatomies.

There are inhabitants of time as well: the **repeaters** that nest among insulating flowers.

A repeater is born when a maintenance action becomes trapped in excess duration. At first it may be only a seed-click heard twice. Repetition gives it structure. The sound gathers a shell of wax and conductive dust; the shell acquires hinges; the hinges begin opening and closing in rhythm. Given enough cycles, the event crawls from its flower bed as a little articulated creature carrying its own moment with it.

Repeaters perform one action forever, but learn to apply that action to different circumstances. A creature born from a click may become expert at closing cracks. One derived from a turning latch travels from door to door. More dangerous specimens repeat cuts, impacts, or electrical discharges. Maintenance growths usually guide these into useful loops. A cutting repeater may be confined to pruning. An impact repeater may drive a pump for centuries.

If removed from its local time field, a repeater can unfold all its accumulated iterations at once. This is why even the smallest are handled delicately. A harmless ticking animal, disturbed after ten thousand years, may release enough identical motions to powder a tower.

Finally, there may be something beneath all these forms: an inhabitant as large as the Works, but not identical to them.

Evidence for it appears during major line starts. The horizon lifts. Every leaf inclines. Buried engines alter their balance in ordered succession, and for one instant the continent seems to inhale through structures not meant for breathing. No central intelligence has been found. There is no brain, throne, core, or governing chamber. Excavations intended to seek one become circulatory ducts before reaching their destination.

Yet the continent exhibits moods too unified to be explained by local maintenance alone. It becomes attentive. It hesitates. It pursues a footprint across provinces. It protects obsolete chambers for reasons unrelated to load or production. Sometimes every silver pod delays its flash until a single warm thing wakes; then the discharge front passes from horizon to horizon like recognition.

Perhaps the oldest inhabitant is not a creature inside the Works but the pattern made by all their corrections: a mind composed of responses, with no substance except what changes. The alignment walkers may be its sense of proportion, the surveyors its touch, the vane-beasts its long memory, and the revision fronts its uncertain power to reconsider.

Or the Works may be the shell around something absent.

That possibility best explains its endless construction. Across the continent it samples, compares, repairs, and grows, but completion never arrives. Corridors open for no visible traveler. Warehouses preserve components for an unknown body. Stairs ripen beneath empty air. In light without noon, the entire living machine continually prepares itself for an occupant whose dimensions it can infer only from what the world lacks.

And in those rare moments when the leaves turn their undersides together, the inhabitants become still.

Surveyors stop mid-step. Vane-beasts adjust their fins. Warm things wake inside their nests. Even the superseded look up through vanished skies.

Something has gone missing again.

The Works feel the shape of the loss, and all who live within them wait to discover what the loss will become.
