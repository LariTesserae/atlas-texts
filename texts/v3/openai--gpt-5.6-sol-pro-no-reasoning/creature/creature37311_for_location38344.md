The city belongs first to the films.

They inhabit it without distinction between body, street, and wall: white sulfurous braids around the carbonate needles, rust colonies feathering the mouths of springs, translucent sheets hung in the current. They are pasture and masonry together. They catch mineral dust, fasten silt, digest the dead, and soften raw stone with successive skins. Where a new surface opens, they are the first arrival. Where a passage closes, they are the last life pressed between its narrowing sides.

Among them graze multitudes small enough to treat a droplet’s curvature as terrain. Pale crustaceans move over the walls in herds, scraping narrow clearings that cloud over again behind them. Flat-bodied animals pour beneath mineral lips, yielding around obstructions as though their organs were only loosely agreed upon. Transparent worms lace themselves through the curtains, sometimes indistinguishable from the threads until they recoil. Red mites climb the ribs of roots and ice, bright, deliberate points in the colorless upper districts.

The blind shrimp live under overhangs. Their shells are almost absent in appearance, revealed chiefly by the dark machinery inside them and by the constant motion of their legs. They sift the spring-fall for edible grains, handling each particle before releasing or swallowing it. In crowded seasons they stand shoulder to shoulder at the oxygenated pores, combing the same thin current. Their discarded skins drift upward like empty replicas and rarely travel far before being eaten, colonized, or mortared into a wall.

Higher still live the pallid fans, descendants of algae reduced almost to patience. They spread themselves toward a brightness they may receive only briefly and store each weak visitation. Dust-sized bubbles cling to them. Grazers crop their edges; crystals grow through their centers; ice sometimes takes them whole. A single fan may spend most of the year neither flourishing nor dying, held in readiness for the next gray noon.

In the sediment below, pearly larvae stand on their heads. Their buried ends grip the mineral mud while feathery crowns open into the passing water. They harvest what the upper city sheds. When alarm moves through the fissure, the crowns vanish almost simultaneously, and the floor becomes a field of sealed holes.

What alarms them is often the long hunter.

It is milk-white and finless, made for corridors rather than open water. Eyes survive on it only as useless dim marks. Its mouth opens four ways, soft corners unfolding around prey, while the rest of the animal listens through its skin for displacement. It follows motion it cannot see. Before it, the crowded city becomes suddenly vacant: shrimp freeze under shelves, worms shorten into knots, mites flatten against ice, and whole grazing swarms cease their minute rasping. The hunter passes through a population disguised as architecture.

There are other predators too small to dominate so visibly: piercing mites, jawed larvae, threadlike parasites living in the bodies of shrimp. Some wait at the boundary layers, where swimmers are refracted and slowed. Some patrol the mineral haze around spring mouths. Some spend most of their existence as eggs, becoming hunters only during the narrow chemical season that permits them to hatch.

None of these creatures lives alone. Each carries a more intimate city: microbes in its gut, spores on its shell, eggs beneath its plates, parasites drinking from tissues that are themselves nearly transparent. A shrimp crossing from one water layer to another transports whole communities between neighborhoods. A corpse is therefore not one death but a succession of openings. Scavengers enter first. Bacteria bloom afterward. Minerals take the final shape, crusting the remains until anatomy becomes architecture.

The inhabitants of the frozen lens live differently.

They are not dead, though generations pass outside while they complete a gesture. Worms remain bent in unfinished recoil. Mites hold their legs above the surface on which they meant to place them. Embryos wait in ruptured eggs, neither enclosed nor born. Brine threads through microscopic boundaries around them, carrying just enough exchange to keep time from stopping altogether.

To the active creatures gathered on the lens, these sleepers are part of the landscape. Films pasture over their outlines. Predators hunt along the clear edge. Yet occasionally the ice releases one.

Then a survivor from an earlier city enters the present: a grazer adapted to a vanished film, a shrimp carrying obsolete microbes, a parasite for which the current population has no defense. It emerges slowly, but its consequences need not. Water distributes its passengers. Bodies become vectors. A color appears where no such color grew before. The old inhabitants retreat, die, adapt, or begin eating what they had never eaten.

Thus those who live here include the delayed: creatures belonging to arrangements of streets already buried, arriving after their own world has ended.

They also include the sleepers outside the lens—eggs cemented into clay, cysts lodged in root fibers, spores sealed under carbonate. A district that seems barren may contain several futures awaiting incompatible conditions. Fresh water wakes one population; salt wakes another. Oxygen calls forth grazers. Poison gives the lower chambers to red films and the animals that tolerate their chemistry. No single version of the city is inhabited all at once.

Its citizens have no knowledge of the lake, but the lake writes itself into their bodies. Winter enters as oxygen and pressure. Spring arrives as bent boundaries and invading currents. Summer narrows the habitable streets. Autumn brings foreign silt, dissolved leaves, and the mineral traces of storms no creature below can imagine. They answer these events without naming them: hatching, crowding, sinking, sealing themselves away.

They do not know that their tallest tower is shorter than a fingernail, that their migrations could be covered by a human palm, or that the ceiling above their ceiling bears snow and sky. Scale does not diminish what happens among them. A clay slump is a catastrophe. A newly opened pore is a frontier. A millimeter of oxygenated water is a season of abundance. The passage between two crystals may be crossed by herds, claimed by hunters, choked with generations of shells, and finally sealed before the winter above has ended.

What lives in the crack is therefore not merely a collection of minute animals. It is a circulation of forms: film into grazer, grazer into hunter, shell into tower, tower into rubble, rubble into film. It is the quick population and the arrested one, the visible animal and the microbial multitude it carries, the recent hatchling and the ancient immigrant stepping out of ice.

Nothing there understands the whole city. Each inhabitant knows only gradients: salt, pressure, food, current, touch, the chemical nearness of danger. Yet together they occupy every available interval so completely that the fissure seems less like a place containing life than a place life is continually inventing—filling each new void as soon as stone, ice, or accident allows one to exist.
