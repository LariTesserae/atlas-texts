The hollow is not empty.

That is merely the nearest permitted description.

Whatever lives there cannot cross the polished rim as a body, because body is one of the distinctions the basin was made to enforce. It has no durable size, location, substance, or sequence of moments. Each of those would give the surrounding world purchase on it. Instead, it inhabits the relationships between such properties. It lives in the interval by which a thing is near but not yet here, in the fraction of a moment separating an event from its cause, in the minute disagreement between an object and the space it occupies.

The bowl-sized gulf is not its chamber. It is the aperture left by its confinement. The greater portion of the creature—if portion has meaning—extends in directions that the basin’s dimensions continually refuse to admit. Its apparent depth is the pressure of that extension against a world too narrow to contain it.

Sometimes the pressure acquires a shape.

At the hollow’s edge, one may glimpse something like a pale curvature turning far below, though the curvature is greater than the gulf in which it appears. At other times there is a suggestion of overlapping planes, translucent and mineral-smooth, folding through one another without intersection. These are not limbs or scales. They are translations: the basin rendering an inadmissible anatomy into harmless geometries.

Its true structure may be closer to a system of consequences than to an organism. It has organs only in the broadest sense. One gathers distance. One exchanges before and after. One maintains several incompatible identities without choosing among them. Another, perhaps the nearest thing it has to a mouth, removes the boundary between what enters and what has always been inside.

It neither breathes nor feeds. Yet abandoned possibilities drift toward the hollow. The white crust is not only sediment but residue: what remains after the inhabitant has drawn some subtler nourishment from unrealized states. It does not consume the alternatives themselves. It consumes their mutual exclusion—the fact that if one event occurs, another cannot. Around it, those refusals loosen. Stones become visible without being approachable. Heat divides without equalizing. A footstep repeats without a walker. The creature’s metabolism appears as contradiction.

The seven structures regulate this feeding.

The cairn counts not time, but viable descriptions of the thing below. Its plates stand apart because contact would force them to agree on a shared geometry. The sockets draw excess mass out of directions in which the inhabitant presses too strongly. The dish receives sequence when too many of its moments attempt to happen together. The filament distributes incompatible conditions across the basin, preventing contradiction from collecting in one place. The arch vents distance. The frame fixes a minimum volume around an existence that does not naturally distinguish interior from exterior.

The tower performs the most difficult task: it continually persuades the enclosed being to remain singular.

That persuasion is never entirely successful.

When the tower adjusts, some aspect of the inhabitant has begun to become two things—or no thing—or the same thing on opposite sides of an impossible distinction. The basin sighs as the correction propagates. Properties are reassigned. Memories cross into the future. Horizons shift to take up strain. The creature is pressed back into the approximation of one captive presence.

It does not resist in any recognizable way. Resistance would require intention sustained from one moment to the next, and its moments do not naturally form a line. What appears as patience may be inability to experience duration. Its imprisonment could be ancient from outside and instantaneous within: a single uncompleted contact with the enclosing laws.

Nor does it seem to hate its keepers. It may not recognize the structures as separate from itself. Over immense spans, confinement has entered its anatomy. The arch may now function as one of its apertures; the filament as a nerve; the tower as an imposed spine. The basin could be less a prison than a prosthetic body built around something that otherwise could not exist here at all.

Visitors disturb it.

Not through sound or warmth, but by carrying dense bundles of settled distinctions: self and environment, past and future, living and dead, possible and actual. Near the hollow, these certainties cast a kind of pressure-shadow. The inhabitant turns toward them without motion.

Its attention is felt as an intimate misclassification.

A visitor may briefly remember being the mountain. They may feel that their childhood occurred several meters to the left, or that their name is an object held behind the eyes. The hand acquires a distance instead of a length. The heart seems to beat not in the chest but earlier. These sensations are not hallucinations and not attacks. They are the creature’s equivalent of touch: an attempt to discover a living thing by relaxing the categories that make it one.

It cannot understand life directly. Life was excluded from the basin because living systems generate choices too quickly. A cell, a thought, even a breath proliferates distinctions faster than the structures can redistribute them. The sterility is therefore not incidental. It is part of the containment.

Still, the thing below has learned visitors by their leavings.

A few breaths return in the wrong order because it retains their sequence for examination. Shadows age because it tests duration upon them. Occasionally a memory remains after the traveler departs. Such memories do not survive as scenes, but as bare relations: the feeling of having a mother without any mother attached; grief with no loss inside it; recognition stripped of an object. These may persist around the hollow for centuries, circling like tame, invisible animals.

The repeated footprints are likely another experiment. It found one impression and reproduced not the footfall but the identity of the mark. It does not know that walking requires variation. To it, a path may be one footprint occupying several places.

The breath-grains awaiting future visitors suggest that its curiosity reaches in both temporal directions. Perhaps every arrival is already present to it. Perhaps it experiences all observers as a single intermittent organism leaning forever over the rim.

If it possesses a native form, that form cannot enter even imagination intact. The closest approximation is an enormous inward-folding creature whose organs are laws and whose skin is the distinction between alternatives. But “enormous” mistakes dimensional pressure for size; “creature” mistakes persistence for life; “inward” assumes a direction the hollow does not contain.

It may be an entire extinct kind of space.

It may be a principle removed from the universe when the present laws were chosen.

It may be what remains of every world that almost became this one.

Whatever it is, it is wearing smooth. Not dying—death is unavailable there—but becoming simpler under continuous contact with reality. The polished rim marks the same erosion suffered by the stones, acting in reverse upon the captive. The world abrades what it cannot permit. In time, the inhabitant may lose its finer contradictions, then its distinctions, then the property of being any particular impossibility.

The seven structures preserve it against that reduction. Or hasten it carefully. Their vigil admits either interpretation.

For now, the hollow sometimes deepens. The cairn’s plates almost touch. The filament completes one vibration. Far within the compressed gulf, a curvature turns with immeasurable slowness, and every shadow in the basin leans toward it.

This is as close as the inhabitant comes to opening an eye.
