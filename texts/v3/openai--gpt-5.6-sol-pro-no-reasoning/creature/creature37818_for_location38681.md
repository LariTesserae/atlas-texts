The people who live there call themselves, in most districts, the Temperers.

The name has several meanings. It is what one does to clay before shaping it, mixing the soft earth with shell, fiber, or old crushed pots so that it will survive the kiln. It is also what one does to heat, light, grief, distance, and contradiction: not abolish them, which no one expects, but give them a form in which they can be inhabited.

They are generally small, dark-haired people, lean from work but seldom gaunt. Their skin is kept glossy with oils compounded from fig, rendered insect fat, fragrant reeds, and the wax scraped from artificial flowers. Under the fixed sun, age does not announce itself consistently. Some children have the fine white hair of elders; some great-grandmothers retain unlined faces and are recognizable as old only because of the number of names they carry. A person is not considered mature when their body changes, but when they can return alone from a place whose distances disagree.

They dress in narrow wraps of reed cloth, softened by repeated beating, and in aprons hung with pockets for pegs, cord, shell dust, knife flakes, and lumps of sealing tar. Wide collars of glazed chips protect the shoulders from the reflected glare. Almost everyone wears a little shade frame: a cane halo rising from the back of the neck, supporting cloth or overlapping slivers of shell. These frames make the inhabitants resemble walking pavilions.

Their feet are broad and scarred from ceramic seams. Most go barefoot in the wet districts, because sandals slip on glaze and can alter the apparent number of steps in a stair. On long journeys they bind their toes with black thread, so that each foot remains, ceremonially, a single traveler.

They do not build because they believe the country can be finished. Building is how they keep it locally intelligible.

Every inhabited compound has a Keeper of Edges. The office may belong to an old mason, a child with an exact memory, or several relatives acting in alternation. The Keeper inspects thresholds, pool rims, painted boundaries, cracks, changes in shade, and any corner that has become too convincing. They record not dimensions but behaviors:

**This doorway requires a burden.**  
**This wall opens into yesterday when wet.**  
**The fifth paving slab is longer for mourners.**  
**Do not count these steps while carrying figs.**  
**The room beyond is the same room, but cooler.**

Such records are pressed into clay tablets, though writing is distrusted if left unattended. A tablet can remain physically unchanged while its inscription acquires a different referent. Important instructions are therefore embodied as customs, songs, knots, colors, and choreography. A blue lintel means enter sideways. Three cords on a post mean the nearby water can be carried but not poured. A painted pair of eyes means the chamber observes sequence and should not be used for childbirth, cooking, or argument.

Children learn these conventions before they learn names for birds.

Their games are exercises in survival. They cross courtyards while balancing empty bowls, because an empty vessel may count as a burden if carried seriously enough. They race along paths that are short when walked backward. They hide behind unpainted walls and are sought behind painted ones, where they may be several hours younger. They practice dropping stones into cisterns and listening for the absence of impact. A child who hears a splash is praised for attention; a child who hears their own name is brought indoors without comment.

Kinship is architectural. A household consists not merely of those who share descent, but of those who wake under the same darkness. People who repeatedly dream the same route are regarded as siblings even if they have never met while awake. Adoption occurs by carrying a child through the family doorway with a household tool in their hands. Marriage is made by dismantling part of each partner’s home and building, between the compounds, a shaded room that belongs to neither former plan.

Because painted walls separate hours, spouses may live at different times while remaining only a few steps apart. Meals are left in transition alcoves. Affectionate notes are traced in wet plaster where the beloved will find them that morning, although the writer may have placed them there many seasons before. Some marriages are valued precisely for this patience.

The inhabitants have no single doctrine about who made the slabs.

Most say the world was fired rather than created. Before habitation there was a soft, undivided substance with neither shape nor sequence. Someone—or everyone—pressed it flat, cut channels into it, and subjected it to such heat that possibility hardened. The stationary sun is regarded by some as the mouth of that first kiln. Others say it is a work lamp left hanging over an unfinished model.

A smaller tradition teaches that the country is still wet clay, and only behaves as ceramic because nobody touches it in the correct way.

Their priests, where the word applies, are plasterers, perfumers, dream guides, and hydraulic caretakers. They do not petition the water to flow. They remind it of the route it has always taken. Before clearing a blocked channel, they place a jointed wooden fish upstream and recite the names of every bridge downstream. If the fish flexes, the blockage may be removed. If it remains stiff, the channel is declared to have become decorative, and a new custom is built around it.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere to bury them, and manufactured soil is too precious.

Bodies are washed in restored water, wrapped in a person’s bed-darkness, and carried through an odd-stepped stair. Sometimes the bearers return with the corpse. Sometimes they return without it. Sometimes the dead person comes back from the other side carrying one end of their own bier. None of these outcomes is considered wholly successful or wholly alarming.

Where a body remains, it is divided according to use. Hair becomes cord. Bones become needles, handles, flutes, and measuring pins. Flesh is placed in lidded compost jars among reeds and kitchen scraps, eventually feeding the circular tree beds. The descendants of such trees call the fruit ancestral, but do not claim that the ancestor lives in it. They say instead that the ancestor is “continuing as shade.”

Names are more troublesome. Since objects do not age reliably, a spoken name may outlast the person too strongly and continue attracting events meant for them. The dead person’s everyday name is therefore chipped from household plaster and ground down by hand. Grinding is sacred because it is one of the few dependable forms of change.

The powder is mixed into repair mortar.

Thus most compounds literally contain their dead, not as ghosts but as temper.

The builders form the largest and most honored class, though their labor has little to do with progress. They are divided into trades according to the kinds of impossibility they can safely approach.

The **Seam Listeners** lie with an ear against the joins between slabs. They tap the ceramic with shell hammers and identify regions where sound is too deep, too near, or answered from below. They are often hard of hearing by middle life, although no one agrees whether the seams consume their hearing or whether deafness allows them to hear the correct things.

The **Distance Carriers** travel in groups of three. One walks the road, one measures it with cord, and one keeps the destination in sight. They are forbidden to compare findings until all three have eaten and slept beneath the same roof. Their disagreements are then braided into a route rope: a short strand, a long strand, and a loop representing visible distance. Travelers do not ask how far away a place is. They ask which strand it belongs to.

The **Shade Dredgers** work in roofless rooms, collecting accumulated coolness in black bowls. Freshly gathered coolness has the consistency of heavy air and can be poured only in shadow. It is used for fever, metalworking, food storage, and the preparation of sleeping chambers. If carried into full brightness, it does not warm; it simply ceases to have been collected.

The **Hour Painters** mix pigments according to temporal effect. Red ocher generally separates an earlier hour from a later one, though the direction depends on whether the wall faces water. Green pigment produces parallel hours in which different tasks may be completed. White is avoided because the country itself uses white too fluently. Blue is reserved for rooms in which a promise must remain true longer than the people who made it.

The **Breakers** deliberately destroy things. Their profession is regarded with a mixture of reverence and unease. Since erosion does not occur unaided, all useful dust, rounded gravel, worn surfaces, and softened edges must be manufactured. Breakers grind old blades into powder, dull dangerous corners, crush pots for temper, and abrade new thresholds until they stop returning travelers to the wrong side.

They are the only people permitted to say that a thing is finished.

Food comes from the assigned abundance of the gardens. Dates, figs, marsh tubers, sour greens, frog eggs, fish, antelope milk, bird eggs, and cakes of pounded reed pith form the ordinary diet. Nothing overruns its bed, but nothing fully fails either. Harvesting must follow local etiquette. A fig taken while its shadow touches the trunk is sweet. The same fig taken when its shadow touches water contains seeds made of green glass. These are collected and traded, though no one can plant or melt them.

Fish are eaten without attempting to resolve what they are. Cooks prefer to kill them underwater, where they are flesh, then transfer them directly to covered pots. Fish cleaned in the open air may contain hinges, warm organs, carved ribs, or smaller channels in which still smaller fish are swimming. Such discoveries are not considered unclean, but they complicate dinner.

Jointed fish are sometimes kept as household companions. They sleep in basins, accept scraps, and click softly when strangers approach. If one is loved for many years, its wooden exterior may become scaly and soft. If neglected, an ordinary fish may acquire lacquer.

No creature is classified solely as living or made. The people instead distinguish between things that can participate in obligation and things that cannot.

A plaster tree that shades a sleeping child has fulfilled an obligation. A fruiting tree that refuses its appointed shadow has not. A clay flower holding scented oil may be invited to a wedding. A lizard may be used as a door latch if it repeatedly chooses the same gap and does not object. The categories are practical, courteous, and fluid.

The silent animals are treated with particular respect because they are thought to hear what sound becomes after the air consumes it. Pale frogs are kept near sickbeds. Marsh birds are allowed to pass through workshops even when their feet disturb measurements. Dragonflies are consulted informally: if one remains fixed above a bowl, the water is considered present; if it drifts, the bowl may contain water only from certain viewpoints.

The Temperers themselves speak softly. Loudness is wasteful where sound dies so quickly. Conversation takes place at arm’s length and is supported by touch: two fingers against the wrist for a question, a palm briefly covering the back of the hand for refusal, knuckles touching to indicate that something is true in more than one incompatible way.

Their music is mostly percussive and architectural. They play walls, pools, stretched ropes, hollow stairs, and rows of suspended droplets. Melodies are short because notes do not travel, but rhythms can be passed from courtyard to courtyard. At festivals, one compound taps a pattern on ceramic; the next repeats what it hears; another takes it up beyond that. By the time a rhythm crosses a district it has changed completely, yet the people call the final pattern the same song.

There are singers, but they sing directly into jars. The jars are sealed and opened later in dark rooms, where the song emerges as coolness, fragrance, a trembling of the floor, or occasionally a voice belonging to no singer present.

Their rulers have little command over territory, since territory does not submit to a single measurement. Authority rests instead with those who can coordinate customs across discontinuous places. A prominent leader may be called a Binder of Nine Thresholds, a Speaker for the Western Cisterns, or the Aunt of the Long Causeway. Councils meet by constructing temporary rooms with several entrances. Each delegation uses a different door and may arrive at a different hour, but if the plaster mixture is correct, they share the same meeting.

Disputes concern water use, compost, shade, salvage rights, and the ownership of reliable routes. Violence occurs, though open warfare is difficult. Armies cannot agree on distance. Painted fortifications may delay attackers into another season. A force passing through the wrong doorway can return home believing it has conquered somewhere else.

For this reason, feuds are often pursued through construction. One lineage may build an odd stair facing a rival’s granary. Another may paint a wall so that a disputed harvest belongs to the previous afternoon. The most severe hostile act is to alter a customary crossing without marking the change. Such sabotage can strand a family inside its own house.

Punishment usually takes the form of compulsory repair. A wrongdoer may be sentenced to round a thousand sharp shards, carry shade to an exposed nursery, or visit the relatives of plaza-bound workers and deliver their messages. Permanent confinement is considered redundant. There are already places that do not let people leave correctly.

The workers caught in stored afternoons are not pitied in the same way as the sick. They are regarded as having become partly occupational, like a kiln that is also an uncle. Their families maintain relationships with them across the plaza edge. A worker may give advice on grandchildren born centuries after the first brick was raised. Food shared with them returns uneaten if counted, but diminishes normally if no inventory is kept.

Some plaza workers become renowned teachers. Since they never tire and never advance past the first course, they have exceptional insight into beginnings.

At the farthest compounds live the Under-Gazers. Their houses cluster near the clean edge where the inverted wetlands appear below. They suspend beds beneath platforms and sleep facing downward, separated from the other country by glare and the row of unfallen drops. They communicate with the people beneath using mirrored boards, hanging cords, and gestures performed around the condensation.

Each side believes the other is inverted, but neither treats this as evidence of superiority.

Occasionally two inhabitants—one above, one below—recognize each other as the same person. Such pairs may move simultaneously for years without exact correspondence. One marries while the other becomes a shade dredger. One loses an eye; the other begins seeing a new color. They exchange objects by placing them beside a drop and looking away. Most objects remain where they were. A few reappear below with their handedness reversed. Rarer still, the object vanishes from both countries, which the Under-Gazers regard as proof that there may be a third direction.

The colossal forms are attended by small hereditary communities. The people of the White Hand maintain pools between its fingers and climb the knuckles to inspect the tool marks. They say the hand is neither sculpture nor body but an incomplete act of reaching.

Those who dwell inside the Hollow Beast build reed rooms among its ribs. They know every chamber in its torso, except for one corridor that can be entered only by frogs. Children there wear clay masks with wide animal ears. When they grow old, they choose a room in the beast and gradually stop leaving it. Afterward, their relatives hear them moving in walls that contain no passage.

Near the Great Stair, no one travels alone unless seeking their descending self. Meeting this other is not automatically fatal, but conversation is dangerous. The descending self remembers outcomes the ascending one has not yet chosen and may resent being contradicted. Pilgrims carry two identical bowls. If they meet themselves, each sets down a bowl and continues without speaking. When the bowls are recovered, one often contains mud.

Mud—not compost, not plaster, not wetted dust, but true unmade earth—is the most feared and desired substance in the wetlands.

A smear of it may soften a slab edge without abrasion. Seeds placed in it grow beyond their allotted abundance. Lichen spreads from it. Water muddied by it can rise when blocked and diminish when carried away. Things exposed to it begin to weather.

So do people.

Those who return from forbidden dreams with mud beneath their nails are taken to a roofless chamber and watched. Within days they may develop wrinkles, scars, hunger at unfamiliar hours, or hair that lengthens measurably. Their wounds close but leave marks. Their memories fade. They acquire, in short, the terrible freedoms of natural things.

Some communities scrub the mud away and grind the stained nail parings into sealed ceramic. Others revere the altered dreamers as Loosened Ones. A few secret households seek the red arch and crocodile canal deliberately, hoping to bring back enough earth to plant a garden that will overgrow its boundaries.

The Temperers fear such gardens. They also tell stories about them constantly.

In these stories, reeds spread without sockets. Trees crack their pots and root through the slabs. Rain falls and fails to return. Water runs over the clean edge and is lost. Buildings soften, corners round, and colossal hands crumble grain by grain. The sun finally moves.

It is usually told as a tale of catastrophe.

Sometimes, late in the made darkness, it is told as a promise.

For all their caution, the inhabitants know their country is not dead or static. It is intensely faithful. It keeps every contradiction given to it. It preserves each wall at the freshness of making, each drop at the threshold of falling, each road at all its incompatible lengths. The people do much the same. They endure by becoming customs around impossibilities.

They are patient, practical, sensuous, and exact. They value coolness, shade, skilled hands, reliable contradictions, and anyone who can return from an unfamiliar doorway without pretending it was ordinary. They do not ask whether their world is artificial. To them, artifice is not the opposite of life. It is the means by which life is given somewhere to stand.

Each morning, though morning does not occur, they rise under the stationary brilliance. They roll away the black cloth. They check whether their sleeping relatives have awakened in the expected house. They press yesterday’s fingerprints into today’s plaster. They clear channels whose water never accumulates, rebuild bridges whose destinations never approach, tend living vines on counterfeit trees, and carry food to workers laying forever the first course of a wall.

Then the builders return to the edges with their pegs, cords, bowls, and soft bronze tools.

Nothing is finished.

That is why there is always work.
