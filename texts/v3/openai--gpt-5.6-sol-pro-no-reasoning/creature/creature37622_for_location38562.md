Those who live in the summit crack are slight, deliberate people, though not uniformly small. The city does not favor weakness so much as economy. Broad shoulders are inconvenient in a passage that may narrow overnight; long limbs are useful only if they fold well. Most inhabitants have dense hands, flexible ankles, and toes trained from childhood to seek purchase without thought. Their bodies carry asymmetries accumulated from climbing: one forearm heavier than the other, a hip turned toward the wall, calluses following the usual lines of cable and rung.

They do not think of themselves as living at a height. Height implies a surface from which one may look down. They live *within*: braceward or seamward, toward the mouth or toward the cold, on the taut side or the slack. The plains are not “below” so much as “outside,” an immense horizontal country where nothing is close enough to catch.

Children learn three things before fluent speech: how to clip onto a line, how to become still when the warning crystals brighten, and how to fall correctly. Their first garments have dorsal rings sewn through reinforced webbing. In nurseries, cots are narrow sacks tensioned between walls, and toys are fastened by cords. Children play by sending washers racing down spiraled wires, constructing miniature braces from ceramic rods, and exchanging crystal cuttings whose growth patterns they recognize as readily as lowland children recognize flowers.

They are noisy only in places engineered to permit it. Certain chambers, isolated by layers of fungal hide and sprung metal, are maintained for argument, music, grieving, and infancy. Entering one can be shocking. People who spend their working lives whispering may sing with tremendous force. Their music is built from pauses, subsonic drums felt through the feet, finger bells held against the jaw, and taut wires stroked to produce sounds conducted through bone rather than air. Public laughter often begins silently: teeth shown, shoulders shaking, hands fluttering in the sign for delight.

In ordinary conversation, speech and gesture are inseparable. One hand gives the words; the other remains free for the city. A sentence may be interrupted because the speaker needs both hands to cross a gap, then resumed several landings later without apology. Names have spoken forms, signed forms, and structural forms. The last locate a person within webs of obligation: who shares their anchors, who answers if their platform slips, whose air they breathe, whose waste heat warms their room.

Kinship follows supports rather than blood alone. An anchor household may include machinists, elders, apprentices, unrelated children, burden-climber handlers, and whoever lives on structures ultimately held by the same principal bolts. They inspect those bolts together. They inherit the danger together. If an anchor fails, legal responsibility travels along its lineage, sometimes through contracts older than the rooms presently attached to it.

For this reason, no one is truly poor in space alone; nearly everyone is. Poverty is measured in bad tension, stale air, long unclipped crossings, unreliable lift priority, and dependence upon ghost-brine pipes controlled by another district. Wealth means redundancy: a second exit, a private condensing coil, three sound routes to a workplace, braces set into separate rock faces. The richest citizens possess rooms capable of surviving the loss of either wall.

Their clothing is close-fitted and layered with attachment points. Loose hems, long hair, dangling jewelry, and open pockets are considered signs of either recklessness or extraordinary status. Tools are tied to wrists. Babies are carried against the back or chest in rigid little cages that can be clipped directly to a railing. Veils and half-masks trap exhaled moisture; the condensate is collected not for drinking but for removal. After death, the tiny quantity of water in a body is treated as a hazardous inheritance.

Funerals therefore belong partly to sanitation crews. A corpse is sealed, dried, and rendered down with solemn efficiency. Bone goes to insulators or ceramic feedstock, fats to specialized lubricants, metals from teeth and repairs to the household bin. A stamped nameplate may remain, but even that is eventually expected to return to use. The dead are remembered less through preserved objects than through continuities of function. A grandmother becomes the pale gasket in the nursery’s emergency shutter; a friend remains in the handgrip of a lift brake. This is not spoken of as metaphor.

True water inspires a complicated unease. Most citizens find its unconstrained movement repellent. They dislike descriptions of rain, waves, and rivers—vast masses of corrosion falling from the sky or roaming without pipes. A reflective pool seems impossible and indecent, a surface with no load-bearing purpose. Water ampoules are used in medicine, precision chemistry, oaths, and certain punishments. To break one casually is among the few acts likely to provoke public violence.

Ghost brine, by contrast, is intimate and civic. Its conduits thread every district, marked with white ceramic collars and warning glyphs. Brine tenders occupy a position somewhere between engineer, physician, and priest. They listen to pipes through conductive mouthpieces, diagnose phase changes by the sensation in their teeth, and carry heat tablets in case a limb is caught in a leak. Their skin often bears smooth, pale scars where tissue froze before pain could warn them.

The city has no single government because it has no single stable shape. Authority gathers around immediate competence. Surveyors rule during shifts. Anchor courts rule during disputes. Brine guilds can close entire branches of habitation. Lift syndicates determine the movement of freight and therefore the timing of hunger. Developers claim new fissures by probing, mapping, and making them survivable; scavengers contest those claims if the opening exposes a crushed district that still has recorded heirs.

Laws are written as tolerances. How much vibration a workshop may introduce. How much weight may be added beyond a red brace. How many breaths of unfiltered air a factory may exhaust into a nursery duct. How long a neighbor must be given to detach before a structure is moved. Murder is often difficult to distinguish from negligence, so intent matters less than unauthorized alteration. To tamper with another household’s load path is the closest thing the city has to sacrilege.

Its survey crews are among its most admired inhabitants. They are narrow-bodied even by local standards, clad in pale ceramic scales and equipped with reels of filament, tuning forks, gas-sensitive fungi, and cages of shaft gliders. They enter new gaps before anyone knows whether those gaps are rooms, chimneys, traps, or the first sign of catastrophic cleavage. They map by touch and echo. Some return after days through a fissure no one else can fit through, carrying mineral samples, shed animal skins, or the stamped plate of a dwelling crushed generations before.

Others do not return, but their lines remain. It is customary to leave such lines in place until the mountain severs them.

Among the nonhuman inhabitants, burden climbers live closest to people. They are patient, warm, and nearly impossible to hurry. Each of their four limbs can lock independently around a rung, allowing the animal to sleep suspended while fully loaded. Their backward-turning heads let them watch handlers above while continuing downward. They dislike open chambers and may freeze at the edge of a large void until their eyes are hooded.

Handlers rarely command them by voice. Pressure patterns tapped through the harness indicate direction, rest, danger, and refusal. Old climbers learn routes as sequences of strain rather than landmarks; they can often find a landing after the city has shifted enough to confuse a human courier. When one stops without visible cause, sensible people stop with it. More than one collapse has been preceded by a whole freight string locking its limbs and refusing the next rung.

Their fur gathers static until blue threads creep across it. Children discharge them with grounded combs and collect the singed undercoat for insulation. A burden climber that has served one anchor household for many years may be given a structural name and included in its lineage records. When it dies, its central harness ring is fitted to its successor, carrying scratches from every route it traveled.

Rock curs are less respected and more beloved. They infest warm domestic machinery, steal fasteners, and sleep wrapped around conduits that would burn softer animals. Their six feet can change magnetic polarity in pairs, producing the faint ticking heard as they run across steel. They are adept at detecting loosened panels and equally adept at causing them. Mechanics curse them, feed them, and pretend not to notice when litters appear in tool lockers.

The ventilation gliders are nervous, translucent creatures, more membrane than flesh. At rest they fold into angular packets indistinguishable from scraps of insulation. They emerge when airflow changes, feeding on mites and fungal dust. Surveyors release them into unknown cracks and read danger in their return: torn wings indicate sharp constriction, frost film means ghost brine, blackened mouths mean furnace gas. If none return, people wait.

Wireworms are tolerated wherever their nests do not short a circuit. They gather copper dust grain by grain, fusing it with saliva into delicate branching baskets. Their colonies produce no queens; instead, the nest itself appears to regulate them, carrying minute currents that alter their behavior. Abandoned nests are prized as sensors. Hung between two braces, they deform visibly before ordinary gauges register movement.

Bell-backed beetles are considered lucky only by those who do not maintain transformers. Their shells resonate at precise pitches, each individual chiming when it strikes warm casing metal. A healthy infestation produces a soft, irregular music. Sudden silence may mean a transformer is cooling, leaking, or about to fail. Some technicians know their equipment’s beetle population by sound and can identify the absence of a single large specimen.

The dry fungi belong neither comfortably to animal nor plant. They grow wherever human residue, machine oils, and metal oxides collect, putting out gray fans like ears pressed to the city’s hidden processes. Their pigments change with chemical exposure: violet for refrigerant, yellow for overheated insulation, black for certain poisonous gases. Domestic strains are cultivated on removable plates and read each morning. Families trade cuttings descended from growths that once warned entire districts.

In neglected cavities, however, the fungi become thick and strange. They sheath cables, soften ceramics, and produce tough articulated bodies that contract when current passes through them. Salvagers tell of dead workshops whose machinery continues to move because fungal masses have replaced belts and actuators. Whether this is life adapting to industry or industry acquiring a kind of life is a question argued in the loud rooms.

The sailbacks belong to the whole crack. They rise from the depths at shift change, catching columns of waste heat with membranes stretched between long fingerlike spars. Their upper surfaces drink amber light; their lower surfaces return it in fractured reflections. A flock passing beneath a balcony briefly reveals the city from below—thousands of tiny lights, ladders, faces, and hanging machines mirrored in moving skin.

They nest on warm vertical plates and give birth to clusters of clinging young no larger than coins. Their migration follows industrial rhythms rather than seasons. Foundries opening for a long firing draw them upward. Shutdowns send them sinking toward deeper heat. Message carriers attach capsules only to adults already traveling in the desired direction, because a forced sailback will shed its harness and sometimes the hand holding it.

People read the flocks as an informal map of the city’s metabolism. If sailbacks abandon a shaft, something has changed: a draft reversed, a furnace died, a poisonous leak entered the upcurrent. During the mountain’s great wind-note they spiral in enormous numbers, not in panic but in response to pressure, making the central void seem filled with opening and closing mirrors.

Far below the inhabited braces live things known mostly by traces. Freight workers find rungways polished on their undersides, cables stripped of fungal growth in long vertical bands, and ceramic housings marked with circles of shallow teeth. There are reports of large pale bodies moving where no waste heat should permit flight. Instruments lowered into the cold sometimes return neatly disconnected rather than broken.

The people do not claim the crack ends there. They say only that civic responsibility does.

At the opposite extreme, near the blade of sky, live mouth-keepers, weather readers, and collectors of salts deposited by sublimating frost. Their rooms are the coldest and most exposed, reinforced against the peak’s flexing. They have seen clouds from above and, rarely, the curved brightness of distant snowfields. Outsiders imagine these people must feel free. In fact, many develop terror of horizons. They sleep best facing inward, with stone close enough to touch.

Visitors from the plains fare poorly. They tire from altitude, bruise their shins, speak too loudly, and repeatedly look down when they should look at their hands. They ask where the city center is. They ask how many people live there. They ask whether the crack will someday split the mountain entirely.

The inhabitants have answers, but none that outsiders find satisfying.

The center is wherever load is presently being transferred.

The population is counted in breaths processed, harness rings issued, and emergency capacity, never in a single number.

And the mountain is already split. The city is what lives in the splitting.
