Those who live above the clouds are creatures of timing, pressure, and vibration.

Nothing there can depend on a landscape remaining still. Water appears and vanishes. Grazing fronts erase a season’s growth in an afternoon. A firm path may soften into a leaf bed, split open, or prove to be the roof of some vast vegetal chamber. Consequently, the plateau favors lives that can wait for a long time and then move all at once.

The great herds are the most visible expression of this rhythm. Their members are low, lean animals with long bodies carried on many short, widely spaced legs. From a distance they resemble dark streams flowing through the vegetation. Their feet spread their weight across the root skin, and the foremost pair constantly tap and probe before committing the body. When danger forces them to run, their feet strike in overlapping sequences, producing a rolling thunder more like machinery than hoofbeats.

They have narrow, wedge-shaped heads for forcing apart the plated surfaces of cushion plants. Rasping mouthparts peel up seasonal tissue without tearing deeply into the enduring root mass. Some possess blunt facial blades that lift crusts for smaller herd members; others trail flexible lips through the exposed seams, gathering mites, shoots, and moisture together. Their hides are ribbed and dusty, usually matching the silver-brown ground, but after rain the grooves brighten with courtship colors.

A herd is not merely a crowd. It is a traveling ecology. Dung-feeders cling beneath the animals. Needle-beaked attendants pick larvae from folds in their skin. Seeds ride in oiled fringes along their flanks. Small scavengers follow within the sheltered center, where wind is reduced and predators have difficulty descending. Newborns are carried for their first days in shallow dorsal cradles formed by the parents’ raised plates, until their many feet learn the herd’s rhythm.

They communicate through the ground. Low knocks pass from animal to animal through legs held momentarily rigid against the mat. Warning signals travel backward faster than the herd itself can turn. Experienced leaders can distinguish firm root shelf from hollow crust by the returning tremor of a single stamp. During migrations they sometimes pause together, thousands of heads lowered, listening to distant water move beneath the living floor.

Overhead are the sailers.

They are broad, light animals built around wings that function as kites. Their bodies are slight, their chests far smaller than those of creatures dependent on constant flapping, and many have long vaned tails used to read changes in the wind. They rise from escarpments and root arches, climb columns of warm air, and travel for hours with scarcely a wingbeat.

Some are delicate insect strainers with combed mouths and translucent wings. They descend through emergences of rain beetles, eating until their throat pouches bulge black. Others are heavy herd-hunters with folded grasping limbs. These circle patiently above birthing migrations and drop with such speed that the wind of their passage flattens leaves.

There are also seed sailers, harmless creatures whose wings bear hooked, replaceable fringes. Flowering plants entice them with resin, salt, or warm roosting chambers. A sailer leaves carrying hundreds of seed cases and sheds them gradually across the plateau. Certain flowers bloom only when they hear the calls of the species that distributes them.

At evening, the sky seems to close as the sailers descend. They hang beneath root shelves, crowd into stone hollows, or anchor themselves face-first into the wind. Colonies never become fully quiet. They cough, click, scrape wing vanes, and call across the dark, filling the escarpments with a sound like distant industry.

Below the apparent ground lives the greater part of the animal population.

Burrowers tunnel through dead fibers without entering the living vascular layers. Some are no larger than grains; others leave passages wide enough for a human arm. Toothless raspers digest cork and fungus. Armored rollers compact waste into plugs that reinforce weakened chambers. Pale thread-bodied hunters move through condensation channels and seize mites at root mouths. The constant action of these hidden animals produces the faint granular rasp audible wherever the plateau is healthy.

The ground itself regulates them. Root tissues swell to close over-infested galleries. Bitter fluids flood chambers occupied by damaging species. Some colonial plants cultivate particular burrowers, feeding them nodules in exchange for aeration or defense. A traveler cutting what appears to be a dead ledge may therefore wound not one organism but an old association of plant, fungus, and animal whose parts have lived together so long that none survives independently.

Persistent pools support stranger bodies. Pale amphibious climbers crowd the walls of hollow trunks, using broad adhesive feet to follow the waterline. During inundation they become active, noisy, and vividly colored, wrestling for floating nests and booming through resonant wood. As a pool shrinks, they climb into prepared cavities, coat themselves in waxy mucus, and seal the openings with pulp. Whole generations may wait inside a trunk for the next deep rain.

Their principal predators are long, nearly blind vibration-readers. These lie wrapped around structural roots, their lower jaws pressed to the wood. They can separate the scratch of a waking amphibian from rain, insects, or the contraction of the trunk itself. Their strikes are short and exact. In the dry season they become so still that fungi grow across their backs.

Where water persists, every layer of life presses inward. Pollinators breed at the margins. Sailers descend to drink on the wing. Herds approach in disciplined files and may wait for days while stronger animals occupy the safest access. Predators crouch among false thorns or hang beneath the lips of leaf basins. Even the water is dense with dormant eggs, transparent larvae, and seedlike organisms that alternate between animal movement and plantlike stillness.

The Quiet Scars have inhabitants of another sort, if inhabitant is the right word.

Within their gray, cellular material are slow changes that resemble growth but produce no shoots, spores, or recognizable young. After temperature shifts, geometric ridges sometimes rise from the surface and subside again. Marker stakes driven into a Scar may be found days later loosened, polished, or displaced in straight lines. Animals turn away before touching it. Burrowers stop their galleries at its boundary.

People crossing those belts report pressure in their teeth and the sensation of a rhythm too slow to hear. They do not camp there, spill water there, or leave their dead. Whatever occupies the Scars is treated neither as plant nor animal, but as a remainder from an older arrangement of the plateau.

The humans are slight by comparison with the systems around them. They live in small, mobile households gathered around skill rather than territory. One person knows how to open cistern plants without introducing rot. Another hears weather in flute-grass. Another memorizes herd cycles, breeds luminous beetles, repairs condensation cloth, or judges the strength of suspended roots by their pitch.

Children learn the country first as a collection of sounds. Before they can travel far, they are taught to recognize the full thud of a cistern, the brittle rattle preceding a root-shelf failure, the wing note of descending hunters, and the unnatural hush that passes ahead of danger. Their earliest maps are songs. Verses encode intervals between water, seasons of flowering, safe wind directions, and the order in which sounding monuments should be heard.

These songs are not fixed. A route singer has both the authority and obligation to alter them when the living country changes. An obsolete verse can kill. Communities therefore meet at seasonal crossings to compare what they have heard: a bridge lowered in pitch, a rain bowl draining too quickly, a breeding colony spreading east, a familiar underground knock absent for three years. Knowledge survives by being performed, disputed, and revised.

Plateau dwellers wrap themselves in overlapping layers of woven fiber, softened hide, and resin-stiffened scales. Their clothing lies close to the body so the wind cannot seize it. Veils protect the mouth and eyes during insect emergences; small sound beads sewn along their edges help companions locate one another in storms. Soles are broad and flexible, allowing the wearer to feel hollows before trusting their full weight to them.

Most tools are designed to negotiate rather than conquer. Scrapers remove only the regenerative layers of edible tissue. Hooked poles collect leaf water without tearing the bowl. Resonant mallets test trunks for inhabited cavities. Needles are made from pollinator spines, blades from root-glass, cord from fibers harvested after natural shedding. A skilled gatherer leaves marks indicating when a cut plant may be used again.

Their settlements look temporary even when generations have occupied the same basin. Shelters crouch against the root mat, fastened with many flexible ties rather than rigid foundations. Walls can be rolled, roofs unhooked, water vessels sealed, and the whole habitation dispersed before a herd front or nesting season. Permanence resides in maintained relationships: with a cistern colony, a stand of fiber plants, a sounding monument, and the other households that return there.

They do not imagine themselves owners of the plateau. Ownership would imply that the thing owned remains where it was left.

Instead, they speak of keeping terms. A family may keep terms with a water hollow by clearing choking growth from its spout. A village keeps terms with migrating grazers by dismantling barriers before the herd arrives. Pollinator columns are paid with patches of cultivated flowers. Dead bodies are placed in selected root seams, where bone and flesh are quickly incorporated; afterward, relatives listen for changes in the local tone and say the dead have entered the country’s voice.

Those who maintain the wind monuments hold a special position. They drill, plug, and tune the mouths in old trunks and standing stones, adjusting each to the prevailing weather. A good monument is simultaneously beacon, forecast, warning, and archive. Its tones tell listeners where they are, but alterations in those tones reveal more: a hollow forming underground, a root bridge losing tension, moisture approaching from beyond sight.

Some tuners claim the largest monuments answer one another across impossible distances. On certain nights, when the air cools evenly and the herds are far away, a note sounded in the west is followed much later by a low reply from the interior. Whether this is resonance, coincidence, or communication with some immense buried organism is unresolved. The tuners do not insist. They listen.

Listening is the central discipline of life there.

Predators listen through their jaws. Grazers listen through their feet. Plants open chambers at particular wingbeats and direct roots toward the vibration of hidden water. People sleep with one hand on the shelter floor. Even the apparent stillness of drought is full of attention: sealed amphibians waiting in wood, eggs waiting in mud, leaves waiting folded beneath their own armored surfaces, villages waiting for a familiar monument to change its note.

Nothing above the clouds truly occupies an empty land.

Everything inhabits a pause.
