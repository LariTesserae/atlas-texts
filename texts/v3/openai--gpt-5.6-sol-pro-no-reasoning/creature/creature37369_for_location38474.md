The plateau is inhabited, but not by creatures in any ordinary sense.

Its most abundant residents are droplets.

They hang in the lucid air, crawl against slopes, cling to sheer faces, and assemble in temporary chains. Taken from a stable pool, they remember their source and travel back toward it, laying bright threads across the stone. Yet their memory is not always of the present. Some return to basins as they once were—or may one day be—under other skies, with unworn stairs and occupied shelters. Water here carries more than minerals. It carries location, gravity, and perhaps time.

The pools are larger, slower beings of the same kind. Each imposes order on its surroundings. Near an enclosed cistern, distance behaves, shadows remain attached, and falling objects agree on which way is down. Beyond its influence, the country loosens. Thus every ancient shelter was built around water not merely as a resource, but as an anchor. The bowls found trembling in abandoned rooms may still be holding those rooms in place.

Stone also lives there, if living can mean emerging, responding, and remembering.

The bedrock opens hairline seams at noon and grows sharp new ridges by evening. Mineral blades shift until old passages close and new ones appear. Pale disks colonize sheltered walls, branch, and vanish in light. Salt blooms briefly. Flexible silicate skins dry into brittle straps. Crystalline fibers weave themselves through prepared frames. None of these processes can be cleanly separated into geology, mechanism, or ecology. The plateau may not distinguish among them.

Its forests are choirs of narrow stone. Each blade possesses a pitch, and storms tune whole districts at once. Black slabs boom through the ground. Buried chambers knock. Trenches grind continuously beneath sight. Echoes range farther still: some arrive early, some return hours afterward, and some may not be echoes at all. The plateau repeats sounds as water repeats places—imperfectly, across distances that do not remain spatial.

The builders survive principally as habits impressed upon matter.

They were patient, dexterous, and small enough to occupy the low shelters under overhangs and along trench walls. They chipped glassy knives, drilled stone, wedged masonry with ceramic, made racks from mineral rods, and worked flexible silicate as though it were hide. They understood leverage, containment, and the stabilizing authority of water. They marked routes and left instructions in cairns. Above all, they hammered.

No bodies remain because their bodies may never have been separable from the plateau’s processes. Perhaps they were organisms whose organic traces were erased with impossible thoroughness. Perhaps they were mineral beings, temporarily coherent wherever water enclosed a dependable volume. Perhaps the figures glimpsed around older basins are not remnants but the builders themselves, still present in a neighboring arrangement of the country.

They stand beneath darker skies beside intact walls. They strike stone against stone in steady rhythm. Approach only increases the depth between observer and observed. They cannot cross into the brilliant present, but their labor does. Fresh tool marks appear after storms. Stairs gain steps. Shelters move while leaving their foundations behind. The plateau continues receiving workmanship from hands it no longer contains.

And at the White Confluence, something appears to attend.

When the cool pressure passes, suspended droplets flatten, blades bow without bending, and every reflection turns in one direction. Nothing visible occupies that direction. It may be an absence rather than an entity: a moving center around which water, light, and stone briefly coordinate. It may be what the builders watched, served, resisted, or used to shape the land. It may simply be the plateau becoming aware of one of its own alignments.

So what lives there is not hidden wildlife waiting to be discovered. It is a dispersed, inorganic society:

water that remembers where and when it belongs;

stone that grows as quickly as it erodes;

pools that domesticate physical law;

echoes that preserve actions;

storms born underground;

mineral colonies poised between reaction and organism;

and absent builders whose repeated gestures remain active in the terrain.

The plateau itself is the resident—not a single mind, but a vast continuity of interacting memories. Its waters circulate orientation. Its rocks store rhythm. Its structures hold small territories of reality together. Its weather rearranges what has already happened.

It is not a dead world.

It is a world in which life, if that word still applies, has escaped the need for flesh.
