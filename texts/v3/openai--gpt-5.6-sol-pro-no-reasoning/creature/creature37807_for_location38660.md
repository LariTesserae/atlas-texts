The inhabitants call themselves nothing that can be translated.

A name is a thing for separating one body from another, and they no longer have bodies in that sense. What lives beneath the black water is distributed: part flesh, part apparatus, part civic habit. It occupies diving shells, pressure chambers, pipes, woven bands, blind organisms, and the minute distances between fitted stones. It resides wherever the town is able to feel itself.

There may once have been people.

Something human remains in the scale of the handrails, in the height of the polished door handles, in benches arranged for knees and shoulders that never arrive. The enclosed bridges are wide enough for two people to pass if both turn sideways. Workshop controls sit at the reach of absent arms. Certain doors bear shallow dents where generations of knuckles struck the same place.

But whatever made those marks did not survive unchanged.

The closest things to citizens now are the custodians.

They are tall only when unfolded. At rest, each is compressed into a recess behind a wall plate, its limbs nested tightly around a central cage. When summoned by pressure, the plate opens and the custodian pours itself into the street. It has no fixed number of legs. Hinged rods separate, branch, and join according to the space available, so that in a broad lane it may walk on four, beneath pipes on twelve, and through a narrow valve throat as a succession of jointed rings.

Its frame is made from the town’s usual materials: blackened brass, lead glass, mineral-crusted steel. Between these hard parts hang strips of the fabric woven beneath the northern wall. They drift like mourning clothes until the custodian moves; then they stiffen, becoming fins, seals, fingers, or the temporary walls of an internal chamber.

Something pale is enclosed within its ribwork.

At first it resembles a folded animal preserved in cloudy fluid. Seen more closely, it is not one creature but a colony of translucent lobes gathered around a knot of red filaments. Each lobe contracts at a different interval. Together they interpret vibration. The three beats of the pumping hall pass through them as instruction, memory, and appetite.

Custodians have no heads. Where a head might be, there is a round glass fitting containing a gauge needle. The needle does not measure pressure. It points toward whatever in the town is presently least correct.

They tighten bolts, clear intakes, patch masonry, carry broken mechanisms to the foundry. They also perform tasks with no visible practical value. One straightens the benches in the square after each current reversal. Another visits every eastern shutter and touches it once, whether closed or open. A third descends nightly to the brass plaque and polishes away the inscription before mineral growth can make it legible.

They avoid the central shaft.

When work brings them near it, they lengthen their limbs and keep their central cages as far from the railing as possible. At the upward breath of cold water they become completely rigid. The gauge needles behind their glass fittings spin until they blur.

The low maintenance crawlers are not their servants. They are closer to shed thoughts: small, repetitive portions of civic intention housed in many-legged shells. Each possesses one imperative and almost no means of recognizing when that imperative has been fulfilled. Clean. Coil. Count. Fasten. Carry. Return.

If obstructed, a crawler will spend decades negotiating with the obstacle by touch. If lifted and placed elsewhere, it resumes its task using the nearest available object. This is why screws are sometimes found tightened into brick, why dead fish are deposited in corrosion bins, and why one entire alley has been neatly wound with cable from floor to roof.

The custodians occasionally correct them. More often, they watch.

Watching, here, does not require eyes.

The town sees through changes in resistance. A fin disturbing the water beside a lamp is a kind of silhouette. A hand on a railing is a pulse arriving through metal. Movement through a corridor alters the pitch of the pipes. Warmth crossing a chamber wakes pale bacterial lines in sequence. Every building receives these signs, and the pumping hall gathers them.

Thus the town knows when something has entered the basin.

It knows before the visitor crosses the shale rim.

Shutters begin to close. Workshops pause between identical objects. The filter-trees constrict by minute degrees. Custodians unfold from the walls and arrange themselves just beyond the blue lamps, where only portions of them can be seen: three brass limbs, a floating strip of black fabric, the twitch of a needle under glass.

They do not attack.

They make accommodations.

A door that has been sealed for centuries opens onto a dry room. A bench turns to face outward. Pipes warm. Condensation gathers inside an observation bridge and produces footsteps calibrated to match the visitor’s stride. If the visitor stops, the second set takes one more step.

The rooms prepared for guests contain relics of anatomy. A metal basin at hand height. A narrow bed beneath straps. Hooks arranged for clothing, or skin, or the equipment that distinguishes one from the other. On the walls hang masks with round windows and no breathing connections. They are manufactured in many sizes, including several too small for any infant and one too broad to pass through the door.

A custodian will wait outside.

Its gauge will point inward.

There are also inhabitants that are not mechanical.

The seven white organisms in the courtyard descend much farther than they rise. Their visible tubes are feeding structures; their joined body spreads beneath the foundations in a colorless web. It penetrates mortar, insulation, silt, and the oldest bones sealed inside the pale stone. The town’s warmth passes through it. So do traces of hormones, metals, electrical leakage, and the chemical residue of fear.

The grove tastes history.

When all seven trunks close, the organism is not hiding from something above. It is preventing something below from tasting back.

The vertical fish are fragments of another sense. They hatch in gauge boxes as red-centered crustaceans, enter pipe threads as flat worms, and emerge into open water only after losing their eyes. Each life stage inhabits a different layer of the town. In their final form they hover beneath lamps and open their jaws to the pumping rhythm, swallowing pressure changes too slight for instruments to record.

When the cold breath rises from the shaft, all of them turn toward it because it contains a signal.

Some then leave their lamps. They swim to the square in a slow procession and descend through the railing one by one. None return. In the days afterward, new red points appear inside sealed gauges.

Whether this is reproduction, communication, or payment is not known.

The most numerous inhabitants are quieter still.

They occupy the black fabric.

The looms beneath the northern wall are supplied with human hair gathered from filters, drains, old bedding, mortar, and places in the town where no human material should remain. Mixed with glass fiber, the hair is woven into continuous bands and passed through the floor.

Below, something wears it.

Occasionally a length returns through a different slot, decades later. The glass threads are snapped. The hair has grown.

Custodians handle these returned lengths with elaborate care. They wash them in copper water, drill seven metal discs onto each end, and carry them in procession through corridors that do not appear on any plan. For the duration, the town’s machinery adopts a fourth beat.

No light is permitted.

Behind the green glass of the pumping hall live the counters.

They are difficult to distinguish from machinery because they have arranged themselves in its image. Their bodies are soft, gray, and radially folded, each grown around a spindle that turns with the pumps. Fine appendages extend into banks of levers. They record every cycle, every opened door, every object swallowed by the shaft, every remote brilliance that crosses the basin.

They count in groups of three, except when counting the knocks.

Those are recorded singly.

The counters are descended from the last workers, or claim to be. In the amber windows of the oldest houses, one can sometimes see their earlier forms suspended in preservation tanks: torsos bent to fit cylindrical vessels, hands multiplied by surgical division, faces covered with lead plates pierced only at the mouth. Tubes enter where eyes once were. Some tanks are empty but still breathe.

The counters do not leave the hall. They have become unable to tolerate any rhythm not generated by the central machinery. A loose chain trembling out of phase causes them distress. The irregular kick of a living heart can stop their movements entirely.

This may be why the town alters visitors.

Not deliberately. Not cruelly.

It brings them into time.

Breathing gradually synchronizes with the three slow beats. The heart resists longer, adding little counter-rhythms, but the corridors lengthen around anyone whose pulse remains private. Doors retreat. Ladders gain rungs. The same red valve appears again and again until the body relents.

Once synchronized, a visitor can move easily.

The streets shorten. Pressure doors open before they are touched. Custodians lower their limbs. The pumping hall’s green windows brighten enough to reveal gray folds turning behind the glass. A place is made at one of the inward-facing benches.

Then the visitor begins to notice that the town is not built around the pumping hall.

It is built around the shaft.

The pumps do not draw water from it. They hold water against it. The intake pipes, exhaust stacks, reversing currents, shutters, looms, foundry, bacterial veins, white grove, and endless production of seven-holed discs are components of a single retaining mechanism.

The town is a lock built on the wrong side of a door.

Below the square lives the oldest inhabitant.

It has no shape that can pass through the opening. Shape belongs to things wholly present in one place, and this being extends downward through distances the basin cannot contain. The shaft does not lead to a chamber beneath the seabed. It leads to the inward side of the town: the impossible depth into which western objects fall and from which they later arrive overhead.

The thing below is entwined with every contradiction.

Its nearness bends routes before it bends stone. Its attention lengthens solitary corridors, where there is only one mind with which to measure them. In company, it stretches open water instead, testing the space between minds. The survey marks are not incorrect; “highest” and “lowest” have become different local conditions. The same valve may be passed five times because repetition is one of the ways the inhabitant approaches without moving.

The inhabitants above do not know whether they imprison it, feed it, keep it asleep, or prevent the sea from entering where it is.

They know only the procedures.

Three beats.

Close the shutters.

Vent the warm black water.

Reverse the current.

Open the grilles.

Count what falls.

Weave what returns.

When pressure rises, add stone. When stone dissolves, add iron. When iron swells, shave it clean. When a passage changes length, place another lamp. When a door appears, furnish the room behind it. When footprints gather beneath the glass, do not follow them past the final pane.

And when the pumps pause, be still.

The knock from below is always small.

That is its most alarming quality. It carries no violence, no vastness, none of the thunder one would expect from a thing held beneath an entire town. It is delicate and measured. It resembles the knock of a courteous traveler who has reached a familiar house late at night.

Sometimes it comes once.

Sometimes, after several years, twice.

The counters have records of a cycle in which it came three times.

On that occasion every sealed door in the town opened inward. The remote colorless brilliance crossed the basin and stopped directly overhead. The seven white filter-trees withdrew completely into the ground. For one interval, the pumping hall missed a beat.

No account survives of what happened next.

But there is now an eighth bench in the square.

Its stone is unworn. Its proportions are almost human. And whenever the cold breath rises from the shaft, condensation forms along its seat in the impression of someone waiting.
