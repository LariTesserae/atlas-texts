The high country is inhabited chiefly by things that know how to become still.

In the lee of boulders and within hairline fractures lies the black growth. At rest it resembles soot pressed into the stone: matte, dry, and so thin that a careless hand might smear it away. It is neither an individual organism nor a colony in any simple sense. Each patch is a continuous congregation of flexible crystal membranes, exchanging charge across microscopic seams. It drinks no water. It feeds on differences—sun-warmed rock against cold shadow, reactive mineral against spent mineral, one gravitational inclination against the next.

Its apparent flatness is a form of waiting. When the buried fields begin to turn, the patch rises. Thousands of translucent vanes unfold from the black film, some no larger than scales, others long as fingers. They do not agree on an upright. Each vane selects a different local down and trembles toward it, so that the whole organism flowers in contradiction. Charge moves through it in faint violet pulses. Dust catches briefly along its edges, is sorted grain by grain, and falls away.

During the strongest transitions, a patch may tear itself loose. It does not fly; it is distributed. Its vanes separate into a dark flock, each fragment following a neighboring gradient until the field relaxes. Most are lost on exposed stone. A few reach cracks and flatten there, becoming the beginnings of new patches. Thus the organism migrates without ever becoming a traveler.

Beneath overhangs live the pale filaments. Their lives proceed too slowly to observe. They penetrate stone by fractions of a millimeter, dissolving only those grains whose charge or crystalline orientation suits them. Behind each advancing tip is an empty tunnel finer than hair. Millions of these tunnels lace the rock, turning whole shelves into resonant chambers.

When the plateau flexes, the colonies sing.

The sound is soft and sustained, often mistaken for the structure settling: glass rims touched by damp fingers, heard through several rooms. No single filament produces it. The note belongs to the architecture of their feeding. Colonies separated by kilometers sometimes answer one another as stress travels through the strata. Whether this exchange carries information is unclear; at their rate of existence, a single phrase might take centuries.

Their dead layers accumulate as green lacquer. The living tips retreat beneath it, leaving translucent bands that record old directions of gravity. Some deposits contain millions of such bands, each tilted differently. In places the lacquer has grown thick enough to preserve hollows where softer stone has vanished entirely. These are colonies inhabiting shapes of their own making, suspended under ledges that should long ago have collapsed.

The animals of the plateau are built low and broad. Most have many legs arranged not in pairs but in overlapping circles around the body, allowing any side to become the underside. Their feet open into dry gripping fans, each lined with mineral hooks. Dull shells cover them in nested plates. The plates shift independently, redistributing strain when gravity pulls from several directions at once.

They possess little that could be called a face. A ring of pressure pits detects the leaning of nearby mass; recessed thermal eyes distinguish warm shelters, living colonies, and newly opened seams. Their mouths are grinding folds on the ventral surface. They graze the green lacquer, black vanes, reactive salts, and sometimes one another’s abandoned shells.

In stable weather they travel along invisible roads. One may see a line of them crossing an immense flat, each several body lengths behind the next, though there is no mark to follow and no scent the air could preserve. The route is held in their joints. Minute asymmetries in the way their limbs bear weight form a hereditary map of dependable gradients. A young animal follows not a place but a sequence of pressures: heavier on the third left arc, then level, then a faint upward pull through the rear plates. Such a sequence may correspond to a colony hundreds of kilometers away.

When instability approaches, they stop simultaneously. Their limbs spread. Hooks bite into pores in the stone, shell plates close, and the soft body contracts into a dense knot. In that posture they can survive being made briefly weightless, inverted, or dragged sideways by dust. They may remain locked for years.

Not all choose well. The plateau is crowded, by its standards, with the evidence of failed instincts. Empty shells cling to ceilings, lie wedged blade-first into cliffs, or occur inside strata exposed by later upheavals. Living animals sometimes visit these shells and touch them with their pressure pits. They may be reading the final gradient preserved in the plates. They may be feeding. The gesture looks like mourning only to those accustomed to faces.

Some wanderers have taken up residence in the constructions. They cluster in immaculate dust halos where loose grains slow before touching the walls. Inside, they occupy recessed platforms and align themselves toward vertical slits. When a slit borrows a sightline from elsewhere, the animals become alert. Their shell plates lift one after another, and their feet make minute testing movements against the floor.

It appears they can feel the distant ground through the stolen view.

Entire populations may wait around one slit for generations until it opens upon the pressure signature of a viable colony. Then, during a favorable rearrangement, they depart. Travelers have followed such migrations and found that the animals do not head toward the landscape visible through the slit. Instead they take a route that seems absurd—sometimes directly away from it—and arrive years later by means no inertial chart can reconstruct.

Within a few of the doorless chambers lives something more difficult to separate from the building. It first appears as a distortion along an interior edge, a dark thickening where wall meets floor. During daylight it remains fixed. At night it advances by altering the room’s proportions: the floor slopes a little more, the platform seems nearer, the corner migrates. Dust placed as a marker is never crossed, yet by morning the dark thickening may be on the opposite side of it.

No sample has been taken. Tools brought near it acquire an inconvenient heaviness and settle beyond reach. The small animals avoid these chambers unless a major transition is imminent. Then they crowd inside and arrange themselves around the distortion as though around a hearth.

The larger beings of the basins are real, though sightings do not agree on their form.

From a distance, one seems to be a low procession of pale arches. From another angle it is a single dark mass, elongated and many-legged. Seen from above, it may look like an absence in the mineral dust: an oval region in which every loose stone hangs a handspan above the surface.

The creature itself occupies the center of that lifted region. Its body is difficult to see because it carries its own down. Dust, light debris, and even the posture of nearby animals orient toward a moving axis inside it. A traveler approaching one feels the body tilt before the eyes find anything. Blood shifts strangely. Hanging equipment leans toward the basin floor and toward the creature at once.

Its visible anatomy consists of vast overlapping ribs or vanes, each polished by airborne stone. Between them hang curtains of black growth, though whether these are symbionts or organs is unknown. Beneath the shell, dense nodules circulate with geological slowness. Their movement changes the creature’s private gravitational field, causing the surrounding stones to rise, sink, or orbit in loose chains.

It does not walk by pushing against the ground. It revises where falling leads.

A front portion becomes downhill; the rest of the body follows. The basin surface may be level, yet the creature descends across it with solemn certainty. Its passage leaves broad areas swept clean of dust and occasional grooves where its private slope intersected the ordinary one too sharply. It feeds at ancient fault exposures, remaining there for decades while reactive minerals disappear from the rock. Afterward the cliff is smooth and dark, not bitten away but somehow exhausted.

These basin creatures approach the ceramic rings during the great transitions. Each settles around one without touching it. When the rings sound, the creatures answer below hearing. Their internal nodules align; stones around them rise in concentric bands. For a few minutes, ring, creature, and buried machinery appear to participate in one system.

Whether the creatures evolved around the machines or are themselves mobile components cannot be determined. Juveniles, if they exist, have never been recognized. Some rings are surrounded by empty shells of immense size, half buried and mistaken for curved ridges. Black vanes grow thickly on these remains.

Then there are the inhabitants implied by the shelters.

They are not present as bodies. No bones, tools, waste, inscriptions, or images identify them. Yet the structures preserve bodily assumptions. A sloping floor expects a being that prefers one direction even when the world supplies several. Recessed platforms suggest rest, observation, or exposure. Vertical slits are set at heights that imply an upright watcher, though not always the same height, and sometimes not a watcher confined to one side of the wall.

In certain shelters, marks appear after upheavals: not footprints, but narrow zones where the floor has briefly borne more weight than the surrounding stone. They occur in groups, pause before the horizon slit, and end at blank walls. The impressions fade as the local field stabilizes.

It may be that the builders are extinct.

It may be that they left.

It may also be that they learned to inhabit the borrowed straight lines: existing not in the rooms, but in the changing relation between one room and another. If so, each gravitational transition briefly restores their geography. A slit opens. A corridor becomes possible across hundreds of kilometers. Weight gathers in the shape of an absent visitor. Then space relaxes, and the route—and whatever occupied it—ceases to intersect the plateau.

Nothing lives on the final stones.

This is the strongest evidence of life’s discernment in the high country. Black growth stops at their boundaries. Pale filaments bend their tunnels away. Wanderers detour even when the polished islands lie directly along inherited routes. Basin creatures give them distances so wide that their private fields flatten and lose shape.

At the edge of a final stone, an animal’s pressure pits find no usable gradient. To step onto it would be to enter a place without persuasion, where down is singular and complete. The creature tests the boundary, withdraws, and goes around.

Travelers sometimes sit there during the upheavals. From that calm they watch the plateau’s inhabitants reveal themselves: black flowers unfolding toward a dozen earths, shell-backed lines locking themselves to the rock, pale colonies sounding within the cliffs, and far below, a great bearer of gravity moving through its halo of lifted stones.

Around them, everything alive negotiates with the world.

The final stone does not.

Perhaps that is why it remains uninhabited. It offers safety, but no difference to feed upon, no shifting pressure to read, no alternate direction by which to travel. In the high country, change is not merely danger. It is climate, road, memory, and nourishment. Stability is useful only as refuge.

Perfect stability is barren.
