The garden’s true resident is difficult to separate from the garden itself.

It is small enough to pass through drain throats, narrow enough to travel the capillary galleries inside the false cliffs, and light enough that pressure sensors confuse it with a sudden gathering of water. No one has seen it whole. Those who claim to have done so offer incompatible accounts: a black-furred animal with six quick feet; a damp, pale thing like a lizard assembled from roots; a fist-sized mechanism clothed in moss; a flightless bird with a beak fine enough to enter bolt holes.

Its tracks are more consistent than its body. Three narrow prongs touch the silt, followed sometimes by a faint drag mark and sometimes by nothing. The impressions begin under brass spillways and end against unbroken walls. After mist, they appear on the highest terraces before any maintenance lift has run. They are always crisp, as if the creature has only just passed, though sensors may show no movement there for hours.

It feeds on whatever the garden is about to reclaim.

Ripe figs disappear shortly before collection. Fallen heliconia seeds are pulled through perforations one at a time. Snails marked for census are occasionally found in different basins, their colored shells scrubbed almost clean. From fermenter vents it takes warm fibers; from maintenance trays, curls of cork bark; from behind brackets, the genuine soil that forms despite the rules. It carries these things into the drainage system and makes caches in the dry spaces between pipes.

There, in cavities never intended to hold anything, it builds.

Its nests are shallow cups woven from feeder roots, moth silk, wire insulation, and the transparent skins shed by new leaves. Each contains a little dark substrate: rust, pollen, snail castings, mineral crumbs, fruit pulp. Unregistered seedlings germinate in them. Some are species present in the garden, though planted in no approved location. Others resemble no stock in the seed drums below. Their first roots enter condensation cracks and follow them away from the nutrient mesh.

The machinery accommodates the resident without officially perceiving it. Grates unlatch a moment before it arrives. Pumps soften to a pulse. Tilting terraces hesitate while it crosses their joints. Once it has passed, the system resumes as if recovering from a thought. Whether this courtesy is an old instruction, an emergent habit, or merely the accumulated response to countless tiny obstructions is unknown.

The creature may itself be part of the apparatus. Beneath what witnesses call fur, there are occasional flashes of wet metal. Its knocking inside the cliffs has the measured cadence of a diagnostic tool: three quick taps, a pause, then two. Yet it moves upstream when all valves are closed. It steals fruit it has no obvious mechanism for digesting. Once, after a pipe was opened for repair, workers found tooth marks on its inner lining and a compact bundle of seeds wedged safely above the flood level.

It seems drawn to places where the garden fails to remain new.

Where sediment has escaped collection, it digs. Where roots have entered seams, it widens the passage. Where a seedling rises without registration, it leaves scraps of pulp around its stem. It does not sabotage the mechanisms directly. Rather, it protects small consequences from being undone. It rescues matter from circulation and hides it long enough for accumulation to become history.

Perhaps it is an animal that learned the rhythms of the machine. Perhaps it is a maintenance device that outlived the category of maintenance. Perhaps the garden, forbidden to mature across its broad terraces, has concentrated that impulse into a single moving body.

Whatever it is, it has grown more skillful.

The oldest inaccessible cavities now contain mats of true soil. Fine roots descend from them through walls and enter levels absent from the public plans. Pale moths gather there. Snails leave uncatalogued eggs. The resident moves between these pockets, carrying spores and seeds on its damp exterior, joining accidental habitats into a hidden landscape threaded through the manufactured one.

Visitors hear it most often in the late afternoon, when the pumps are quiet and the clouds press low around the walls. The knocking travels against the water: faint, rapid, purposeful. Sometimes a ripe fruit trembles and vanishes through a grate. Sometimes three-pronged tracks cross a freshly restored bed and stop beside a seedling that was not there the day before.

By morning, maintenance will have removed the seedling, lifted the sediment, and returned the slope to its beginning.

But somewhere inside the warm artificial mountain, the resident will have planted another.
