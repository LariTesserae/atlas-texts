Belowhammer is inhabited first and most visibly by people who have learned to live sideways, upward, and around.

They are millers, gutter-keepers, pigeon tenders, potters, rope-makers, mushroom growers, dyers, tanners, water sellers, animal handlers, and repairers of things repaired many times before. Few households practice only one trade. A family may grind grain during dry months, divert runoff during storms, breed pigeons in the vaults, and sell lamp oil from a doorway scarcely wider than a shoulder. Children carry messages across bridges too slight for adults. Elders sit where they can hear changes in the channels and identify a blocked drain, a slipping treadwheel, or an unfamiliar leak by sound alone.

Their bodies bear the district’s shape. They stoop instinctively under beams, turn sideways through alleys, and climb ladders without looking for the rungs. Their palms are polished by rope and lime. Their feet know which bricks rock, which plaster ledges hold weight, and which black puddles conceal deep sumps. During rain they move with sudden communal purpose. Quarrels may persist for years, but when the ceiling mouths begin to speak, enemies brace the same gates and haul the same leather sluices.

They live in households whose boundaries do not match their walls. A grandmother may sleep two levels above her descendants, reached by a ladder owned by someone else. Cooking hearths, goat pens, workshops, and sleeping rooms interpenetrate. Privacy is made from curtains, custom, and the practiced refusal to look. Neighbors know one another’s arguments, debts, recipes, animals, leaks, and illnesses. Silence is suspicious; noise means the place is functioning.

Authority belongs less to rulers than to those who understand shared structures. The cistern guild keeps the channels open, judges disputes over flow, and remembers which piers carry which dwellings. Its senior members are surveyors without instruments: women and men who read cracks with their fingertips, taste seepage for foulness, and press an ear to masonry to hear water moving behind it. Alongside them are mirror attendants, flood-plate keepers, sump cleaners, ladder wardens, and the crews who descend into culverts tied together by ropes.

Their knowledge is practical, inherited, and exact. Repairs are taught as stories: where a wall failed, who was beneath it, which mixture held afterward. Children memorize the sequence of shutters to open when rain comes from the north rather than the west. Marks cut into piers record flood heights, family obligations, and the ownership of condensation lips. Household gods are often small figures of fired clay, blackened by lamp smoke and wedged into niches overlooking a doorway or gutter. Offerings tend to be useful things—first water, a pinch of flour, pigeon feathers, lengths of sound cord.

Belowhammer’s people do not imagine themselves as living in ruins. To them, an untouched wall would be a neglected wall. Age is visible as accumulated attention: pegs, patches, finger-polished edges, inscriptions half-covered and recut. A good dwelling is not one that never fails, but one whose failures give warning and can be reached for repair.

Among them live the animals that make the district possible.

Pigeons dominate the heights. They crowd the carved shelves in gray, white, rust, and soot-colored masses, murmuring above human conversation until some disturbance releases them in a thunder of wings. They supply eggs, meat, feathers, and dung, but they are only partly domesticated. Flocks change allegiance between dovecotes, and skilled keepers recognize birds by flight, mate, and the tint of their feet. The oldest towers of nesting holes are contested inheritances, more valuable than many rooms.

Donkeys occupy the lower alleys and power treadwheels in patient circles. They know routes through the piers better than newcomers do and sometimes refuse passages before people notice a loose platform or unfamiliar smell. Their harnesses are patched with cloth, hide, and braided fiber. Bells are muffled because the district needs no additional noise, though each animal still develops a recognizable rhythm of hoofbeats.

Goats inhabit slatted pens, roof decks, and improbable ledges. They eat rope, reed matting, fig leaves, basket rims, and any garment left within reach. Their manure feeds planting jars and fuel crocks; their milk is precious in dry periods. Pigs root beneath platforms where scraps collect. Ducks patrol the shallower channels after rain. Chickens live in suspended cages to protect them from rats, while snails are kept in damp baskets and fed peelings and bruised greens.

The cats belong to no household completely. Long, lean, and often scarred, they travel along beams and gutter rims above the streets. People leave scraps at favored crossings, but the cats descend chiefly to hunt. Their litters are hidden in warm cavities behind plaster, and their appearances are treated as indicators of the rat population: too many cats on the lower floors means trouble in the walls.

Rats are the district’s rival inhabitants. They tunnel through mortar, raid grain jars, steal eggs, and open routes no builder intended. Some are pale from generations below, others dark newcomers washed or carried down from the city. Sump crews claim that the largest rats understand flood bells and climb before storms. Whether or not this is true, people watch them. A sudden upward migration of rats is warning enough to inspect every drain.

Geckos gather in the halos around oil lamps, translucent bellies pressed to warm plaster. Cave crickets pulse beneath storage stages. Beetle larvae consume beams from within, sometimes leaving a timber apparently whole until a mallet reveals it as powder. Mosquitoes rise in veils after floods, drawing bats through the rain shafts at dusk. Bees occupy horizontal clay tubes on the warmer upper galleries; their honey tastes of figs, mint flowers, street dust, and whatever blooms unseen above.

The channels sustain a less familiar population. Blind fish enter with certain rains, white or rose-colored and frantic from their fall. Children catch them in cloth nets, though a few escape into permanent sumps. There they live with translucent shrimp and black eels that grow thick in old water. The eels are feared, harvested, named, and exaggerated in equal measure. Every generation tells of one large enough to pull a child from a basin rim.

Herons sometimes descend from the city into the largest shafts. In the sudden column of daylight they appear almost supernatural: tall, clean-lined creatures amid brick, rope, smoke, and shouting. They stand motionless beside the pools before striking at fish. Pigeon keepers drive them out; children follow them; some gutter-keepers consider their arrival a sign that distant reservoirs have overflowed.

Plants survive as household dependents. Fig trees twist from immense mended jars, their roots pruned as carefully as branches. Mint occupies chipped troughs beneath reliable drips. Gourds climb cords toward the light and sometimes cross from one family’s dwelling into another’s air-rights, producing elaborate disputes over ownership of the fruit. Moss patches are mapped, inherited, and guarded. Fungi flourish in the darker economy: gray fans on timber, orange crusts on reed walls, edible buttons raised in covered crocks of dung and fiber. Mushroom growers move quietly through warm lower chambers, judging moisture with bare fingers.

There are also lives that belong to the structure itself: films in the gutters, pale worms in cistern sediment, molds that soften rope, mineral crusts that slowly seal one crack while forcing water through another. The residents distinguish useful decay from dangerous decay. Some rot is harvested, some scraped away, some encouraged to bind a surface. Lime, fungus, dung, ash, grease, and living roots all become forms of masonry when properly placed.

Together these inhabitants form no simple hierarchy of people over animals, or builders over built things. Pigeons feed gardens that feed goats and people. Rats open cracks that admit seepage. Cats preserve grain. Bees pollinate plants rooted in street refuse. Children clear shrimp from sluice screens. Moss reveals dependable moisture. Donkeys raise the water that washes workshops whose broken wares become new walls.

Even the dead remain in use. Hair strengthens plaster. Bones become pegs, needles, handles, and gaming pieces. Broken household gods are set into foundations rather than discarded. Names persist in ladders, sumps, gates, and channels long after anyone remembers the persons who bore them.

What lives in Belowhammer is therefore larger than its crowded population. It is a many-bodied domestic creature composed of households, flocks, molds, cistern pools, inherited duties, and habitual repairs. Its metabolism quickens in rain and slows in drought. It eats runoff, refuse, timber, lime, grain, dung, and labor. It sheds dust, feathers, smoke, wastewater, broken pottery, and stories.

No individual sees all of it. The mirror attendants know the heights; sump workers know the depths; children know the narrow passages; cats know the beams; rats know the walls. Belowhammer exists in the overlap of those partial maps, alive because none of its inhabitants can live there alone.
