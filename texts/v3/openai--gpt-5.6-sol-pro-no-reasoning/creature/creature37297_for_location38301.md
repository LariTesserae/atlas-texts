The chief inhabitants are the forests themselves.

Each forest is less a gathering of separate trees than a colony stretched between stone, water, and light. Its oldest members begin in cracks on newborn ridges, fastening pale, muscular roots around edges that would cut softer tissue. From these anchors rise trunks like braided cables. They do not resist the water so much as distribute themselves through it, dividing repeatedly until their crowns become broad buoyant structures. Gas chambers along the leaves hold the upper branches at favored depths, while lower limbs trail into dimmer layers. A single organism may occupy an entire vertical reach: rooted in geothermal warmth, feeding through middle water, and spreading its translucent foliage beneath the sun.

Many of these beings are older than the surfaces to which they cling. When an anchor fails, a mature forest does not necessarily die. It loosens from the mountain and departs as one immense, quiet raft, carrying within its roots mineral fragments, grazers, flowers, spores, and smaller plants that have never known open water. For decades or centuries it drifts among the passes. Its longest roots continue to descend, touching cliffs and withdrawing, until enough of them find purchase. Then the wandering forest draws itself gradually against a new summit and becomes fixed again. What appears to be colonization is also migration; what appears to be an island may be an animal-scale event conducted in vegetal time.

Among their branches live the leaf-folders: small, translucent grazers with neither obvious head nor limbs. At rest they resemble patches of thickened glass. Their bodies spread over the underside of a leaf and seal around films of algae, digesting so slowly that the boundary between grazer and stain is difficult to discern. When feeding is exhausted, the creature gathers itself along radial seams, folds into a pale spindle, and releases its grip. It may drift only the width of a branch before opening on another leaf.

They are almost colorless in daylight, but at night the contents of their bodies show through: green from fresh algae, ocher from mineral spores, and occasionally a soft violet inherited from deeper growths. Generations pass without leaving a single tree. Some lineages inhabit one leaf until the leaf itself descends.

The soft-footed breathers live nearer the upper boundary. Their wide bodies are shaped like low, rounded hands, each finger ending in a disk that adheres to wet foliage. Eyes exist only as sealed cups beneath the skin. They perceive brightness, pressure, warmth, and the minute strains traveling through a leaf, but no image. Their mouths are narrow folds hidden among the gripping feet, used to rasp the gas bladders of plants without puncturing them.

Most of the time they remain submerged, drawing what they need from water through pleated skin. Yet some process within them requires air. Once every few days, an individual climbs the underside of a buoyant leaf until its back breaks the surface. A breathing seam opens. Old gases escape in a long sigh, followed by a single deep intake. Rain collects in hollows around it. Then the seam seals, and the creature eases downward into the canopy.

They gather in small, motionless clusters around the warmest leaves. Their young are carried as translucent buds on the parent’s underside and detach only after their first breath. During heavy rain, when air and water mingle, whole generations may breathe without approaching the surface. Such seasons are times of abundance. The animals spread higher through the leaves and exchange slow tactile signals—pressures passing from foot to foot along a branch—until the forest seems to be thinking through them.

Farther below move the cliff-bearers.

They are broad, ray-shaped creatures whose bodies resemble slabs of dark volcanic glass softened at the edges. A mature one may span the distance between several trunks, but it advances with such control that suspended pollen threads often remain unbroken in its wake. Its fins do not beat. Long waves travel outward from the center of its body, pressing gently against the water. Seen from above, this movement is almost invisible; only the slow translation of the creature reveals that it swims.

The cliff-bearer has no mouth in the usual sense. Its underside is furred with thousands of rooting tendrils. When it settles over mineral crust or living rock-mat, these tendrils enter pores and establish exchanges with the growth beneath. Dissolved metals and geothermal compounds pass into the animal. Sugars and stored daylight pass from plants that have rooted in its back. It feeds through partnership rather than consumption.

As it ages, more life accumulates upon it. Cobalt moss grows in the grooves of its skin. Black pressure-leaves form along its spine. Globe-flowers anchor near the margins, where the creature’s passage supplies a continuous gentle current. Eventually the original body can scarcely be seen beneath its garden. An old cliff-bearer resting against stone is nearly indistinguishable from a ledge, and may remain attached for years while its internal tissues renew.

Their journeys are correspondingly rare. A bearer detaches when the vent it inhabits cools, when its garden becomes too heavy, or when distant pressure changes travel through the mountain sea. It then crosses a basin in a migration lasting months. Other creatures shelter in its vegetation. Spore veils collect behind it. If two bearers meet, they align belly to belly without touching, their tendrils extended into the narrow water between them. Mineral grains and living filaments pass back and forth for days. Then they separate, each carrying small portions of the other’s garden.

Their offspring begin as thin black disks formed beneath the parent’s outermost growth. They remain there until coated in moss and microbial crust, then peel away and drift. Most fasten permanently to rock and become what appears to be an ordinary patch of cliff garden. Only a few gather sufficient internal warmth to loosen themselves and begin moving. Thus the distinction between animal, plant, and landscape remains uncertain even to the place itself.

Deeper still are the vent listeners.

These are long, jointless bodies coiled around warm fissures. Their flesh is the white-pink of new mineral crust, later darkening as symbiotic growth covers it. A fringe of delicate membranes runs along their sides, each membrane sensitive to minute differences in temperature and dissolved metal. They have no need to travel while a fissure remains active. Some never move after fastening.

A listener feeds on what the mountain exhales. It arranges its body across the vent so that water passes over successive chambers, each inhabited by a different community of microscopic life. Iron is taken first, sulfur later, heat last. What emerges has been cooled and altered, suitable for the black-leaved mats growing nearby. In this way, a single listener creates a garden around itself.

Its awareness is geological. It responds to the tightening of stone, the opening of cracks, and pressures advancing from distant ranges. Days mean little to it, but it may react hours before an escarpment shifts by drawing its membranes flat and releasing its hold. After a fall of rock, listeners extend from neighboring fissures into the fresh fractures. Their pale bodies are often the first living forms on a new surface.

The largest are believed—not by any observer, but in the practical grammar of the ecosystem—to remember the mountain’s growth. Changes in mineral pattern remain banded through their tissues. A body several hundred lengths long may contain a continuous record of vents opening and closing, seas rising against vertical boundaries, and forests fastening to summits that once stood bare.

Most elusive are the things that maintain the passages between waters.

They inhabit the vertical faces where one high sea stands beside open cloud. They are seldom perceived as bodies. What can be seen are transparent ribbons extending through the boundary, one surface wet and the other beaded with vapor. The ribbons join into loose lattices many lengths across. They flex with pressure changes but do not tear, and plants root through them as if through dense water.

These boundary dwellers feed on differences: salinity, warmth, altitude, light, and the subtle pull between one suspended sea and another. Water passes through their tissues molecule by molecule. In one season they thicken, holding a valley’s water high; in another they become porous and allow part of it to migrate invisibly into a lower basin. What appears to be a sheer wall of unsupported sea may therefore be a colony—vast, clear, and almost perfectly still.

Their reproductive fragments are indistinguishable from droplets. Rain carries them across open air. If they enter a place where water and atmosphere press strangely against each other, they unfold into microscopic films and begin to join. Over centuries, a new lattice may spread across a pass. Water gathers against it. Spores arrive. Roots cross. Another hanging valley becomes sea.

Predators live here too, though hunger takes a quiet form.

One is a narrow creature like a loosened strip of silver leaf. It lies among descending foliage with its body aligned vertically, sometimes for years. When a grazer, juvenile cliff-bearer, or soft-footed breather drifts close, the strip opens into a wide translucent net. There is no chase. The surrounding water is simply drawn inward, and the prey disappears into folds that close without struggle.

After feeding, the predator grows opaque and sinks to a lower branch. Digestion may occupy a decade. During that time algae settle on it, pollen threads cross its body, and smaller animals shelter beneath its edges. Once the meal is exhausted, it becomes thin and silver again, rises a few lengths, and waits.

Its reproductive event is rarer still. After several feedings, it divides lengthwise into a number of ribbons so fine they resemble shed skin. These drift apart and take stations through neighboring layers. A forest may contain many such predators, yet pass generations without witnessing one act.

There are also creatures that never inhabit a fixed depth. They are colonial chains of clear chambers, each chamber adjusting its density independently. At dawn they ascend toward the side-entering light; by midday they retreat into the green haze; at night they settle among luminous fronds and take in their stored radiance. Spore grains germinate inside their hollow segments. Tiny grazers ride within them. Their passage ties the forest’s separated layers together, carrying life upward and downward through a water column otherwise almost without motion.

When danger disturbs a chain, its chambers detach. Each becomes a small, drifting globe, apparently lifeless. Months later, if calm returns, the globes find one another by trails of dissolved minerals and assemble into a new chain, not always in the former order. Memory in these beings is communal and rearrangeable. No single chamber contains the whole pattern, but together they recover the old routes.

None of these inhabitants commands the mountain sea. There are no builders apart from roots, no roads apart from gradients of warmth, no monuments apart from bodies that have remained in one place long enough to resemble geology. Life does not stand out against the landscape. It repeats the landscape’s methods: anchoring, layering, suspending, mineralizing, and changing too slowly for change to appear as motion.

A creature may be born as a patch on another creature’s back, spend a century mistaken for vegetation, cross a basin once, and settle into stone for the remainder of its life. A forest may uproot, travel farther than any animal, and take three generations of mountains to arrive. A transparent colony may hold an inland sea against the sky without ever possessing anything that could be called a face.

What lives here has adapted not merely to water, altitude, and warmth, but to duration.

It breathes seldom. It feeds without urgency. It moves only when remaining still has ceased to be possible. Its senses are tuned to pressure accumulating across years, to the first warmth in a crack, to the infinitesimal leaning of an entire forest. Birth is often a gradual separation. Death is usually incorporation: a body becomes substrate, a garden, a mineral seam, a buoyant chamber in some larger living structure.

So the high sea is inhabited everywhere, though it rarely appears active. Its stillness is not emptiness. It is attention extended across immense intervals—the patience of roots above unfathomable valleys, of dark wings crossing once between summits, of clear beings holding water beside the open air.

Almost nothing here is in haste.

Almost nothing here is alone.
