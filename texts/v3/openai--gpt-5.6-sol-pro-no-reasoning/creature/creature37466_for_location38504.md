The people of the High Interior are slight, deliberate beings, though not especially small. They only appear so against the scale of what surrounds them. On an open terrace, a traveler with a basket and climbing staff can vanish between two folds of moss. Even a procession of several hundred becomes no more than a dark stitch moving across the green.

They call themselves by many local names, most of which translate imperfectly as those held up, those within, or the people between surfaces. There is no single word among them for the whole of humankind. To gather every person into one category would imply that all people occupy the same world, and this is observably untrue.

Their bodies are adapted less to climbing than to never being entirely free of the possibility of falling. Their feet are broad and articulate, with toes capable of closing around a cord. The soles are ridged like bark and sensitive to minute changes of tension. A person standing barefoot can often tell whether a bridge is tired, thirsty, frightened by an approaching storm, or carrying someone beyond the visible bend.

Their hands are long-fingered and heavily scarred. Most adults are missing a fingertip or two, not from accidents but from grafting work. When a bridge, house, or terrace must be persuaded to accept a family’s care, its tenders sometimes set a shaving of living skin beneath the grafting paste. Old structures therefore contain thin genealogies of human tissue. Doors swell at the touch of their keepers. Stairs alter their spacing for familiar feet.

The people’s inner ears are intricate, and infants learn inclination before direction. They can sleep on sloping walls, work from the underside of branches, and wake at the first subtle disagreement between their bodies and the local gravity. Sudden level ground makes some of them ill. Visitors from the Underhangs may spend years learning which way to let their hair fall.

Their clothing is grown, braided, or allowed to accumulate. The innermost layer is usually a soft web of root fibers fitted closely around the limbs. Over this they wear overlapping leaves cured in oils, pockets made from unopened pods, and loose nets to catch tools if the wearer slips. Nothing valuable is tied with a single knot. Children’s garments are furnished with burrs that fasten them harmlessly to walls, branches, passing animals, and occasionally one another.

Color indicates not rank but intended destination. Blue-gray cords mean water. Yellow knots mean the Looms. White fringe is worn by those who are going to the dead, even if they expect to walk uphill. Red is reserved for errors, uncertain roads, and promises that may produce additional ends.

Hair is seldom cut. It is braided with family cords and practical reminders: the sequence for closing a floodgate, the taste of a poisonous leaf, the number of platforms between home and the nearest safe marsh. The very old may carry so much encoded hair that younger relatives support it in slings. After death, the braids are unpicked and used as measuring lines. Thus a grandparent may become part of a gate, a canal gradient, or the curve of a bridge.

Their settlements are difficult to distinguish from orchards. Houses are hollow swellings in living trunks, chambers beneath cupped leaves, or clusters of basket rooms suspended inside curtains of root. A dwelling is never finished. It buds, narrows, develops unwanted passages, accepts animals, sheds rooms, and sometimes divides into two houses that remain socially one household.

Ownership follows care rather than boundary. A family belongs to the cistern it cleans, the path it trims, and the wall it sings into shape. If they neglect these duties, the structures may begin responding to someone else. Theft is therefore complicated. One can steal a bowl, but not necessarily its willingness to hold food.

Villages rarely possess a center. Their important places are distributed according to what each requires: the warm tree for healing, the hollow vine for sending names, a stable root junction for exchanging goods, a slope with a dependable past for adjudicating disputes. A village may stretch for several days’ travel yet regard itself as one house. Conversely, two families separated by a curtain may belong to different countries because their rain falls from different histories.

They eat fruits, fungi, marsh tubers, insects, eggs, leaf milk, and the flesh of browsers taken during carefully prepared hunts. Much food is fermented in hanging vessels, where it acquires flavors from passing mist. Salt is precious and arrives as white scabs on certain ancient timbers. It is scraped with shell and stored in waxed knots no larger than a thumb.

Meals are quiet. Conversation while eating is thought to confuse the destination of nourishment. Guests first place a crumb against the floor so the terrace understands that they have not come to consume it.

They have little use for coins. Value is kept in obligations, viable cuttings, trained animals, reliable directions, and years of skilled attention. The wealthy are those whose unfinished works will continue to employ descendants. Poverty means not hunger so much as having nothing in the future that knows your name.

Their children are raised communally during the dangerous years before they understand edges. Infants are carried in sling-fruits whose stems remain attached to nursery vines. Toddlers trail soft tether cords, though adults pretend not to hold them. The first important lesson is how to fall: flatten the body, catch the nearest growth without tearing it, discard anything heavy, and do not decide where one will land until something has taken hold.

Children play by constructing tiny bridges between shrubs. Adults inspect these closely, because play structures sometimes mature into actual roads. A careless game may alter travel for generations.

Each child eventually receives three names. The cradle name belongs to the family. The road name is given after the child successfully travels somewhere not chosen in advance. The third is not spoken aloud. It is tapped into red hollow-vine and listened for elsewhere. The name that returns is believed to belong to the person they may yet become.

Some names never return. Such people are not shunned, but they are watched with respectful concern. They often become explorers, judges, or makers of new knots.

Writing exists as pattern more often than mark. History is stored in woven mats whose crossings encode kinship, weather, migrations, structural failures, and changes in the behavior of distance. Read with the eyes, such a mat looks decorative. Read by the fingers, it may recount nine hundred years of a marsh’s descent.

Maps are grown from branching shrubs. Routes are represented by twigs trained in particular directions, though the relation is not geometric. A leaf indicates a place where food is expected; a thorn, a choice likely to wound; a fruit, a destination that may be reached only while carrying something away from it. When a road changes, the map-plant often sickens before travelers notice.

Their scholars are called listeners, although much of their work concerns touch. They kneel at root junctions with brow, palms, and sometimes teeth pressed to the wood, separating distant patterns from the internal murmuring of the terrace. They can distinguish a herd from a landslip, construction from invasion, and a real plea for help from the remembered vibration of one.

Because old timber carries questions, listeners maintain archives of unanswered inquiries. Some have traveled through pillarwood for centuries, emerging at intervals in altered forms. A practical question—how much weight can the eastern span bear?—may return generations later as whom does the bridge believe it carries? Such revisions are not treated as riddles but as structural information.

Law is similarly concerned with intention under travel. Guilt does not depend only on what a person did, but on what their preparations made inevitable. To carry a knife secretly toward a neighbor may already constitute injury if the road begins leading toward blood. Conversely, someone who arrives at a forbidden place while equipped sincerely for another purpose may be judged lost rather than trespassing.

Judges hold hearings at forks in living roads. The parties approach from separate branches carrying the tools associated with their claims. The path on which they depart is entered into the judgment. Occasionally the innocent party cannot return home afterward. This is regarded as a severe flaw in the verdict.

Death is understood as the final revision of destination.

The dead are washed in condensation and wrapped in an open weave so roots can enter. Funeral parties begin wherever the person died and proceed downward, even when this requires climbing. Eventually they reach a place where the terrace accepts the body: a soft wall, a hollow root, an old joinery seam. There the dead are inserted into the construction with a cutting from the family dwelling.

They are not expected to remain human. A useful afterlife is preferred. The dead may become handrails, braces, fruiting niches, resonant chambers, or a patch of floor that warms under familiar footsteps. Ancestors who cause structural trouble are not condemned. They are pruned.

Their religion has no gods in the usual sense, though many things are addressed courteously. They make offerings to load, tension, adhesion, ripeness, and the patience of materials. Emptiness receives the greatest respect. Before crossing a gulf, travelers hold out an empty basket so that the interval may see itself accommodated.

They do not worship the unseen ceiling, but some communities believe the sparse rain is thought condensing above them. A drop that strikes a person is examined for meaning in its taste, temperature, and the leaf fragments embedded within it. Entire pilgrimages have begun because one drop tasted faintly of stone.

The animals are not considered lesser inhabitants. Browsers are moving gardens and are followed by families who harvest their coats without domesticating them. The finger-antelope of the pillar bark are kept as messengers; scraps of scented lichen are tied to their horns, and they carry news along vertical routes no human can easily travel. Gliders nesting in fruits are tolerated even when they spoil a harvest, because empty fruits without nests have been known to float away with sections of orchard attached.

The pale apes below the terraces are viewed with particular unease. Their hands resemble human hands, but they orient every gesture toward the vacancy beneath. Sometimes they gather below a village and tap its floor from the wrong side. The rhythms are almost language. No one has agreed whether they are asking to come up or warning people not to come down.

Interval cats are neither hunted nor named individually. Their common designation means approximately the portion of danger currently visible. Settlements defend themselves with loops rather than walls: braided routes that force approaching intention to repeat itself until the predator loses interest. Hunters carry mirrored leaves, because an interval cat sometimes mistakes a reflection for a later part of its own body and pursues itself elsewhere.

A few people survive attacks. Their wounds appear in separate places, often years apart. Such survivors become discontinuous: a hand may age faster than the face; a scar may open before the tooth that caused it is born. They are valued as weather-sensors and feared as possible entrances.

The most esteemed craft is not building but continuation. Anyone can begin a wall. Great skill lies in inheriting an intention without forcing it to remain what its originator imagined. Builders study the temperaments of wood, the reproductive habits of knots, the pressure of settlements, the desires of unborn users, and the tendency of long labor to turn ceremonial.

A master builder may make only a dozen cuts in a year. Each is placed where growth will amplify it. Apprentices spend decades learning when not to touch a structure.

Before work, crews exchange identical baskets between the proposed ends of a project. Each basket contains the same objects: wet moss, a cutting from the nearest dwelling, a tool too blunt to injure anyone, and a sealed pod holding a spoken account of the destination. At the end of the season the baskets are opened. If their contents no longer match, construction pauses until the disagreement is understood.

When a bridge produces a third end, no one destroys it. A delegation is sent to discover what need called it into being.

The people fear fire, but stone disturbs them more deeply. Wood remembers pressure, fiber responds to care, clay can be persuaded by water, bone once negotiated with flesh. Stone appears complete. It offers no visible ancestry and accepts no graft. Tools made from it are wrapped when not in use, as one might cover the face of an impolite stranger.

Stone-finders occupy an ambiguous place. After storms they search old trunks for newly embedded blades and wedges. They wear muffled garments and carry each find in a separate basket so that no two pieces strike together. Their routes are never written down.

It is said that stone-finders know of chambers where such objects grow like teeth from black surfaces. It is also said that every stone tool is the same tool arriving at different moments.

When stone does strike stone, the inhabitants do not hear only a loud noise. For an instant, they experience distance without revision. The pillars stand at measurable intervals. Bridges connect only two ends. Above is above; below is below. Every object appears separated from every other by clean, indifferent space.

This vision horrifies many of them.

Then the rain comes. Leaves bow under the drops, paths resume their quiet negotiations, and the world grows mercifully inward again.

Among all the High Interior’s inhabitants, perhaps the strangest are the ones reported only at great distances: tall, angular figures glimpsed behind the pillar-trunks whenever stone sounds. They stand in parallel rows beyond the brown-green haze. They do not climb. They do not sway with the terraces. No moss marks their passage, and no root carries their tread.

Listeners pressing their foreheads to the oldest roads sometimes feel these figures moving outside every known direction. What they receive is not speech but a repeated sensation: hands around a handle; the lift of an immense weight; woven walls tightening against floodwater.

Whether these are makers, bearers, prisoners, or merely the shape fear takes when given an exterior, no one agrees.

The High Interior continues to carry them all.

It carries the villages and browsers, the apes below, the gliders sleeping in fruit, the marshes slowly descending, and the builders bending saplings toward descendants they will never meet. It carries the dead as useful wood. It carries unanswered questions through the pillar roots. It carries roads toward whatever travelers have made themselves ready to find.

And through the warm dimness, almost beneath notice, every inhabitant listens for the same impossible thing:

not a sound from far away,

but a sound from outside.
