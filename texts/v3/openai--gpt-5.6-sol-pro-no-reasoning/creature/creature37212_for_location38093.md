Those who call the underside of the leaf a city are the people small enough to mistake its veins for hills.

They are not insects, though in darkness and at a distance they are often taken for them. They have two arms, two legs, upright bodies, and faces arranged for speech. But their proportions belong to a world of narrow clearances: shoulders that fold forward easily, flexible ribs, long grasping toes, and joints capable of drawing the body almost flat against the ground. Most stand no taller than the width of a fingernail. They are fine-boned without being fragile, made resilient by cartilage and dense cords of tendon. A falling droplet can injure one; a collapsing sand grain can kill. Yet they endure pressures that would crush larger creatures of similar shape, and they recover quickly from cold.

They call themselves, in the nearest translation, the **Held**.

The name has several meanings. They are held beneath the leaf, held together against water, held in the memory of their houses, held for a little while before everything living changes shape. To be “unheld” means not merely homeless but unconnected: without a work crew, a fungus allotment, a chamber whose inhabitants will notice one’s absence.

Their skin is smooth and darkly mottled—umber, gray-green, rust, the blue-black of wet soil. The pattern shifts slowly with diet and season. Children raised near algae beds often carry a green cast for years; canal workers become pale at the hands and feet from continual immersion. Their eyes are broad, glossy, and almost entirely black. They see well in weak green light but poorly in sudden brightness. At the leaf margin they wear veils woven from floss to protect themselves from the outer day.

They have very little hair. What grows on the head and along the spine is fine, sensory, and usually bound close with sap. Much of what appears to be clothing is not clothing in the ordinary sense but a second climate: layers of fungal felt, moth scales, milkweed fiber, shed spider silk, and thin membranes peeled from seed interiors. These are wrapped and rewoven daily as humidity changes. A person going from a warm compost chamber to a frostward district may double their apparent size beneath pale insulation.

Their hands are their most conspicuous feature. Each has four fingers and two opposing thumbs, all ridged with microscopic hooks. They climb root hair, carry loads along ceilings, and feel vibrations too slight for hearing. A gate crew resting its palms against a vein can distinguish rain from beetle footsteps, a feeding snail from a burrowing grub, and distant water from the flexing of the living roof.

They do not trust the eyes alone. Sight is local and easily deceived. The Held smell direction, taste contamination on the air, and read the ground through their feet. They navigate by gradients: colder toward a puncture, sourer near yeast gardens, tighter vibrations near the central rib. Their personal names are often tactile patterns tapped against the wrist. In darkness, introductions are made by clasping hands and exchanging rhythms.

Speech exists, but it is close speech. Breath carries poorly through damp felt and crooked chambers, and loudness attracts attention. Their voices are low, dry, and full of clicks made behind the teeth. Many words differ only in pulse and spacing. At greater distances they use root-drumming, taut fibers, scent packets, and living signals. A particular fungus bruises blue when a canal rises. Aphids disturbed in sequence along a roof-vein can pass an alarm faster than a runner. Some districts breed springtails with vivid markings and release them in coded groups, though the messages tend to arrive incompletely and all at once.

No single kind of Held governs the city. The differences between them are mostly occupational, but work changes the body over generations.

The **Vein-Keepers** live nearest the roof. Their limbs are long, and the pads of their hands secrete a temporary adhesive useful for climbing the leaf’s underside. They tend aphids, inspect living tissue, seal larval punctures with translucent resin, and listen for the soft internal crackle that means a vein is drying or becoming overfull. Their faces are often stained amber from sap. Outsiders accuse them of caring more for the leaf than for the city; the Keepers answer that there is no difference.

The **Silt Hands** excavate and build. They are broad through the hips and shoulders, with powerful digging nails reinforced by caps of beetle shell. They know individual grains by shape and behavior. Quartz holds but cuts fibers. Mica lies. Clay remembers water and returns to it. Their builders do not draw finished structures. Instead they keep genealogies of stress: which wall came from which collapse, where the soil swelled, what root passed beneath it, how many freezes a support has survived. A respected architect is someone who can say not where a building stands, but how it is likely to fail.

The **Canal Crews** are compact, pale-eyed, and scarred by grit. They operate the spill gates and clear obstructions during floods. Their tools are tied to their bodies because anything dropped in moving water is gone. Most wear buoyant belts made from hollow stem segments. In an inundation these belts save some and wedge others beneath the roof, so every canal worker carries a hooked blade for cutting companions free.

The **Warmers** cultivate compost, yeast, and heat-producing molds. Their chambers are among the few places where infants and the very old can survive hard frost. They have a reputation for softness because they work in warmth, but theirs is one of the dangerous trades. Fermentation consumes air. Rot breeds poisons. A warming bed can sour invisibly and kill everyone asleep around it. Warmers carry rotifers in droplets sealed inside transparent bladders; changes in the animals’ movements warn of fouled water or failing air.

Then there are the **Hollowers**, who live in gall chambers and abandoned casings. They tend the dead.

The Held do not bury bodies. Soil is too unstable, and burial would surrender useful matter to chance. Nor do they burn them. The dead are washed, wrapped in a chosen culture of fungus, and placed in a communal hollow. What grows there depends on the person and the needs of the district. Some bodies feed edible mushrooms. Some are colonized by tough, felt-forming molds used in winter clothing. Those who died of infection are given to aggressive yeasts in sealed husks, then opened only when nothing recognizable remains.

This is neither thoughtless recycling nor a promise of rebirth. The Held believe identity persists as obligation. A dead gate worker may become material for a gate rope, but no one says the rope is the worker. Instead the rope is owed care because it contains the consequence of that life. Objects made from the dead are named, maintained, and eventually dismantled ceremonially. Waste is not sin among the Held. Forgetting is.

Children belong first to chambers rather than couples. Pregnancy is rare, long, and dangerous in the cold. Newborns cannot regulate their own temperature and spend their earliest months inside shared brood-pouches lined with floss and warmed skin to skin. A child may know several milk-parents, many sleep-parents, and one or two adults who teach a trade. Kinship follows survival: the people who kept you warm, carried you through a flood, or fed you through a failed harvest become your close relations whether or not blood connects you.

Their infants cling instinctively. Their first deliberate act is usually release.

This is celebrated. At the edge of a moss bed, a child uncurls its hooked fingers and stands unsupported while the chamber holds its breath. An elder then places before it three objects: a grain of sand, a root fiber, and an empty shell. The child may touch whichever it wishes. Adults pretend not to interpret the choice and spend years doing so.

The Held eat constantly and little. Their meals are pastes of algae and yeast, sliced mushroom flesh, liverwort tips, pollen ground with water, aphid honeydew, eggs taken from managed mites, and the soft tissues of recently molted insects. Meat is uncommon but not forbidden. Springtails are trapped in damp pits, grubs are sometimes butchered after floods, and anything killed by a predator may be scavenged if it has not begun to sour. Hunger narrows their ethics. Abundance complicates them.

Aphids occupy an uncertain place between livestock, orchard, and weather. Whole districts depend on them, yet no one fully controls them. The Vein-Keepers stroke them for honeydew, move their young to fresh feeding sites, and defend them from larvae with hooked poles. They also cull diseased herds and pierce adults during famine. Old work songs apologize to the aphids while doing both.

Other animals are neighbors only in the most practical sense.

Springtails are tolerated, admired, eaten, read as omens, and occasionally used as transport by reckless messengers who loop a fiber around the base of an antenna and cling through the leap. Mites are groomers, thieves, parasites, and moving walls. Certain slow brown mites are allowed into sleeping chambers because they consume molds that irritate Held skin. Red mites are killed on sight in some boroughs and worshiped as flood-guides in others.

Snails are treated as natural disasters with appetites. When one approaches, roof-tenders tap evacuation rhythms through the veins. The people salvage what they can, smear bitter fungus across important routes, and wait for the vast mouth to pass. A snail can remove an aphid pasture, a neighborhood, and three generations of trained rootwork in a night. It also leaves behind a road of slime smoother than anything the city can construct. Weeks later, sled caravans travel the shining ruin.

Centipedes are not fought. The city becomes still around them. Lamps are covered. Vibrating tools cease. Children are pressed into wall hollows beneath insulating scraps. Decoy pulses are tapped into expendable roads to draw the hunter away from crowded chambers. Even so, a centipede sometimes stops, lifts its head, and begins probing cracks with its forcipules. Entire families have died without making a sound.

Spiders can be bargained with, or so the Thread Speakers claim.

Thread Speakers are a scattered, distrusted profession. They study tension and pluck coded patterns into abandoned web lines. Most spiders respond only as spiders do: advancing, retreating, repairing, or striking. Yet certain long-jawed hunters appear to learn that vibrations at one cadence precede prey left at a boundary, while another cadence means the line will be cut. In return—or by coincidence—they place webs where fungus gnats are numerous and leave some Held routes open.

The distinction between partnership and conditioned behavior matters less beneath the leaf than it might elsewhere. The city itself is built from persuaded organisms. Roots are redirected with moisture. Fungi are pruned into walls. Moss is trained into catchments. Bladderwort traps are disarmed and made into cisterns. The Held do not command living materials. They offer them conditions and build around their answers.

This is the core of their craft: **hortitecture**, the art of making agreements with growth.

A hortitect thinks in hungers. A root wants moisture and depth. A fungus wants food, darkness, and spread. A leaf wants light, water, and intact veins. A gall wants to enclose. Moss wants a surface that remains wet but not drowned. To build is to arrange these wants so their consequences shelter people for a while.

“For a while” is part of every design.

Doors are made with expected narrowing points. Streets include sacrificial bends where roots may be allowed to seal them. Houses have soft walls intended to be cut open when they swell. Maps are dated by growth stage rather than by season: Before the Western Root Forked; During the White Fruiting; After the Second Roof Scar. The city has no permanent center because permanence would be an error in judgment.

Its closest thing to government is the **Council of Pressures**.

The council does not represent territories, since territories migrate and disappear. It represents forces: Water, Roof, Hunger, Rot, Passage, Warmth, Predation, and Memory. The holder of each pressure is chosen by those who labor under it and may be removed after any major failure. Their meetings are held astride the central rib, where vibrations from both boroughs can be felt. Decisions are not framed as laws imposed upon the city but as weights that must be balanced.

Water may demand the abandonment of a low chamber. Hunger may insist it remain occupied for its yeast beds. Passage argues that the evacuation route cannot be blocked. Roof warns that cutting another outlet will kill tissue above them. Memory recites what happened the last three times such a choice was made.

Debate can last until the choice is made for them.

The notched bark tablets kept by Memory are difficult to read. They record quantities, routes, deaths, obligations, and repeated mistakes. Stories are stored differently. They are cultivated.

A historical event is assigned a living colony—often mold on a prepared strip of pith. Keepers regulate its moisture and food so that it develops in a characteristic pattern. White branching may represent evacuation; a red ring, predation; a bare gap, names lost. To remember the event, a reciter studies the colony and tells the story according to its growth. Because the pattern changes, so does the telling. The Held regard this not as corruption but honesty. Memory that does not change is dead material, and dead material must be eaten into usefulness.

Their religion, where it can be separated from civic maintenance, concerns enclosure.

They believe every hollow breathes eventually. A chamber fills with mist and empties. A lung fills with air and empties. A seed fills with stored life and breaks. An egg encloses an animal, a gall encloses a larva, a chrysalis encloses a transformation, the leaf encloses the city, and the outer world may itself be the wet interior of something too large to perceive.

For this reason, sealed spaces are approached with reverence and suspicion. No unopened husk is cut casually. No dormant chrysalis is moved without a vigil. To open an enclosure is to interfere with the future it contains.

The Held have no gods with stable faces. Their sacred figures are conditions: the Weight Above, the Water Returning, the Mouth at the Margin, the Root That Arrives, the Unbroken Skin. Prayers are less requests than rehearsals. Worshipers recite what must be done when the sacred condition manifests.

When Water Returns: lift the young, loose the gates, cut what tangles, follow grit.

When the Mouth Comes: abandon green, keep still, gather afterward.

When the Root Arrives: do not wound the tip. Learn its intention before it learns yours.

Festivals are similarly practical. The First Frost festival is an inspection of insulation accompanied by food and tapping songs. The Opening of a New Gall combines public ceremony with an assessment of whether anything dangerous remains inside. At the annual Naming of Pebbles, newly exposed stones receive communal names based on shape, location, and the events they have survived. Since every pebble is a landmark, unnamed stone is a kind of civic disorder.

Beauty among the Held is the visible trace of successful accommodation. They admire repaired surfaces: sap patches glowing silver-green, a scar reinforced with silk, a cracked shell stitched in contrasting fibers. Perfect symmetry suggests either inexperience or death. Their body ornament follows the same taste. Adults wear beads of mica, polished grit, larval skins, aphid wax, and tiny plaques cut from translucent leaf tissue. Important workers display their scars with pigment rubbed into them.

Music is made through surfaces. Stretched root fibers serve as strings. Hollow stems hum under moving air. Droplets are released onto tuned shells. Large ensembles cannot synchronize by sound alone, so performers sit connected by a web of threads and follow vibration through their ankles. Much of their music is nearly inaudible, felt as tremors in the floor or sternum.

Their greatest compositions are flood songs. These coordinate labor: lift, brace, pull, release. Verses can be shortened if water rises quickly. A skilled leader may conduct hundreds of workers across several chambers without ever seeing them.

They laugh often, though outsiders might not recognize it. Held laughter is a rapid opening and closing of the hands. In darkness, a joke can move through a crowded hall as a rustle of fingers. They find obvious dangers funny after surviving them, especially doors that become walls, bridges that grow in the wrong direction, and official predictions contradicted by animals.

They fear sudden silence more than noise.

Normal silence is full of fine activity: chewing, dripping, scraping, moving water, the granular murmur of soil. Sudden silence means the springtails have vanished, the mites have climbed, or prey has sensed something the Held have not. At such moments everyone freezes. Hands go to the floor. Lamps are covered. Whatever question was being discussed remains unfinished.

Many customs arise from predation. No one runs alone on open cuticle. Children learn shadow drills before they learn names for colors. Travelers carry grit to throw across fresh slime and powder to reveal spider lines. Every public chamber has narrow refuge cracks too small for beetle jaws. At the city’s open end, watchers study the young frog.

They call it the **Green Horizon**.

Most have never seen more than one portion of its face: an eye, a fold of throat, the blunt edge of a jaw. It is too large to understand as an animal in the ordinary sense. It waits beyond the leaf where the light is painful. When it breathes, the air changes. When it shifts its weight, distant passages tremble.

Its tongue is the one catastrophe for which there is almost no warning. A wet pink surface enters, adheres, and withdraws. People, aphids, structures, loose soil, and lamps vanish together. No ruin remains from which to learn.

The Held have nevertheless built defenses. False vibration lines lure strikes toward weighted husks. Bitter molds are grown near exposed plazas. Watchers release a sharp scent when the frog turns its head. None of this is reliable. The Green Horizon feeds when it will.

Some sects teach that those taken by the tongue enter the outer world intact. Others say this is a comforting obscenity. Canal workers, who recover more bodies than priests do, tend toward neither view.

Death is common enough to be ordinary, but extinction is imaginable.

The Held know the leaf is alive, and therefore know it will die. They have watched margins yellow. They have tasted changes in the sap. Frost damage spreads farther each cycle. The roots pinning the leaf to the soil may preserve it, consume it, or merely delay its collapse. No pressure-holder can say.

There are arguments over departure. The **Marginists** want to establish chambers beneath neighboring leaves before this roof fails. They send expeditions through outer brightness, losing many travelers to cold, wind, and open-sky predators. The **Deepeners** believe survival lies below, among soil pores and buried roots. Their tunnels repeatedly flood or collapse. The **Keepers of One Roof** insist that attention spent on escape hastens the city’s death.

A few return from beyond.

They come light-blinded and dusted with unfamiliar spores, carrying reports that sound like fables: a reed whose hollow could house every citizen; water without a ceiling; stones larger than districts; an animal footprint filled with rain; another leaf lying nearby, smaller but drier, with no people under it.

Such news never arrives unchanged. By the time it reaches the western dark, the empty leaf has become a promised country, then a predator’s wing, then a place where no fungus grows and the dead cannot be used.

Meanwhile, life remains immediate.

Aphids pulse at the ceiling. Warmers turn the compost beds. Hollowers inspect the dead. Silt Hands prop a wall that was level yesterday. Children race shed mite skins down a slime path until an elder scolds them for wasting transport material. Two Thread Speakers argue over whether a spider has accepted tribute or merely eaten it. In a gall chamber, a council debates the rationing of yeast while condensation slowly fills the cistern behind them.

And in the western dark, the new root touches soil.

The Held gather around it without approaching too closely. It is white, translucent at the tip, and faintly luminous from the fungal threads already investigating its surface. It advances by increments too small to see and too strong to stop. Grains shift beneath it. A roof chamber creaks. The nearest road has begun to rise.

The hortitects taste the moisture around the root and confer by hand-sign.

It may drink from the canal and weaken the western cisterns.

It may split the roof.

It may thicken into a support large enough to stabilize three failing districts.

It may bring rot, food, passage, poison, or all of them in sequence.

Before morning, the people lay damp threads to guide it away from the brood chambers. They pack one side with dry grit and paint the other with algae wash. No one pretends this is command. They are making a suggestion to a hunger older than their city.

Workers remain beside it through the cold, palms pressed to the ground.

They feel the root enter.

They feel the soil answer.

And while the leaf clicks under frost above them, the Held begin composing the name of the street that does not yet exist.
