The nearest thing to a people in the little city lives inside the eastern wall.

They cannot be seen, and perhaps “inside” is only a concession to the habits of larger minds. The limestone yields no cavity when sounded. Hairline probes meet uninterrupted rock. Yet there are moments when the tapping separates into individual blows, and then it is possible to imagine the workers by the patience of their labor.

They must be smaller than the city suggests. The benches, handholds, tools, and thresholds do not describe one consistent anatomy because they were not made to fit bodies. They were made to fit tasks. Whatever the makers are, they borrow shapes from the work before them. At a channel they become low, soft things able to seal a leak with their whole undersides. At a ceiling they divide into hooked extremities and hang in rows. Before a drill hole they narrow around the turning grit. While carrying a clay ring they become an arrangement of supports, briefly radial, with no front except the direction of motion.

Away from labor, they may possess no shape at all.

The gray membranes in the stone are their quietest state. Each concentric pattern is not a colony but a folded citizen, spread thinner than skin across a mineral pore. Water passes through it and acquires metal because the membrane is listening: sorting dissolved traces, measuring pressure, receiving the city through taste. A single worker may remain in this form for centuries, if centuries have meaning where every event returns unchanged.

When sufficient pressure gathers, it loosens from the pore without tearing. The rings contract. A minute colorless thickness enters the capillary channels and is carried elsewhere. It emerges near a workshop as a clear bead that moves against the slope. Dust adheres to it: clay first, then shellstone, then black grains of volcanic glass. Thus clothed in the city’s substance, it can push, scrape, brace, or strike. What would appear to a human observer as a tiny tool moving by water pressure may be a worker wearing its temporary body.

They do not speak across air. They inhabit resonance.

The three deep impacts are a summons or a name. The trembling hiss distributes some instruction too complex for any single avenue. The ringing stone marks a transition. The western bellow gathers what has been said and returns it through every wet wall. Each district receives the same chord differently according to the shape of its chambers, so that one city-wide sound becomes thousands of local meanings. A household may be a group of chambers tuned to one another. A civic assembly may be a harmonic held between reservoirs. Memory may reside not in minds but in recurrence.

This is why the sequence never varies. It is not a song endlessly repeated, nor a mechanism caught in a cycle. It is the whole population maintaining itself. To alter one note would be to alter a street, a pressure, a body, perhaps an ancestor. Their city and their language have long since become the same structure.

The colorless mites are not citizens. They are weather.

To the makers, a mite crossing the roofs must be a vast, warm catastrophe: many-legged, soft-jointed, carrying unfamiliar salts on its feet. Its passage flexes bridges and changes the pitch of buried halls. Yet the city has learned its routes. Roofs beneath its steps are reinforced. Pools where it pauses have overflow channels too fine to notice. When it vanishes into the fissure, the altered pressures travel for hours through the seam, and the membranes receive them as the upper city might receive news of a storm.

The threadlike swimmers are closer to instruments than animals, though they remain alive in their own obscure way. Coiled, each one closes a minute circuit in the cistern wall. When the low note arrives, it uncoils and touches two mineral contacts, allowing a pulse to pass through the water. They are relays grown rather than built. Whether the makers bred them, found them, or gradually tuned the architecture around their reflex cannot be known.

The solitary crustacean is something else.

Mineral accumulations on its shell form ridges corresponding to the buried streets beneath the western district. Each time it crosses the plaza, its first touch upon a pillar releases a pressure pulse into the lowest reservoir. Its second dislodges a few grains of copper-bearing sediment. At the third, it waits while the milk-white arches brighten by an amount nearly too small to see. Then it returns to the drain.

There may be many such creatures, each arriving only when the city’s circulation has prepared the same route. Or there may be one creature continually renewed by mineral deposition, shedding its living center and inheriting the same stone coat. To the city, the distinction may be irrelevant. It performs its office. It comes alone.

The builders themselves are absent from the streets because they have passed beyond occupancy. They no longer live among the buildings; they live as the relationships that keep the buildings possible. They are the membranes regulating the pores, the swelling cords holding gates at precise angles, the clay cells drawing water upward, the pressure crystals answering flow with light. None of these is individually alive. Taken together, none is convincingly dead.

Their ancient bodies may once have been more definite. The varied benches could record different castes or stages: broad resting forms, narrow clinging forms, soft aquatic forms kept in the shallow basins. The rows of handholds suggest communal movement across walls and ceilings. The polished rims indicate repeated contact by something neither entirely rigid nor entirely flesh. But erosion has removed the evidence with the same care by which it improved the city. It has rounded anatomy into function.

The faces on the civic pillars are blank because expression was never their most important feature. Water has erased eyes and mouths while preserving the cavities behind them. Those cavities tune the falling drops. Each lost face now produces a note. Portraits have become voices.

Nor are the tomb-like halls necessarily tombs. They may be places where a maker was disassembled into useful continuities. Mineral portions went to lintels and pins. Membranous portions were spread across the rock. A practiced pattern entered the waterways as rhythm. Thus the dead were not buried beneath the city. They were distributed through it. A dark stain in a gutter may be a lineage. A fixed gate may be an elder whose last contraction has not ended.

At the center, the hollow ring tower holds them all.

The chord rising through it is the closest the city comes to a collective body. Every cistern contributes pressure. Every gutter adds a thin vibration. Every chamber cuts away certain frequencies and strengthens others. Inside the tower these arrive together, not blended but nested, until the complete city is present as sound. The builders may gather there without moving. Their dispersed selves coincide in the chord.

The impressions around the tower are therefore not footprints. They are acts of self-recognition.

At intervals too subtle to observe, the black glass softens—not with heat, but under a precise arrangement of vibration and water film. The city presses its current pattern into the floor. Old districts remain recorded because every vanished passage still contributes a buried resonance. The impressions are not maps drawn from above. They are the shapes the city feels itself to have.

The final impression includes the eastern chamber because, in the city’s sense, that chamber already exists.

Behind the wall, the makers labor in a time different from sequence. The stone point strikes. Grit turns. Clay is pressed into place. These are not three events following one another, but three permanent conditions of construction. No dust appears because no material has yet crossed from possibility into the seam. No passage opens because the room is never approached through distance. It is being built into the city’s pattern first—acoustically, hydraulically, and only lastly in stone.

Perhaps when every existing channel has weathered to the proper curve, when every threshold has lost the final roughness left by its maker, the eastern wall will cease to be solid without ever breaking. The room will simply have become more true than the limestone occupying its place.

Or perhaps completion would be fatal. The unfinished chamber may be the slight absence around which equilibrium turns. It gives the tapping somewhere to originate, the city somewhere to extend, and its makers a form they can continue to assume: not inhabitants, not ghosts, but builders.

They live there as an unfinished act.

Their hands are the tapping.

Their breath is the balanced water.

Their long, communal body is the city itself, held between two stones and listening forever for the room beyond the wall.
