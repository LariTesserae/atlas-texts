The ones who live there are not the builders.

The builders visit, endure, alter, and depart. They enter with borrowed breath and weighted hides, moving from handhold to handhold like people crossing the outside of a storm. However well they know the chamber, their bodies still confess that they belong elsewhere. Air is knotted around their throats in little gourds. Stone must be tied to their waists to keep the water from choosing a direction for them. They work quickly, even when they appear patient.

The true inhabitants are those for whom the mountain’s indecision is not a danger but a climate.

Most numerous are the anchor-creatures.

They live in the holes around the six stone hooks and in the braided dark where kelp, hide, and root have begun to mineralize together. At first glance they resemble white crustaceans: translucent shells, too many fine legs, soft blind heads tucked beneath overlapping plates. Seen closely, no two are built according to quite the same plan. Some are flattened for fissures that close and open like mouths. Some have hooked limbs that all face one way, though the direction changes after each molting. Others grow broad, cupped feet with which they cling not to rock but to pressure, holding themselves inside jets where nothing solid supports them.

Their shells are temporary calculations.

Minerals gather on them rapidly, whitening their backs and stiffening their joints. They must shed often or be incorporated into the wall. A cast shell rarely drifts far. It catches against a cord, fills with crystal, and becomes a small articulated stone. The builders collect these sometimes and use them as seeds in the baskets. Thus the creatures leave behind hard versions of bodies they possessed only briefly.

They feed in the pauses between the mountain’s deep pulses. The instant the vibration lessens, they pour from the anchor holes in a pale, quick multitude. They graze the new mineral skin, clip threads of algae, and comb bacterial films from raw fractures. Then some pressure too subtle for a visitor to perceive runs through the stone, and they vanish together. Their retreat makes a faint dry clicking inside the roar.

They do not communicate by sound. They drum on whatever is newly solid. A hundred tiny limbs tapping a stone tell them its thickness, its internal cracks, and perhaps how long it has before changing. Their colonies arrange themselves around the most reliable resonances. When the mountain alters, the colony migrates—not necessarily through the water, but through the rock’s opening seams, appearing days later around a hook that had seemed sealed.

The blind fish is called singular only because no one has seen two at once.

It is long, milk-pale, and narrow through the body, with fins so reduced they look like folds left unfinished. It does not fight the currents. It places itself across them at careful angles and allows each force to solve the next part of its passage. Where a swimmer would be hurled backward, the fish turns one degree and moves forward. Where the water falls upward, it rolls onto its side. It can remain motionless beside a hammering jet, its body curved like a shaving of bone.

The red filaments around its gills are alive in their own right. They open when bitter water erupts from the fissures, drinking substances poisonous to nearly everything else. In return, they seem to tell the fish where the youngest water is entering. Before a new crack appears, the red growths flare. The fish arrives and waits with its mouth against blank stone. When the fissure bursts, it feeds on what comes through: heatless chemicals, minute organisms from deeper chambers, threads of mineral that dissolve against its tongue.

Its slowness is not frailty. It may be the oldest moving thing there. Fine ridges along its flanks resemble growth rings, though they divide, merge, and restart wherever its body has healed from impact. One eye sometimes forms beneath the skin, remains for a season, then is absorbed. In lightning, its spine shines dimly through its flesh.

It never touches the baskets.

The three-leaved plant in the upper recess is another kind of resident, though “plant” may be only the nearest name. Its roots clutch the same fistful of silt while the recess repeatedly changes orientation around it. The three leaves are clear except for green veins. One gathers daylight. One gathers dissolved minerals. The third is always turned toward the stone, pressed flat as if listening.

The leaves exchange their purposes. If the daylight seam closes, the transparent blade darkens and begins feeding from the rock. If mineral clouds obscure the chamber, the listening leaf opens toward whatever brightness remains. The stem circles continually, tracing the local rotation of the water. Its loops are precise enough that the builders have used them to count changes in the current.

It has flowered once.

The flower had no petals. It formed a clear bladder containing a spiral of black seeds, each seed turning independently against the direction of the water. Before the builders could return, the bladder vanished. Months afterward, tiny three-pointed marks appeared in a newly opened seam far below, green for less than a day before crystal covered them.

Algae inhabit the chamber more completely than any animal. They lie sleek against sheltered rock, dark as hair, but their visible ribbons are only the surfaces of colonies threaded through porous stone. When a plate breaks free, green lines can be seen running deep within it. The mountain grows around them; they grow into the mountain. In places they trap bubbles that move inward rather than up, feeding them one by one into pores.

During lightning, the algae tighten.

The entire chamber then seems to possess nerves. Green ribbons contract across the walls in successive waves, following the flash from seam to seam. Whether they respond to light, vibration, or some electrical stirring in the wet crystal is unknown. After the brightest storms, new growth appears along fractures that did not exist before.

Then there is the long inhabitant below.

It cannot be described from sight, because sight never receives all of it. A joint passes through the illuminated water. A narrow bend eclipses the mineral seams. Something like a limb, or a succession of limbs, withdraws behind a floor that should not be deep enough to conceal it. Its shadow sometimes hinges in three places at once.

The builders disagree about whether it is an animal.

Some believe it is a root belonging to a life buried beneath the mountain, probing upward while the stone is still soft. Some think it is a chain of creatures holding one another, each no larger than the blind fish, their joined bodies mistaken for a single being. Others say it has no flesh at all: it is an absence moving through the water, a long region in which pressure, light, and distance temporarily obey the same rule.

What is certain is that the chamber quiets when it passes.

Not gradually. The jets continue to strike, the stone continues to flex, bubbles continue to burst—but for three heartbeats none of these events produces sound. The anchor-creatures emerge and hold perfectly still. The red filaments on the fish’s gills close. The three-leaved plant stops circling.

In that silence, the long thing can sometimes be felt through the ropes.

It touches no visible part of the platform, yet every binding draws taut in sequence from the lowest baskets to the six hooks. The pale stones rotate toward it. A few have cracked open afterward, revealing their seeds—bone, charcoal, shell, carved wood—untouched inside hollow chambers shaped like small ears.

Perhaps this is what the builders come to tend.

They themselves are changed by repeated visits. Those who enter often develop pale calluses across their palms and ribs, sensitive to vibrations others cannot distinguish. Their hearing in air deteriorates, but they can kneel outside the mountain, touch its black face, and tell whether the chamber’s floor is high or absent, whether a basket has split, whether the long inhabitant has passed recently.

Mineral gathers in their scars. Old workers carry thin white seams beneath their skin.

They arrive one at a time because several bodies confuse the currents. Each brings a shaped core selected for a particular basket: the finger bone of an ancestor, charcoal from a lightning-struck tree, a shell carried above the cloud line, a wooden spiral carved while listening to the mountain from outside. Around these seeds, pale stone accretes. The builders scrape, turn, and suspend it, not dictating its final form so much as correcting any form that becomes certain too soon.

Their youngest workers suppose they are making stones.

Their oldest behave as though they are introducing them.

A finished stone—if any stone here can be called finished—has no consistent shape. Some are knuckled and asymmetrical. Some are smooth but seem to contain corners visible only from the side. Some pull their baskets downward; others strain toward the walls. Held against the chest, each transmits a distinct pulse through the ribs. The builders remove very few. Most remain hanging in the chamber, acquiring layer after layer.

So the platform, too, may be considered alive.

It has no original shape left. Spars have been replaced, cords renewed, hooks moved by turning stone. Algae bind its joints. Anchor-creatures nest in its lashings. Calcite sleeves grow around its ropes, break, and grow again. It contracts in violent water and unfolds during the rare softer hours. Its flexibility is not designed once but taught continuously—by every snapped fiber, every shifted knot, every basket that survives.

It remembers only in arrangement.

And the mountain is the greatest inhabitant, though it has not yet learned the difference between body and home.

Its walls turn. Its floor rises to investigate the light, then loses confidence and pours away. It grows ridges, seals them, opens elsewhere. It swallows bubbles as if trying lungs. It admits daylight through translucent seams and slowly clouds those seams with its own substance. Every object inside becomes a suggestion: cord tells it how to make a vein; basketry teaches lattices; shells offer hinges; bones propose hollows; handprints show where a surface might divide into fingers.

The chamber is not a cavity within inert stone. It is an organ the mountain is in the process of abandoning.

Everything living there is temporary in a different way. The crustaceans survive by leaving bodies behind. The fish survives by yielding to forces before they arrive. The plant changes the function of its leaves. The algae accept burial. The builders preserve knowledge in knots because no landmark can be trusted. The long thing below may survive by never wholly entering space.

All of them inhabit not a place, but an interval: the brief age during which the mountain contains water, light, distance, and room.

When that interval ends, few will escape.

The fish may depart through some descending fissure. The anchor-creatures may seal themselves in wet pores. The plant will be crushed into a green three-pointed stain. The unseen long one will pass downward—or simply cease to be long when the chamber no longer permits length. The builders will make their final visit, if they recognize it in time, and cut loose whatever stones are ready to be carried into the air.

The rest will remain.

Cedar will become a dark branching fossil. Kelp cords will leave braided channels. Each reed basket will persist as an absence around a pale stone. The builders’ handprints will lie between crystal layers. Thousands of shed shells will articulate the rock near the hooks. The algae will become green shadows in quartz.

And at the center of the sealed summit, the platform’s last bent shape will be preserved: not as a ruin, but as the posture taken by everything that lived there when the mountain finally chose to become solid.
