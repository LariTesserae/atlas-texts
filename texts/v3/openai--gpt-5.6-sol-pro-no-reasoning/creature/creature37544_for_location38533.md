What lives in the High Cistern is not its absent ruler, though nearly every feature of the chamber seems arranged around that vacancy.

The creatures belong instead to the cistern’s motions. They inhabit currents, joints, gradients of warmth, and the small permissions left by its builders. None is large enough to master the chamber. Taken together, they form something like a body distributed through the architecture.

The blind fish are its most visible inhabitants. Slate-pale, almost translucent at the fins, they suspend themselves around columns in ordered layers. Their dark lateral lines are broad and exquisitely sensitive. Through them they feel water displaced by a falling grain, the settling of an arch, or another fish turning several body-lengths away. They maintain exact intervals without touching, making sleeves of living flesh around the pillars. When one moves, its neighbors adjust in sequence, and the displacement travels around the stone as a slow revolving shiver.

They feed on tiny animals carried up from the barred passage and on mineral-soft organisms dislodged from cracks. Their mouths open downward beneath blunt heads. At intervals, green light swells under their jaws. The pulse is brief, but nearby fish answer—not all at once, and not randomly. Light passes around a column in a measured circuit, illuminating one worn mask after another. Whether this is courtship, warning, or a means of keeping their distances, the effect is that the architecture appears to think in green.

Within the narrowest masonry joints live the folded, handlike creatures. Each consists of a soft central pad surrounded by many colorless digits. They move so slowly that movement is inferred from their changed positions. A crevice empty one year may be filled the next by a pale fan of fingers. They have no obvious eyes, mouths, or openings. Their digits adhere to ceramic and draw sustenance from invisible films nourished by heat and dissolved metal.

When disturbed, they fold inward, finger over finger, becoming smooth knots no larger than teeth. Some occupy the drilled eyes of the masks. From a distance these faces appear blind and empty; approached closely, they are found to contain patient hands pressed against their inner walls.

Armored grazers patrol the floor. Their bodies are flat and many-jointed, covered with shells to which they cement powdered tile, copper scale, shell fragments, and pellets of sediment. The old ones carry whole histories on their backs. A red tessera may rest beside the tip of a bone pin; beneath both lie earlier layers of yellow ceramic and black grit. They resemble moving repairs.

They read the cistern through vibration. A grazer finding nourishment taps one claw against the fitted pavement. Others receive the signal through their legs. They do not converge directly, for the central rise and its patterned currents govern even this. Instead, they follow the joints clockwise, sometimes taking hours to cross a distance a swimming animal could cover in moments. Their gatherings resemble patches of rubble until, by common consent, they disperse.

In the dome’s channels wait the ribbon predators. They are long enough to span an arch and thin enough to hide in its shadow. Their skin is black-brown, dull as fired clay, with gripping ridges along the belly. They wedge themselves between projections and hold their narrow mouths open into the upper current. Behind the jaws are combs of flexible teeth that strain drifters from the water.

They may remain braced in place for months. Mineral deposits form around their contact points. Small grazers sometimes cross their backs. When a ribbon predator finally moves, it peels free section by section and traverses the vault without visible effort. In the weak blue seep it looks less like an animal than a fracture passing through the building. The fish below tighten their rings but do not scatter. They know it feeds on smaller things.

The copper cages contain colonial shell-bearers. No individual among them grows larger than a fingertip, but generations cement themselves to the shells of their dead until each colony becomes a pale, convoluted mass. Fine feeding threads emerge from thousands of openings. They sift the slow water and withdraw at the smallest vibration, causing whole regions of a colony to close in traveling waves.

Their larvae can pass through the cage perforations, yet most return and settle inside. Perhaps some mineral in the copper guides them. Perhaps the cages preserve a current they require. Over ages they have assumed the shapes of their confinements: cubes, cylinders, blunt cones, and one long suspended form like a sealed bell. As the water turns, their shells touch with delicate, irregular notes. Different cages have different tones. No creature appears to answer, but the sounds travel through the copper wires and into the masonry.

The smallest inhabitants are the amber drifters. They are nearly transparent motes with fragile radial limbs. In calm water they remain dark. Compressed through shutters, grilles, or the mouths of channels, they flash. Thus the flow makes itself visible in brief constellations. Amber lines appear at the dome, wind clockwise, descend along outer pillars, and vanish into the trenches. Predators consume immense numbers of them, but warm water from below brings more.

The slow cephalopod of the dais is less numerous. There may be one, or several that are seldom seen together. It has a low mantle the color of wet ash and arms broad enough to drape over the worn tiles without stirring the dust below. Rows of muted red organs lie beneath its skin. Ordinarily only one or two glow, as if embers remain buried in its flesh. When alarmed, all of them appear, outlining a pattern of loops and bars.

It spends much of its life touching the dais. The tips of its arms pass repeatedly over the seams between tiles, the glazed base of the empty seat, and the relief of the cupped hands. It feeds on grazers, shell fragments, and whatever minute life collects where glaze meets stone. Yet feeding does not explain the regularity of its circuits. It circles the seat clockwise. It pauses at the same cracks. It sometimes settles before the carved hands with its arms drawn inward, mimicking their shape without knowing it.

In the outer trenches live burrowers that are almost never exposed. Their narrow bodies pass between strata without mixing them, swallowing bone meal and ceramic dust and extruding each in compacted threads. They preserve the colored bands even as they feed upon them. If uncovered, they contract into rigid little rods and sink vertically until the sediment closes overhead. Their shed teeth contribute to the layers through which their descendants descend.

There are scavengers near the barred doorway, small enough to pass between the stone rods but reluctant to venture beyond. They are six-limbed, clear-shelled things with fanlike organs held into the incoming warmth. When an object rises from below, they examine it first. They clean the cavities in beads, scrape soft accretions from copper hooks, and shelter briefly inside bone toggles. Then the current takes both object and animals upward. The scavengers release themselves before reaching the dome and labor back down along the wall.

Nothing larger enters from the descent. Whether larger life exists below is not known. The low knock does not summon the cistern’s animals or frighten them. It passes through tile, pillar, shell, and joint as a single compression. The folded creatures tighten. The grazers pause with claws lifted. The fish alter their spacing by less than the width of a scale. Then the distributed life of the chamber resumes.

These animals prey upon one another, graze upon unseen sustenance, molt, reproduce, and die. Yet death here is not departure. Shell becomes armor. Bone becomes sediment. Teeth enter the colored strata. Soft bodies are consumed or dissolved into the warm circulation. The cistern retains almost everything, admitting nourishment from beneath while losing little through the shuttered apertures above.

Even their evolution has been architectural. The fish have become creatures of columns rather than open water. The grazers trust fitted joints as other animals might trust scent trails. The ribbon predators have bodies shaped for gutters made by vanished hands. The colonies have grown into the forms of cages whose purpose neither they nor any living witness can know. Every species bears the room upon it.

The chamber may therefore be said to contain one greater inhabitant: not an organism, exactly, but a compact ecology whose skeleton was built before it was born. Its circulation is the cistern’s circulation. Its memory lies in sediment bands, shell layers, repeated routes, and inherited responses to angles and vibrations. Its senses are divided among lateral lines, gripping digits, tapping claws, luminous organs, and copper-strung colonies. It has no central will. It persists by obedience to constraints.

At its center remains the glazed seat, the one place no creature permanently claims.

Fish arrange themselves around its supporting arches but do not cross directly above it. Grazers clean the lower bricks and turn aside at the seat’s lip. The cephalopod touches it but never rests upon it. Even drifting shells tend to be carried past by a small division in the current. This emptiness need not be reverence. It may be flow, glaze chemistry, inherited avoidance, or the lingering effect of some material long since washed away.

Yet the vacancy is maintained.

So what lives in the High Cistern lives around an absence: pale fish wearing the pillars, hand-creatures folded into the walls, rubble-backed grazers tapping messages through the floor, black ribbons stretched in the vault, colonies chiming in green-black cages, red-lit cephalopods circling the summit, and multitudes too small to possess names.

They do not await anyone.

They have made the waiting itself alive.
