What lives there does not live upon the plain. Nothing could root in that raw, reforming crust, or graze among edges that open without warning and close under fresh salt. The inhabitants keep beneath it, in the flooded chambers where the stone is thinner than darkness and the noise passes through flesh before it reaches water.

The smallest are the most numerous: soft, colorless things no longer than a finger joint, though their reach can be many times that. They have bodies like translucent knots, folded compactly inside mineral pores. From each knot extend several fine limbs, some jointed and hard at the tip, others fluid as mucus. The hard limbs scrape new deposits from below, leaving the tiny ringed holes that appear overnight in pearly films. The soft ones enter cracks too narrow for the body and taste what the seepage carries—iron, sulfate, copper, and the minute chemical differences between one sealed pocket and the next.

They do not feed on growth. There is no growth to eat. Instead, their inner tissues hold chains of reactive salts. Where waters of unlike composition meet inside them, a small exchange occurs: bitterness against metal, acidity against crystal, one dissolved burden precipitated while another is released. They live by keeping substances apart for a little while. Their bodies are temporary partitions.

This makes them exquisitely vulnerable to sameness. If the waters around them mingle too completely, they become still and clear. Their joints loosen. Mineral needles form inside their tissues, and in time the dead creature is indistinguishable from the lining of its chamber. Many of the finest crystal nests are colonies that failed when a barrier broke.

They sense the world through vibration. Along their bodies lie rows of hollow grains, each tuned to a different pressure. A deep note causes one grain to turn; a whistle tightens another; the hard ringing of a crystal cleft makes whole clusters fold shut. To them, the country is not dark. It is full of contours made audible: chambers outlined by resonance, fractures flashing into perception, distant collapses blooming through the crust like silent lightning.

At intervals they gather. Hundreds press themselves into a fracture and link their soft limbs, becoming a pale mesh through which seepage must pass. The mesh sorts the water, mineral by mineral. On one side, chalk accumulates; on the other, blue-gray crystals grow. Such colonies may persist for decades, extending as the fissure extends. The oldest acquire stone around every individual until the distinction between nest, reef, and animal can no longer be made.

The larger inhabitants are built on the same principle.

They occupy the low flooded vaults and seldom fit through any single opening. What appears briefly as a smooth back is only one lobe of a body distributed through several connected pools. Pale limbs pass through submerged cracks; flexible joints hinge around stone; broad membranes rest against chamber walls and receive their vibration. A mature one may spread beneath an entire basin while possessing nowhere a central mass that could plainly be called its head.

Its parts remain separate whenever they can. A limb lowered into iron water reddens and hardens. Another, immersed in a gypsum pocket, stays soft and milk-white. A third may become glass-clear in bitter brine. Channels within the animal keep these fluids from mixing. Its life depends on preserving the differences the plain continually threatens to erase.

Thus its slowness. A single movement requires an elaborate rearrangement of internal boundaries. Before one limb can be withdrawn, valves of muscle and crystal must close behind it. Before another enters a neighboring pool, its tissues must be emptied, folded, and filled with the new water a little at a time. What is heard in the fog as one displacement, a long pause, then another is not hesitation. It is the labor of remaining several things while moving as one.

No mouth is visible because feeding takes place across all its surfaces. No eyes form because the rock itself supplies a more exact sense. Inside each broad membrane are taut mineral fibers grown from dissolved salts. Landscape notes pluck them. From the resulting tremors the animal knows the width of a cavity, the thickness of a wall, the pressure behind a sealed fracture, and the approach of another body. It can detect a new crack long before the crack reaches open water.

The solitary frames and half-sunken chambers belong, in some fashion, to these creatures.

They were not quarried or raised by hands. A mature animal selects loose basalt by embracing it from below, testing each fragment against the surrounding resonance. Over years it nudges suitable pieces upward through flexing crust and mineral mud. Stones whose notes oppose one another are drawn together. Deposition seals their natural faces. When the animal finally withdraws, a low ring or roofless enclosure remains: not a dwelling in the surface sense, but an instrument of silence.

During certain phases of its life, the animal needs such silence. It enters the cavity beneath a chamber and spreads itself against the stones. The canceled vibration permits delicate internal walls to form without being shaken apart. Clear water gathers above. From below, the animal extrudes minute translucent bodies into the central depression. Some chambers therefore serve as nurseries. The hexagonal crystals found filling others are nurseries in which development has ended differently—or perhaps correctly, since no clean boundary exists here between incubation and mineralization.

The door-shaped frames have another use. Beneath each is a narrow seam connecting waters that would otherwise remain apart. The upright stones hold different resonances, and the lintel forces them into a shared beat. An animal resting below can use that beat to synchronize remote portions of itself. From a distance, the vibrating air and fog alter the light within the frame, making the opening seem to contain another hour. Up close, the effect collapses because the observer’s body changes the resonance.

Whether this is construction, reproduction, or merely a consequence of the creatures’ metabolism cannot be separated. Their acts leave geometry. Their bodies leave stone. Their dead become valves, reefs, blades, and ringing plates that direct the lives of those after them.

The oldest presence may not be an animal in any ordinary sense. It lies beneath the central flats, dispersed through an immense network of water-filled cavities. No portion of it moves more than a few handspans in a year. Its pale joints are mistaken for salt veins; its membranes line pools thought to have mineral bottoms. Pressure passes through it and emerges as the plain’s deepest note.

It may be one being, or a congress of countless smaller ones joined so long ago that their boundaries have ceased to matter. The distinction changes with the season. When mineral bridges form, separate chambers begin to pulse together. When fractures open, parts fall out of accord and become independent. The great inhabitant is continually assembling and disassembling without birth or death occurring at any single place.

At times it quiets a region. Pillars cease moaning one after another. Rings flatten on the pools. Hanging drops remain suspended long past their usual fall. In that interval, the pallid creatures below unfold from their pores, and the distributed giants open their internal channels. Substances kept apart for years are briefly allowed to meet. New tissues precipitate in luminous sheets. Old partitions dissolve.

Then the note returns. The waters divide. The inhabitants withdraw into difference again.

Nothing listens on purpose, perhaps, but everything living there is made by listening. The plain enters them as pressure, pitch, interval, and beat. They answer not with calls but with altered channels, selected stones, sealed cracks, and bodies arranged to preserve one water from another.

They are the unfinished note’s hidden anatomy: pale, patient, many-bodied things inhabiting the brief distinctions from which life there is made.
