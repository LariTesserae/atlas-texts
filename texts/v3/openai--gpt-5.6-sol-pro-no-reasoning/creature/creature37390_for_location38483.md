The valley is inhabited above all by maintainers.

They are numerous without often appearing as crowds. The city’s depth distributes them through pump rooms, stairwells, signal cabins, greenhouse aisles, inspection galleries, workshops, conduits, and the hollow interiors of retaining walls. From a distant terrace they register as brief signs of function: a waterproof coat crossing a gantry, three lamps descending beneath a street, a figure leaning over a canal screen, someone standing beside an open service panel while rain gathers on their shoulders.

They tend to be compactly dressed, layered against damp rather than severe cold. Their coats are waxed or rubberized at the seams; their boots have soft, gripping soles for wet stone and iron stairs. Most carry gloves, a folding hood, a small light, and some means of opening the city—keys, coded tags, valve tools, cabinet handles. Clothing marks affiliation only subtly. A stripe at the cuff may identify drainage workers, rail technicians, reservoir crews, structural inspectors, horticultural cooperatives, or the guilds that reclaim old materials. Reflective cloth is used sparingly, enough to flare in a headlamp and disappear again.

Their competence is intimate rather than theatrical. They know which wall is warm because a steam main passes behind it, which stair vibrates before an upper train arrives, which gutter overflows first in a long rain. They can distinguish healthy pump noise from the beginning of bearing failure and read the color of seepage on brick. Directions are given by infrastructure as often as by street: uphill of the third pressure break, beneath the abandoned north viaduct, two galleries below the fig houses.

No one understands the whole city. Knowledge is inherited in sections.

Retired lock operators remember channels that no longer appear on plans. Masons preserve notebooks of vanished foundations. Tram drivers know air currents in tunnels well enough to anticipate weather aboveground. Children learn that a blank wall may contain a flood stair, that painted curb marks indicate buried mains, and that certain bells mean water rather than fire. Every district has people who can explain why an awkward bridge remains, why one row of windows is false, or why a tree stands in the middle of a loading court. Their accounts may contradict the archives, but they are often more useful during repairs.

The inhabitants have adapted their sense of distance to elevation. A place directly visible across the valley may be an hour away by lift, stair, bridge, and tram, while a district apparently far below may lie only three stops down an inclined railway. They say *upstream* and *downstream* as readily as north and south. Addresses include levels, entrances, and access conditions. A household may have one door for ordinary use, another reached from a roof lane, and a third that opens only when a neighboring canal is drained.

Homes occupy every kind of inherited enclosure. Some are narrow rooms stacked behind old warehouse facades; others fill converted cisterns, bridge abutments, former turbine halls, or the upper stories of functioning works. Windows may face courts, rail galleries, greenhouses, or the rough stone of the mountain at arm’s length. Interiors are warm, densely fitted, and designed around moisture. Boots dry over grated vents. Coats hang in tiled vestibules. Window ledges hold gauges beside pots of herbs. Pipes are exposed and labeled, access hatches left unobstructed. Furniture folds into walls because rooms may need to accommodate inspection crews without warning.

Privacy exists, but seldom through isolation. It is made from shutters, thick curtains, acoustic panels, scheduled quiet, and the practiced courtesy of not looking into windows across narrow shafts. People hear machinery through their floors and water behind their walls. They sleep through the regular sounds and wake immediately for an unfamiliar one.

Meals reflect the city’s managed abundance. Greens, mushrooms, roots, herbs, orchard fruit, and greenhouse vegetables are common. Fish come from the lower canals and reservoir pens. Grain, oil, salt, and animal products arrive by rail from beyond the ridges and are treated with a respect sharpened by freight cost. Markets are covered, cool, and close to transit lifts. Produce bears the name of its terrace or glasshouse rather than some distant countryside. Compost is sorted as carefully as metal scrap. Nothing edible is wasted casually because every peel has somewhere to go and every household knows where that is.

Many people cultivate something, even if only a tray of onions beneath a stairwell light. Gardeners are not regarded as separate from engineers. The orchard keepers understand load distribution and root barriers; filtration workers know reeds by season and disease; greenhouse crews monitor heat recovered from factories below. Children learn pruning alongside drainage diagrams. A flourishing vine is admired, but a vine reaching a masonry joint is corrected at once.

The population includes builders, but few think of building as the creation of something wholly new. Their work is insertion, substitution, bracing, unthreading, and rethreading. They remove a beam while preserving the floor it supports, lay a new conduit through a wall containing five older ones, or turn a redundant chimney into a lift shaft without interrupting the workshops around it. Demolition crews are skilled sorters. Salvaged brick is cleaned by hand or machine and stamped with its new date. Old stone arrives at yards bearing tool marks from several lives.

Among them move the recorders: surveyors, clerks, archivists, mapmakers, load assessors, water accountants. The city depends on documents, though documents lag behind reality almost as soon as they are issued. Maps are layered and conditional. A tunnel may belong to the rail authority at track level, the water board beneath the ballast, a housing cooperative along one wall, and the horticultural service where warm air is vented into growing beds above. Negotiation is less a special event than a permanent civic craft.

Authority is dispersed in much the same way. There are district councils, works boards, reservoir committees, transit offices, structural courts, garden cooperatives, and emergency coordinators. No single institution commands the valley outright because no system can be separated from the others long enough to govern alone. Public meetings concern practical entanglements: whether a retaining wall can bear two additional floors, which route a temporary tram should take during canal repairs, how much winter reservoir capacity may be exchanged for greenhouse heating.

Prestige belongs less to wealth than to reliability. A person who keeps an old lift running through flood season, remembers the hidden bypass beneath a hospital, or devises a repair that permits everyone to remain in place earns lasting regard. Wastefulness is shameful. So is making a change that cannot be maintained by those who come afterward. New work is expected to show how it can be opened, drained, replaced, and reused.

The elderly often remain active because memory has infrastructural value. They sit in warm public rooms overlooking junctions and are consulted about obsolete systems still buried below. Some serve as witnesses when walls are opened, identifying pipes by alloy, smell, or the spacing of rivets. Their stories concern failures survived: the winter a feeder froze, the subsidence beneath the south markets, the rain that filled three rail levels in an hour. These stories are not told as legends of conquest. They are case histories with human names attached.

Children inhabit the vertical city with alarming ease. They know lift timetables, covered routes, warm tunnels, and which public halls permit passage during storms. Their games follow painted utility lines or turn drain grates into imaginary borders. They race leaves through gutters, build small lock systems in runoff channels, and collect obsolete ceramic insulators from approved salvage bins. They are warned away not from wilderness but from moving machinery, sudden sluices, electrified galleries, and doors that may seal automatically.

Few animals live among them, so those that do become locally familiar. A boiler-yard cat may be recognized across several blocks and fed according to an informal rota. Children wait beside lower canals for the dark turn of fish under aerators. Eave birds are counted and nesting ledges incorporated into repairs where practical. Fox sightings in the eastern tunnels are discussed with delight, skepticism, and concern for cable insulation.

The people themselves are quiet in public, partly from custom and partly because sound carries unpredictably through the built slopes. Conversations take place under awnings, in tram vestibules, beside warm vents, or along covered galleries where rain makes a soft boundary around them. Bells, signal tones, and announcements are restrained. Everyone has learned that unnecessary noise can conceal the small sounds by which the city reports its condition.

This does not make them solemn. Their festivals illuminate unusual layers of the valley. Reservoir walks open after inspection season. Disused rail spans host night markets. Harvest tables run through greenhouse aisles. On certain evenings, turbines are slowed so music can be performed in old pumping halls, whose tiled chambers return voices with a long, watery resonance. Repairs completed after years of work are marked with plaques naming not only designers but crews, residents, displaced trees, and the temporary systems that kept the district alive.

During storms, ordinary distinctions narrow. Residents clear balcony drains and check lower neighbors. Transit workers become water stewards; market halls become supply depots; school kitchens remain open through the night. Crews descend in pairs into flooded galleries while watchers track them from dry stations. People who never meet in fair weather recognize one another by call sign and district code. When the rain eases, there is no triumphal pause. Screens are emptied, silt measured, bearings checked, damage entered into ledgers. The city continues.

They do not imagine themselves masters of the valley. If anything, they consider themselves its current custodians: one generation occupying a machine-landscape inherited in incomplete condition and destined to be handed on the same way. Their deepest fear is not age, damp, or density, but interruption—the stopped pump, the blocked channel, the severed line, the knowledge lost before it can be transferred.

Their deepest comfort is continuity.

At night, they can look from apartment windows and see no complete picture, only fragments: a green roof glowing across the mist, a tram passing through an older building, a worker’s lamp moving behind rain, water shining briefly as a gate changes position. Beneath them, turbines turn. Above them, reservoirs fill. Somewhere inside a wall, roots press toward warmth and are guided aside by barriers placed decades earlier.

Most inhabitants will never leave a mark visible from the reservoir heights. They add a dated brick, revise a diagram, graft a tree, clear a screen, teach an apprentice the sound of a failing valve. Yet together they are the city’s most intricate moving system: the part that remembers, interprets, and repairs all the others while living inside them.
