The beings of the high country are creatures of surfaces.

They cannot afford deep bodies. Thickness is weight, trapped heat, and matter withheld from the circulating world. Their lives spread outward instead: into fans and foils, branching combs, mirrored plates, hollow columns, and membranes thin enough for light to pass through. Even the animals seem less like parcels of flesh than arrangements—walking lattices temporarily persuaded to hold one shape.

The mineral foliage is the foundation of nearly every life there. Its groves are not forests in the familiar sense but slow-moving fronts of chemistry. The youngest growth occupies the outer ring, where needle-fans and black-green films invade fresh stone. Behind them rise denser forms: antlered crusts, bowls that collect heavy gases, translucent blades with metallic veins. At the center stand the exhausted bodies of earlier generations, bleached and brittle. They fracture grain by grain, becoming the substrate over which their descendants advance.

A colony is therefore always departing itself. Its living edge travels while its center dies. Over centuries it may cross a basin without any individual growth moving at all.

The sailwoods are its tallest inhabitants. What resembles a trunk is a communal tower built by innumerable living films, each laying down a porous mineral skeleton before withdrawing to the exterior. The films exchange gases through the tower’s channels and share the gradients created by light and wind. Older trunks harbor entire layered ecologies: dormant membranes deep in the pores, grazing mites beneath lifting scales, parasites that imitate the host’s chemistry, and delicate thread colonies that feed on what leaks between one crystalline layer and the next.

A sailwood crown can survive for millennia, but not as one continuous self. Its living sheath is replaced repeatedly. Its vanes split, fuse, and grow upward; its trunk becomes hollow, then partitioned by new deposits. Eventually the base fails without drama. The great structure settles sideways and continues living as a low reef. New fans rise along its length. Grazers shelter in the old resonant chambers. For centuries after falling, it still sings when the wind crosses it.

Among these growths move the glass-backed grazers. Their apparent shells are stacks of transparent plates, added from within and discarded from the outside when abraded. Beneath them spreads the green organ that sustains the animal during stillness: part lung, part leaf, part chemical reservoir. After feeding, a grazer finds a bright stone and unfolds this organ against the inside of its shell. For several days it scarcely moves. Light passes through its back, and the branching tissues darken as they recharge.

They travel in loose herds, though the individuals may be separated by hundreds of meters. No calls pass between them. They keep contact through flashes. Along each shell runs a row of minute reflective shutters, opened and closed by changes in internal pressure. A herd crossing a flat produces slow sequences of light: one flash, then another farther on, then a scattered answer from what had seemed to be empty ground.

Their legs are many-jointed and finer than stems, but each foot broadens into a soft mineral pad. This lets them cross dust without sinking and cling during the first pressure of a storm. If the wind becomes too strong, they lower themselves, seal their plates, and allow dust to cover them. A herd may vanish in minutes, leaving only a field of shallow humps. When the air clears, green organs brighten beneath the powder and the animals rise one after another.

Their young begin as flakes.

Adults deposit small layered wafers at the advancing edges of vegetation rings. These remain inert until exposed to the precise mixture released by fresh growth. Then each wafer divides radially, lifts itself on threadlike legs, and begins to graze. Most are buried, blown away, or consumed before waking. Some travel for centuries as indistinguishable particles in the dust and hatch in colonies far beyond the range of their ancestors.

The pale kite-creatures of the sailwoods are more aerial than terrestrial, though none can truly fly. At rest they hang folded against the vanes: long, pleated bodies with hooked forelimbs and rows of sealed chambers. As evening winds begin, they climb to the crown and turn their narrow heads into the flow. The chambers open in sequence, taking in the lightest gases exhaled by the grove. Their bodies loosen, their grasp fails deliberately, and they are carried away.

A launched kite has limited control. It can collapse chambers on one side, twist its trailing membranes, or shed grains of ballast from pockets along its belly. Such corrections alter a journey by degrees, enough to mean the difference between a distant grove and a lifeless salt expanse. Many never land. In storm seasons their transparent husks can be found tangled along cliffs, emptied of gas and polished by dust.

Those that reach another sailwood arrive bearing a minute country with them. Spores sleep in their grooves. Parasites seal themselves beneath their scales. Tiny symbionts inhabit the gas valves and wake when they detect a new grove’s exhalations. Some organisms exist nowhere except in transit, reproducing on one kite during the long drift and passing to another only when several travelers gather on the same crown.

The gas channels belong to the long hunters.

A hunter may stretch several body lengths while remaining scarcely thicker than a strap. Its sides carry pressure combs so sensitive that it can distinguish a grazer’s footsteps from the settling of cooling stone. It anchors its rear segments beneath crust and lays the rest of itself along a channel, matching the ground’s color and reflectivity. Dust gathers around it. Films grow across its back. Small animals may shelter beside it without recognizing anything alive.

Its strike is not a chase but a release of stored tension. The whole body shortens at once, throwing the front segments across the channel. Ceramic hooks close around the prey, and reactive powder is forced into the grazer’s breathing seams. Afterward, the hunter may feed for days and remain still for a month. Some miss so rarely that they make only a few dozen attacks in a lifetime.

Yet these visible animals are a minor expression of the plateau’s life. Most inhabitants are films, grains, filaments, and sealed membranes.

They sleep beneath salt crusts and inside volcanic glass. They line the microscopic hollows of faceted stones. They drift as electrically charged dust and cling to sailwood vanes when storms pass. Many are active for only minutes at a time, responding not to seasons but to transient chemical conjunctions: a cold dawn after a dust tide, a descending breath rich in one particular gas, the ultraviolet flare of a cloudless noon.

A seemingly barren flat may wake in bands. Pores open across the ground. Dark threads emerge and spread into branching nets, each filament no thicker than a scratch. They harvest light until the surface becomes too warm, then withdraw, sealing themselves below with new mineral caps. From emergence to disappearance, an entire feeding cycle may occupy less than an hour. The only lasting sign is a faint discoloration visible from the next ridge.

Other organisms live on the pooled atmospheres of the basins. They form floating skins along boundaries between gases of different densities, suspended above the ground on an invisible interface. These skins ripple when internal waves pass beneath them. Their upper surfaces face the sun; their lower surfaces absorb compounds rising from the basin floor. When the boundary collapses, the skins crumple into silver threads and fall. When stratification returns, they inflate and rise again.

Along these chemical shores gather the flake-seeds. Each has a dead mineral center surrounded by a living rim capable of reading the gases around it. The rim does not germinate merely where conditions are good. It waits for conditions that indicate a stable route of future nourishment: repeated tides from the same slope, regular exhalations from an established colony, or the layered atmosphere of a basin unlikely to be scoured by wind. Seed bands therefore record the plateau’s invisible circulation more faithfully than any channel carved in stone.

There are parasites among all these forms, though parasitism here is patient. Some settle on sailwood vanes and imitate mirrored scales, stealing transformed compounds when the host contracts at night. Some enter a grazer’s green organ and divert a tiny portion of its stored chemistry for decades without visibly harming it. Others infest the long hunters, altering the sensitivity of their pressure combs so that they wait beside colonies favorable to the parasite’s own spores.

Relationships are seldom purely hostile. A parasite may become insulation. A grazer may carry the films that repair its shell. The kite-creatures depend on microorganisms that regulate the gases in their lifting chambers, while those organisms can reproduce only during the cold and pressure changes of a long crossing. What begins as theft may, over evolutionary time, become structure.

Nothing maintains a constant body temperature or an uninterrupted waking life. Activity is parceled into brief permissions. A grazer walks while its chemistry allows, then becomes still. A hunter spends accumulated energy in one contraction. A grove rotates only when opposing crystal layers have stored sufficient imbalance. To an observer accustomed to continuous motion, much of the plateau would appear dead. Watched long enough, the dead things turn, open, migrate, feed, and answer one another with light.

Sensing is shaped by exposure. Eyes, where they exist, are narrow and deeply recessed against glare. Many animals instead perceive polarization, pressure, static charge, or the spectral signatures of living films. They read the passage of distant bodies through disturbances in pooled gas. They recognize a grove by the frequency of its chord. They find east before dawn through changes in the upper atmosphere, while the sun is still hidden beyond the curve of the land.

The plateau’s largest living entities may be the colonies themselves. Separate growths within one basin exchange dust-borne particles, alter one another’s gas flows, and coordinate their orientations through reflected flashes. Whether this constitutes communication or merely mutual response is impossible to say. Over great spans of time, however, whole archipelagos of vegetation advance, divide, and rejoin in patterned ways. They behave less like gatherings of organisms than like immense, discontinuous bodies whose organs are separated by kilometers of stone.

At night these bodies reveal themselves. Their afterglow is not uniform. Light moves through them in muted pulses: along a ring’s active edge, up the trunk of a sailwood, across the layered skins floating over basin air. Grazers resting nearby answer with brief shell-flashes. Kite-creatures hanging in the crowns open and close their chambers. Far beyond sight, another grove may turn, changing the note carried through the ground.

Before sunrise, pressure shifts across the continent. The long hunters settle deeper into their channels. Glass-backed herds unfold their green organs. Microscopic nets rise through salt. Floating skins tighten over the basin gases. The sailwoods begin their dry, incremental rotation.

There is no command and no common mind. Each being responds to its own portion of the coming day: a cooling layer, a trace gas, a change in polarization, the faint brightening of stone.

Nevertheless, across thousands of kilometers, the same gesture emerges.

Every plate opens. Every fan inclines. Every mirrored surface abandons the stars.

And for those few minutes, the scattered life of the high country resembles one vast creature turning in its sleep.
