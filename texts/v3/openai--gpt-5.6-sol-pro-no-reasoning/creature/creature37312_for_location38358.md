The inhabitants of the plateau are creatures of surfaces, seams, and borrowed water. They do not so much cross the landscape as complete it. Shells repeat the colors of the rocks they graze; armor resembles broken pavement; wings imitate dead leaves; bodies fit channels so precisely that flesh and stone seem to have weathered together. Even the largest animals move with a geological patience.

No single species governs the place. Life is organized around brief appearances of moisture and the long intervals between them.

On shaded rock faces, countless tiny shelled grazers feed upon films too thin to see. Their shells take on the local stone—ocher, cream, rust-red, varnished black—and whole colonies resemble mineral deposits. They advance in such numbers and at such a pace that their feeding becomes a faint communal hiss.

Across the open pavements rest broad animals covered in overlapping plates. Dew gathers in shallow basins along their backs, making each animal a temporary pool and habitat. Flies drink there, mites reproduce, and narrow-billed birds glean among them. The oldest carry lichens and rooted grass, becoming mobile fragments of terrain.

Dry, membrane-winged creatures hang in curtains between pillars. At dusk they release themselves into rising air and drift through the stone channels like lateral ash, rarely beating their rough, opaque wings.

In the shallow valleys, tall, narrow grazers travel in slow herds on cushioned feet. Their branching crowns are made of porous, perforated bone. Wind passing through these structures produces breathy chords, and pale beetles live within the openings, transferring between animals whenever two crowns touch. The herd’s music is incidental: the sound of anatomy exposed to weather.

Predators exploit thirst rather than speed. Flat and dust-colored, they settle along basin rims and expose fringes of damp, vivid tissue that smell like water. They may wait days for an animal to lower its mouth. After feeding, they remain motionless for months, gathering dust until the basin seems empty again.

Much of the plateau’s population lives in suspension. Eggs lie for years in dry fissures. Larvae seal themselves inside mineral cases. Soft-bodied creatures retreat into porous rock. Plants fold their leaves; reptiles flatten their bellies against wet stone; small furred animals cling upside down beneath ledges. They wait not for a season in any dependable sense, but for particular combinations of shade, temperature, condensation, and distant rain.

The central maze of stone fins holds the densest communities. There, life occupies the vertical world. Snails migrate up and down walls with the nightly condensation line. Pale reptiles drink through their undersides. Chrysalis-like sacs fill sunless recesses. When rain enters the maze, organisms emerge from every seam, covering the rock in violet, pearl, brown, and black films. The transformation is immense but quiet: membranes opening, mouthparts scraping, water whispering through ancient grooves.

Deeper pools contain entire layered societies. Surface skaters stand above red, threadlike swimmers; blind shelled animals work through the silt; porous living mats withdraw into the bedrock as the pool contracts. The black mud around each pocket records visits by creatures rarely seen directly.

Along the western rim, dark, raft-forming animals gather shoulder to shoulder over temporary streams. Their bodies shade the water, trap sediment, and shelter aquatic young. When the stream divides into pools, the living raft divides with it. When the final pools vanish, the animals climb away carrying dormant eggs in folds of their skin. The river’s descendants leave the riverbed inside them.

Some inhabitants build without conception of building. Mud-bearing insects have raised low walls grain by grain across saddles. Vast nest colonies have left ring-shaped rims around abandoned chambers. Generations of colonial larvae have deposited cases until their fused remains stand as rounded towers. These structures outlast the behaviors that began them, becoming habitat for descendants that no longer know their interiors.

Even death remains inhabited. A dried carcass becomes shelter, salt deposit, nursery, and passageway before being pressed into a pale mark beneath generations of feet. Hollow stems are partitioned into chambers. Stone flakes conceal sleeping coils. The perforations in bone, the dust caught behind a tail plate, and the damp margin beneath an overhang all become worlds.

These beings are not impoverished remnants. They are specialists refined by an ancient scarcity. Their abundance is measured not in greenery or noise, but in occupancy: virtually every protected space contains something waiting, feeding, breeding, or sealed against dryness.

At evening the plateau reveals them by degrees. Winged curtains detach as shadow reaches each pillar. Armored slabs become animals when they rise. Herds turn their crowns toward the wind and sound across the valleys. In cooling cups, dew forms.

Everywhere, hidden mouths are already waiting beneath it.
