The chamber is inhabited less by separate species than by provisional answers.

Its most numerous creatures are the transparent mites that procession over every newly brightened surface. They are scarcely larger than grains of coarse salt, glass-clear except for the red and golden particles traveling through their bodies. Those particles do not remain in fixed organs. They exchange places, gather, disperse, and sometimes pass from one mite to the next when two meet mouth to mouth. The files they form are not trails toward food or shelter. They are boundaries. Where a line of mites completes a circuit, the stone inside it becomes—for a little while—floor, wall, pool-bed, or ceiling. When the procession breaks, the designation loosens. This may be why water so often disregards the chamber’s shape: the mites have redrawn it while no one watched.

The blind grazers are heavier, many-jointed things with blunt mineral teeth. Their bodies resemble pale roots taught to walk. They feed on the tender skins of the stone buds, rasping away translucence and leaving dull tracks that heal behind them. In dry intervals they press themselves into those tracks before they vanish. The swelling rock closes over their legs first, then their backs, until only a row of breathing pores remains. Weeks or years later, rain loosens them. They emerge larger, bearing new segments that may be limbs, offspring, or simply remembered pieces of the wall.

The milk-white amphibians live wherever the rock has begun to flake. Each carries one blue, pulsing organ in its throat, vivid as a bead of wet sky. It serves as heart, eye, and rain-clock. During dry periods the pulse may slow until centuries could pass between beats. At the first condensation it quickens, and the animals unfold from beneath the flakes with grave, deliberate movements. They drink droplets that have not yet landed. They take mites without disrupting the mites’ files. Sometimes two crouch facing each other and exchange their blue organs, swallowing and regurgitating them until neither seems certain which life it has received.

Their eggs are not laid in water. They are placed in predictions.

An amphibian approaches the grooved wall and waits until a mark appears resembling its own death. It presses its soft belly to the groove, leaving behind a translucent capsule. What hatches depends on whether the mark is read. Unobserved, the capsule produces several small amphibians. Closely examined, it may produce a fossil of the parent. Touched, it may yield only a cold blue pulse that travels into the stone and surfaces elsewhere years later.

Above them fly the membrane-winged creatures. No two observers—if observers could enter—would agree on their anatomy. From below they look insect-like, with narrow bodies and six transparent wings. Reflected in suspended droplets, they appear to be tiny folded chambers carrying animals inside. Their routes pass through distances not yet made continuous. They vanish behind a mineral rib and return from the underside of an arch; they circle a crack before the crack has opened; sometimes they arrive first and perform the flight afterward.

They feed on radiance. Settling along a fresh fracture, they insert hair-fine mouthparts into its white depth and drink until the stone grays. Only then do they cast shadows. The shadows begin attached, but feeding makes them heavy. After enough circuits they tear loose, flutter down like soot-black skins, and lie trembling on the floor.

From these fallen shadows rise the black stalks.

The stalks are neither wholly plant nor wholly consequence. Their roots branch through darkness rather than stone, and their hollow cases breathe at a rhythm unrelated to any animal’s respiration. Inside each case, the chamber’s air is altered: distance thickens, sound remains whole, and water falls in the expected direction. The cases seem to cultivate finished laws. They cannot contain them for long. When a case ruptures, its little pocket of certainty collapses into a patch of darkness, which gathers beneath rocks and in the mouths of recesses.

Small pale scavengers come to eat it. They have no consistent number of legs. Darkness consumed at the front of the body emerges behind as a delicate gray shell, often shaped like an object absent from the chamber: a leaf, a feather, a fish scale, the knuckle of a hand. The scavenger abandons this shell and continues, while the chamber slowly absorbs the foreign shape. Thus the place acquires impressions of a world it has never touched.

The translucent moss fans participate in another economy. Seven exist, though not always seven can be found. During rain they open into thin, pleated cups and imprison single droplets between their leaves. Within each captive bead, the wet interval continues after the rest of the chamber has dried. Mites march there. Winged creatures circle microscopic fractures. Occasionally a blue-throated amphibian appears inside, no larger than dust, and lives out an entire season before the fan closes.

A folded fan may vanish from one wall and emerge elsewhere because the moss does not move through the chamber. It moves through its own stored water. All seven fans may be one organism opening in several possible locations, or seven moments in the life of one fan.

The crimson-tipped roots descending from above are more alert than the moss. They do not seek water. They turn toward locomotion, especially toward movements that have not yet produced sound. When an animal passes beneath, a root tip follows it with slow, blood-colored attention. If the animal dies, the root withdraws. If it returns incorrectly—inside stone, missing a limb, carrying another creature’s memory—the root touches it and begins to branch.

These branches do not penetrate flesh. They grow into discrepancies.

Around a resurrected amphibian, they may form a delicate red cage enclosing the place where its absent limb should be. Around a mite walking within a mineral inclusion, they spread across the rock until they find the point at which walking became imprisonment. What the roots take from such encounters is unknown. Nothing visible travels upward. Perhaps the plain above is nourished not by matter but by corrected mistakes.

The chamber’s hunters keep these populations from becoming stable.

The thread-thin animals in the fissures are almost indistinguishable from fractures because the fractures are partly their bodies. A hunter extends itself as a weakness through the stone and waits for prey to cross. Then the weakness closes. Digestion occurs within the wall, leaving behind an exact pale outline. These embedded silhouettes are not remains but vacancies: every part of the prey has been removed except the shape it occupied.

During later rains, such vacancies may fill with the wrong substance. Water can acquire legs. Stone can begin pulsing gold and red. Once, perhaps, an outline fills only with sound, and for a day the wall gives off the dry brushing of an invisible grazer.

The flat drifting predators are rarer. They resemble wet paper, shed skin, or blank scraps torn from some larger plan. Their undersides never meet the ground. Instead, they fasten themselves to a nearby location and pull it close. A winged creature several body lengths away abruptly finds that it has always been under the predator. There is a click, and the intervening distance springs back.

These hunters do not eat flesh. They consume routes. After a capture, some path through the chamber becomes impossible: a ledge can no longer be reached from the floor, two adjacent stones become indefinitely far apart, or a mite-file marches forever without arriving at its own end. The predators grow darker with every route consumed. The oldest are almost invisible except when passing across one of the chamber’s rare shadows, where they show as pale omissions.

Near the temporary basin stand the five little arches. Something inhabits them, though never underneath.

It lives in the sequence they make.

When there are five arches, it is dormant. When rain adds a sixth or removes one, the change passes along the row like a cautious animal trying its limbs. Stone wafers lift and settle. Gaps narrow. One arch may exchange its height with another. Condensation avoids them because droplets approaching the row lose the distinction between arrival and departure.

At times, minute tracks appear beside the arches: paired depressions no larger than pinpricks, leading from the first arch to the last without passing between them. The unseen builder may be constructing a body out of relations—span, interval, repetition—rather than tissue. If the row ever reaches its correct number, whatever lives across it may become capable of entering the chamber as an ordinary creature. The chamber seems reluctant to permit this. It keeps miscounting.

The three white egg-like objects in the square recess contain another kind of resident. Through their shallow doorways one may glimpse staircases descending farther than the shells allow. Small figures sometimes move on those stairs. They are upright, slow, and colorless, with heads bowed beneath burdens that cannot be seen. None has reached the doorway.

During heavy rain, faint tapping rises from the eggs. Because loud sound is divided here, this tapping may be the distributed remnant of one ancient blow—or one future knock—still trying to occur. The figures halt when it is heard. They turn their faces upward. For a moment their features resemble those of whatever creature is nearest the recess: amphibian, mite, grazer, even root. Then they resume their descent.

No creature rules the chamber. If it possesses a central life, that life is the chamber’s incompleteness itself.

It feeds through the cycles of brightening and dimming. It tests organs in animals, direction in water, memory in stone. It uses mites to establish surfaces, wings to measure impossible routes, predators to prune distances, moss to preserve intervals, and roots to examine errors in resurrection. The arches may be its first attempt at a skeleton. The grooved wall may be its nervous system, firing events before it understands how cause should follow effect. The basin may be an unfinished mouth.

With every rain, this diffuse inhabitant draws closer to having a body. Ribs rise from the floor. Buds thicken overhead. Walls wrinkle and reconsider their positions. The animals become feverishly active, as if each were a temporary organ called into service. Water collects wherever circulation is needed. Shadows root. Stone germinates. Death loosens.

Then the fissures close.

The water departs. The white radiance fades toward gray. Creatures retreat into flakes, walls, stored droplets, predictions, and distances. The chamber releases whatever shape it had nearly achieved.

In the dry silence, something remains awake—not yet beast, landscape, or structure, but the possibility shared by all three. It waits beneath the pale plain with no entrance and no final dimensions, surrounded by its small rehearsing lives.

When rain returns, it tries again.
