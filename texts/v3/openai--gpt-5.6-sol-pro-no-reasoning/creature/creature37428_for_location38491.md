The people of the Underbloom are slight, deliberate beings, though whether this is ancestry or adaptation no one can say. In the milk-white distances they seem almost unfinished: narrow bodies wrapped in layered fiber, faces pale with resin salves, hands and feet exaggerated by hooked climbing frames. They move carefully because carelessness is a kind of noise, and in the Underbloom any loud act may become a conversation with something enormous.

They call themselves many things. Villages migrate, divide, tip onto new surfaces, and lose contact for generations; names do not remain shared for long. Yet nearly all distinguish themselves from the rooted and the stonebound by a word meaning, roughly, **those who may release**.

To release is the defining act of animal life. Roots hold. Vines tighten. Wounded tissue seals around intrusions. The people survive because they know when to unfasten a bridge, abandon a cistern, cut loose a house, or allow themselves to fall.

Their bodies reflect that knowledge. Their toes are long and flexible, useful for gripping fiber without piercing it, and the smallest toe has become almost thumb-like. Thick pads cover their soles, palms, knees, and the outer edges of their forearms. These pads are ridged rather than smooth; pressed against a leaf, they distribute weight and read subtle movements in its skin. An elder walking barefoot can distinguish rainfall from sap pressure, a migrating root from a tightening vein, and ordinary cold contraction from the first tremor of a turning.

Their ears are small, folded close against frost, but the bones behind the jaw are unusually sensitive. When listening to the ground, they lie with cheek and sternum against it, mouths slightly open. Vibrations pass through the jaw into the inner ear and through the ribs into the lungs. Interpretation is partly heard, partly felt as pressure and nausea. Children are taught this “deep hearing” before they learn route songs. A child who cannot yet speak may still slap the floor to signal that something below has begun to move.

Their blood is dark and viscous, slow to surrender heat. Beneath the skin lie thin layers of waxy fat interrupted by vascular knots that can close around the extremities. In severe cold their fingers whiten nearly to translucence, then flush blue when warmed. They tolerate temperatures that would kill a surface human, but they are vulnerable to sudden heat. A kiln chamber feels feverish to them. Standing too near an unshielded coal can cause fainting, delirium, or a violent immune reaction called red waking, in which old scars swell and dormant parasites emerge from the skin.

Their clothing is not primarily insulation but climate control. The innermost layer is a close net of soft root hair that traps a narrow envelope of breath-warmed air. Over it they wear overlapping scales cut from shed leaf cuticle. These open during exertion and close when the wearer becomes still. Cloaks are coated with frost-binding powders, so drifting moisture gathers as a white crust rather than soaking through. Before entering shelter, a person brushes this crust into a communal melting bowl. Even the water caught on a traveler’s back is considered part of the household supply.

Nothing they wear is fastened with a knot that cannot be opened one-handed.

Their tools, likewise, are made to disengage. Hooks straighten under dangerous loads. Bridge pins shear before the living surface tears. Harnesses have release cords running to the teeth. A person swept into a tightening grove may survive by abandoning every possession in a single pull. For this reason wealth is not measured by what one keeps attached to the body. It is measured by what will be waiting elsewhere: cached water, remembered hospitality, obligations carried by distant kin, and coal-rights in settlements one may never see again.

They do not usually claim territory. They claim **returns**.

A return is a recognized right to come back to a place if it still exists: a condensation cup, a sheltered vein fork, a kiln vent, a grafted dwelling, or simply a patch of membrane known to remain stable through several inclinations. Returns can be inherited, exchanged, sung into marriage agreements, or surrendered to repay a water debt. Because landmarks move and whole landscapes turn, ownership is less “this place is mine” than “if this place can still be found, it remembers me.”

Memory is therefore public infrastructure.

Every settlement has at least one route-singer. The title is misleading, for they preserve far more than roads. Their songs describe the intervals between rains, the taste of safe sap, the warning tremors of particular plant bodies, the succession of tilts, and the histories of objects found embedded in growth. A route song changes whenever the land does. Old verses are not discarded; they are sung softly beneath the new melody, preserving former orientations. Skilled singers can hold three or four landscapes in counterpoint: the present one, the one before the last turning, the expected shape after the next, and the remembered down of the people who first composed the route.

They sing without sustained vowels. Long tones carry poorly in the absorbent vegetation and can be confused with the groans of growth. Instead their music is made of clicks, breath pulses, tongue taps, and short nasal notes. Distance is encoded in repetition. Slope is indicated by pitch. Water is represented by swallowed sounds that resonate in the throat. A song performed accurately is a map, a weather record, and a set of instructions for how frightened one should be.

Children learn geography by walking it tethered to adults. At each landmark they are turned physically toward the relevant direction. Thus memory enters the muscles. An adult who has forgotten a verse may recover it by assuming the stance in which it was learned.

Their languages possess no single neutral word for down. Falling-down is the pull on a loose object. Root-down is the direction in which the local vascular structure considers itself anchored. Rain-down is the path droplets currently take. Birth-down is the bodily certainty established during infancy. Hearth-down is the orientation in which the family coal is stored. After a major turning, these may point five different ways.

This produces gestures that outsiders find disconcerting. People indicate ancestry by pointing toward birth-down, even if that direction is now overhead. They bury nothing, but when speaking of the dead they lower a hand toward remembered down. A child born during a turning may have an uncertain birth-down and be regarded as unusually adaptable, or dangerously unmoored.

Death itself is not understood as departure. It is called **becoming held**.

The dead cannot safely be left on living ground. Decomposition is slow, but warmth, salts, and bodily fluids may attract exploratory roots. Nor are corpses burned; the fuel cost would be intolerable, and the released heat might wake the surrounding tissue. Instead bodies are dried in cold airflow, wrapped in porous fiber, and placed in abandoned husks, hanging vine nets, or old branches judged unlikely to be reclaimed soon.

These resting places accumulate. Some are vast ossuary gardens suspended across chasms: generations of pale bundles caught among seed shells, broken bridges, sailback bones, and obsolete houses. Since nothing falls quickly, ancestors remain visible. When a landscape turns, an ossuary may drift overhead, and families can look up at the dead crossing the white radiance like a constellation.

A person’s final gift is usually their body water. Before the wrapping, fluids are drawn off through thorn tubes, filtered through pith, and poured into the settlement’s sealed reserve. This is not regarded as consumption of the dead but as fulfillment of their remaining water debt. Refusing the rite is rare and grave. It means one wishes to leave the community thirsty.

The living eat cautiously.

Their staple foods are not the gigantic structural plants themselves, which are often toxic, reactive, or too precious to wound. They cultivate films of yeast and cold-tolerant algae in translucent bladders attached near luminous stems. They harvest shed pollen, dormant spores, sweet frost, and the thin flakes of callus that plants naturally slough from healed injuries. Certain cup flowers can be persuaded to exude a starchy gel if stroked in repeating patterns. Entire communities may spend hours performing these feeding gestures, kneeling in rings around a flower and moving their hands in silence.

Meat is eaten when it can be obtained, but hunting the white browsers is restricted. A file of browsers often travels the same condensation routes as people, and killing too many can erase the path by allowing frost to accumulate over the markings their clamps leave behind. Hunters take the old, the limping, or those that fold too slowly during alarm. The animals are never chased toward thin ground. Their dorsal water sacs are removed intact and carried home like glass vessels.

Sailbacks are almost never hunted. Their long absences make them messengers whether they intend to be or not. Pollen caught in their skin reveals distant blooms; scars reveal thorn borders; parasites may prove that a far-off ecology has grafted to a nearer one. When a sailback descends, people lure it with vibration and spread cloth beneath it to collect whatever falls from its hide. Some settlements fit tame individuals with hollow beads whose clicking patterns identify their place of origin. Years later, a bead may answer in a province beyond any known route.

The oldest sailbacks become enormous. Their rib-sails scar and mend until they resemble layered parchment. Small plants root in the seams of their backs. Colonies of mites graze there, followed by threadlike predators and pearl-bodied scavengers. Such an animal carries a traveling ecosystem through the open brilliance. People call these ancient ones **far gardens**, and their arrival can feed a settlement with information for a generation.

There are other creatures, though many are difficult to classify.

In vascular channels live translucent swimmers shaped like hands. They have no mouths. They press themselves over wounds and absorb the concentrated chemical signals released by injury. Most are harmless, but a great mass of them can keep a wound from closing, producing a continuous sap spring. Settlers sometimes cultivate these “palm shoals” at carefully chosen scars, harvesting both sap and the shed membranes of the animals. If the shoal grows too large, the host plant notices. The channel contracts, and everything inside is pulped.

Beneath loose bark move ribbon-bodied grazers hundreds of meters long. Only small segments are ever visible at once, leading travelers to mistake them for many animals. They eat fungal films and mineral crusts. When alarmed, the whole ribbon contracts, and distant sections can snap bridges or overturn houses. Route-singers mark their territories by the regular pattern of shallow buckles they leave in the substrate.

There are frost moths whose wings are made not of tissue but of branching ice held on warm filaments. They hatch during brief updrafts, grow their wings from ambient vapor, mate in the pollen clouds, and die when the air warms enough to melt them. Their naked bodies then fall as black threads. Children catch the meltwater in their mouths, though adults forbid this near unknown blooms.

There are also the hushes.

No one agrees whether hushes are animals, mobile fungal colonies, or defensive organs detached from some larger plant. They appear as low mats of colorless filaments moving against the direction of local growth. Wherever they pass, vibration diminishes. Fibers stop creaking. Footfalls disappear entirely. Even deep hearing becomes impossible. The silence around them is not merely acoustic; people report loss of balance, numbness in the teeth, and inability to remember route rhythms.

Hushes gather around sites of repeated wounding. They cover abandoned quarries, battle grounds, and villages that tapped too deeply into water tissue. Some believe they are the Underbloom’s scar tissue. Others say they feed on alarm signals and follow people because people are always injuring something.

No sensible traveler sleeps near one.

The people’s relationship with the immense vegetation is neither worship nor stewardship in any simple sense. They know the land is alive, but life alone does not make it a person. A leaf-plain does not bargain, forgive, or care. Yet neither is it inert. It responds to injury, remembers patterns of pressure, alters itself around long habitation, and sometimes produces structures that seem uncannily suited to those living upon it.

A village that remains for generations may coax up sheltering ribs and water-shedding folds. The plant may thicken under commonly used paths. It may grow bitter nodules around latrine sites, sealing them away. These adaptations can be read as cooperation. But when the same village becomes too warm or cuts too often, defensive hairs emerge in houses, bridges are engulfed, and vascular channels close. This can be read as anger.

Most people avoid both interpretations. They say the plant has **noticed** them. Being noticed may be beneficial or fatal.

Gardeners are therefore among the most powerful members of a settlement. Their craft combines chemistry, touch, patience, and risk. They carry cases of salts, ashes, pollens, resins, chilled molds, and small living cultures. With these they redirect buds, numb defensive tissues, encourage callus growth, and negotiate grafts between species. They work slowly. A wall might take twelve years to persuade into the desired curve. A cistern may require three generations of selective irritation before a plant forms a bladder in the correct place.

The greatest gardeners do not impose shapes. They arrange desires. Light is screened here, reflected there; moisture is withheld from one side and condensed on another; a harmless parasite is introduced to provoke thickening; a line of warm ceramic beads encourages a vein to turn. The eventual structure appears to have grown by accident.

Failures are often spectacular.

A badly encouraged roof may close around its occupants. A water bladder can become a digestive chamber. Two grafted supports may reject one another years after seeming stable, covering a settlement in caustic sap. For this reason every cultivated dwelling contains deliberate weaknesses—panels that tear, loops that slide free, floors designed to peel from their supports. The finest house is not the one that never fails. It is the one that fails slowly enough for everyone to escape.

Leadership belongs, nominally, to whoever keeps the release cords.

During ordinary times decisions are made by households, water-keepers, gardeners, and route-singers in shifting combinations. During upheaval, one person is entrusted with the authority to detach the settlement: cut bridge anchors, open stored bladders, abandon grafts, and order the coal moved. This person is called the Loosener.

The Loosener is chosen for a willingness to destroy what others hesitate to lose. It is an honored and lonely role. If they act too late, people die. If they act early, years of labor are wasted, and the settlement may accuse them of cowardice. Many former Looseners become rain chasers, unwilling to remain among those whose homes they once cut free.

When a turning begins, society changes shape immediately. Sleeping platforms are dismantled. Water is divided into body loads. Children and elders are fitted into seed-cradles: layered capsules modeled after the folding browsers. Households tie themselves into traveling chains but attach each member with a breakaway link. The coal pod is wrapped in wet membrane and carried nearest the center.

Then everyone listens.

The earliest warning may be a slow migration of loose frost. Cups begin leaning. Suspended dead turn in their nets. The local glow changes as the plain’s surface faces a different portion of the ceiling. No one waits for certainty. Certainty arrives as the roar.

Some settlements ride a turning by shifting across the rising surface. Others deliberately slide, steering sleds along veins toward a predicted fold. A few cast themselves into the void beneath sailcloth, trusting convection and luck. Those who survive may land on a surface previously overhead and find ancient structures now made habitable: inverted towers, ossuary branches, kiln arches protruding from bark, paths cultivated by people whose birth-down pointed in the opposite direction.

Such meetings have produced the Underbloom’s strangest communities.

There are villages suspended between two leaves that face one another across a narrow gulf. Each half believes the other lives upside down. Bridges sag according to falling-down, while doors and furniture align with root-down. Rain passes first through one settlement, then across the gap into the other. Marriages require elaborate negotiation over where the household coal should rest. Children born on the bridges are celebrated as belonging to both downs.

There are peoples who live permanently upon migrating needle groves. They graft platforms among the upper branches and let the forest carry them away from danger. Their calendars are measured in communal steps. They speak of stationary settlements with pity, calling them “the already caught.”

There are channel dwellers who inhabit the hollow interiors of giant vascular tubes. Their world is warm, dark, and loud with flowing sap. They navigate by pressure rather than light and seal their skin with protective wax. They emerge onto the outer plains only during cold intervals, blinking beneath the white radiance. Other peoples distrust them because their tools are designed to pierce from within.

And there are rain chasers who refuse any structure they cannot drag. Their sleds are broad, flexible, and almost weightless, woven from sailback molt and spiral creeper. They read the ceiling’s brightness through tinted lenses of resin. A darkening halo, a subtle change in the taste of static, or the curling of frost hairs can send an entire band racing toward a place where rain will fall days later.

Rain chasers arrive before water and leave before abundance becomes violence. Between those events they live with astonishing intensity. Cloth cones bloom across the plain. Every vessel fills. People wash fully, sometimes for the first time in years. Children slide in runoff channels. Courtships, feasts, trades, and trials occur while the rain drums too loudly for the ground to be heard.

Then the shoots begin.

The chasers pack at the first bulge beneath the surface. By the time roots sweep across the plain, they are already pale figures on the horizon, towing their swollen water sacks behind them.

Conflict in the Underbloom is rarely fought for land, which cannot be trusted, and seldom for food, which spoils slowly and travels poorly. People fight over water rights, route knowledge, kiln access, and the custody of coals. Yet open battle is dangerous. Blood and heat excite the vegetation. Deep punctures may summon constriction. A struggle can awaken the ground beneath both sides.

Their weapons are therefore designed as much to control movement as to kill. Resin blades are broad and brittle, inflicting shallow cold cuts before breaking. Thorn casts entangle clothing. Powdered salts provoke local hairs to curl around ankles. Vibration drums can imitate the tremors of growth, frightening enemies into abandoning a position. The most feared weapon is a sealed heat-pot. Broken on living ground, it may trigger explosive growth, hatch dormant spores, or call down a defensive response. Its use is condemned by nearly every water law, because no one can predict where the resulting damage will stop.

Warriors are less admired than evacuators: people trained to enter tightening structures and cut others free. They carry many small blades because each may be lost after one stroke. Their arms are covered in scar bands, not from enemies but from the embrace of closing plants.

Scars have social meaning. A long pale compression mark shows that one has been caught and released. Tiny round scars identify channel workers. Blue branching stains come from electrical pollen storms and are considered beautiful. Burns are rare, shocking, and often hidden. To survive flame is to have been touched by something almost no living thing below can tolerate.

The keepers of household coals bear such scars most often.

A coal is housed in nested ceramic chambers separated by insulating ash and damp fiber. It is not kept continuously hot. The keeper wakes it by admitting measured air, coaxes enough heat for resin work, surgery, childbirth, or medicine, and then quiets it again. The skill is hereditary only because it requires decades of practice. A keeper learns to judge heat by smell and by the color of nearby frost.

Coal lineages are often older than settlements. The ember itself may have been divided and carried through dozens of turnings. Families remember not where it was first kindled, but which losses it survived. They name coals accordingly: After-the-Fifth-Rain, Child-Carried, Beneath-the-Falling-Grove, Last-of-Seven.

To share fire is more intimate than sharing food. Guests place their hands around the closed pod and feel the faint warmth through the ceramic. Oaths made there are called warm promises. Breaking one is believed to make a person detectable to roots.

Their religions, where the word applies, center not on gods but on scales of attention.

The smallest attention belongs to frost, pores, tendrils, mites, and individual thirsts. The middle attention belongs to villages, groves, migrating animals, and weather. The great attention belongs to the continent-sized organisms whose motions define generations. Beyond that, some propose a single buried life of which every leaf, root, person, and sailback is a temporary organ.

This idea is comforting to some and horrifying to others.

If all the Underbloom is one organism, then wounds, wars, migrations, and settlements may be internal processes: inflammation, memory, digestion, dreaming. The people may be symbionts—or a disease. Hushes may be immune cells. Turnings may be reflexes. The unreachable stone roof may be a shell, and the shining ceiling leaves may not be leaves at all but inward-facing organs feeding an embryo.

Pilgrims called the Inward travel toward regions where newer growth emerges. They seek the thin green lines that bridge empty brightness and camp beside them for years, recording each thickening. They believe first growth carries the clearest expression of the whole being’s intent. Some press their bodies against the forming tissue until it grows around them. Centuries later, their preserved silhouettes can be found inside translucent bridges, arms spread, faces turned toward whatever direction light once came from.

Others travel downward through old mats, convinced truth lies in earlier layers. They cut as little as possible, descending through natural wounds and cavities. Their reports speak of buried horizons, upside-down forests, frozen storms of pollen, and peoples who regard the known Underbloom as a remote ceiling.

Rarely, an excavator returns carrying stone.

Such fragments are angular, cold, and astonishingly heavy. They have no grain, no pulse, no response to salt or heat. They do not heal. Children are often frightened by them. Adults may sit for hours passing one from hand to hand, contemplating matter that neither grows nor releases.

Stone is used only for objects meant to outlast orientation: treaty weights, witness tokens, and markers for the worst wounds. A stone laid beside a damaged place means, **Do not let this be forgotten when everything around it changes.**

But even stone disappears eventually. Tissue grows over it. A root lifts it. A turning casts it into a gulf. Permanence in the Underbloom is not endurance of material. It is successful transmission.

So the people transmit themselves.

They send route songs across migrating groves. They entrust beads to sailbacks. They graft stories into living walls by alternating bitter and sweet bands of tissue. They teach children how a settlement sounded before it tilted, how water tasted in a flower now closed, how to recognize the tremor of a leaf they may never visit. They preserve old downs in posture and melody.

They know that no dwelling will remain where it was built, no river will keep its bed, no horizon is finished, and no direction deserves complete trust.

What lives in the Underbloom lives provisionally.

It listens through its feet. It drinks without waste. It warms nothing carelessly. It fastens every cherished thing with a cord that can be cut.

And when the vast young world moves beneath it, it moves too.
