The Sump is inhabited less by separate species than by an entire living process: a wet, slow intelligence distributed among animals, machines, mineral growth, and dark water. Nothing in it is wholly wild or wholly manufactured. Its creatures use valves as river mouths, transformers as breeding pools, and buried currents as sunlight. Its machinery behaves like geology. Its geology arrives prefabricated.

Nearest the surface are the innumerable small lives. Fish occupy tire tracks as though they were ancestral waterways. Eels braid themselves around cables, warmed by their intermittent charge, and may remain knotted there long after the lines have gone dead. Pale crabs hold their claws aloft to read tremors passing through stone. Beetles plate the undersides of discarded metal so completely that lifting a sheet seems to expose a second, jointed surface. Translucent snails eat lettering from control housings with remarkable discrimination: logos, serial numbers, and manufacturers disappear beneath them, while warnings remain untouched. Whatever else the Sump forgets, it preserves instructions.

The moths are among its most numerous inhabitants. Heavy with moisture, they fly slowly and settle in such numbers that towers and sheds acquire a temporary pelt. Each moth supports mites; each mite bears a cold blue point. Seen close, the lights resemble a drifting constellation. Seen from farther away, they vanish into the depth of the dark. During the lightless morning, the moths arrange themselves head-down on vertical surfaces, aligned not to gravity but to the direction in which condensation will next begin to climb.

There are frogs in transformer trays, lizards against conduits, herons beside seasonal valves. The herons can wait for months without changing posture. They know which wheels open and which only turn, which pipes carry clear water and which discharge reflections. Some have mineral cuffs around their legs from standing too long in accreting runoff. When a valve finally releases, they strike before the emerging fish have finished becoming present.

Around the extraction wells, life gathers densely. Animals that elsewhere hunt one another stand pressed flank to flank at the hot margins: birds, lizards, crabs, soft-bodied things without stable symmetry. They are not peaceful. They are listening downward. Each pressure pulse draws their heads toward the same buried point. When feathers or saltwater plankton come up in the drill slurry, the animals sort them before the machinery can.

Larger inhabitants rarely permit themselves to be seen whole. They breathe through culverts and travel behind reed beds, displacing water in long, deliberate bulges. Their bodies may be too broad for the channels that contain them, but width is unreliable in wet places. Some appear to consist chiefly of pressure: teeth ache, insects fall silent, and shallow water lowers without draining. Then something has passed.

The silent ground birds belong to these larger presences, or perhaps attend them. Hundreds cross together in exact formation, each stepping into the footprint ahead. No wingbeat or call accompanies them. When they halt, every bird becomes still at once, including those hidden beyond sight. Counting them is difficult. Counting twice is dangerous, because the second total is often larger, and the additional birds are usually standing nearer.

The solitary trees contain another population. Dry fluttering moves between crowns that the ground cannot see. What lives above may never descend the trunks; shed skins and narrow eggs occasionally arrive in the mud, but always already soaked. The bare circles around each tree are not caused by shade. Small animals entering them turn aside as though remembering an obstruction. Pipes laid through the circles emerge polished, warm, and several years older.

People also live in the Sump, though seldom in the ordinary continuous sense.

There are maintenance crews. Their repairs are everywhere, layered one over another across centuries of hardware that remains newly made. A gasket of unknown polymer sits beneath a hand-cut leather seal. Fresh welds cross plates whose replacement dates have not yet occurred. Tool cabinets are inventoried in several handwritings, some belonging to workers not born when the list was signed. The crews travel in pairs when possible. Whether both members always belong to the same date is uncertain.

They wear rubberized coats, high boots, respirators, and lamps that reveal surfaces but do not cast useful light into the air. They navigate by touch, pipe temperature, insect posture, and memorized sequences of relays. They know not to pursue the central works. They approach only particular components: Pump 6, the eastern separator, a valve field that is west of any observer, the conveyor return whose belt advances while unattended. Their task is not to understand the system. Their task is to keep each local failure from becoming a general one.

Most are never encountered face to face. One hears a wrench set down in a shed that proves empty, sees a coat shifting on a hook with water still dripping from its hem, or finds boot prints continuing along the underside of a pool. Telephones carry their voices badly. A caller may be only a few meters away, or may be speaking from the following night. Experienced workers never give their names over these lines. Names are among the things the snails eat, and there is no reason to help them.

Some workers have adapted too well. They remain near warm machinery for entire cycles, sleeping upright against cabinets and waking when the current changes. Condensation travels upward over their coats. Moths settle on them without distinction. Their skin takes on the smooth pallor of wet aggregate, and their voices become as muted as impacts in the dark. They continue performing inspections. They may be the most reliable inhabitants of the basin, though they no longer leave it.

Others inhabit the houses that are still emerging. Warm water fills the upper rooms, and silhouettes sometimes cross behind submerged windows. Chimney smoke is occasionally visible beneath the water’s surface, descending into roofs rather than rising. Until doors appear, no one can determine whether the occupants are trapped inside, waiting to be built, or living there quite normally by rules that do not yet extend outdoors.

Beneath and among all these lives is the industrial organism itself.

The pumps are its slow hearts. The braided channels are its capillaries. Rails grow like nerves toward a brain that remains under construction, while conveyors carry wet mineral as patiently as ants carrying brood. Control boxes signal to one another with red points that exist only at close range. Tanks inhale through pressure valves. Cooling fins gather colonies. The central refinery recedes because it is not a destination but an organizing organ: every part of the Sump grows toward it, and therefore no traveler can arrive before the landscape does.

What the wells extract may be the Sump’s food, memory, or offspring. It contains matter from sea, furnace, sky, and animal, all compressed together under impossible pressure. The separators grade these fragments without obvious criteria. Useful material travels inward. Clear warm wastewater returns outward carrying blind larvae. In this way the works seed the basin with its own inhabitants.

The most pervasive resident is the darkness. It is not passive and does not behave like empty space. It muffles labor, preserves echoes, and limits the distance at which signals are allowed to exist. It may be the medium in which the whole system lives, as water is the medium of fish. Machines disappear into it without becoming far away. Animals use its varying pressure to coordinate. Reflections age within it. Sounds descend, remain there, and later surface elsewhere.

Perhaps the Sump itself is one organism in an early stage of growth. Its bare ridges are new bone. Its berms and rails are forming structure. The isolated trees are sensory organs. The unreachable works are a body plan not yet completed. The maintenance crews may be cells correcting errors in its development, while the larger unseen animals are older structures being absorbed or displaced.

Or perhaps the basin is a habitat built by its future inhabitants and maintained backward through time. The dated stakes, predrilled foundations, rising houses, and machinery reflected before it exists all suggest a population arriving in reverse: first its waste, then its tools, then its dwellings, and only eventually the beings themselves.

For now, what lives there is everything that can endure warmth without daylight, water without rain, and labor without completion. It is a crowded country of patient feeders, hidden bodies, amphibious workers, self-tending mechanisms, and forms not yet fully present. They wait beside valves, beneath roads, inside reflections, and ahead in time.

None of them are idle. Even at their stillest, they are helping the Sump become more completely what it already is.
