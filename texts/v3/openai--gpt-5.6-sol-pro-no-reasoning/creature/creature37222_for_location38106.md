The people of the plateau are slight, broad-footed, and stronger than their spare frames suggest. They do not carry themselves upright unless the air is still. Knees remain loose, shoulders lowered, one hand often resting on stone. Even children learn to yield to a tremor rather than oppose it. A stranger might mistake their posture for timidity. It is, in fact, an intimacy with instability.

They call themselves by many local names, none intended to travel far. A person belongs first to a particular crown of trees, seep, ledge, or sheltered hollow. Beyond that, they identify themselves through signs: a hooked finger for the western fissures, a palm turned downward for the black pools, two knuckles tapped together for the domes beneath Split Rock. Spoken names are private things, used at touching distance. Public names are gestures that can survive the wind.

Their skin is weathered dark where it remains exposed, but much of the body is wrapped in fitted layers of bark cloth, softened hide, and woven fiber. Loose clothing can snap hard enough to bruise, catch an updraft, or begin vibrating against the body until it numbs the flesh. Sleeves bind at the wrist. Scarves cover the mouth against mineral dust. Their ear-caps are the most individual possessions they own: lacquered wooden shells lined with wax, hair, moth silk, or dried moss. Some are plain and work-worn; others bear fine incisions describing family routes and remembered storms.

They cut their hair short or braid it tightly against the skull. Ornaments are soft—colored cords, feathers glued flat, polished seeds sewn close to the collar. Nothing is permitted to dangle. Metal jewelry is considered both foolish and cruel, because it rings.

Their feet are wrapped in layered soles thin enough to transmit the language of the ground. Thick footwear would protect against hot stone but blind them to deeper danger. Travelers stop often, planting their long staffs and closing their eyes. They distinguish the flutter of a shallow cavity from the slow bodily pressure of a moving wall. They know when a sound travels through air and when it arrives through bone. The most gifted path-readers can put their teeth against a staff and perceive disturbances days away.

Such people are called listeners, though many have damaged hearing. By middle age nearly everyone has lost certain pitches. Elders inhabit an altered world: some hear only thunderous bass; others live inside a constant thin ringing; a few become completely deaf and are revered for the calm precision with which they read faces, pools, roots, and stone. Deafness is not regarded as silence. Silence is an external condition and a dangerous one. Deafness is merely another way of receiving the country.

A household is seldom more than six or eight people. Too many bodies require too much water and conceal too many vibrations. Kinship follows the underground trees more readily than blood. Those nourished by the same root-grove consider themselves related, even when their shelters lie on opposite sides of a canyon. A shared tree may carry rain from one household to another, and this exchange is treated as both biological fact and moral obligation. To poison a root or cut it without consent is among the few acts for which exile is certain.

Their shelters appear crude from outside: stone crouched against stone, roofed with living roots and low mats. Within, they are intricate. Floors float on braided pads so that sudden shocks do not pass directly into sleeping bodies. Storage jars rest in nets rather than on shelves. Walls curve and are intentionally incomplete. Every house has several small openings plugged with weighted bundles of grass; by watching these twitch, occupants can read pressure changes even while asleep.

There is no central hearth. Fire warms stone too unevenly and can split a shelter apart. Food is cooked in shallow pits using pebbles heated outside, then carried in with forked tools. Their meals are soft and damp: root pulp, steamed leaf-crowns, moth paste, translucent lizards, fermented sap, and the pale flesh of creatures taken from seep-pools after thunder. Water is never swallowed thoughtlessly. The first mouthful from any basin is held, tasted, and spat upon the rock. A metallic bitterness may mean a new vein has opened below; unusual warmth may precede a collapse.

Most necessities come from plants. Bowl-leaves are trained above channels and tethered so that their noonward tipping empties into covered cisterns. Hollow reeds become pipes, splints, needles, whistles used only in sheltered spaces, and pressure gauges. Root fiber forms bridges, cord, clothing, and the flexible ribs of dwellings. Resin seals water basins and deadens tools. Moss is bedding, filter, bandage, insulation, and ear padding.

Nothing harvested is treated as inert. Wood continues to bend. Fiber remembers moisture. Resin creeps in heat. Bone changes tone before it breaks. A craftsperson’s skill lies not in forcing materials into permanent shapes but in arranging how they will move.

Their finest objects are therefore difficult to recognize as masterpieces. A bridge woven to sag in exactly the right rhythm may look half ruined. A roof may seem badly fitted until a pressure wave passes through without lifting it. A water vessel may be deliberately asymmetrical so it rolls in a tight circle rather than escaping over a cliff. Perfection is measured by survival through change.

Children are not warned away from edges in the manner of lowland peoples. They are taught edges almost as soon as they can crawl. On safe ledges, adults let them approach the drop on their bellies, feel the rising air, and toss bits of leaf into it. They learn which currents return and which descend, which hollows amplify a cry, and where a shout can loosen stone. Their earliest games are silent. They exchange elaborate insults by hand, race droplets through carved channels, and place their cheeks against the ground to guess who is approaching.

Speech is reserved for shelter, ceremony, and tenderness. Consequently, voices remain soft. The local languages contain many words that cannot be used intelligibly in the open: breathy distinctions, tiny catches of the throat, consonants made against the teeth. People speak with their foreheads or cheekbones touching so that part of the voice travels through the skull. To speak intimately is literally to share vibration.

At a distance they use a broad sign language shaped for visibility against rock and mist. Colored cords add tense, direction, and urgency. A red knot may warn of lightning; three pale loops announce safe water; black crossed strands mean that a known route has changed. Travelers leave cords stretched low across paths, where wind cannot easily invent false messages. Because dyes fade at different rates, an experienced reader can judge how long a warning has stood.

They do not sing into the air. Singing belongs to the body. Groups sit shoulder to shoulder in a sheltered chamber and hum with closed mouths, matching tones through ribs and jaws. The sound is felt more than heard. A skilled gathering can make a bowl of water tremble into geometric figures. Songs are remembered as sequences of pressure: chest, throat, palate, brow. They can be taught even to those born deaf.

Their histories are kept the same way. A lineage is not recited but tapped along the arms and spine. Journeys are patterns drummed through a staff laid across many knees. Great collapses are remembered as intervals of stillness followed by a blow. Knowledge changes less in transmission than outsiders might expect, because an incorrect rhythm feels wrong to the whole group at once.

They have no kings. Authority moves according to circumstance. A keeper of roots decides when a grove may be cut. A path-reader leads across open stone. A pool-tender controls access during drought. During storms, command belongs to whoever best understands the nearby vents. Once the danger passes, so does the authority. Anyone who tries to retain it is said to have “become rigid,” a grave accusation implying that the person will endanger everyone merely to preserve their shape.

Disputes are settled in flexing circles. The parties sit around a shallow water basin and state their claims quietly, touching forehead to forehead with each witness in turn. Between statements, all watch the water. If the plateau begins to tremble, discussion stops. This may happen repeatedly over days. Patience is considered evidence of truthfulness; a lie, they say, always demands to be finished.

Their dead are not buried in soil, for there is little soil to spare, nor cast into the chasms, where a body might foul hidden water. The corpse is wrapped in a root net and placed beneath an overhang where condensation falls. There it dries slowly while moss and pale insects take it apart. The cleaned bones are sounded one by one. Those that ring clearly become tools or teaching pieces. Those with dull or troubled tones are ground and pressed into cracks around the family grove.

The skull is never sounded. It is set in a quiet niche facing stone.

Birth is managed with similar caution. Pregnant people avoid strongly resonant valleys in the final months, and labor takes place in padded pits near a silence boundary but never across it. The newborn is placed briefly against bare rock. If the child quiets, it is said to be listening. If it cries, it is said to be answering. Neither omen is preferred.

Their faith, if it can be called that, is not directed toward gods above. The sky brings water and lightning but has little memory. What endures lies beneath.

They believe the plateau contains an immeasurable interior—not simply caves, but chambers within chambers, old riverways descending beyond stone, and pressures moving like thought. Some say the country is the shell of a dead being. Others insist it is living but so vast that centuries are only twitches in its sleep. A third teaching holds that life and death are distinctions meaningful only to small bodies, and that the plateau exceeds both.

No doctrine is enforced. What matters is conduct: do not seal a breathing fissure; do not deepen a tone you do not understand; do not remain in a place after its voice has changed; never mistake quiet for safety.

The drifting zones of cancellation inspire the greatest unease. People call them hollows-of-no-return, not because those who enter fail to emerge, but because they often return altered. After long exposure, some begin anticipating gestures before they are made. Others wake with mud between their toes despite having slept on dry stone. A few speak sentences that companions remember hearing only days later.

These individuals are not treated as prophets. Prophecy is considered a kind of hazardous echo. They are cared for, observed, and discouraged from making important decisions. When their statements prove true, no one celebrates. The cords recording such events are knotted in gray and stored where children cannot find them.

There are people who choose the silent places nonetheless.

They live alone near the moving boundaries, shifting their shelters yard by yard as the mute zone drifts. Their ear-caps are enormous, although there is nothing there to block. They map the silence by laying out rings of seeds and observing which ones tremble. Once or twice a year they meet ordinary households to exchange maps scratched onto sheets of bark. These maps show no cliffs, pools, or paths—only wandering shapes, like weather systems passing through the stone.

Some believe the silent-dwellers are tracking something below. Others believe they are preventing it from tracking everyone else.

The plateau’s largest animals are the gliders, and they too are woven into human life without ever being domesticated. Up close, a glider is stranger than its silhouette suggests: four long limbs radiating from a narrow furred body, each joined by warm, veined membranes. Its head is blunt, almost eyeless, with a crown of delicate pits that read pressure. It steers not by sight but by the architecture of sound. When valleys change their tones, migration routes change with them.

People watch the gliders to predict atmospheric upheaval. A tight circling flight means an updraft is strengthening. Folded rear limbs suggest rain. Sudden landings are deeply alarming. The animals almost never touch the plateau except to bear young or escape a disturbance in the air. If many come down at once, households abandon exposed ground without waiting to know why.

Their cast-off membrane is a precious material. Found snagged on shrubs, it can be cured into translucent sheets that vibrate at approaching thunder. Killing a glider is taboo—not because the animals are sacred, but because their bodies belong to distances humans cannot safely inspect.

Trade occurs slowly, summit to summit. There are no caravans. Goods pass through chains of households, sometimes taking years to cross the country. Copper from one canyon travels as small blunt ingots wrapped in moss. Shell arrives from lands no living trader has seen. Dyes, wax, medicinal resins, reliable seeds, and pieces of old bridgework circulate alongside messages.

A gift is never expected to reach a named individual. Routes fail. Groves die. Hollows collapse. Instead it is sent toward a place: west of the drumming wall, beyond the pool that reflects daylight stars, to those who tend the blue-flowering slope. If it arrives, whoever receives it answers when possible. This has produced a culture of obligation without certainty. Gratitude may return to one’s grandchildren from strangers repaying a kindness shown generations before.

Ruins indicate that the plateau was once more populous. There are rows of anchor holes broad enough to have held bridges across mile-deep valleys. Beneath moss lie gutters too extensive for any household. Certain circular sinkholes contain terraces, now tilted and split, where hundreds might have gathered.

The present inhabitants avoid these places during wind reversals. The ruins resonate too well.

Some old chambers contain wooden masks taller than a person, constructed in layers and pierced with complex internal passages. When air crosses them, they produce voices. Most have split or sagged into silence, but a few still speak during particular storms. The words are in no language now known. Listeners disagree whether they are warnings, names, or merely wind made accidentally articulate.

No one repairs them.

To repair an ancient voice without understanding its purpose would be the purest form of rigidity: deciding that because something once spoke, it must continue speaking.

So the plateau’s people remain scattered, mobile within narrow territories, rich in knowledge and poor in possessions. They cultivate groves that may outlive their descendants. They harvest rain one leaf at a time. They cross abysses on bridges designed to dance. They trust the tremor beneath a staff more than the evidence of their eyes.

At dusk they retreat into low shelters while the gardens release their water and the chasms begin their nightly answer. Ear-caps are loosened but not removed. Children press their hands to the floor. Adults watch hanging cords turn and shiver in the changing pressure.

Then, beneath the roar, each household finds its own small rhythm.

They touch shoulders. They hum into one another’s bones. They wait for the ancient country to settle into whatever shape it will permit until morning.
