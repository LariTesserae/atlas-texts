The city is inhabited by a sparse, patient people who do not claim to have built it.

They call themselves human when distinction is unnecessary. Their bodies preserve the familiar plan—two hands, two eyes, upright gait—but the basin has revised them subtly. They are long-limbed and lightly made, suited to heat that never quite leaves the stone. Their skin bears faint branching marks where moisture gathers first, so that before rain they seem traced with darker veins. The youngest sometimes have reflective pupils like the dusk insects; the oldest cast shadows that lag by half a breath.

They live in small, discontinuous communities: several inhabited floors in an otherwise empty tower, a family terrace beside kilometers of vacant infrastructure, a market clustered beneath a transit span. Population is counted in lit windows, not names. It changes according to weather. After the reservoir deepens, censuses often find extra households whose members possess keys, debts, kinships, and memories no one recalls granting them. Conversely, a familiar apartment may remain furnished and warm while everyone gradually forgets who occupied it.

The people regard this not with superstition but with civic caution. They keep records in several incompatible forms—ink, knotted root, mineral scratches, remembered songs—because no single medium remains trustworthy through every atmospheric event. Important agreements are witnessed by both a person and a plant. Children learn early that continuity is something maintained, not assumed.

No one wastes water, and no one owns it. Dawn condensation is gathered communally from leaves, cables, walls, and street basins, then distributed according to need. Drinking vessels are porous, open, or deliberately flawed. A sealed jar is treated as a dangerous animal: carried gently, never shaken, and surrendered to municipal keepers before its internal tide grows strong enough to influence doors.

Their occupations are adaptations to the city’s moods.

There are shadow clerks, who measure the movement of buildings and order slight rotations, temporary screens, or alterations of height so that no structure keeps the same shadow too long. Door stewards mark trustworthy thresholds with braided fibers and sit beside newly formed seams until their destination stabilizes. Weather listeners sleep with their palms on floors, interpreting distant rain from changes in buried resonance. Garden negotiators mediate between roads and migrating groves. Surveyors map not only position but accessibility, since two adjacent chambers may be farther apart than districts at opposite ends of the basin.

The most respected are the returners. When an elevator begins opening into an obsolete district, or a warm-wind door reveals another landscape, returners cross the threshold with open vessels, growing cuttings, and cords tied to witnesses outside. They do not always come back. Those who do may return within minutes wearing years of age, or after years unchanged, carrying unfamiliar seeds and news of streets that have not yet appeared.

Their technology is intimate rather than commanding. Tools are coaxed awake by heat, breath, pressure, or living contact. A household may possess a ceramic membrane that cools food by dreaming of rain, a wall niche that produces one necessary object per season, or a strip of dark material that stiffens into a bridge when held between shadows. Repair is less a matter of replacing parts than restoring relationships among materials. Machinery is spoken to, not because it is necessarily conscious, but because courtesy makes its behavior more predictable.

Many residents wear loose garments woven from mineral fibers and cultivated films. In dry weather the fabric hangs dull and pale. Before rain it darkens, opens microscopic vents, and clings at the wrists. Jewelry commonly includes drops of unsealed water suspended in loops that never quite close. These drops tilt toward the central reservoir before the condensation events begin.

Children range farther than adults. They crawl through half-formed doors, follow silver insects across warm plazas, and can sometimes see which shadows will disappear. They play games on the temporary channels, assigning each branch a district that might arrive during the next deepening. Their cautions are practical: never step where pavement has begun to bloom; never answer a voice from beneath a dry basin; if an elevator makes you feel larger, keep your hands close to your body.

The oldest inhabitants spend much of their time in condensation gardens. Soft-bodied grazers gather around them without fear. Their memories are valued but treated as weather-sensitive. Some remember the first towers descending from an empty sky. Others insist the city was once fully populated and became sparse only after the reservoir misplaced most of its people. A few claim to remember living in the inverted neighborhoods above the reeds, beneath a brighter sun.

Not all the inhabitants are human.

The groves possess slow forms of intention. They relocate toward favorable ground, shelter delicate systems, and occasionally arrange themselves around a building that has not yet appeared. A grove may adopt a household, extending roots beneath its rooms and producing leaves shaped to collect exactly the amount of water its occupants require. If neglected, it withdraws over several seasons, leaving the dwelling hotter and acoustically empty.

The red canes are communal weather organs, but they also retain traces of the air that passes through them. Skilled listeners can hear old humidity in their songs: storms from vanished years, breath from extinct animals, voices spoken in courtyards before those courtyards existed. No one cuts a singing cane. Fallen stems are made into flutes whose notes sometimes open distant doors.

The green membranes that cover abandoned transit lanes are seasonal intelligences, or fragments of one. They spread after rain, sample heat and mineral content, and roll away carrying what they have learned. Their destination is unknown. Children sometimes leave geometric questions scratched in the pavement before a storm. On rare occasions the membranes arrange their pores into answers before departing.

The silver insects feed on residual warmth and possibly on attention. Their temporary wings are made from whatever distinction separates surface from air. They gather around newly arrived structures, tracing their edges as if inspecting workmanship. When one lands on skin, it leaves a cool spot and a fleeting memory of flight over a city much denser than the present one.

The tall birds are believed to move between versions of the basin. Their low calls travel through solid matter and cause old foundations to answer. They probe storm channels for larvae, but also for routes: a bird may insert its narrow beak into a crack and draw out a thread of landscape—foreign moss, blue sand, rain colder than the local air. Their disappearances are not treated as deaths. Benches sometimes continue vibrating with their calls for days.

There are also things that inhabit absence.

Some shadows do not belong to objects. They lie across warm ground until an insect crosses them, then contract almost imperceptibly. Vacant façades sometimes watch through the expectation of windows. Certain empty apartments maintain body heat, consume water, and alter their interiors when unobserved. Municipal records classify these presences as residents if they obey water law and do not interfere with transit.

Beneath the city live the systems themselves: membranes, conduits, dormant foundations, gravitational exchanges, and temporary rivers. Whether these form one organism is debated. They respond too coherently to be mere infrastructure, yet too diffusely to speak. Their low tone changes when districts are threatened. They reroute water around sleeping children, deny doors to those carrying sealed vessels, and occasionally raise an entirely new tower in answer to needs no living planner has voiced.

At the center, among the dark reeds, dwell the reflected.

They appear only when the air above the reservoir deepens. At first they resemble inverted citizens walking the streets of the suspended neighborhoods, their feet turned toward an unfamiliar sky. Closer observation reveals discrepancies. They pause before their local counterparts do. They gather around buildings that have not yet arrived. Some stand beneath the exact point where an observer watches from below and lift open hands as if feeling for rain.

The people of the basin disagree about what the reflected are. Ancestors. Descendants. Lost citizens. Architectural proposals given temporary bodies. Inhabitants of the place from which the city is still being transferred.

During the deepening, a few descend.

They emerge from reed shadows, unreliable doors, elevator chambers, or the warm interiors of new towers. They are usually wet despite the absence of rain. They know the city’s rules but not always its current streets. They may recognize strangers intimately and fail to recognize their apparent families. Most are taken in. Hospitality is safer than contradiction.

By morning they are either gone or have become ordinary enough to cast reliable shadows.

The newest towers may be their dwellings—or living things in their own right. They arrive warm, connected, and faintly resonant, with rooms proportioned for bodies not yet present. The vines climb them immediately, not as invaders but as greeters. Silver insects inspect their seams. Groves begin, slowly, to move nearer.

Then people enter. They bring porous cups, bedding, cuttings, lamps kept close to the floor. They wait to learn where the doors lead. They listen to the walls contract in the night.

And by the next rain, the city has inhabitants who remember that tower always having been there.
