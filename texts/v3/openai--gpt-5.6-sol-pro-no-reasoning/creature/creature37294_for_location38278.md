The basin is inhabited not by a single ruling species, but by a complete and evenly balanced commonwealth of creatures. None dominates. None multiplies beyond its allotted abundance. They live as the water lives there: continually renewed, never accumulating.

Most conspicuous on the ground are the **ash grazers**, tall, narrow-bodied animals that cross the open flats in widely spaced files. Their coats are the color of the ridges, each hair hollow and nearly translucent, so that an animal standing in full light seems edged in silver while one in shadow almost disappears. Their legs are disproportionately long, but their steps are short and exact. Broad inner nostrils warm the thin air before it reaches the lungs, and the rib cage extends deep into the belly. They feed without visibly diminishing the sparse plants, cropping only the outermost leaves of each cushion before moving on. A herd may take all day to pass a seep, yet by the next brightness it has resumed the same contour on the opposite slope, as though grazing were only one part of a larger circuit.

The **stonehorns** remain closer to broken ground. They are squat, heavy animals, low at the shoulder, with feet that unfold under weight into rough, many-lobed pads. Their horns do not point outward. They curl tightly around the skull, protecting the ears and jaw from flying grit and from the basin’s punishing sound. Older beasts develop shallow grooves across the horn, and when they lower their heads near a sounding column, those grooves hum in sympathy.

Stonehorns turn slabs in search of beetles, roots, and crusts of mineral salt. They use fist-sized rocks as hammers, holding them between the hard inner edges of their forefeet. At certain pools they push flat stones into rings, not to enclose the water but apparently to keep the wet gravel from collapsing beneath them. No individual seems to understand the whole arrangement. Nevertheless, each nudges one or two pieces before drinking, and the work of generations resembles the beginning of architecture.

Among the scree live innumerable **seam-dwellers**: small, warm animals with round ears, blunt faces, black eyes, and whiskers pale enough to catch the mineral light. Their bodies are flatter than they first appear. They can empty their lungs, loosen the joints of the shoulder, and pour themselves into cracks scarcely wider than a thumb. Beneath the visible stones lies an inhabited depth of chambers and passages. Their faint movements can be felt through the ground even when the basin’s chord makes them impossible to hear.

They gather cast hairs, wing-filaments, dry flower sheaths, and translucent moss. Yet their nests never become thick, and their refuse heaps never grow. Beetles consume the scraps; the seam-dwellers eat the beetles; loose bedding is drawn gradually into the gravel by freezing water. Nothing remains long enough to become evidence.

The white ground birds are called **liftings**, because a resting flock is indistinguishable from a bar of pale stones until it rises all at once. Their feathers are stiff, short, and powdered with reflective dust. They crouch rather than perch, orienting their bodies so precisely toward the sun that their shadows vanish beneath them. Their beaks have dark, reinforced edges, but for cutting cushion roots they prefer flakes of volcanic glass. A bird will test several shards, carry one to a plant, saw through a fibrous strand, and then leave the tool where it fed. The same flake may be used by many birds over many years without becoming anyone’s possession.

Their nests are no more than existing depressions recognized and reused. Eggs resemble rounded pumice. Chicks freeze motionless at the passage of a shadow, and sometimes the distinction between chick, egg, and stone becomes uncertain until one opens an eye.

Around the hidden pools live mirrored beetles, nearly invisible in direct light because their shells return the color above them. Beneath overhangs they become points of blue-black brilliance. They feed on lichen and on the mineral films laid down by evaporation. Their larvae mature under translucent stones, in the greenish half-light where the inverted moss grows.

The thin-winged insects above the seepages do not beat their wings as lowland insects would. They hold them tense within the basin’s constant vibration and steer by altering the angle of their bodies. The great chord flies them. Their swarms are therefore densest at particular notes: silver gnats around the high whistles, broad dark midges where the stone gives a bass tremor. When removed from the sounding air, they fall as if dead. Returned to it, they lift immediately.

Predators also live here, though they seldom reveal themselves. Long-backed hunters lie in gutters of matching stone, their fur banded like sediment. They do not chase. They place themselves beside the ancient routes of seam-dwellers and ground birds and wait for life to pass within reach. Above them drift small black fliers with thick, opaque wings, able to break from the larger circuits and drop almost vertically onto prey. A kill causes a brief disturbance, but never a surplus. By the following light, hide, feather, tendon, and bone have entered other bodies or vanished under the gravel.

Above all these move the **circuit fliers**.

They are not feathered, though from the ground their overlapping multitudes can resemble immense skeins of birds. Each has a light, narrow body suspended between two long, translucent vanes. These wings contain rows of open channels. Air passing through them produces the dry resonance that fills the basin whenever a flock crosses overhead. The divided tail is composed of sensitive filaments, some longer than the body; they tremble separately, reading pressure, pitch, and the reflected vibrations of nearby stone.

The fliers do not navigate chiefly by sight. They inhabit the chord.

Every column, cavity, ridge, and gutter contributes a stable tone, and together those tones form a map more exact than landmarks. The old lead because age roughens and complicates the channels in their wings, allowing them to distinguish subtler harmonics. The young fly inside the curves, sheltered from errors by the bodies surrounding them. Over years they move outward through the formation, learning the basin as a sequence of resonances.

Their circuits are not arbitrary. They carry minute life between the isolated pockets of the country: pollen caught on their tail-filaments, insect eggs adhering to their limbs, spores lodged in the fine ridges of the wings. What appears to be endless flight is the circulation of the basin itself. The plants remain separated, yet are not alone. The pools have no outlets, yet exchange life. The distant herds occupy one ecology because the fliers continually stitch its scattered pieces together.

They land only in concealed clefts high on the stone columns. There they cling vertically, folding their vanes until they resemble skins of ice. Their young begin life wingless and pale, feeding on condensed moisture and mineral growth inside the rock. At maturity they crawl toward the sounding apertures. The wind opens their vanes for the first time and takes them directly into the established route.

Sometimes one fails and falls. These are the shed forms found beneath the flight paths: transparent vanes, hollow wing-ribs, tails still trembling long after the body is gone. They do not accumulate because seam-dwellers drag them below, birds take them for nest lining, and insects consume the salt left on their surfaces.

No creature appears to command the basin’s restoration. Its constancy arises from all of them together. The grazers prune without stripping. Stonehorns open buried food and preserve the seep margins. Birds cut roots and carry fragments. Beetles erase remains. Seam-dwellers turn the hidden soil. Predators prevent abundance from becoming pressure. The circuit fliers distribute what would otherwise remain trapped in isolated hollows.

Yet this alone does not explain the place completely.

Each species behaves with a regularity too exact to be merely habitual. Herds return to old contours even after panic scatters them. Birds abandon tools at nearly identical intervals. Predators cease hunting when no visible obstacle prevents another kill. The fliers preserve their routes through darkness, injury, and the death of their leaders. It is as if the standing chord sets not only direction but proportion: how many may drink, how far one must graze, when pursuit must end, where a stone should rest.

The creatures feel the basin continuously through horn, bone, wing, whisker, foot, and hollow hair. To them its noise may not be noise at all. It may be boundary, memory, instruction, and census. Every living body enters the chord and alters it by an infinitesimal amount; every other body hears that alteration. The whole country knows, without thought, what it presently contains.

A human could live there only by entering this web of measure.

Such a person would learn to sleep with cloth packed around the ears and one hand against the ground. They would drink after the stonehorns, harvest only where the ash grazers had already passed, and search abandoned bird-flakes for cutting edges. They would soon stop striking metal, whose ringing confuses the insects and draws disorienting harmonics from the columns. They might begin to recognize the circuit fliers individually by damaged vanes or unusual tail patterns, only to lose them again among thousands.

Building would prove nearly impossible—not because stone could not be moved, but because every alteration would change some portion of the sound. A wall would create turbulence. A roof would silence a patch of frost. A blocked aperture might shift a flock’s route and sever the exchange between two distant pools. Shelter must therefore be borrowed from shapes already admitted by the basin.

Over time, the traveler too would become provisional: wrapped in hide, carrying horn and glass, leaving behind almost nothing. Footprints would persist for a while among older prints. A sleeping hollow would hold the body’s warmth until morning. Perhaps a few seam-dwellers would learn the traveler’s scent, or a flock would widen its curve around an upright figure.

But the country would not make a place for them. It would only measure their presence.

And if they died there, the inhabitants would dismantle them gently and without ceremony. Hair would line nests. Sinew would be worried loose by beetles. Bone would bleach, split under frost, and enter the mineral gutters. A tool might remain somewhat longer, especially if made of metal, ringing whenever the chord found its note. Eventually even that foreign sound would cease—or be absorbed so completely that no later listener could tell it had ever been new.

The basin’s inhabitants are not timeless. They are born, feed, teach, hunt, fail, and die. But they live within a totality that does not permit any life to become an event large enough to divide before from after.

Each is solitary in the immense brightness.

Together, they are the means by which the landscape remembers only itself.
