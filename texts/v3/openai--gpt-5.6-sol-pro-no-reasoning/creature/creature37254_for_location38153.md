The people of the basin are narrow communities stretched across enormous distances.

They live in pyloned shelters, tram compartments, pressure kitchens, clinics, workshops, and temporary rooms that vibrate with the machinery beneath them. Few are born there, but some have spent so long below that the old surface has become an inherited idea: an unroofed place of uncontrolled weather, mentioned with the imprecision of myth. They distrust photographs of blue sky. It looks to them like a systems failure.

Their bodies show the basin’s demands. They are compact beneath layers of insulation, shoulders permanently rounded against dripping ceilings and low access ways. Their faces are pale from artificial light, often patterned at the temples and bridge of the nose by respirator seals. Fingertips are callused despite heated gloves. Many have small black scars where cold metal adhered to skin, or luminous identification marks tattooed into the wrist so that bodies can be recognized during outages. They move carefully even in safe rooms. A cup near the edge of a table is shifted inward without thought. Before sitting, they touch the chair to test for vibration.

They listen more than they look.

A basin worker can distinguish a healthy pump from a cavitating one through three bulkheads. Sleepers wake when familiar machinery falls silent, not when it starts. Children learn the warning tones before they learn letters, tapping them on pipes and tabletops: once, hold fast; twice, go high; three times, empty your pockets and lie down. Adults discourage the game, though they played it themselves.

No single trade defines them. There are weld readers, condensation mappers, cold-seam inspectors, algae tenders, hydraulic geometers, corrosion physicians, tram pilots, pressure cooks, and crews whose sole work is to walk unfinished districts and report what has changed. Some maintain machines. Others maintain conditions in which machines may safely deteriorate. A respected occupation is that of the listener, who kneels with jaw or instrument against the plate and identifies buried faults by their rhythm.

The erosion keepers work in the eastern fields. Their suits are scored white by abrasive spray. They watch artificial gullies form and speak of them as temperaments: a channel may be greedy, stubborn, deceptive, or too eager to divide. They intervene with small barriers and redirected jets, then wait to see what the water teaches. Older keepers insist that a useful ravine cannot be designed outright. It must be persuaded into revealing itself.

Around the manifold reservoir live the most permanent households. Their quarters are warm enough for bare hands, and their walls are lined with objects that moisture cannot easily spoil: stamped metal charms, ceramic portraits, braided wire, glass ampoules of colored salts. Cloth, paper, and untreated wood are rare and intimate. A scrap of wood may be kept in a sealed case and passed down through a family, less for beauty than for its dry, fibrous smell.

They measure time by cycles rather than days. A childhood might be remembered as occurring during the Long Retrofit, the Orange Floods, or before the western towers began exhaling. Birthdays drift because maintenance calendars are periodically resynchronized. What matters more is the first suit, the first solo crossing, the first fault correctly named, the first time one is trusted to remain outside during a starting pulse.

Children grow up in warm galleries behind pressure glass. They watch rain strike the outer walkways and press their palms against windows when duct grazers pass. To them, the six-legged machines are not animals, exactly, but neither are they merely equipment. They give them names based on damaged panels, gait, or light color. A grazer that returns regularly to the same intake may be greeted for years. When one is dismantled, children leave twists of wire near its maintenance bay.

The adults indulge this cautiously. They too attribute personality to machines, though they hide it inside technical language. A pump is said to be load-sensitive rather than nervous. A gantry has alignment memory rather than spite. A district is thermally unpredictable rather than moody. Yet workers apologize after striking housings, pat railings before difficult crossings, and lower their voices near foundation-engine shafts.

Not everyone is human.

The translucent catch-basin shrimp live in populations divided by valves and filtration combs. Each pool produces its own slight variation: long antennae in the dark sumps, red organs near warm conduits, flattened bodies in fast culverts. They graze on films of bacteria and chemical residue. Pump reversals cast them into neighboring systems, making every pressure event a migration. Basin biologists catalogue them, but maintenance crews read them more practically. Shrimp climbing the walls mean oxygen loss below. Shrimp clustered beneath clear ice mean a warm leak. A basin suddenly empty of them is closed before the instruments finish confirming why.

The pale moths live deeper in the infrastructure. Their larvae eat fungi growing in cable jackets and insulation foam. Adults emerge singly, heavy-winged and damp, drawn to ultraviolet fault lamps. Their appearance is regarded as an omen only because it is often useful: a moth circling where no fault lamp is visible may reveal radiation leaking from a cracked housing. Some technicians follow them.

There are seal-gnawers too, rarely seen. They are warm-bodied, hairless creatures no longer easily classed as rats or anything else brought from above. Their teeth are dark with metallic deposits. They nest around junction boxes and line their hollows with shredded insulation. Traps seldom catch them; they seem to recognize local current cycles and leave before shutdowns. What eats them is unknown. Now and then, tracks much larger than theirs appear in iron silt and end at a drain too narrow to admit the animal that made them.

The vegetation inhabits permission.

Sedges persist where trough heaters and drainage failures overlap. Moss occupies the undersides of pipes, away from direct purge rain. Fungal fans spread through insulation until a sensor detects altered resistance and sterilizes the section. Horsetail colonies gather around hatch rims, nourished by heat and trace minerals carried upward on condensation. They flatten when underground freight passes, rise afterward, and have become informal indicators of rail traffic in districts where signals no longer function.

Workers sometimes transplant them. Officially this is prohibited, as unregistered growth complicates environmental monitoring. Unofficially, nearly every long-term shelter has a warm crack where something green or nearly green is being kept alive. These hidden gardens are small enough to cover with one hand. They are fed with rinse water, skin salts, crumbs from fungal protein loaves, and minute quantities of contraband substrate.

The carbon scrubbers host a broader ecology. Their porous cladding supports algae, bacterial films, mites, moth larvae, and threadlike worms that survive inside condensation channels. Maintenance crews call each scrubber’s living surface its coat. Some coats are thin and yellow; others hang in dark green folds. A healthy coat improves capture efficiency, until suddenly it obstructs airflow and must be stripped away. Thus the scrubbers are periodically shorn by duct grazers, and the removed growth is taken to digesters or, in lean settlements, processed into bitter food.

The grazers themselves travel farther than most residents. Low and broad, they cross flooded plates, frost fields, and service corridors impassable to lighter carts. Their six legs articulate around wheel hubs, allowing them to climb grates and brace during pressure tremors. Beneath their armored mouths are combs, scrapers, thermal blades, sampling tongues, and flexible tubes that drink runoff.

They communicate constantly. Their coded bursts announce right of way, contamination, unstable flooring, and the condition of the intake just cleaned. But there are exchanges that do not appear in any maintenance protocol. Two machines may meet in an empty district and remain facing each other for minutes, pulsing low tones through the plate. They may exchange route fragments from sealed sectors or outdated maps of structures no longer present. Because their navigation systems retain every successful path, the oldest grazers remember versions of the basin that no human has seen.

Occasionally one follows a vanished causeway.

It will arrive at the edge of black water, lights fixed on a route dismantled decades ago, and wait for permission to proceed. Crews must either overwrite the memory or construct a temporary bypass. Some refuse the overwrite. They say old routes are evidence, and that a place which rebuilds itself so quickly needs creatures capable of remembering where the ground used to be.

Beyond the inhabited works, other maintenance systems move beneath the floor. Buried cutters migrate through composite layers, opening channels and consuming failed reinforcement. Pipe-crawlers nest in valve chambers and emerge during low pressure. Ceiling drones hang folded among the baffles, waking only when condensation changes weight. Most are simple, but simplicity becomes ambiguous when a machine has repeated the same task for centuries, adjusting a little each time.

The basin’s oldest inhabitants may be its distributed microbes. They live in water films, aggregate pores, corroded seams, filter membranes, and the lukewarm darkness around power trunks. Some descend from introduced cleaning cultures; others arrived with crews, freight, food, or the water itself. Together they digest oils, pit alloys, seal microscopic cracks with mineral deposits, clog sensors, purify reservoirs, and exhale gases that set off alarms. The basin studies them while depending on them. Attempts to sterilize entire sectors have ended badly. Metal corrodes differently in their absence. Water turns chemically aggressive. Sludge refuses to settle. Afterward, technicians reseed the district from archived films.

There are also rumored inhabitants.

Crews on the lowest maintenance decks report knocks traveling against the direction of flow. Survey drones occasionally count one more heat source than the number of workers assigned. During reversed rain, suspended droplets have been seen bending around shapes not visible by any other means. Such reports are usually attributed to pressure distortion, sensor lag, or fatigue.

Still, some shelters place a bowl beneath the ventilation intake before a major purge. It contains warm water, a shaving of insulation, and three steel nuts. No one agrees whom it is for. The custom predates the current warning system.

Whatever lives in the basin lives conditionally. It occupies tolerated temperatures, forgotten cavities, scheduled silences, leaks not yet severe enough to repair. Human beings and shrimp, moss and machines all survive inside margins calculated for other purposes. None possesses the place. Even the foundation engines serve structures farther below, or farther above, or not yet constructed.

Over generations, this has shaped a particular humility. Basin dwellers do not speak of conquering their environment. Nor do they call it home without qualification. They say they are stationed there, even if their grandparents are buried in sealed thermal walls and their children know no other sky. They understand habitation as a temporary agreement between pressure, maintenance, memory, and error.

At the end of a work cycle, lights move along the elevated walks toward the shelters. Duct grazers continue in the fog. Shrimp gather beneath warming grates. Moths batter softly at fault lamps. Hidden sedges tremble as freight passes far below.

Then the people seal their doors and sleep inside the machine, listening for any change in its breathing.
