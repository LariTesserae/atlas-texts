The valley is inhabited less by separate species than by collaborations.

The first and most extensive inhabitant is the ground itself: an old, composite hide of mineral crust, peat, roots, microbial glass, discarded machinery, and water held in several contradictory states. It breathes underfoot. Marsh shelves swell during the rains and subside afterward, exhaling marsh gas through reed vents. Dark colonies spread over the open slopes, each apparently a cushion plant but actually a compact commonwealth. The hard outer leaves gather ultraviolet light; pale leaves beneath distribute heat; fungal threads carry moisture inward; tiny shelled animals graze passages through the dead layers. A colony may survive for thousands of years by moving slowly across the rock, dying behind itself and growing ahead. Individuals are impossible to identify. The whole hillside may be one organism, or many organisms that ceased finding the distinction useful.

The tree-ferns of the ravines are similarly entangled. Their broad crowns are gardens in their own right, loaded with orchids, mosses, rain crabs, egg cases, and black nectar birds. Silver fluid rises through clear vessels in their trunks. Whether these channels were inherited from an engineered ancestor or invaded later by some buried network is uncertain. When a trunk falls, its transparent veins remain active for years, pushing droplets into the soil and feeding rings of new growth. Fallen groves therefore continue to function as plumbing long after they have become nurse logs.

The crown crabs spend almost their entire lives above the ground. Their flattened shells resemble wet bark, and small ferns root in grooves along their backs. During rain they descend in deliberate spirals, carrying packets of spores beneath their abdomens. They mate in temporary pools caught in fern crowns, producing translucent young that swim through the canopy water before hardening into climbers. A crab placed on open earth becomes disoriented. It searches for branches even when none are present, raising its claws toward the sky.

The black nectar birds move through this overhead wetland in dense, argumentative flocks. Each has a narrow head, a brush-tipped tongue, and wings jointed so finely that they can fold between crowded stems. Their feathers absorb nearly all visible light, but in ultraviolet they are patterned with bright bars that change as the birds mature. They pollinate the orchids and carry mites, fungal dust, and occasionally young crabs between isolated crowns. Their great flocks are not governed by a single leader. They follow delays in one another’s reflections, especially near old devices, and can turn as one even when the foremost birds have already vanished behind rock.

On the open marshes live the long-legged grazers. They are broad-bodied, patient animals with six weight-bearing toes joined by tough webs. Their feet spread over floating peat, testing each step before the body follows. Green-gold scales along the spine host photosynthetic cells, so family groups spend the brightest hours nearly motionless, aligned toward the sun. The scales do not feed them completely, but they sustain pregnancies and long crossings where forage is poor.

Their breathing is visible. A row of chambers between the ribs expands in sequence, drawing thin air through branching passages before it reaches the lungs. When alarmed, the chambers pulse rapidly and the animal’s flanks ripple from shoulder to hip. Calves are born without the necessary microbial cultures in these chambers. Adults inoculate them by breathing into their nostrils and licking the soft scales of the back.

The grazers remember paths that no longer correspond to the surface. They veer around buried hazards, cross apparently bottomless channels at exact points, and pause where the distance ahead has lengthened after rain. Their oldest females can detect the deep modulation through the bones of their feet. When the buried systems alter rhythm, whole herds relocate days before pools tilt, suspended slabs shed water, or the seasonal image in the western ring begins to change.

Small gliding lizards follow the herds. They bask on the animals’ warm backs, feeding on biting insects, then launch toward hanging stones when disturbed. Their rib membranes are ridged with metallic fibers obtained from the husks of marsh organisms. Near the western cliffs they can remain suspended for minutes at a time, tilting their bodies into invisible gradients. Juveniles often misjudge these fields and come to rest in the air itself, paddling helplessly until a gust or an adult’s collision knocks them free.

The frogs belong chiefly to the water, though “water” in the valley includes wet rock, mist-filled chambers, plant vessels, and droplets held motionless above the ground. Some species hatch in ordinary pools. Others deposit eggs in the trembling beads beneath suspended stone. Their tadpoles mature inside those droplets, grazing films on the water’s surface from within. When the drops finally become heavy enough to fall, dozens of minute frogs arrive at the moss beds in a single splash.

Their evening chorus is also a forecast. Pressure changes below the plateau travel upward through flooded cracks long before rain is visible. The frogs feel these changes through disks in the jaw. Each population answers at a slightly different pressure, so song advances across the basin ahead of a storm: bass pulses from the reed beds, ticking calls under rocks, then the glassy notes of canopy species. What sounds like anticipation may simply be the valley hearing the weather through them.

Insects occupy nearly every remaining interval. Metallic raspers cling to warm mineral crusts. Long-bodied pollinators climb into orchids and emerge powdered blue. Transparent midges breed in the condensation channels of abandoned towers. The largest are the slow singers of the upper slopes, armored creatures with deep resonating cavities along their abdomens. They anchor themselves among cushion plants and sustain a note for hours by vibrating membranes too slowly to see. Moss grows on old individuals. Smaller animals shelter beneath their plates. A singer may remain in one place so long that roots close over its feet, and when it finally falls silent the hillside retains a cavity shaped like its voice.

Below the lily mats live the broad ones.

They are seldom exposed, and no single sighting reveals their complete form. A smooth back rises beneath a floating island; a fin or limb folds through black water; reeds bow outward in a widening circle. They appear to be slow amphibious filter-feeders, wider than they are long, with mouths opening along the underside. They move through peat channels by lifting entire rafts rather than forcing their bodies beneath them. The lifted vegetation brings oxygenated water into stagnant depths, and many marsh species depend on the disturbance.

Old broad ones carry gardens on their backs. Their skin accumulates silt, bulbs, snail colonies, and rooted lilies until they become almost indistinguishable from floating ground. Grazers sometimes step onto them. The creature waits, submerged and tolerant, then lowers itself so gently that the animal has time to step away. What preys upon the young is unknown. Certain sudden silences in the frog beds may mark their feeding, but so may many other things.

At dusk the six-winged scavengers leave the cliffs. They are pale, almost naked creatures with narrow torsos and three pairs of wings, though only the front pair retain strong flight muscles. The others are living vanes: thin, sensitive surfaces that adjust continuously to the valley’s weakened and redirected gravity. Their long tails carry mineral nodules that shift position as ballast. Far from the old fields they fly poorly. Within them they can hang motionless, rise without effort, or slide sideways into a cliff opening no wider than their folded skulls.

Their cries are quiet because the answer matters more than the call. The returning echo passes through stone, soil, and buried structures, revealing cavities inaccessible to sight. Each scavenger learns the underground echo-shape of a territory. They locate carcasses by hearing how decomposition changes the resonance of the ground. A grazer concealed under dense reeds is not found by smell but by the slight deadening of an otherwise familiar subterranean note.

Not all predators are large. Beneath certain suspended slabs lives a colonial animal easily mistaken for a root mat. Its strands hang among real roots, ending in leaves that are not leaves but soft traps. Nectar birds brush them and are held without struggle. The colony draws nutrients slowly, sometimes keeping prey alive for days. Its feeding bodies are distributed through the mud above the slab, while the hanging strands serve as mouths. Feathers accumulate below because feathers are the only part it cannot digest.

Other hunters exploit the regional rules. Long-bodied marsh cats—rarely seen except as paired reflections—stalk where distance contracts in dry weather. They may appear several strides closer than their sound suggests. Thin aquatic predators pass through channels too narrow for their skulls because the water, but not surrounding matter, enters folded spaces near old machines. Even harmless animals make use of such routes. Migration here is partly geographical and partly physical: a seasonal movement between different expressions of the same terrain.

The oldest inhabitants may be the glass-husked microorganisms layered through the peat. They build transparent cases in geometric forms unlike ordinary shells: lattices, coils, nested chambers, tiny toothed rings. Some metabolize iron or sulfur. Others respond to the buried modulation, orienting in unison when its phase changes. Their accumulated remains alter how water, sound, and weak fields move through the marsh. Over centuries they have grown reefs around the machinery, perhaps protecting it, perhaps parasitizing it, perhaps becoming its outermost working parts.

The engineered filaments braided through ravine roots may be their larger relatives. They heal when cut, but not like plants. Their broken ends explore the surrounding soil, testing roots and mineral surfaces until they find a viable connection. Sap passes through them. So do electrical impulses, heat, and minute silver droplets. In some places the forest uses the network to redistribute water. In others the network uses the forest to extend itself. Neither process can now be separated from the other.

Even the artifacts have acquired populations specific to them. Blue-green ferns live only under the ruined arch’s ribbon of cool air. Pale snails graze the mathematically spaced lichen bands on the western ring and never cross the bare intervals. A species of moth lays its eggs on the ceramic posts of the vanished road; the larvae feed on nothing visible, yet emerge whenever the distance between posts lengthens after rain. Around the perfectly level marsh, trees lean toward the imposed plane, and arboreal animals navigate by it more reliably than by the horizon.

No people openly inhabit the basin now. If descendants of its builders remain, they do not maintain houses, roads, or boundaries recognizable as such. Yet small arrangements recur: chimes kept clear of vines, ceramic steps swept by no obvious feet, plaques polished beyond what rain alone should accomplish. Occasionally food shells are found stacked beneath shelters. The stacks may be made by crabs. The chimes may repel climbing plants through vibration. The clean steps may lie within one of the valley’s subtle exclusions. None is proof of a hidden caretaker.

What lives here has no need for one.

The valley’s inhabitants have incorporated its impossible conditions into bone, root, migration, courtship, and song. They do not distinguish natural rain from water released by a suspended slab, nor a cave from a folded space, nor a season from the slow image inside the ring. A machine that has shaped a watershed for ten thousand generations is not machinery to them. It is climate, habitat, landmark, and ancestor.

When warm rain approaches from the east, the grazers turn their photosynthetic backs from the first hard drops. Crown crabs begin their descent. Nectar birds press themselves under fern fronds. Six-winged scavengers climb into the altered air above the cliffs and hold there, pale against the darkening cloud.

And throughout the western marsh, before the rain has arrived, the frogs call from pools, roots, leaves, and hanging beads of water. One species begins, then another answers farther downslope. The chorus spreads across the old mechanisms beneath the peat until the entire basin seems to sing—not in warning, exactly, and not in welcome, but in recognition.
