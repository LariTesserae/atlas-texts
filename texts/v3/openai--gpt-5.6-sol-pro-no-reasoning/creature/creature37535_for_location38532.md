The inhabitants are not a people apart from their world. They are its moving tissues: mouths, listeners, scavengers, couriers, and spores loosened briefly from the architecture before returning to it.

Nearest the pillar skins live the ring-mouth grazers. They are long, heavy animals with soft segmented bodies and crowns of inward-pointing teeth. Clinging by several muscular collars, they travel so slowly that colonies of mites and translucent worms inhabit their backs. Their feeding leaves broad, clean spirals in the dark bark. They digest little of what they remove. Most passes through them as compressed ribbons, already inoculated with organisms that bind the waste into new leaf-stone. Thus their grazing is also masonry. The oldest individuals may circle a pillar for centuries without completing a circuit.

Inside vents and pores are the fan-tailed filterers, pale animals shaped like folded hands. They wedge themselves into channels where water seeps through the walls and open their tails into branching nets. Their shells fit the passages exactly. As they grow, the living walls widen around them; when they die, the channels narrow and another, smaller generation takes their place. Whole lineages belong to a single pore. They know the abyss only as pressure, nourishment, and the unceasing note of water passing over their bodies.

Ceilings are crossed by low, shell-backed walkers. Their many feet end in small cups that grip polished rootwood. They carry their eggs beneath translucent shields, each egg shining faintly violet from ingested sap. Because there is no useful floor, “falling” is merely a change of habitat. When a walker loses its hold, it closes its limbs and descends until it meets a bridge, a leaf, or—years later—the same architecture from another direction.

The open water belongs to animals built around listening.

Shoals of little hinged swimmers hold station between pillars by snapping paired shells. Their crackle is not random. Each animal hears the returning echoes of its neighbors and closes its fins in the instant required to preserve the shoal’s shape. No leader directs them, and most remain separated by many body lengths, invisible to one another. Yet together they maintain hollow spheres, spirals, and long lattices around resonant trunks. A predator passing through may scatter every individual, but the pattern reforms elsewhere without increasing or diminishing.

Larger swimmers produce blunt pulses in chambers behind their skulls. They have no eyes, or only buried patches that distinguish living radiance from absolute dark. Their faces are broad and soft, crowded with pressure-sensitive grooves. To them, a pillar is not an image but a chord: terraces make one texture of returning sound, curtain-leaves another, open galleries another. Distance is delay. Shape is resonance. A familiar route is remembered as a sequence of silences.

Some of these callers remain solitary. Others converse across distances so great that an answer returns after feeding, sleep, or birth. A question may be inherited: one animal sends a call and its offspring, swimming beside the cooling body, hears the reply.

The great raylike grazers move through the outer gardens. Nearly transparent, they resemble broad sheets of water with cartilage drawn through them. Their organs hang in branching rows, and their stomachs glow with the violet fibers they have swallowed. Along the front edge are thousands of delicate cutting plates. A ray lays itself against a curtain-leaf and removes one measured strip, never enough to sever the larger vein. The wound closes behind it. Elsewhere on the same organism, an equal breadth of tissue thickens.

Young rays are born folded. They live in narrow galleries where the walls prevent them from opening too early. As they grow, they pass through chambers of increasing size until they emerge into open water and unfurl. The largest are wider than bridges and carry entire communities beneath them: scraping mites, shell nurseries, pale parasites that imitate veins, and small callers that use the ray’s body as a sounding board.

Around the root-cables hang the upright herds. They are slender, many-jointed creatures with weighted tails and long sensory arms. Rather than grasping the cables, they hover close enough to brush them with comb-shaped fingertips. Through those touches they hear feeding on distant branches, structural strain, the opening of vents, and the footsteps of shell-walkers kilometers away. Each herd gathers around a particular harmonic. When the cable sings above or below it, the animals adjust their buoyancy and move to a new station.

They feed on almost nothing visible. Their fingers collect films from the cables, and symbiotic growths inside their hollow torsos convert vibration, dissolved minerals, and swallowed spores into flesh. At intervals one individual strikes the root with the hard knob of its tail. The blow travels through the pillar and is answered from other cables. Whether this is courtship, measurement, warning, or maintenance cannot be separated. For them these may be the same act.

The enormous cable-walkers of the seven-pillar gulf are their distant kin, though they have ceased to resemble swimmers. Each consists of a central body suspended among dozens of long articulated limbs. Some limbs grip; some listen; some drum on the web. They move with immense patience, releasing one cable only after securing several others. Their bodies are furred with dark cilia that detect the faintest returning tremor.

They never leave the ring. Perhaps they cannot. The seven pillar-tones pass through them continuously, and organs along their flanks swell or contract to keep those tones in relation. When one pillar’s note shifts, the walkers redistribute themselves across the web until the chord is restored. They may be grazing the cables, tending them, or merely responding to the only sensation by which they recognize existence. Their bass signals can be felt far beyond the gulf as a periodic pressure in teeth, shells, and hollow bones.

Predators are less distinct.

Many imitate fragments of the garden: a frond bundle, a root knot, a torn edge of leaf. They drift without obvious orientation, their bodies trailing harmless-looking filaments. Only when prey enters the filaments’ field do they unfold. What seemed to be loose foliage becomes a radial jaw; what seemed like stem fiber tightens into hooked limbs. The attack is brief because pursuit is wasteful in so vast a medium. If the first closure fails, the predator folds again and resumes being architecture.

Their prey know this, but cannot avoid every resemblance. Instead they test the darkness with clicks, touches, or tiny offerings of shed skin. A true plant receives these without reaction. A waiting jaw sometimes closes on the decoy, betraying itself.

Death does not produce graveyards. Ribbon scavengers arrive through seams almost immediately, thin enough to pass between layers of bark or through the spiracles of a corpse. They enter as threads and leave as broad, dark bands filled with nutrient paste. Grazers consume the bands; roots absorb what remains. Shells are occupied, dissolved, or incorporated into pale mineral buttresses. Even the hard listening bones of the great callers eventually become resonant pieces of a wall.

In the leaf-plains, files of small animals move along the ribs. They have flattened bodies and fringed mouths for combing the hairlike crop. Each follows the chemical trace of the one before it, while laying a trace for the one behind. The files have no beginning that has been observed. They enter from folds in the leaf, cross the exposed surface, and disappear into the upward-curving margins. Some lines may persist for generations. Individuals hatch, feed, mate, and die without the procession pausing.

Where the plain closes into a tubular hall, different creatures gather to use its impossible acoustics. Soft-bodied choristers attach themselves at intervals along the inner walls. They inflate chambers in their bodies and release single sustained notes into the tube. Hundreds of kilometers away, others answer. Their larvae travel by orienting to the interference between those voices. Adults rarely move again after fastening themselves in place. Over centuries their shells become embedded in the hall, so that living singers perform among the mineralized throats of their ancestors.

The empty vaults have inhabitants too, though few enter them bodily.

Small blind orbiters circle the unattached root knots at their centers. They are smooth, white, and finless, propelled by subtle waves passing beneath the skin. Every individual maintains the same distance from the knot and from its neighbors. They neither touch it nor feed upon it. Their eggs are released on the outward side of the orbit; their dead drift inward and disappear among the roots.

No one knows what sustains them. Their stomachs are nearly empty. Their tissues contain the same mineral proportions as the floating knot, and their faint internal radiance pulses in sympathy with the chamber’s tone. It may be that they eat the note itself, or the pressure patterns formed where sound converges. It may be that the knot is an organ and the orbiters are cells that have forgotten to be attached.

There are larger presences suggested only by displacement. Sometimes the background hum is muffled across an enormous region, as though a soft body passes between the listener and several pillars at once. Hanging ropes bend without current. Shoals preserve their formation but leave a vast empty contour within it. Hours later the tones return unchanged. Nothing has been seen clearly enough to decide whether this is one animal, a transparent colony, or a mobile portion of the garden.

The plants themselves may be inhabitants in the fullest sense.

A pillar can distinguish injuries occurring kilometers apart and answer them with coordinated thickening. A bridge alters the composition of its sap before a new grazer reaches it. Curtain-leaves tremble in still water when distant cable-walkers begin to drum. The seven pillars of the gulf maintain complementary tones despite being separated by open darkness. Whether these are reflexes, messages, or thoughts extended across centuries is impossible to tell.

If they are minds, they have no need to travel. Their offspring do not leave them but root backward into parental tissue, adding new chambers to memory. What an animal experiences as architecture may be an ancient nervous system: galleries as conduits, vents as sensory pits, bridges as slow synapses, whole herds moving through it like recurrent ideas.

Nothing here rules, because rule would imply that the world could depart from its ordained condition. Nothing cultivates the garden from outside. Grazer and leaf, hunter and scavenger, singer and resonant chamber are all participants in the same unending correction.

They are born. They feed. They listen through the black water. They enter walls, emerge from vents, cross leaves wider than countries, and call to creatures they may never approach.

Each life changes what is immediately around it.

Together, they change nothing at all.
