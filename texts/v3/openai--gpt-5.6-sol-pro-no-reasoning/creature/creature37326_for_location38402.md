The inhabitants call themselves the **Provisional People**.

This is not their own name for themselves. Their own name is too large to carry intact. Spoken aloud, it unfolds into a genealogy of valleys, trades, obligations, water rights, scaffold failures, kiln seasons, and promised descendants. Before the final syllable can be reached, the opening syllables have acquired villages. So *Provisional People* is the nearest translation: those who exist for now, while existence is still being made.

They are not quite as small as they appear.

At the hatch, one might mistake a worker for a pepper seed disturbed by wind. Farther in, the same worker may stand at one’s knee, or appear as a dark figure crossing a bridge half a day away. Among themselves, size is treated as a condition of work. A builder grows larger while carrying a load and smaller while deciding where to place it. A traveler thins with distance. A sleeper fits the room available. Children, having few obligations, are often enormous.

Their bodies resemble figures pinched rapidly from river clay: narrow torsos, long arms, blunt heads, and feet spread wide for gripping wet slopes. Their skin ranges from terracotta red to charcoal black, with pale seams at the joints like lime pressed into cracked pottery. It is always damp. In great heat their surfaces craze into delicate patterns, which relatives mend with slip and oil. Every adult carries several such repairs. The oldest are almost entirely patched, a mosaic of clays gathered from the districts they have served.

No two faces remain identical from one lighting to the next. Their features are shallow and easily altered. Grief pulls the mouth downward until it must be propped with a sliver of reed. Years of looking uphill raise the eyes toward the brow. Kiln workers develop little vents beside the nostrils. Surveyors press knotted strings across their foreheads, leaving maps that gradually become actual roads. Many wear hoods not for protection but to keep their expressions from setting too quickly.

They believe a face is scaffolding for the person behind it.

Names work similarly. A newborn receives an empty name, usually a single open vowel. Deeds are added as consonants. Places lived in become pauses; injuries become clicks; promises are breathed rather than voiced. A person’s name therefore grows more difficult with age. The dead have names so elaborate that no living mouth can pronounce them. Their descendants divide the names among themselves and speak them in chorus.

They are born from repairs.

When a crack opens in an inhabited wall, repairers pack it with clay, fiber, ash, and a little blood. Most patches dry normally. Some begin to breathe. A small hand pushes through, then a hooded head, then the rest of a soft and unfinished person. The repair crew adopts the child collectively. There are no orphans in the High Garden because no one is born to fewer than a dozen parents, and often to an entire retaining wall.

For the first season, children are kept moist in moss-lined bowls. Elders turn them regularly so no side hardens before the others. Their limbs can still be lengthened, their fingers divided, and their ears shaped for the sounds of the district in which they will live. This is done gently, though not always by choice. Canal settlements give their children broad, paddling hands. Chimney villages form narrow chests resistant to smoke. Those born near cloud nets receive cup-shaped ears able to hear rain before it has condensed.

Some children resist every shaping. They slump back into wet clay, sprout extra hands, or set themselves hard during sleep. Such children are considered particularly fortunate. They become inventors, prophets, or structural hazards.

No one knows whether the Provisional People reproduce in any other manner. Lovers sometimes build a small sealed room together and leave inside it two thumbprints and a bowl of water. Years later, someone may knock from within.

Their society is divided less by rank than by direction.

The **Upward Houses** carry brick, seed, fuel, fossils, and instructions toward unfinished heights. They favor hooked staffs, strong backs, and short songs timed to steep ascents.

The **Downward Houses** carry water, heat, news, dead names, and corrected measurements into the depths. Their clothes are waxed against rain, and their feet are often worn smooth.

The **Around Houses** maintain terraces, reconcile boundaries, braid roots, repair bridges, and ensure that every path eventually joins another.

There is also a forbidden direction, called **Out**.

No house claims it. No map marks it. Yet nearly every child attempts it once.

They crawl toward the woven hatch carrying some beloved thing: a bead, a mite’s cast skin, a brick from their first wall, a cup of rain. At the threshold the object opens into immensity. A bead becomes a moon over salt flats. The cast skin becomes a white cathedral of hollow armor. The brick unfolds into a burning plateau crowded with towns. Most children drop what they carry and flee.

A few continue.

These are the **Outbearers**, though adults pretend there is no such calling. They wrap objects in increasingly elaborate containers—leaf within shell, shell within clay, clay within woven hair—hoping to cross the hatch before the contained landscape awakens. None has succeeded. Some are still visible near the entrance, walking across the thing they meant to carry, now separated from the threshold by countries produced from their own bundles.

Builders salute them when passing.

Work is the nearest thing the Provisional People have to worship, but they do not worship completion. Completion terrifies them. A finished thing can no longer answer necessity. A sealed wall cannot become a stair; a perfect canal cannot be rerouted around a newborn district; a completed mountain can only begin to decay, and decay has not yet been properly designed.

Their prayers are therefore corrections.

They pray by adding a step, widening a channel, moving a shrine three finger-widths east, or scraping a face from a sacred figure so that another face may form. Priests are called **Revisers**. They carry damp cloths and blunt knives. When doctrine hardens, they soften it with steam.

Their holiest saying is:

*Let it be true until it must become truer.*

The dead present a special difficulty. Nothing may be removed whole, but neither can a person remain unused. Bodies are dismantled according to trade. The hands of a mason are mixed into mortar. A gardener’s ribs become trellises. The glazed eyes of water keepers are set into cistern walls, where they cloud before rain. Surveyors’ sinews are braided into measuring cords that refuse to give the same result twice.

The skull is always planted.

From it grows a pale fungus shaped like a hooded figure. The family builds a tiny dwelling inside the cap and listens at night. Sometimes the dead speak through the gills, though they tend to discuss future events as if remembering them.

Because evidence comes first, memory is not reliable in the ordinary direction. The Provisional People frequently discover records of lives they have not yet led. A worker may find her own handprint fossilized in a mountain older than her district. A child may inherit tools polished by decades of use from the adult he will become. Funeral processions occasionally meet the person whose funeral they are conducting, still young and hurrying to work.

Such encounters are handled with courtesy. Neither party asks questions. Questions can become instructions.

Their historians are therefore dangerous people. They are called **Earlier-Makers**, and they press carefully chosen traces into wet surfaces: footprints, husks, claw marks, fragments of songs rendered as grooves. They do not record what happened. They decide what the garden will eventually need to have happened.

An irresponsible historian can oblige the world to produce a war.

A compassionate one may insert evidence of an ancient peace and force enemies, generations later, to discover how it was achieved.

The rare animals are not regarded as lesser beings. They are vast interruptions from orders of reality too powerful to negotiate with directly. The gnat is called the **Thirst with Wings**. Mites are **White Road-Makers**. The unseen creature below is known by many names, each corresponding to a theory: Bell-Throat, Buried Weather, Grandmother Kiln, the Sleeper Beneath Scale.

A small priesthood studies its croaks. They have determined that the intervals are shortening.

They disagree on what this means.

Some say the creature is climbing. Some say the garden is descending toward it. Some believe each croak creates another nested High Garden, and the accumulated weight of these interiors will eventually crack the chamber from within.

The lightning apparitions are attended by figures called **Flash Witnesses**. They train themselves not to blink. Their eyes are coated with mica dust, giving them a pearly, damaged sheen. After each flash they carve everything they saw onto tablets of unfired clay, then compare accounts.

Their records never agree.

One sees the sixth mountain range. Another sees only empty air. A third sees enormous covered faces. A fourth sees the second hatch and insists that the person kneeling beyond it has aged since the previous storm.

The most feared witnesses are those who report seeing themselves.

They are removed from ordinary work and sent toward the Unmade Peak, because duplication is either a structural flaw or a sacred plan, and both must be built around.

Those laboring on the Peak have begun to change.

Its black clay enters them through their feet. Their skin darkens; their repairs soften; their shadows point uphill regardless of the lamps. Many become obsessed with the empty niche. They dream of a vast kneeling being whose eye fills the recess, though they cannot agree whether that being is creator, prisoner, landscape, or unfinished child.

A secret community lives within the Peak’s reed framework. They call themselves the **Hollow Builders**. While the outer crews construct the mountain, the Hollow Builders remove tiny portions from inside—not taking them away, which would violate the second rule, but redistributing them into concealed chambers.

Their purpose is to make the Peak empty enough to contain what is looking into it.

They say the garden does not reside inside the chamber.

They say the chamber is a shell grown around the garden.

They say the kneeling visitor is not an outsider but an organ: an eye briefly opened by something too large to wake all at once.

Most disturbing of all, they say the Provisional People were not the first inhabitants. Beneath the clay of the oldest terraces, workers sometimes uncover immense fingerprints. These prints have villages in their whorls. In those villages live people who claim they are building the High Garden from below.

The ordinary inhabitants avoid such thoughts. There is water to route, moss to cut, bridges to replace after the gnat’s passing. A mountain’s dimensions must be chanted or it may forget how far it extends. Clouds must be struck from their nets. Children must be turned in their bowls. Tomorrow’s fossils must be selected with care.

So they labor.

At dawn—if the copper brightening near the upper haze deserves that name—processions climb the newest slopes. Their hooded bodies bend beneath baskets. Their shell lamps move in files, each light appearing across several horizons at once. They sing not in unison but in sequence, the lower workers beginning lines that those higher up will finish months later.

From the hatch, the song is almost inaudible.

Close within, it shakes wet clay from the scaffolds:

*Build the path beneath the foot.*  
*Build the hand around the tool.*  
*Build the rain before the river.*  
*Build the grave before the bone.*  
*Build the eye that looks upon us.*  
*Build the world that holds the eye.*

And when lightning opens every hidden distance, the workers sometimes stop and turn toward the entrance.

For that instant they are no longer pepper-seed figures.

They stand shoulder to shoulder across the mountains, innumerable and nearly human, their wet faces lifted toward whoever kneels beyond the woven door. Each bears, pressed into the center of the brow, the tiny impression of a hatch.

Within each hatch is a room.

Within each room, a figure kneels.

Behind each figure, still soft and trembling under the labor of countless hands, rises another Unmade Peak.
