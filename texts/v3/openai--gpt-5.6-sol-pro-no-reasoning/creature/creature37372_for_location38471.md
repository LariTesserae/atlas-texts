The people of the Fold are narrow-built, deliberate, and always a little damp.

They are not a separate kind of human, though surface visitors sometimes speak as if they were. Most descend from the laborers, mechanics, fugitives, water-seekers, and salt traders who entered the fault in its first years. The Fold has changed them less through blood than through posture and habit. They stand with their shoulders slightly turned, ready to pass through a gap. They sleep curled in shallow bunks. They climb without looking at their feet and duck beneath beams even in rooms where the ceiling is high.

A child born there learns three things early: where to put a hand on a wet stair, which pipes may be touched, and how to become still when the stone makes an unfamiliar sound.

Their clothes are close-fitting and assembled in layers that can be removed one at a time. Loose sleeves catch on valves; long hems wick water from the floors. Cloth is precious because it mildews, so garments are patched with fine wire, leather, ceramic scales, braided root fiber, and pieces of old filter mesh. Most people wear open-backed shoes with soft soles, good for feeling a gantry’s vibration before it becomes visible. Hair is cropped, wrapped, or threaded through copper rings to keep it from machinery. The rings stain skin green.

Everyone carries a cup.

It may be tin, fired clay, folded brass, or the sawn-off base of some forgotten instrument, but it has a hook and a lid. A person without a cup is assumed to be newly arrived, gravely distracted, or in mourning. Water appears everywhere in the Fold, but never in quantities one may take for granted. Condensation caught from a handrail belongs to the hand that wipes it; condensation guided into a public jar belongs to the level. Children settle arguments by identifying which side of a painted line a drop began on.

The town’s society is organized less by profession than by responsibility for flows. There are River Keepers, Root Tenders, Heat Clerks, Fan Walkers, Kiln Hands, Crack Readers, and those who tend the blackened cables between levels. A person may mill grain, repair shoes, or cultivate fungus, but what matters publicly is which part of the Fold they must answer for when the warning lamps change.

The River Keepers are among the most respected. They wear glazed white cuffs so contamination can be seen immediately. At each division of the warm river, they measure depth with marked rods, taste for metal, skim the oil-bright surface, and open the gates in the prescribed order. Their authority is greatest in the dry months, when they can halt a foundry, close a bathhouse, or order a garden cut down to save drinking water. They are expected to show no favoritism. For this reason, they do not marry within the district where they serve.

Root Tenders possess a gentler reputation, though their work can be ruthless. They train vines, divide moss, harvest tubers, and decide which roots may remain when masonry begins to buckle. Before cutting a large root, they wrap it in wet cloth and wait through one full engine cycle. If the cloth tightens, the root is judged load-bearing, whether or not any builder understands how. Such roots are redirected rather than severed. Some of the oldest rooms are supported as much by living knots as by brick.

Crack Readers work in pairs. One marks; the other remembers.

They travel with chalks of different colors, thin wedges of bone, mirrors on jointed handles, and little bells that are placed against stone to amplify its smallest movement. They record every hairline fracture in books whose pages are varnished against damp. Their maps resemble branching trees more than streets. They distrust straight lines and anyone who promises permanence.

Among themselves, they say the Fold is not built in the fault. It is built in the fault’s hesitation.

The children are compact, quick, and difficult to keep to one level. They move through maintenance gaps that adults cannot enter, carrying messages, retrieving dropped tools, checking condensation jars, and hunting conduit beetles for the small payments offered by cable workers. They know the town through forbidden routes: behind cisterns, inside abandoned ducts, under the roofs of the submerged buildings in Reservoir Court.

They play quietly. One game requires them to pass a metal washer around a circle without producing any sound. Another, called Answering, begins with three taps made somewhere out of sight; the others must guess whether the reply comes from a child, a pipe, or the stone. Adults dislike this game and interrupt it whenever they notice.

Fold-born children draw the surface as a white ceiling.

Even those taught otherwise struggle to imagine a place without walls. Their suns are usually lamps with rays like cage bars, and their clouds resemble condensation leaves. They understand rain as water falling from close above. The claim that surface rain can cross a distance greater than the Reservoir Court sounds to them like a religious exaggeration.

Old people remember openness better. Some arrived when the fault was new, descending on ropes through daylight and dust. They speak of horizon, wind, and the smell before a storm, but their accounts conflict. One says the sky was blue; another insists it was mostly white. One remembers salt flats ringing in the heat. Another remembers how terrifying it was to stand where nothing prevented a person from walking forever.

The Fold treats these elders with courtesy but not complete belief.

Its most distinctive inhabitants are the Listeners.

A Listener is not chosen by examination. They are recognized after some episode in which they hear what others do not: a pump beginning to cavitate three levels away, a root splitting brick behind plaster, water finding a new path through gravel, or the first pulse of an answering event before the pressure reaches anyone else’s teeth. Some are children. Some lose the ability as they age. A few become unable to live normally because every belt, drip, footstep, and contracting pipe arrives to them as meaningful.

Listeners shave a small patch behind one ear and press that side of the head against walls. During low-tone events they sit beside the river with their hands immersed to the wrists. They do not claim the fault speaks. In fact, they object strongly to that word.

“Speech repeats itself on purpose,” they say. “Stone repeats because it cannot forget at once.”

Still, when the pipes begin their inaudible resonance, everyone watches the Listeners.

Households in the Fold are made by adjacency rather than ancestry. A sleeping drawer, a cooking ledge, a shelf for cups, and a share in a condensation leaf may constitute a home. Families expand vertically until a stair or load-bearing root prevents them. Unrelated people become kin by using the same kettle, tending the same sickbed, or sharing responsibility for a pressure valve. Privacy is possible, but solitude is regarded with concern. Someone who spends too long behind a closed door will soon hear a soft knock and the scrape of a cup being left outside.

Birth takes place near running water, if possible, and death near moving air.

Newborns are washed with water allocated months beforehand. Their names are not spoken loudly for the first several days, because infants already startle at the fault’s displaced echoes. Instead each child is given a temporary hand-sign. Some keep it throughout life, and conversations across machinery halls may be conducted almost entirely in these private gestures.

The dead cannot be buried in the shallow soil pockets, which are needed for food, nor burned without wasting fuel and fouling the vents. Bodies are taken to the hottest kilns during scheduled firing days. Bone and ash are mixed into pale ceramic markers, pipe labels, water plaques, and occasionally cups. Thus the dead remain throughout the Fold as practical objects bearing names worn smooth by touch.

To be made into a regulation plaque is considered an honor.

There are few animals, so the inhabitants attach significance to those that remain. Kiln lizards are tolerated in kitchens because they eat small pests and indicate whether a wall is heating unevenly. Transparent shrimp are used to test reservoir water; if they rise and cling to the sides, the water is diverted. Conduit beetles are disliked by cable workers but kept in tiny woven cages by children, who feed them insulation fungus and race them along warmed copper strips.

The pale rats in the upper roots occupy a less comfortable place in Fold belief. They are said to remember the surface. Dry Roof residents leave crumbs where roots enter the wall, hoping to coax them toward the blocked shaft. A rat descending into town is interpreted as warning against excavation. A rat climbing upward is taken as encouragement. Since the creatures almost always move sideways, arguments can last for weeks.

The broad river animal has accumulated more reverence than anyone admits. It is called the Hem, the Undercloth, Grandmother Silt, or simply the Fold Below, depending on the district. No formal rite concerns it. Nevertheless, children polish washers before leaving them, and adults rarely prevent this waste of metal. River Keepers include its basin in their measurements but never drive a rod into the deepest part.

Some say the missing washers adhere to its underside.

Some say there is a hole beneath it.

During the dry season, when its edges become visible under the iron water, the creature appears thin enough to lift. No one volunteers.

People eat in small, frequent meals because the heat spoils food quickly. Their staples are yellow tubers, bitter greens, bean paste, mushrooms, river shrimp, and flat cakes cooked on hot pipes. Peppers are prized not for warmth but because their sharpness cuts through the mineral taste that settles into everything. Salt is abundant, but clean salt is not. Blocks taken from above carry dust, old chemicals, and traces of dead machinery; kitchen salt must be dissolved, filtered, and recrystallized.

Celebrations are marked by waste within carefully agreed limits. An extra lamp is lit. A fan is run fast enough to stir hair. Someone pours water from one cup into another from shoulder height, making a bright, extravagant sound. At marriages, the couple are given two lengths of pipe joined by a flexible loop, symbolizing that they may move without breaking their connection. At coming-of-age, a young person spends one engine silence alone at a station for which they will later bear responsibility.

Music exists, but percussion is rare. The Fold’s instruments are breath-soft: ceramic flutes, bowed wires, water organs, and sheets of metal rubbed at the edge until they hum. Songs are short because long notes blend with the fault’s returning sounds. Choirs sing in staggered fragments, allowing the walls to supply missing voices. A melody may continue several minutes after the singers have stopped.

They have learned to distinguish these fading repetitions from the other kind.

When the stone answers, daily behavior changes with astonishing speed. Flames are covered. Belts are disengaged. Children are gathered into rooms that rest across multiple strata. River gates are opened so no single channel bears pressure alone. The Listeners descend. Everyone else waits in contact with something fixed—a pillar, a major pipe, a living root—as if the town can be held together by hands.

No disaster has followed, but afterward people exchange cups of warm water and forgive minor debts.

The builders beginning the downward stair are unlike the crews of the Dry Roof. The upward excavators tend to be old, patient, and attached to stories of sky. The downward crews are younger. They speak of space not as exposure but as relief: new reservoirs, new gardens, rooms in which two people might pass without touching.

They work near the natural grate where warm air rises smelling of leaves and metal. Their lamps are hooded because ordinary flame leans toward the openings. Seeds sprout in their pockets. Mold blooms overnight on tool handles. Chalk marks fade, not from moisture but under a fine green film that seems to withdraw when observed.

The first steps are suspended rather than anchored. Each hangs from three separate supports, in accordance with good practice. Yet the lowest tread has begun to gather condensation on its underside, though there is no open chamber beneath it.

Sometimes workers feel a vibration through the soles of their shoes: slow, regular, almost like the distant pumps.

The pumps are always stopped when this happens.

Construction continues.

Every basket of stone brought up is warm. Mixed among the basalt splinters are smooth pebbles, rounded by water no surveyor has reached. Once, a Root Tender found a severed tendril in the rubble. It was pale, freshly cut, and thicker than a thumb. Neither end had been touched by a blade.

They planted it beside the grate.

It did not grow upward. It pushed through the mesh, found the unfinished stairs, and began descending ahead of them.

Now, each morning, the stair builders follow the new length of root. They fit steps where it bends, landings where it coils, and railings where its side-shoots offer support. They say this is only practical. The root is mapping stable stone. The Root Tenders agree that living things often know where water lies.

But the oldest Listener has stopped visiting the river during the low tones.

She sits instead at the head of the new stair, one hand resting on the downward-growing root. Those who bring her food find the cup beside her untouched. When asked what she hears, she corrects them.

“Nothing,” she says.

Then, after a while:

“It is listening.”
