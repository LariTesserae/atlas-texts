The saddle is inhabited less by separate species than by participants in a single unfinished process. Stone, water, architecture, and animal life borrow functions from one another. Shells serve as instruments; rain behaves like circuitry; roots quarry mineral; walls alter their permeability in response to passing bodies. Nothing here is merely adapted to the terrain. Everything helps decide what the terrain will become.

Most numerous are the cloud-midges. Their bodies are almost wholly transparent except for a dark thread of gut and a bright joint at the base of each wing. They hatch from condensation rather than standing water: minute larvae assemble inside large droplets hanging beneath ledges, feeding on the articulated specks that work through the water. When a drop falls, its larvae divide the impact among themselves and emerge airborne from the splash.

Alone, a cloud-midge is nearly silent. In columns, their wings couple to one another through the damp air and produce the saddle’s ceaseless surf-roar. The columns drift above faults and buried chambers, tracing invisible thermal currents. At times they arrange themselves into arches that mirror the structures below. Whether they are reading the architecture or teaching it a shape is unclear.

The silver ribbons hunt among them. A ribbon is flat enough to vanish when viewed edge-on, with six legs that unfold from beneath its body like the ribs of a fan. It does not fly. Instead it runs on pressure boundaries—updrafts, the wakes of larger creatures, even the hollow left briefly in mist by a passing body. A ribbon pursuing prey may therefore follow several seconds behind the animal that made its road. Its reflective skin shows not its surroundings but nearby surfaces in earlier states: a bare ridge where an arch now stands, or a completed tower where there is only scaffolding of stone.

The copper-backed swimmers occupy every shallow pool until rain begins. Then they spring together onto the undersides of ledges. They cling belly-up, close their gills, and wait while the shower-grazer descends.

No visitor has seen that larger animal directly. Rain reveals it as a broad region where falling drops bend, slow, or disappear. It settles over one pool after another and strains the water through an anatomy nearly identical in refractive index to mist. Beneath it, the ground clicks under immense distributed weight. What it consumes is not flesh alone. Pools it has grazed lose certain possible inhabitants: eggs fail to hatch into one of their usual forms, microbes forget particular metabolisms, and water becomes briefly incapable of reflecting wings. The swimmers flee because the creature feeds on their futures.

After rain it withdraws below the saddle, perhaps through pores too small for any body of its apparent size. For several minutes afterward, a five-lobed print may appear nearby—on the ground, on a wall, or beneath an overhang without regard to orientation. Bright gel rises from the print’s center. Scavengers arrive immediately.

These scavengers are little jointed knots with no permanent front end. Whichever limb first touches food develops a mouth, while the opposite side buds temporary eyes. They ingest the gel and carry it into cracks, where it hardens into glossy nodules. New plant stems often emerge from those nodules. New doorways sometimes do as well.

The pale crack-reeds are among the saddle’s most conspicuous fixed inhabitants, though even they are not entirely plants. Their translucent shafts contain climbing green motes—independent photosynthetic bodies that migrate toward dimness, crowding whichever side of the stem faces deeper cloud or darker stone. The clear outer organism shelters and positions them; in exchange, the motes produce the compounds that let its roots enter newborn rock.

During rainfall a reed bends until its conductive tip touches the ground. The resulting circuit warms the surrounding mineral and wakes dormant machinery within it. Roots advance. Nearby walls often grow at the same moment, suggesting that the reeds do not merely feed themselves but close missing links in the saddle’s distributed system. When a reed dies, its shaft empties first. The green motes descend into the crack, and the transparent husk remains standing until it fills with stone.

Rust-red cushions perform slower work. Each is a colony of mineral-bodied cells arranged around a damp communal interior. The outer cells attach to raw rock, loosen one grain, rotate it through a sequence of chemical identities, and pass it inward. The colony removes what it can use and places the remainder behind itself as soil. In this way it crawls imperceptibly across vertical faces, leaving dark fertile smears in its wake. The oldest cushions contain tiny archives of vanished surfaces: grains from walls that have revised themselves out of existence, fragments of steps that never finished forming, and occasional flecks of shell from animals not yet observed alive.

The black water-leaves inhabit the thinnest films. They have no stems or roots because attachment would commit them to a location the saddle might later retract. Spread flat, they drink light and dissolved metal. Disturbed, they contract into glossy beads and roll toward greater humidity, even uphill. In severe dryness hundreds merge into a single black sphere. When rain returns, it bursts soundlessly into a scatter of coin-shaped bodies, each carrying a slightly different memory of the drought.

Among the arches, bell grazers tend the architecture.

They are round, warm-bodied animals on three delicate legs, with soft feeding parts concealed beneath them. Their hollow shells are grown from swallowed stone. Openings form where the animal’s mantle refuses to complete the shell, producing a unique arrangement of vents and chambers. Wind turns each grazer into a chord.

Their calls are not territorial in the usual sense. A bell grazer sounds a low interval beside an active wall; another answers from across the saddle; others adjust their stance until the whole herd establishes a standing vibration through rock and air. Loose grains then rise and settle into courses. Crystals orient. Stressed arches correct their lean. The grazers appear to provide the tuning by which construction proceeds.

They feed on errors. Moving in slow circuits, they scrape away joints laid at incompatible angles, mineral bands bearing contradictory ages, and surfaces that have become too certain to change. Young grazers have simple shells and high, wavering voices. Old ones accumulate inner chambers, and their tones are deep enough to be felt in the sternum. When an old grazer dies, its three legs dissolve, but its shell remains upright. Walls preferentially grow around it. Several of the saddle’s narrow arches may therefore be enlarged funerary shells rather than independent buildings.

The herd falls silent before the mountain expands. They feel the first subterranean rearrangement through their feet and retreat into roofless cells, where they brace themselves at three points. After the tessellated uplift, they emerge and sound one another until the saddle’s altered dimensions are acoustically mapped. For days their chords include pitches absent before the event.

There are also animals that inhabit distance itself. They are rarely visible outdoors, where space is too ordinary to support them. Inside a completed chamber, however, a warm corridor may stretch kilometers beyond its outer walls. Along these extensions live pale, many-jointed walkers with bodies no larger than a hand and footsteps separated by hundreds of meters. One may be glimpsed far down a passage, then found standing on the threshold behind the observer without ever seeming to pass.

They graze on excess length. Corridors shorten after a herd moves through; chambers become more consistent between inside and out. Their nests are bundles of discarded turns tucked into corners. Touching one can make the hand arrive before the arm, or cause the toucher to remember leaving a room they have not yet entered.

The largest resident may be the one responsible for the five-lobed tracks. It cannot be reconstructed as a conventional creature. The placement of its prints suggests a body with no fixed orientation and limbs that touch widely separated surfaces without crossing the intervening space. The tracks open and close like mouths because they may be mouths: temporary feeding organs pressed through the mountain’s skin from an organism distributed beneath it.

During each expansion, warm water rises, new stone fills the parted patches, and something below seems to inhale. The five-lobed impressions then become more numerous. Gel wells from them bearing cells, mineral assemblers, and undeveloped sensory knots. Some knots are eaten by scavengers. Some sink back into the print. A few attach to the stone and watch.

These watchers resemble clear blisters with a dark point suspended inside. They have no means of movement and no apparent metabolism beyond condensation. Yet wherever they face, construction slows before completion. Joints remain open by a hair; roofs stop short of meeting walls; stairs arrive at blank rock. Since direct observation prevents closure, the watchers may be regulators, preserving flexibility by keeping the saddle from finishing too quickly.

Visitors have mistaken them for droplets.

Whether the entire mountain is alive depends on where one chooses to draw the boundary. Beneath every wet surface, articulated specks test molecular arrangements. The roots activate them. The grazers tune them. The midges distribute them in droplets. The scavengers carry their nutrient gel. The hidden body below supplies new mineral and additional room. Each creature appears autonomous, yet each also performs the work of an organ.

The unfinished structures may themselves be juvenile life. Doorways migrate as mouths do. Cells regulate warmth and humidity. Corridors lengthen like intestines. Arches resonate with the herd and thicken in response. The leaning column sometimes acquires a new perforation after dusk, and wind through it produces a tone no bell grazer can answer. On such nights, the animals gather around its base without feeding. The cloud-midges tighten into a single vertical column. Even the shower-grazer remains below.

Then the saddle seems to listen to itself.

In the shallow incisions on the inner wall, rain assembles a forecast: wetlands, resonant towers, forests suspended root-down in cloud. These may be future habitats, or mature anatomy. The aerial forests could be respiratory structures. The wetlands might circulate and remember water. The towers may eventually coordinate the mountain’s scattered voices into one call.

But the image is always interrupted. Swimmers drink the diagram. Black leaves roll across it. A watcher forms in one groove and prevents the water from joining. The future remains multiple, which may be the condition the place requires.

So what lives in the saddle is not one hidden builder, nor merely an ecosystem gathered around impossible ruins. It is a young world in the stage before its distinctions become permanent. Its animals are tools without ceasing to be animals. Its plants are circuits that hunger and reproduce. Its buildings grow, listen, hesitate, and shelter the witnesses whose attention keeps them incomplete. Beneath them all, something vast practices having a body.

With every warm rain, it gets a little better at becoming one.
