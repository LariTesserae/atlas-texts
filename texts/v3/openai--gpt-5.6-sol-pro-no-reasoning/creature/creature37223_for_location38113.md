The people of the basin are slight, sure-footed, and deliberate. They have the compact strength of those who carry more than they roll: broad shoulders, practiced hands, calves hardened by stairs. Their clothing is layered wool and felt, gray, moss-green, clay-white, with brighter cords marking household, occupation, and route. Soft shoes quiet their passage over stone. Quarry workers pad their tools; herders wrap the hooves of goats before leading them across civic courts.

They do not know birth and death as transitions they themselves undergo. A child remains a child, an elder remains elderly, and each inhabits that condition completely. Children are not treated as unfinished adults, nor elders as people nearing an end. A child may have centuries of expertise in canal fish, hidden passages, or the precise behavior of finches. An elder’s authority comes not from being closer to the past—for everyone is equally old—but from the breadth and reliability of their attention.

Families are therefore founded by attachment rather than descent. No one remembers arriving, and no infant has appeared within living recollection, though there are children everywhere. Households adopt one another through shared meals, sleeping rooms, trades, and routes. A person may belong to a gardener’s court, a quarry crew, a rain fellowship, and three separate kin circles without contradiction. Inheritance matters little when possessions cannot be permanently lost. What passes between people is responsibility: tending a pool, maintaining a public stair, remembering when a difficult doorway is safe.

Their greatest virtue is exact notice.

A citizen is admired for seeing that the seventh bird has not yet settled, that the bronze plate bears condensation along its lower edge rather than its rim, or that the white goat has crossed above the arch instead of beneath it. Such distinctions are not pedantry. They are how one finds one’s way through a city whose places overlap according to circumstance. Every person carries a private atlas of sequences, and much of education consists of reconciling these atlases with those of others.

Surveyors are among the basin’s most respected inhabitants. They wear belts hung with cords, beads, wax tablets, and small tokens representing rain, animal, cloud, water, and communal action. They measure neither land alone nor time alone, but the agreements between them. A surveyor might describe a house as twelve paces beyond the silver-leaf grove, available after the finches descend, while three people are drawing water and no one stands beneath the second lintel. Their maps are woven rather than drawn. To read one is to run the fingers along crossings, knots, loops, and changes of texture.

Builders possess a more troubling status. Since every dismantled wall returns and every new wall remains, construction adds to the world without clearing it. Builders are creators of both shelter and complication. They proceed cautiously, first erecting full-sized plans from cord and poles, then waiting through several civic cycles to learn what routes the proposed structure might intersect. Even so, errors accumulate. A pantry may share its interior with a public stair under certain conditions; a bedroom may open briefly into a goat path high above the basin. Master builders are valued less for preventing impossibility than for making it inhabitable.

Quarry workers strike the mountains knowing that each removed block will persist in the city while the cliff restores itself. They regard stone not as a finite substance but as a kind of permission. Before splitting a face, they press both palms to it and remain still. This is not worship exactly. It is courtesy toward something that can be taken from endlessly and never consumed.

Gardeners are more conservative. Plants restore themselves, but gardening still matters because arrangement matters. A berry eaten returns; a branch trained against a warm wall remains trained without surrendering its former shape. Over long repetition, gardens have become layered compositions in which one plant may occupy several histories at once. Gardeners prune, bind, harvest, and transplant not to increase yield but to refine access. Their art lies in persuading abundance to become legible.

Food is plain and intensely local: bitter leaves, beans, berries, small fruits, goat milk, eggs, canal fish, coarse cakes baked on stone, mushrooms from wet overhangs, and aromatic reeds. Hunger returns, though bodies do not waste away. Meals are therefore neither necessary in the mortal sense nor merely ceremonial. Appetite is one of the city’s reliable motions, and sharing its satisfaction binds households together. Feasts differ from ordinary meals chiefly in arrangement, scent, color, and the number of people willing to occupy the same sequence.

Hunters and herders live with an old unease. An animal can be killed, butchered, and eaten; later it will be present again in its accustomed place. Some insist it is the same animal restored. Others say the pattern has supplied another. The animals themselves offer no answer. A goat may shy from a person who once injured it, yet also approach along its unvarying route. Consequently, slaughter is carried out quietly and with formal apology. Many households eat little meat, not because life is diminished by killing, but because it is unclear what killing means.

There are healers, though no wound remains. Their work is pain, fear, and the interval before restoration. They set bones that may later unset themselves back into wholeness, close cuts, cool burns, and sit beside the injured until the body resumes its accustomed form. They also treat afflictions that the basin does not correct: dread of a doorway, obsession with echoes, route-confusion, weariness of repetition, and the vertigo caused by remembering too many versions of the same room.

Memory, rather than age, is the principal burden of life.

People do forget, but unevenly. Some preserve immense sequences with perfect clarity. Others deliberately surrender memories through disciplines of silence, isolation beneath fixed rain, or the retelling of an experience until its details become communal and no longer feel privately owned. Specialists called unbinders help citizens loosen memories that have become intolerable. They do not erase them. They alter their paths, attaching a grief to a bird’s circuit, a fear to a garden task, or an old quarrel to a route rarely taken.

The most feared affliction is not death but fixation. A person may begin repeating a gesture with the certainty that it must be completed correctly: setting down the same jar, crossing the same bridge, speaking the beginning of the same sentence. Because repetition is already woven into the city, the illness can be difficult to recognize. Friends intervene when the act ceases to connect with anything beyond itself. The sufferer is led by unfamiliar routes, clothed in altered colors, and surrounded by patterns they have not learned.

Disputes rarely concern theft or permanent harm. They concern access, attention, noise, and the creation of inconvenient conditions. A new annex may make another household’s doorway unreliable. A herd driven too early may alter the sequence by which an entire ward reaches its workshops. Courts are held in open cistern yards, and judgments take the form of obligations: reroute the goats, attend the crossing, hold a screen before the rain, provide witnesses until a passage stabilizes. Punishment by confinement is considered cruel and dangerous. Exile is impossible.

Their speech is rich in aspect and poor in tense. They distinguish between what occurs habitually, what is presently manifest, what remains true while unwitnessed, and what returns upon attention. They have separate words for a thing restored, a thing duplicated, a thing encountered elsewhere in its cycle, and a thing whose sameness cannot be established. “Before” often means “under the prior arrangement.” “After” means “when this has made that possible.”

Names behave similarly. Each citizen has a quiet name used within the household, a public name associated with trade, and several situational names tied to routes or duties. One person may be Reed-Tender beneath the rain, Third Cord in the survey court, and She-Who-Waits-for-Goats near the eastern stair. Children often possess the most names because they slip into passages adults cannot use.

Government is distributed among custodians rather than rulers. The city has councils for water, roads, animals, construction, quiet, and sequence. Their authority overlaps, as does everything else. Major decisions are enacted experimentally in a small district and observed through many repetitions before being extended. There is little urgency, but there is also no final resolution. A debate can persist for what would elsewhere be generations, its participants unchanged, its arguments growing more precise.

The keepers of quiet hold unusual power. They line workshops with felt, regulate quarry blows, and settle disputes over speech carried across the basin. Shouting is permitted only to prevent immediate injury. Certain words may not be called aloud at all, particularly personal names and commands. The fear is not merely that an echo repeats sound, but that repetition grants it standing in the world. In secluded halls, scholars test this belief with whispers. Some claim to have heard echoes answer with words that were never spoken. Their reports are kept on wax rather than recited.

Religion, where it can be called religion, attends to permanence without personifying it. There are shrines to continuance: a dry stone beside a rain curtain, a bowl that is always full but never overflows, a lintel through which two incompatible courtyards can be reached. Offerings are made by arrangement, not destruction. Leaves are laid in patterns, cords are knotted, cups are turned mouth-down. The most solemn rite is to leave something unobserved and return together to discover what has become of it.

The city’s central disagreement concerns whether anything truly new is possible.

Builders say yes. Their walls exist where no walls stood in experience, and the city’s complexity demonstrably increases. Traditionalists reply that every innovation may be merely another portion of the complete arrangement becoming accessible. Explorers support both positions. They return from beyond the passes with accounts of unknown neighborhoods, yet those neighborhoods correspond too closely to familiar ones. Sometimes they bring back a tool whose owner recognizes it as one still hanging in their workshop.

Explorers are not bold in the ordinary sense, since they cannot die and cannot leave. Their courage lies in enduring disorientation. Beyond the passes, a traveler may meet companions approaching from the opposite direction who insist they have not yet departed. They may enter a distant house and find their own sleeping mat, or hear a conversation they remember but have not had. Expeditions train for years in identity discipline: reciting names, routes, attachments, and chosen obligations so that resemblance does not dissolve them.

Some return serene. Some refuse ever to approach the walls again. A few come back convinced that every citizen is one person encountered under different conditions. These are treated gently and never allowed to organize public routes.

Art is made from juxtaposition rather than preservation, since nothing needs preserving. Painters work on plaster that retains both old and new images, producing layered figures visible from different angles or during different civic sequences. Weavers make maps that can also be worn. Sculptors prefer clay because stone carries too much civic consequence. Performers stage quiet dramas along routes, allowing spectators to encounter scenes in varying orders. No two people see the same play, although every scene is repeated exactly.

Their music favors sounds that decay quickly: breath, gut strings muted by the hand, fingertips on clay. Sustained tones are distrusted. Choruses sing facing inward so that no voice escapes toward the mountain walls. The highest musical skill is to end a note so completely that the basin gives nothing back.

Festivals introduce variation without disruption. Citizens exchange occupational colors, cook familiar foods in unfamiliar courtyards, reverse customary walking orders, and pass through the fixed rain wearing masks of birds, goats, fish, and moths. Masks are important because faces never age. To wear another face, even briefly, is one of the few transformations wholly under human control.

Love exists intensely here, but without the promise of growing old together. Lovers cannot measure devotion by years, bodily change, or a shared approach toward death. They measure it through chosen recurrence. To love someone is to continue meeting them without necessity: at the same pool, after the same birds pass, while the same droplets stand on bronze. Separation is always possible, though departure is not. Former lovers may avoid each other for immense spans by choosing incompatible sequences, living in adjacent districts that never quite coincide.

Grief also exists. A person cannot be permanently killed, but they can become unreachable—lost in overlapping rooms, trapped in an obscure route, or so altered in habit that friends can no longer meet them. Some vanish beyond the passes and are glimpsed only as distant figures on other ridges. The city calls these people “elsewhere.” Memorials to them are not tombs but appointments: a place and circumstance at which someone continues to wait.

Many citizens harbor small private rebellions against completeness. They cast marked pebbles into pools to see whether they return. They whisper novel words into cloth. They enter doors at forbidden moments. They try to surprise animals from their circuits or remain awake through several rounds of communal activity. Usually the pattern absorbs these experiments. Occasionally something happens that no one remembers happening before.

Such events are never announced loudly.

A finch lands on the wrong roof. A rain curtain misses one drop. A child finds a warm stone. A surveyor’s cord develops a knot no hand tied. For a little while, witnesses gather in silence, frightened less by the anomaly than by the hope it produces. They record every circumstance. They argue over whether the event was new or merely unnoticed.

Then they return to their tasks. The goats cross the eastern stair. The stalls open. The padded hammers touch the suspended boards. Throughout the terraces, people lift jars, split stone, train branches, exchange hand signs, and thread themselves through the city’s impossible rooms.

They are neither prisoners nor immortals in the simple sense. They are custodians of a world that restores everything except certainty, living forever inside the difference between recurrence and return.
