They call themselves the Attendants, though there is no agreement about what they attend.

The name may once have referred to the buried systems. It may have meant the basin, the rain, the long experiment of keeping incompatible forms of life from destroying one another. Now it means anyone who wakes beneath these sweating hills and accepts that the day’s first task may not exist until after breakfast.

They are recognizably human, for the most part. The distinction matters less here than it would elsewhere. Generations of damp, pollen, engineered symbionts, and intimate exposure to municipal organisms have made small revisions.

Their skin is often patterned with pale branching marks, fine as leaf veins, which darken before heavy rain and glimmer faintly when the subsurface machinery becomes active. These are not tattoos, though many ornament them with ink. Along their fingers and toes grow narrow ridges that improve their grip on wet bark. Their feet are broad and flexible from a lifetime on yielding roads. Most carry a local ecology in the folds of their clothing: harmless mites, dormant spores, seed burrs, tiny snails, and the translucent eggs of insects whose purpose may be domestic, civic, or unknown.

Nothing they wear remains only clothing for long.

Garments are woven loosely from reed fiber, root silk, shed bark, and fungal felt, allowing water to pass without dragging at the body. Collars sprout moss. Belts put down tendrils if left on a wall overnight. Rain capes are waxed by living scale insects kept in stitched channels along the shoulders. Formal robes contain pockets for pollinators, and mourning clothes are seeded with pale flowers that open only after repeated wetting.

Children are taught to shake out their sleeves before entering a house, not from cleanliness, but courtesy. A nesting creature may have mistaken them for public space.

The Attendants do not build in the ordinary sense. They negotiate growth.

Their most respected workers are called Tuners. A Tuner must hear the difference between a house settling, a root preparing to divide, and a buried mechanism attempting to instantiate a railway station through someone’s kitchen. They carry coils of colored cord, tuning forks of bone and dull silver, stoppered gourds of scent, packets of dormant insects, and shallow ceramic bowls. Their expertise consists of interventions so slight they resemble superstition until an entire bridge turns, slowly and correctly, toward the opposite bank.

Tuners work in groups because the town responds poorly to certainty. One sings into the structure. One watches the water. One interprets the insects. One remains deliberately idle in case the system requires an uncommitted observer. Apprentices are often assigned to misunderstand the plan aloud. Occasionally the buried intelligence seems to recognize an error only after hearing it expressed.

There are also Keepers of Passage, who maintain the rule that doors remain open during heavy rain. They carry no authority in dry weather. As soon as runoff begins, they can enter any dwelling, redirect furniture, cut temporary channels through walls, and requisition rooms for strangers, animals, or water. Their insignia is a knot that cannot be tightened. Many are elderly, because floods trust people who move without haste.

The pollen couriers are tended by Scentwrights, who compose messages without words. A request for medicine might smell to humans like crushed pepper and warm clay, while the pollinators perceive an exact address, degree of urgency, permissible routes, and whether repayment is expected. Public announcements bloom from chamber flowers in bands of color. Private ones arrive as a dusting on the wrist.

Errors occur. A declaration of marriage once cross-pollinated with a reservoir warning and caused three families to evacuate into one another’s households. They remained together afterward, though the town records still disagree on whether this constituted an accident, a wedding, or successful flood management.

At the marsh edge live the Gut Gardeners, who follow the broad-backed herbivores along their browsing corridors. They know which seeds must be swallowed, which conduits emerge from dung, and which architectural plants become aggressive if grazed from the wrong direction. Their arms are scarred from reeds and their voices are soft enough not to disturb digestion. Children admire them because they can identify an animal by the seedlings in its footprints.

Within the houses, domestic labor is inseparable from anatomy. Families massage cramped walls. They scrape excess translucence from sleeping chambers. They feed mineral paste into cracks and trim ducts before they begin breathing into unsuitable rooms. A healthy dwelling smells of resin, wet stone, yeast, and the particular people who inhabit it.

Ownership is therefore a weak and temporary idea.

A household may care for a trunk for seven generations and still wake to find its western chambers attached to another family’s stairwell. Such rearrangements are negotiated over hot infusions and diagrams drawn on leaves. If the disputed room first appeared in a child’s dream, the child speaks before either household. If animals have already nested there, their routes must be preserved. If the room contains silver capillaries, nobody sleeps in it until a Tuner determines whether it is a bedroom, a conduit, or the beginning of an event.

The Attendants value hospitality, but not from uncomplicated generosity. They know walls can change allegiance. Anyone refused shelter today may be sleeping in one’s own kitchen tomorrow without having moved.

Their councils meet in flowering chambers that alter color with the water reserves. Debate is conducted around the condition of the room. When the petals pale, speakers shorten their remarks. When they deepen to violet, proposals involving wells are suspended. Pollinators drift among the participants and sometimes revise the chamber’s verdict by landing in unexpected combinations.

No vote is considered valid until something nonhuman has interfered.

The council includes human delegates, but also appointed advocates for migratory birds, architectural plants, marsh water, unclassified organisms, and structures not yet present. The advocate for future structures is traditionally the youngest person able to remain silent through a full thunder cycle. Their function is to reserve corridors, light, drainage, and social patience for things the town has not imagined.

The dead remain involved in civic life, though not as ghosts.

Bodies are given to root crypts beneath the oldest dwellings, where memory tissues separate slowly from flesh. What returns is incomplete: habits of pressure, fragments of sensory preference, recurring spatial desires. A grandmother may survive as a house’s tendency to warm one niche at dawn. A bridge may retain the caution of the engineer buried near its anchorage. A council chamber that interrupts pompous speakers with sudden drips is believed to contain several generations of municipal clerks.

Names are not reliably preserved. Arrangements are.

For this reason, the Attendants ask of their elders not “Who were you?” but “What did you hold together?”

Their children learn practical uncertainty before arithmetic. They are taught to test each stair with heel, ear, and shadow. They memorize the difference between thirteen actual steps and thirteen visible ones. They learn never to eat blue-veined fruit while the underground thunder is audible, though every generation produces a child who does and returns with an account no adult can verify.

Games are played with temporary geography. Children race along routes that exist only while two clouds overlap. They float leaf boats through houses during rain and wager where the current will carry them. They collect shed door membranes and make them into kites that pull toward rooms not yet grown.

They are less frightened by change than by repetition.

A doorway that remains in the same place too long is watched. A pond that reflects the same stars on consecutive nights is covered. If frogs call in an identical rhythm three evenings running, Tuners are summoned. Perfect recurrence suggests that one of the buried intelligences has narrowed its attention and may be trying to force the basin into a fixed state.

They remember the disasters of classification.

In one, a council declared a fast-growing vine to be infrastructure and pruned away its flowers. Pollinators abandoned the district, door membranes thickened shut, and for a season every road led to the same empty courtyard. In another, a luminous fungus was designated ornamental, only for the town to lose nocturnal drainage because the fungus had been instructing water where to go.

Since then, every useful thing must be permitted uselessness, and every beautiful thing is assumed capable of structural responsibility.

At dawn, the Attendants emerge from amber chambers into steam-bright streets. They inspect the vein marks on one another’s skin and exchange weather predictions. They empty beetles from drinking cups, coax stairs into comfortable angles, and listen at their thresholds before stepping through. Breakfast may be sour rootcakes, marsh eggs, clear fruit, and algae scraped from the inner face of a warm window. They eat quickly when the ground is quiet. Quiet rarely lasts.

When the humming begins, they do not flee.

Tuners lift their instruments. Keepers open every door. Scentwrights release warning swarms. Children are called down from the bridges, then immediately climb onto safer roofs for a better view. The elderly place bowls of rainwater at likely corners. Families take hold of one another and of the town, feet spread on the flexing rootways.

They know the emergence may crush a house, cure a failing cistern, uncover an old district, or introduce a species that will spend the next century arguing chemically with the doors. They know it may be magnificent and inconvenient. They know it may arrive already inhabited.

When the water domes and green shoots appear within it, the Attendants watch the birds descend with twigs.

That is the first clue.

Not a tower, then, or not only a tower. Not merely a forest. Whatever is rising expects nests. It may expect songs, eggs, predators, shade. It may have remembered an architecture in which habitation begins above the ground.

The people step forward as the surface splits.

Some carry cords. Some carry bowls. Some carry food for strangers.

And among them waits the child appointed to speak for structures not yet present, silent until the new thing has finished becoming local enough to hear.
