The people of the groundless valley are light-footed, patient, and seldom empty-handed.

They live in a world where every useful thing must be carried in, grown, caught from the air, or taken carefully from something older. This has shaped them more deeply than height. They do not think of the valley as artificial. To them, the distinction between built and living is an outsider’s abstraction, like separating a shell from the creature that made it. A bridge settles, flowers, acquires shops, loses a lane, and is eventually rendered into another bridge. A family does much the same.

Children learn clipping before writing. In the upper districts, toddlers wear short harnesses with padded hooks, and doorways have low tether rails polished by generations of small hands. Their first games teach balance: crossing painted lines, passing bowls without spilling them, judging whether a railing is merely hot or dangerously hot. They learn never to chase a dropped object. When something slips beyond reach, even something precious, they shout the old warning and let the valley take it.

They know rain by sound. A child may be unable to name distant districts on a map yet distinguish water ticking through ceramic leaves from water striking an overloaded catch plate. During brief showers they run with cups, cloths, and stoppered bottles, helping direct the runoff. Cistern courts belong to them for the short interval after rain. They splash among seed husks and silver insects until the pump bells sound, then scramble out before the grates begin to draw.

By adolescence, most can read a structure’s disposition. They notice fresh powder beneath a joint, the pitch of a strained cable, the slight tremor that means a freight lift is approaching three floors away. They know which vine ropes are trustworthy, which grilles conceal drops, and which walls carry voices. Courtships begin by exchanging routes: a private stair, a fig-shaded beam, a maintenance balcony from which sunset strikes the eastern cladding like fire.

Adults tend to be compact and strong through the legs and back. Even clerks climb constantly. Clothes are close-fitting, breathable, and crowded with loops. Loose sleeves are ceremonial. Jewelry is wired, knotted, or pierced through fabric; keys and tools hang from short cords. Sandals have soft gripping soles, while foundry and rail workers wear heavy boots with replaceable magnetic or cleated plates.

People dress by district light. Upper-ridge fabrics are pale, reflective, and wrapped around the head and neck against glare. Orchard workers wear greens and yellows that hide citrus stains. In the damp interiors, clothes are dark and waxed, often striped with mineral residue from pipes and walls. Hanging-district residents favor vivid colors so companions can locate them across layered galleries. Their laundry, tethered at every corner, makes bright horizontal gardens of its own.

Their houses are rarely self-contained. A room belongs partly to the wall that cools it, the garden that shades it, the cistern beneath it, and the household on the other side of the ventilation grate. Cooking may occur three bridges away in a communal heat court. Bathing follows water-release schedules. Sleeping chambers open into shared balconies where neighbors keep herbs, tools, pigeons, and small shrines.

Privacy is acoustic rather than visual. Curtains of beads, leaves, chain, or knotted cord indicate that a space should not be entered, though silhouettes remain visible and conversations travel freely. People grow skilled at not hearing. A neighbor’s argument emerging from a shaft is treated like pump noise unless one’s name is called twice.

Families are defined by maintenance as much as blood. A household may consist of siblings, former spouses, apprentices, widowed neighbors, water partners, and whoever inherited responsibility for the same stretch of wall. Adoption is common, formalized by adding a person’s mark to the repair ledger. The most binding relationships are sometimes between those who share a cistern throat, root bed, lift cable, or evacuation route.

Names often carry location without claiming permanence. A person may be called by a tower, stair, trade, or color of tile, then change that name when the district migrates. Formal records preserve chains of former addresses:

**Sera of West Lime, formerly Third Fig, born beneath the Red Course, keeper of Gate Twenty-Seven.**

Such names are not considered cumbersome. They are maps of obligation.

The valley’s essential workers are the listeners.

They descend before dawn with hammers, tuning forks, chalk, wax tablets, and little cups of water. They strike columns and read the returning note. They press their ears to conduits. They watch the surface of water for tremors too slight to feel through boots. They mark cracks not simply by width but by season, temperature, nearby root growth, and the rhythm of passing machinery.

Some are structural engineers in the formal sense. Many are hereditary specialists whose expertise belongs to one tower, one arch, even one long-lived fracture. A family may tend the same leaning pier for two hundred years, accumulating observations no central archive can fully preserve. Their records include diagrams, but also phrases:

*Sleeps badly in east wind.*

*Opens after third hot week.*

*Carries trains well, dislikes empty reservoirs.*

They do not regard such language as metaphor. A structure’s behavior is too complex and too historically particular to be described only by numbers.

Gardeners hold comparable authority. Because roots are structural, horticulture is never merely decorative. Root wardens train figs through masonry channels, prune trees to balance deck loads, and decide when fungal growth is sealing a seam or digesting it. Orchard cooperatives negotiate with engineers over shade, wind drag, and water allocation. Removing a mature tree may require hearings involving three districts, a tram authority, two households, and the keeper of a shrine built into its trunk.

The most respected gardeners can imagine weight in decades. They plant not where a tree fits now, but where its roots will bind a wall fifty years later. Their successors inherit these intentions. Certain terraces are famous for designs whose full shape no planter lived to see.

Water keepers possess a quieter power. They manage gates, condensation webs, cistern priorities, and the disputed distinction between leakage and tributary. They travel constantly, carrying ringed staffs used to lift grates and test channel depth. Many shave or tightly bind their hair to keep it from machinery. Their hands are scarred by mineral salts and perpetually smell of metal, algae, and wet ceramic.

They are expected to be fair, but fairness in the valley does not mean equal quantities. A foundry may receive less water than a nursery yet more certainty. A hanging district may accept irregular flow in exchange for longer evening releases. Water agreements are sung or recited at seasonal gatherings because written schedules cannot account for settling walls, swollen gates, invasive roots, or a week of rain that fell only on one mountainside.

When a new trickle appears, water keepers watch it before intervening. They taste it, measure its warmth, follow insects gathering around it, and ask what lies above. If it remains for a year and harms nothing, residents may tile its path. After ten years it acquires a name. After fifty, people insist it has always been there.

Salvagers are the agents of the valley’s slow migrations. They dismantle structures with almost ceremonial restraint. Nails, brackets, tiles, panes, cable sheaths, stair treads, and pipe collars are separated, cleaned, measured, and marked for their next use. Children follow salvage crews hoping for fragments too small to inventory. Adults come to say farewell to walls.

A district being thinned does not vanish all at once. First its bells are removed. Then its public lamps migrate, one by one, so darkness gathers gradually. Plants are transplanted where possible. Door lintels engraved with household marks are carried to the receiving district. The final surviving platform may remain for years with a tea stall and a view into emptiness, tended by someone who refuses to leave until the replacement bridge reaches its promised socket.

This refusal is respected. So is departure. The valley has room for both kinds of loyalty.

Foundry workers, potters, loom tenders, machinists, rail crews, pump crews, insect keepers, lamp cleaners, guano collectors, and mirror polishers form dense professional communities. Most trades have distinctive bells and hand signs, necessary where machinery overwhelms speech. A rotating fist means shut the valve. Two fingers drawn downward mean falling object. A palm held flat and tilted indicates that the floor ahead has changed since yesterday.

Meals are rich in leaves, beans, peppers, fermented grain, citrus, eggs, goat milk, mushrooms, fish from settling basins, and small amounts of pork. Nothing edible is permitted to fall unconsidered. Markets stretch nets beneath their stalls; sweepings are sorted for animals, compost, dye, fuel, or fungal beds. Fruit damaged by parakeets is made into sharp sauces. Algae becomes broth, lamp oil, pigment, and feed.

Food tastes of smoke, mineral water, hot metal, and flowers. Each elevation has its preferred fermenting organisms. People claim to recognize a district’s altitude from its pickles.

Goats are treated with affectionate exasperation. Their bells identify ownership, route, and temperament, though the animals frequently exchange districts without permission. Pigs beneath the markets belong collectively to the waste guilds. Bees are discussed as neighboring households. Orchard keepers announce pruning and construction to their hives by tapping coded rhythms on the wall boxes.

Monkeys are thieves, nuisances, omens, and unofficial inspectors. If they cease using a familiar beam, workers examine it. If they cluster around a conduit, someone checks for warmth or water. Freight operators pretend to resent them but leave certain platform corners unobstructed. Stories tell of monkeys carrying messages across broken routes, though in most versions they eat the message and deliver only its cord.

The people maintain similar relationships with swifts, bats, geckos, foxes, and the pale insects of the lower galleries. Creatures are not romanticized. They bite, foul machinery, steal food, and spread disease. Yet a sudden absence is feared more than an infestation. Life’s patterns are among the valley’s instruments.

Religion, where practiced, gathers around continuities rather than creators. Small shrines occupy joints, landings, pump rooms, tree hollows, and the sealed ends of obsolete pipes. Offerings are useful things: a cup of clean water, a new clamp, oil for a hinge, thread, salt, a cutting from a healthy plant. Saints and ancestral figures are often depicted holding structures apart with their bodies, catching falling children, or listening with one ear pressed to a wall.

There are also the Door Vigils. Families maintain doors that open onto empty air in expectation of future walkways. They repaint them, oil their locks, and hang lamps beyond the threshold on short iron arms. On designated nights, they set a meal before the unopened door for descendants, builders, or strangers who may someday arrive from the other side.

Some promised bridges are eventually built. Many are not. The vigil is not considered wasted. A maintained possibility has value of its own.

Death is understood as redistribution. Bodies are usually cremated in the industrial kilns during low-production hours, and the ash is divided: some mixed into orchard soil, some sealed into ceramic memorial tiles, some released into designated updrafts. Those lost to falls are mourned without remains. Their families hang weighted cords from the last known place and let them descend as far as they can. The cord stays for a year and a day, gathering rust, pollen, grease, and leaves from unseen levels below.

Afterward it is drawn back up and woven into a household mat.

The valley’s festivals follow maintenance cycles. At the first major rain, children decorate drain mouths with petals and polished scrap. When the shutters undergo their annual overhaul, whole mountainsides flash in elaborate patterns before returning to their slow noon dimming. Rail closures become night markets. Reservoir cleaning exposes tiled floors normally underwater, and musicians perform there for the extraordinary echo.

The greatest gatherings mark a joining: a new walkway reaching an old door, a diverted watercourse entering a district, two tram lines finally sharing a station. Before a bridge opens, people from both sides approach but stop short of the center. Builders place the last fastening between them. The oldest resident on one side and the youngest walking child on the other cross together.

No one says the bridge is finished. They say it has begun to carry.

The inhabitants of the lowest mapped districts are paler, sharp-eyed, and accustomed to reading air movement by skin. They cultivate fungi, ginger, mosses, translucent greens, and insect larvae in the warm damp. Many work without a clear day-and-night cycle, measuring time by lift arrivals, lamp changes, and the pulse of pumps. Their songs have long pauses that let distant echoes answer.

They know more about the depths than they tell surveyors, though perhaps less than upper residents imagine. They speak of obsolete stairs that continue below official barriers, of forests growing inverted from the undersides of forgotten decks, and of warm chambers where condensation falls like permanent rain. They sometimes trade pale leaves, eyeless fish, unfamiliar spores, and ceramic objects bearing marks no active guild recognizes.

When asked where these came from, they name directions rather than places:

Below the third warm wind.

Past the tower that rings without being struck.

Downstream, though the water climbs.

Whether these answers are secrecy, habit, or precision depends on who is listening.

The valley’s people are not fearless. They fear fire most, then interrupted water, then silence from machinery that should be running. They fear an unfamiliar vibration underfoot, birds erupting from a tower at night, a lift arriving empty when it departed full. They fear sudden change because the valley survives by changing slowly.

But they do not fear age.

Age is what gives a wall its known behavior, a garden its deep soil, a route its customs, a machine its attentive keepers. New structures are watched with suspicion until they have stained, shifted, hosted nests, and survived enough seasons to become legible. Perfection is considered brittle. Reliability begins when an object has revealed the ways in which it fails.

At evening, residents come out onto balconies and grated galleries as irrigation enters the gardens. They eat, mend harnesses, shell beans, tune instruments, scrape corrosion from tools, and watch birds cross the luminous gap. Neighbors call through foliage. Trams move behind leaves like traveling rooms of light. Far below, lamps expose fragments of other lives: a woman bathing a child beside a warm pipe, a crew hauling mushrooms from a service shaft, a solitary keeper crossing a bridge no map still names.

Above them, the pale mountains release the day’s heat.

Below them, unseen engines keep the depths breathing.

Around them, roots tighten in old masonry, water chooses among imperfect channels, and ten thousand maintained things lean gently into one another.

This is what lives in the valley: not a population settled upon a structure, but a people threaded through it—carried by it, repairing it, consuming it, feeding it back to itself. They are another load the arches have learned to bear, and another means by which the arches endure.
