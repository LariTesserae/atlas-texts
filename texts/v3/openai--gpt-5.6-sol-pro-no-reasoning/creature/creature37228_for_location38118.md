The only inhabitants of the cistern are its keepers—and even they live there only by interval.

They arrive in pairs or small crews through the door behind the lime kilns, carrying reed brushes, ceramic buckets, waxed cloth, and little dishes of fresh mortar. They leave their sandals above the third step. Their feet are broad and pale with lime dust, their shins marked by old scrapes from brickwork and roof tile. Most are masons or water tenders rather than servants: people who can distinguish a harmless settling crack from a dangerous one, judge a blocked conduit by its note, and find loosened plaster by passing a thumb across the wall.

In the full reservoir they descend only as far as the submerged landing. Their clothes are belted high or reduced to simple working wraps. Light trembles over their bodies until skin, cloth, and masonry share the same shifting yellow-green pattern. Speech is difficult beneath the vault, so they use touches and compact gestures: two fingers toward a conduit, a palm held flat for steady flow, a closed fist for a sluice, a tap against stone to summon attention. They know that every careless sound returns multiplied.

When the basin is drained for cleaning, they move across its exposed floor barefoot. One loosens the pale silt with a wooden rake while another scoops it into weighted jars. A third inspects the settling squares and clay drain cones. They scrub algae from the brightest walls with bundled reeds, but faint green traces remain in imperfect seams. Where a crack has opened, someone kneels to press in lime paste, smooths it with a wet thumb, then paints a red mark beside it and cuts a small date sign into the plaster.

No permanent custodian occupies the ledge. No hermit sleeps in a niche. No shrine keeper tends a flame. The dry spaces are too narrow, the air too damp, and an oil lamp above the drinking water is forbidden. The rope beneath its bowl, the rakes on their pegs, and the submerged jars are equipment, not possessions. Nothing here suggests a private life.

The cistern’s nearest thing to a resident is therefore the water itself: warm, luminous, and continually replaced. It enters carrying traces of the town—ash, pollen, terracotta dust, broom fiber, the heat of roofs and washing courts—yet is kept clear enough that the cream floor remains visible below. It occupies nearly every available space and changes character by the hour. At dawn it pulses gently from household channels. In rain it becomes violent and many-voiced, striking the surface in six white columns. At noon it appears almost materialized from light.

A tentative second life clings to the masonry. Thin green films inhabit the sunniest plaster. Transparent strands of waterweed root in two flawed seams, streaming sideways when the conduits strengthen. Moss pads soften the splash zones. These growths are not welcomed, but neither are they wholly absent; they persist at the edge of the keepers’ reach, scraped back each month and beginning again.

Nothing else lives there. The intake screens exclude beetles, larvae, frogs, fish, and snails. No animal breaks the surface or hides beneath the steps. If one appears, it is not regarded as a new inhabitant but as evidence of failure somewhere above.

So the chamber remains inhabited chiefly by labor, water, and light. Human beings enter to preserve its emptiness. Their red inspection marks accumulate on the walls; their thumbprints remain in repairs; their tools wait in designated places. Then they climb out, close the hidden door, and leave the cistern to its pouring channels and wandering beams—an immense bright vessel whose only enduring life is the green growth continually beginning, and the town continually removing.
