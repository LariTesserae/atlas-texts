What lives in the basin is not one species so much as an arrangement of thirsts.

Nearest the surface are the runners: translucent, many-legged creatures scarcely distinguishable from the films they inhabit. Their backs are broad and clear, bending the basin’s weak light so that each resembles a mobile lens. At rest they bead themselves into the shallow cups of the crust. Alarm flattens them further, legs folded beneath, until even a searching eye sees only rain trembling on stone.

They gather wherever two waters disagree. At the meeting of clear seepage and black brine, soft mineral curds continually form, and the runners harvest them before they can harden. Their vibrating mouthparts comb iron, lime, and manganese from the wet boundary. A large congregation sounds like sand being poured somewhere far away. Their bodies take on the chemistry of their feeding grounds: rust-colored joints beside iron springs, milky backs around carbonate leaks, dark mirrored shells near the oily vents. Whether this is camouflage, nourishment, or the beginning of petrification is unclear.

They molt often. Their cast skins do not decay. They become flakes in the crust, preserving tiny jointed limbs until another layer seals them away. Thus much of the pavement is made, in part, from generations of runners.

Above them drift the sac-fliers. They are pale, loose-bodied things, more bladder than animal, with fine vanes or cilia that barely trouble the mist. They do not fly by beating the air. Instead they lean into electrical gradients, slipping between charged chimneys and waking cables. When the buried works send out a pressure pulse, the fliers answer with cold blue light. A swarm then reveals the hidden system more clearly than any plan: conduits glow beneath the crust, valves appear as brief knots of brightness, and every active chimney acquires a revolving halo.

Their light may be communication, discharge, or waste. They pulse more rapidly when hunting. A flier settles over a runner like a collapsing membrane, enclosing it for a few moments. When it lifts, the runner remains as an empty mineral likeness, delicate enough to break beneath a droplet. In quiet periods the fliers contract under ledges and rust collars. Their pale bodies gather soot and precipitate until they look like old caulking or clusters of dull stone. Some remain still for months, waking only when the correct circuit beneath them carries current again.

Deeper in the place live the pipe-creatures. They are long, blind, and slow, with overlapping scales the colors of tarnished machinery: blackened copper, gunmetal, lead white, bruised violet. Their heads are blunt enough to pass through valves, and their bodies compress around bends no vertebrate spine should tolerate. Fine sensory filaments trail from their jaws and test water for warmth, dissolved metal, vibration, and charge.

They spend most of their lives inside the buried network, following heated channels and feeding where drains deliver runners and dormant fliers. Their passage produces the traveling knocks heard beneath the basin. What sounds like a loose hammer may be a plated body negotiating an elbow. What seems to be a valve cycling may be a creature forcing itself through the valve.

Heavy rain enlarges their world. When alleys flood and corroded grilles disappear beneath the water, the pipe-creatures emerge. They move between the chimneys without haste, curving around columns as though remembering passages that existed before the mineral forest formed. Droplets chime against their backs. Occasionally one pauses beside the half-buried hut and presses its head to the wall, attending to the cabinets within.

By dawn they usually return underground. Those stranded by falling water settle into seams and become rigid. Mineral skins accumulate over them, making low ridges indistinguishable from pipes. Some may remain that way until a later flood softens their joints. Others may never have been animals at all.

There are smaller inhabitants as well, if inhabitant is the right word. Threadlike forms occupy the pores of fresh carbonate and turn collectively when pressure changes. Dark motes circulate through the perfectly spherical beads without breaking their surfaces. Something delicate and branching lives on the inner faces of condensation-fogged windows, withdrawing whenever the glass is wiped. None appears to feed in an ordinary sense. They sort ions, equalize charges, exploit minute differences of temperature, and leave behind substances the machinery can use.

The machinery itself may also be alive.

It senses interruption, though belatedly. It redistributes flow when a leak is stopped. It incorporates foreign tools and converts them into outlets. It changes its production after receiving punched cards whose encoded purpose has otherwise been lost. Its organs are tanks, pipes, valves, buried wheels, and mineral chimneys, but many of those organs are replacements formed by corrosion. The old metal fails; deposited stone assumes its shape. A pipe wall dissolves while the accretion around it hardens into a new conduit. A broken valve becomes a colony of smaller vents. The works survive by ceasing, piece by piece, to be the machine that was built.

Its timescale is difficult to read. A pressure cycle might be a breath, a reflex, a step in an unfinished industrial program, or all three. The basin’s creatures respond to it as animals respond to weather. The works in turn respond to their movements: runners clear soft deposits from outlets, fliers bleed charge from active columns, pipe-creatures open constricted channels with their bodies. Predator, scavenger, maintenance organism, and machine component are not separate roles here.

Even the water behaves like a population. Clear, black, metallic, and unwetting varieties emerge from different mouths, meet, separate, exchange burdens, and travel according to rules not fully explained by slope. The spherical beads wait together in hollows, then migrate at a shared vibration. Rain entering the basin loses some ordinary freedom and joins this circulation. Water leaving at the southern ditch becomes common again, but whatever pattern moved through it remains behind.

No single inhabitant governs the place. The runners do not understand the fliers. The fliers follow fields produced by mechanisms without known operators. The pipe-creatures patrol a buried anatomy whose boundaries shift each year. The machinery obeys instructions issued through damaged cards, blocked vents, rainfall, footsteps, and echoes. Each lives inside the consequences of the others.

Together they form the basin’s true occupant: a cool, patient metabolism distributed among animals, minerals, water, and ruined industry. It has no visible center and answers every touch somewhere else. It feeds on gradients. It remembers by accretion. It repairs itself through corrosion and reproduces by turning whatever remains within its boundary into another wet, trembling part of the works.
