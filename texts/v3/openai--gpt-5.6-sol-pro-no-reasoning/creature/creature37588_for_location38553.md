The people of the dry bloom are difficult to distinguish, at first, from the systems they maintain.

They are tall without seeming large, narrow through the chest and hips, with long hands adapted to reach between fibers without tearing them. Their bodies are pale in the local manner—not bloodless white, but the layered colors of things grown away from weather: ivory, gray-violet, resin amber, sometimes a faint metallic green around the joints. Across their skin run fine branching lines. These are not veins exactly, though they darken with exertion. They are conductive tissues, channels through which salts and electrical impulses pass.

They are warm-bodied, but only modestly so. In the city’s steady air, heat is too expensive to waste. Their skin is dry and faintly textured, made of overlapping microscopic scales that close against dust. They do not sweat. When overheated, they unfold thin vascular fans from behind the jaw, under the arms, and along the ribs, exposing dark pleated surfaces to the moving air. At rest these fold flat and resemble seams in a garment.

Their eyes are broad, dark, and highly reflective. Pupils contract into branching shapes beneath the full municipal lamps and widen again at plum twilight. Color matters less to them than polarization, electrical corona, and the subtle shimmer that reveals stress in a living wall. They can look at a road and see where its fibers are pulling apart. They can tell which ceiling fronds have ceased turning before any human observer would notice a change in the light.

Most have no external ears. Instead, a collar of sensitive bone surrounds the neck and upper chest. Through it they hear the cavern twice: once through the air and once through the ground. A voice is therefore not only a sound but a pressure pattern arriving through the vegetal mat. Their speech is soft, economical, and rich in low consonants made with the throat closed. Important announcements are often transmitted directly into the streets, where they can be felt through the feet long before they are understood as words.

Their mouths are small. Teeth are dark, glassy plates that replace themselves from behind as mineral grit wears them down. They eat little in public. Much of their nourishment comes as dense wafers of fungal tissue, compressed salt paste, aromatic gas trapped in porous cakes, and translucent slices cut from cultivated storage organs. Food is chewed slowly and followed by long periods of stillness while symbiotic chambers in the torso extract useful compounds. To visitors, their meals smell more like batteries, cedar chests, and hot pottery than anything edible.

They do not drink.

The city’s absence of water is written into every part of them. Their cells suspend chemistry in waxes, ionic gels, and ammonia-rich colloids. Their blood is not liquid enough to spill. If wounded, they release a soft puff of pale granules; exposed tissue contracts and plates over with clear resin. Scar tissue remains glossy for years. Surgeons work with heated ceramic blades, electrostatic combs, and grafts grown from the patient’s own shed scales.

Their clothing adds to the impression that they are partly architectural. Coats are laminated from thin bark, flexible at the joints and rigid across the spine. Work garments contain pockets of smothering powder and strips of conductive cloth for grounding static charge. Masks are common near pollen works and stone-cutting fronts. Helmets carry sensitive rootlets that test the air for combustible gases. Rank and profession are shown less by color than by surface: polished resin for transit workers, soot-black felt for turbine crews, ribbed ceramic for fire brigades, soft white fiber for nursery keepers.

Children are grown in seed chambers.

A chamber belongs neither wholly to a household nor wholly to the municipality. It begins as a thick-walled pod in a residential nursery, supplied by grafts from several adults and by a civic root that contributes the city’s old, conserved lineages. Over years it swells into a warm, softly ticking room. The child develops inside layered membranes rather than fluid, folded tightly among cushions of nutritive pith. Families visit to speak against the shell and tap rhythms into its floor. Long before emergence, the child learns the vibration-signatures of its household, district, transit line, and local clock-flower.

Birth is called opening. The chamber divides along a prescribed seam and exhales warm powder. The new person comes out able to grip, listen through the feet, and close their eyes against the lamps, but otherwise helpless. The emptied chamber is not discarded. It dries, hardens, and becomes the child’s first room. Many adults still live within enlarged versions of the pods from which they emerged, adding partitions and balconies as the walls continue to grow.

Kinship consequently follows grafts rather than couples. A person may have several source-parents, a chamber-tender, and an entire maintenance cohort recognized as family. Households are broad, often occupying one interconnected stack of seed rooms. Privacy exists, but solitude is regarded with mild concern. To be wholly disconnected from neighboring roots, voices, and floor vibrations feels to them like drifting.

Their names change as they grow. The first name is a rhythm tapped by the family on the unopened chamber. The second is chosen after emergence and can be spoken aloud. Apprentices receive a work-name from the system they learn to tend: a station, orchard, road segment, herd, or lamp gallery. In age, some relinquish personal names altogether and are addressed by the place with which they have become most closely entangled.

Most inhabitants are not citizens in the political sense alone. They are attached to a district physiology.

One cohort regulates the flow of nutrients beneath an industrial quarter. Another tends the reflective ceiling colonies. Another walks the frontier, reading fresh fractures by placing their palms and sternums against the stone. Tram conductors train the living switches to recognize schedules. Building keepers shave excess growth from doors and coax cracked walls to knit. Physicians and horticulturists study together for years before specializing, since infection in a resident and blight in an apartment block may arise from the same organism.

Engineering is performed as negotiation. A young tower cannot simply be ordered upward. It must be exposed to particular voltages, loaded with carefully distributed weights, pruned along remembered lines, and rewarded with mineral mixtures when its tissues thicken correctly. Bridges are exercised before opening: crews march across them in measured patterns while listeners beneath the road judge the pitch of every supporting fiber. Factories have moods recognizable by changes in odor, static, and internal clicking. Skilled machinists speak of them as colleagues, though not necessarily as friends.

The oldest engineers carry symbiotic implants called keys. These are knots of conductive root grown around the forearm, spine, or ribs. Pressed against a utility trunk, a key allows its bearer to feel the direction of flow through a whole neighborhood. With training, they can redirect a ventilation branch, quiet a conveyor vine, wake dormant lamps, or induce a damaged street to shed its surface. The connection is intimate and dangerous. Sudden feedback may leave branching burns under the skin or overwrite the body’s rhythms with those of a pump or public clock.

No one knows with certainty whether the municipal root web is conscious.

It remembers, certainly. A road long deprived of traffic may still harden at the old commuting hours. Nursery walls reproduce lullaby rhythms after their singers are dead. During emergencies, remote districts sometimes close vents or divert stored gases before any order reaches them. Some call this distributed reflex. Others say the city has a mind too broad and slow to recognize individual lives, just as an inhabitant cannot recognize each mineral grain passing through their own tissues.

Belief gathers around this uncertainty.

Temples, where they exist, are bare cavities kept deliberately free of roots. Worshipers sit on exposed stone and experience the unsettling silence of a place that does not answer through their feet. The central religious question is not whether the cavern lives, but whether anything can exist outside relationship. Some sects seek deeper union with the root web, grafting themselves permanently into civic orchards at the end of life. Others insist that personhood requires periodic severance and make pilgrimages across untouched rock wearing thick, vibration-deadening soles.

Death is usually a gradual return of function. The body is placed in a digestion orchard, but its most useful tissues may first be grafted elsewhere. An eye membrane can become part of a lamp regulator. A worker’s key may be joined to the station they served. Mineral plates from the teeth and bones are ground into nursery feed. Families do not say the dead have gone into the ground. They say the dead have widened.

Some refuse this dispersal. Their bodies are sealed in ceramic and stored in dry galleries beyond the inhabited districts, where neither fungus nor root is permitted to enter. Such preservation is variously regarded as sacred, selfish, or tragic.

Fire brigades occupy a special place in the city. Their members are trained from childhood to move toward heat despite every instinct. They wear chalk-white armor and carry bladder-lances that suffocate flame with inert gas. When a district burns, they must decide what can be saved and what must be killed before it carries fire into the municipal root system. Sometimes they cut a living road completely across, severing transport, communication, and nutrient flow in one stroke. Survivors remember the silence after such an amputation more vividly than the blaze.

Because voices travel so far, the people value restraint. Arguments are held in padded rooms or communicated through hand pressure. Public laughter is brief and low. Music is often played into the floor: complex percussion too soft to hear clearly in air but perceptible across whole plazas as patterned tremors. At dusk, households sit with feet or palms against the walls while distant performances pass through the city. Each building alters the composition slightly. A song reaching the frontier may bear little resemblance to the one begun downtown, having accumulated the resonances of every district between.

Their visual art tends toward controlled growth. Artists score young bark, implant mineral threads, or train pale tissues around temporary frames. Some works take generations to reveal their intended form. Others exist for only one artificial night, opening when the lamps dim and collapsing into powder by morning. The finest pieces are said not to depict anything, but to make surrounding growth behave differently.

There are also people who live beyond the regular city.

Frontier crews inhabit mobile seed houses dragged over raw stone by walking engines. Their bodies are stockier, their scales thicker, and their hearing adapted to the clean, alarming reports of opening fractures. They seed stone-cutters, erect the first bracing trees, and test sealed pockets for useful gases or ancient poisons. They may spend years beyond the municipal root web, communicating by lamp flashes and ceramic message drums driven into the rock.

Herders cross the open districts with the great gas-boned browsers. They wear broad grounding veils and sleep in shelters folded from the animals’ shed tissue. Their routes follow the slow maturation of ceiling fronds rather than roads or district lines. Though they seldom enter dense neighborhoods, they carry news from distant pumping houses and discover failures before instruments do. They know which plazas are beginning to buckle, which empty towers have filled with predators, and where the floor has started transmitting unfamiliar rhythms from below.

There are scavengers, too, in the abandoned industrial zones. They strip old machinery, harvest feral cablewood, and coax forgotten warehouses into producing unauthorized chambers. Some are fugitives from graft debts or civic service. Others prefer systems small enough to understand. The city describes them as disconnected, but they have their own hidden networks: thin roots threaded through wall joints, coded pollen released into ventilation drafts, hunter packs trained to tap messages across vacant lots.

Not everything that moves through the streets is a person, though the boundary is disputed.

The knot-predators learn household rhythms and sometimes wait beside doors like patient pets. Conveyor vines develop preferences for certain handlers. Old walking engines continue pruning after their crews have vanished, adjusting their routes around newly grown obstacles. In the deepest digestion orchards, colonies of tiny animals arrange discarded mineral fragments into repeating branching forms. Engineers dismantle these structures. By the next work period, they have been rebuilt.

And beneath the ordinary floor signals—traffic, pumps, herds, root-switches—listeners sometimes detect another vibration.

It is very slow. A sequence may take years to repeat. It appears strongest near the eastern excavations, where the city is forcing open new stone, but echoes have been measured beneath districts centuries old. Some believe it is merely the cavern settling under altered loads. Some think an older root system lies entombed in the rock. Frontier crews tell of crystal faces that pulse under their hands and faults that close after being cut.

The inhabitants continue expanding anyway.

They cross the pale plazas in quiet groups, long coats whispering against their legs. They pause to let six-legged grazers pass. They touch walls in greeting. They consult flowers to know the hour. Above them, leaves turn by minute degrees toward the iron lamps, while beneath them cables carry food, power, memory, and messages through fissures no surveyor has mapped.

Seen from a high railway, they appear sparse enough to be accidental: a few dark figures in a kilometer of ivory ground, a maintenance crew under a single tree, a herder following delicate animals into the blue-gray distance. Yet almost every surface responds to their passing. Roads firm beneath their feet. Doors loosen. Lamps brighten fractionally. Root-switches begin to click ahead of them.

They live in the city, but not upon it.

They move through the outermost tissues of something they have cultivated for so long that neither side can now survive separation. Whether they made the city, were made by it, or are simply the mobile organs of the same vast dry organism is a distinction their language no longer expresses.
