The people who live in the basin are tenders of passage.

They tend water first. Every household knows the rills around it as closely as other people know rooms: which one clouds before a storm, which wall sweats in autumn, which sluice sticks when alder seeds collect beneath its pin. Children learn the settlement by following runoff. They are trusted early with leaf hooks and little gates, then with lifting bridge slats when the flood bells sound. An adult may forget a distant cousin’s exact relation but not who receives the overflow from that cousin’s roof.

No single occupation contains this work. A weaver clears channels; a cheesemaker repairs willow banks; a carpenter checks fish screens on the way home. Yet some inhabitants are especially skilled at reading the basin. They watch springs, taste cistern water, judge the pressure behind retaining walls, and hear blockages in the changed pitch of a wheel. Before heavy rain they move through town carrying poles, mallets, coils of rope, and wedges. Their authority lasts as long as the danger. During a surge, arguments stop when one of them points and says, “Open that,” or, “Take the goats higher.”

They are not engineers in the sense of imposing a design. They are negotiators with gravity.

Their bodies show the shape of the place. Most are strong through the legs and shoulders from climbing wet stairs, balancing loads, hauling sledges, and crouching beside low channels. They walk with short, deliberate steps and use walls, branches, and hanging ropes without seeming to notice. Children develop an astonishing certainty on slick roofs and narrow boards. Older people move more slowly but rarely less securely; they know which stones rock, which rails are living wood, and which moss will shear away under a hand.

Clothing is layered, belted close, and repeatedly patched. Wool is common, as are felted capes, reed rain-cloaks, bark-fiber aprons, leather gaiters, and wooden-soled shoes with replaceable pegs. Almost everyone carries a small knife. Many wear baskets rather than packs, because baskets can be hung above floodwater, set in channels to rinse vegetables, or turned over to shelter chicks. Bright color appears in narrow bands—madder red, weld yellow, alder brown, the deep gray-green of iron dyes—because large pieces of cloth are too costly to color evenly.

Their hands are seldom empty. They carry pruning hooks, spoons, plugs, fish traps, kindling, babies, bundles of nettles, jars of seep water, injured birds, and lengths of wood intended for repairs not yet identified. Nothing useful travels through town only once. Ash goes to soap or gardens. Broken pottery lines drains. Hair and combed wool stop drafts. Plum stones become gaming pieces. A warped roof peg may finish its life as a sluice marker.

Families are correspondingly difficult to enclose. A household may spread through several structures and elevations: grandparents in a cliff room, sleeping children across a bridge, cousins above a dye shed, goats beneath all three. Kinship matters, but so do shared roofs, adjoining channels, grafted trees, and mutual flood obligations. A person may be “of the East Rill” as readily as of a bloodline. Marriages are negotiated partly around water rights and maintenance burdens. Disputes concern shade, roots, silt, access ladders, and whose ducks have fouled whose washing pool.

Ownership is less absolute than layered. One person may possess the chestnut timbers of a room, another the stone beneath it, another the vine across its face, while everyone upstream retains the right to reach the gutter in an emergency. Such arrangements generate constant quarrels, but also constant contact. People must keep speaking to one another because the town gives them no way to live independently.

Their meetings occur while working. Decisions are made around blocked screens, over communal ovens, on the market platforms, or knee-deep in a damaged marsh bed. Memory is held by elders, but also by objects: notches on spill posts, painted stones at former waterlines, cords knotted to record labor owed, and songs whose verses list the order in which gates must be opened. Children chant drainage routes during games. Some can recite the path of a drop from the northern cliff to the southern lip, naming every trough, wheel, garden, and pond it passes.

The dead remain woven into those routes. Ashes may be placed beneath newly planted willows or mixed into clay used to mend a household cistern. Names attach to bridges, springs, pear trees, and bends in channels more often than to graves. A person is remembered as the one who trained a particular branch over the grain court, found a hidden seep, bred the pale ducks, or rebuilt a terrace after the great slide. Shrines are small and wet: a niche in the cliff, a shelf beneath a root, a cup kept full by a perpetual drip. Offerings are practical—wax, bread, feathers, first fruit, a carved gate pin.

The basin’s people have no ideal of cleanliness that means dryness. Clean water is moving water, correctly separated from tannery runoff, animal pens, dye pots, and washing places. Mud is not dirt when it arrives as useful silt. Moss is welcome on a cistern and dangerous on a stair. Rot is watched rather than universally opposed: bad in a roof beam, valuable in a mushroom bed, inevitable beneath bark, promising in compost. Even children understand these distinctions.

They are patient with decay but intolerant of neglect.

A gate left uncleared, a roof allowed to pour onto a neighbor’s wall, or a goat tethered where it can strip a stabilizing hedge is treated as a serious offense. Repairs are expected before excuses. Public embarrassment is often delivered through work songs improvised at the offender’s expense. Fines, where imposed, are commonly paid in labor, timber pegs, woven screens, or days spent dredging lower channels.

Metalworkers hold an unusual place. Their shops are small, dark, and crowded with fragments: worn teeth, cracked blades, bent nails, buckles thinned by generations of filing. They rarely make an object entirely new. Instead they weld, edge, pierce, straighten, and fit precious metal to abundant wood. A good smith knows when iron is necessary and when a hardened beech pin will serve better. Their status comes less from wealth than from custody. Families bring them tools bearing the marks of grandparents and expect every usable shaving to be saved.

Woodworkers are everywhere. Some specialize in waterwheels small enough to hold in two hands; others make ladders, sledges, troughs, shingles, loom frames, fish pens, or the flexible ribs of elevated paths. The finest builders understand living trees. They brace branches without strangling them, set platforms that can be loosened as trunks thicken, and predict which fork will bear a room in twenty years. Planting and construction are the same profession at that level.

There are reed cutters, coppice keepers, charcoal burners, fish tenders, herbalists, dyers, potters, shepherds, millers, beekeepers, fungus gatherers, and people who harvest bark without killing the tree. Many change work with season and weather. A marsh gardener may spend winter carving spoons. A summer herder may weave baskets through the rainy months. During flood or fire, all trades dissolve into the common tasks of carrying, cutting, opening, shoring, and counting heads.

The animals are not merely possessions. They are fellow occupants with tolerated habits, recognized routes, and particular reputations. Ducks belong to households but travel as if the channels were theirs. Goats answer to names only when convenient. Dogs know which market stalls will feed them and which spill gates they must not cross. Pigs are released where they can turn silt and eat fallen fruit, then lured home by distinctive knocks on wooden bowls.

Some creatures belong to no one but are treated as neighbors. A heron that repeatedly raids a fish pond may be cursed, netted, carried downslope, and return within days to general admiration. Householders leave openings for swifts beneath certain eaves. Frogs are moved out of drinking cisterns but welcomed in garden pools. Spiders over beds are removed; spiders over grain bins are protected. Children know the hidden places of newts, beetles, and nesting thrushes, and enforce complicated, shifting rules about which may be touched.

The forest animals are granted less intimacy but not less personality. Foxes become known by their scars. Pine martens acquire names that are mostly insults. Bears are discussed for years after a visit: what they ate, which fence they broke, who shouted uselessly, whose dog showed courage, whose dog hid in a cheese cupboard. Killing such an animal is possible, but it is considered a grave escalation. The preferred response is noise, coordination, and a clear way out.

Sound organizes everyone. People identify neighbors by tread on bridges and families by the bells of their animals. Calls are pitched to carry between terraces. Names often have shortened forms for shouting across water. Work songs keep groups synchronized when hauling logs or raising a sagged platform. Children mimic the warning bells in play until adults snap at them for causing confusion.

At night the settlement does not become quiet so much as change register. Wheels are disengaged one by one. Looms stop. Voices retreat indoors. Then come drips in clay cups, frogs in the reed beds, cattle shifting beneath floors, and the soft knock of loose gates. A sleeper wakes immediately to an unfamiliar rush of water. Several households may emerge with lamps before anyone knows whether there is danger.

Hospitality reflects these conditions. A stranger is offered something warm, a place to hang wet clothing, and careful instructions about where not to step. Guests are asked where they came from, but just as urgently whether the upper road is washed out, whether chestnuts are blighting beyond the ridge, or whether lowland salt prices have risen. News travels with practical weight. Stories are valued for the same reason channels are: they carry something from elsewhere.

The inhabitants can seem suspicious because they study newcomers for competence. A person who notices a clogged grate and clears it without being asked is quickly liked. One who blocks a passage, wastes dry fuel, or tosses refuse into running water is remembered. Prestige comes from usefulness under pressure—calm hands in a flood, a good eye for sick livestock, the ability to mend tools, settle quarrels, find lost children, or predict where a bank will fail.

Beauty is recognized in fitness rather than perfection. A wall is admired when its roots have taken hold. A basket is beautiful if it balances properly on a steep path. A roof earns praise by feeding three cisterns without leaking into a bedchamber. The most esteemed buildings are not the newest but those that have accommodated many changes without ceasing to serve. Their beams curve around trunks; their floors rise in patched stages; their doors open above old waterlines.

People decorate what they continually touch. Gate handles are carved into fish, leaves, snails, and bird heads. Loom weights bear family marks. Flood poles are painted in bands. Children press seeds and fern fronds into wet plaster. Fine reed work appears under eaves where it remains dry enough to last. The town has little room for monuments, so artistry inhabits hinges, cups, bells, pegs, combs, knife handles, and the ends of bridges.

Their celebrations follow water and harvest. The first safe overflow after the season’s great rains is greeted from the southern terraces with bells and lamps. In late summer, vine leaves are cut back and hung in garlands over the market paths. Chestnut season draws people into the high woods for days at a time. Weddings may include the joining of two small channels into a shared basin, though everyone understands that such symbols require annual clearing.

Children are everywhere because there is nowhere set apart for them. They work, play, listen, and obstruct in the same dense spaces as everyone else. They sail bark boats through rills, pole across flooded gardens, steal sour plums, collect snails, climb pollarded trees, and dare one another to touch the coldest cliff springs. They are taught danger by degrees rather than fenced away from it. A toddler is tethered near a deep channel; an older child is trusted to cross it; an adolescent may be sent into rain to warn the lower terraces.

Old age brings a different usefulness. Those who can no longer climb far sit where several paths meet, mending, sorting, shelling, watching children, and noticing changes in the sound of water. They preserve accounts of floods older than any current structure. When someone proposes a new wall or diversion, an elder may recall that the same place failed forty years before, or that buried beneath it lies an old channel still likely to wake.

They do not think of themselves as living inside untouched nature, nor as having subdued it. The distinction would make little sense. The forest is harvested, encouraged, feared, planted, and inherited. Water is guided but never presumed obedient. Animals are fed, eaten, cursed, protected, and accommodated. Houses are human works, yet depend on roots, moss, reeds, living trunks, and the tolerances of the slope.

What lives there, finally, is not just a population of humans among abundant plants and animals. It is a crowded association of beings and materials, held together by repeated acts of attention.

The people belong to it because they participate in its maintenance. So do the pollarded beeches that hold up paths, the willows knitting collapsed banks, the ducks clearing small weeds from channels, the worms opening wet soil, the fungi softening dead wood, and the reeds slowing the basin’s final outflow. Even the cliff is an inhabitant in this sense: always present, always changing, feeding the troughs and sweating into bedrooms.

No one controls the whole arrangement. No one fully understands it. Knowledge is distributed among households, trades, species, and generations. The town survives not because its residents have made the mountain stable, but because they have learned to recognize movement early and answer it together.

They live by keeping things open: channels, paths, canopies, agreements, exits.

And when rain thickens the air, they listen.
