The principal inhabitants are the **Continuants**.

That is not what they call themselves; they possess no language in the ordinary sense. The name belongs to the absent technicians, appearing once in a handwritten maintenance note beside a hydrophone array:

**DO NOT INTERRUPT CONTINUANTS DURING TRANSIT.**

It was first assumed to refer only to the silver schools. Later observations made the category less certain.

A Continuant is less a species than a living component of the sea’s standing chord. Its body occupies water, but its true anatomy extends through time and resonance. The narrow silver migrants are the clearest example. Each has a hard, bright flank, rigid triangular fins, and a black eye that seems empty until viewed in one of the quiet shadows. There, very faint concentric structures can be seen moving behind the eye—not turning inward, as lenses do, but outward, as though the animal were looking along the path of whatever sound leaves it.

Their flesh is almost colorless. Their bones are hollow crystal lattices full of pressurized water. When struck, every bone rings at a slightly different frequency. A complete skeleton produces the interval assigned to its school.

They do not swim by pushing water backward. Their fins make only minor corrections. Instead, they incline themselves toward an arriving vibration and are drawn along its pressure gradient. This is why they travel with such precision. Each migration follows the acoustic track laid down by all its previous migrations. Every passage strengthens that track, making deviation progressively more difficult. An individual forced from formation will struggle without apparent direction until the school’s returning call reaches it. Then its body snaps into alignment so sharply that bones sometimes fracture.

The fracture remains forever. So does the correction.

Their wide spacing is necessary. If two approach too closely, the resonances of their skeletons interfere. Both begin to tremble, their outlines doubling in the water. They separate immediately if able. If trapped together, they may become locked in a rapid, violent oscillation that never resolves. Several such pairs hang near narrow passes, still alive, beating against each other like clappers in a bell.

Their synchronized turns are not commanded. Each animal hears the same ancient turning signal arriving from the first circuit of the school. In that sense, they obey themselves.

The warehouse-sized bells are more difficult to distinguish from machinery. They consist of transparent domes ribbed with pale mineral cartilage. Beneath each dome hang thousands of filaments, some ending in mouths, some in sensory knots, and some in polished nodules of metal that the animal gathers from exposed veins in the mountains. They spread around pinnacles and contract at long intervals, filtering microscopic flakes from the water.

What they consume is not food alone. They collect stray vibrations.

Each filament is tuned to a narrow band. When an anomalous noise enters the bell—a broken tool striking rock, an unfamiliar engine pulse, a human voice—the filaments stiffen around it. The dome closes. For a while, the sound can be heard circulating inside the animal, distorted and weakening. Yet because no sound can truly dissipate, the bell eventually incorporates it into the rhythm of its contractions and releases it in a form compatible with the larger chord.

They are the sea’s dampers and arrangers.

Their transparent tissues are crowded with swallowed noises. In the quiet zones, one may place a hand against a bell and feel brief remnants passing beneath its skin: a latch closing, a burst of laughter, three struck notes, a warning spoken too quickly to understand. The largest bells contain entire industrial accidents, folded into slow pulses that take decades to cross their bodies.

The jointed predators are called **measures** in surviving station diagrams. They resemble chains of dark vertebrae, sometimes kilometers long, with no clear distinction between head and tail. Each segment has hooked limbs folded flush against it and a circular mouth lined with pressure-sensitive plates. Most of the time they extend motionless across established migration routes.

When a school arrives, only one segment attacks.

It unfolds, seizes a silver migrant, and cuts from it a precise quantity of resonant bone. The victim survives. The measure passes the fragment from mouth to mouth along its length, each segment shaving and retuning it until the final piece is released into the water. These pieces drift toward particular mountains and lodge in fractures, where they ring.

Thus the predators repair the ranges.

They do not choose damaged prey by weakness. They select whichever body bears the frequency required elsewhere in the sea. Nor do they consume enough to satisfy any recognizable metabolism. Their hunger is geometric: an agitation that increases when an interval in the planetary resonance narrows or widens. Feeding restores the interval, and the chain becomes still.

The armored crack-dwellers perform finer work. They are small, many-legged creatures with shells like iron filings fused into beads. Colonies line fractures in basalt, all facing inward. Their mouths abrade the stone one crystal at a time. Their hind limbs place each removed grain elsewhere along the crack.

From a distance, they seem merely to feed. Over long observation, it becomes evident that they are changing the shape of the cavity—microscopically, ceaselessly, without ever completing the alteration. They tune the mountain as a musician might tune an instrument, except that every removed grain remains suspended nearby and every adjustment must be balanced by an opposite adjustment somewhere else.

Their colonies possess no queens or reproductive chambers. New individuals occasionally crawl from depths too narrow to inspect. None have ever been seen to hatch. Dead ones remain attached among the living and continue to vibrate when the rock sounds.

There are larger inhabitants as well.

Something inhabits the pipelines.

It is never seen entire. Inspection drones encounter pale muscular closures wrapped around the inner walls, followed by long empty stretches, followed by structures resembling gills or ears. At open valves, clear strands sometimes extend into the ocean for several hundred meters. They withdraw when engines elsewhere change pitch.

The pipelines may have been constructed around these organisms, or the organisms may have assumed the pipelines’ shape. Their pulses coincide with the pumps, but not consistently enough to determine which drives the other. When a pump is manually stopped, the pipe’s occupant continues its contraction. A few seconds later—before any physical signal could arrive—the pump restarts.

These beings seem to carry timing rather than matter. Their distributed bodies connect stations that otherwise have no apparent purpose, keeping distant actions in phase. The ringing telephones may be incidental organs of the same system. On rare occasions, after the receiver is lifted, something moist can be heard adjusting itself inside the line.

And then there are the **custodians**.

Evidence of them appears in every facility: a pressure door opened since the last survey, a wrench moved from one bolt to another, a new line marked on a checklist. Cameras show no motion. The changes exist between frames, even when the recording interval is reduced to its practical minimum.

In the quiet zones, one sometimes sees a shape standing inside a control room far across the water.

It is approximately human only because the room was built for humans. It has the vertical proportions of a person in heavy diving gear, though its outline wavers as if assembled from many slightly displaced moments. Frost geometries brighten along the window in front of it. Warning lamps cast blue and violet bands through its torso.

Approached directly, it is absent.

Yet the chair inside may be warm—not warmer than the sea, which would be impossible, but carrying the exact sensation of remembered warmth. A glove may hang in the water with its fingers newly bent. Gauges that cannot advance may indicate a reading no previous visitor recorded.

The custodians may be workers caught in the founding instant, neither present nor gone. They may be pressure-images produced by the stations. They may be the stations’ way of representing maintenance to observers equipped to recognize hands and bodies.

What they maintain is not clear, because nothing here can deteriorate. Perhaps their task is not to prevent failure but to preserve the act of prevention: the reaching for a lever, the checking of a seal, the decision that a system must continue. If so, they are as arrested as every wounded animal, endlessly approaching the instant in which their work would be finished.

All these beings fear silence, though fear manifests differently among them.

The silver schools bend around quiet zones and leave smooth, lens-shaped absences in their formations. Bells close before touching the boundary. Measures pull their segments into taut arcs. Armored colonies cease scraping and press flat against stone.

It is not death they sense there. Death is harmless in a sea where consequences cannot advance.

Silence threatens separation.

Inside the quiet zones, a creature becomes less bound to its earlier motions. Wounds sometimes bleed there for the first time. A fin may complete a stroke not already contained in the school’s route. Hunger sharpens into need. For several seconds, or minutes, an animal may become singular.

Most retreat at once.

A few enter.

These are usually damaged migrants driven from formation, bells overloaded with discordant sound, or armored creatures dislodged from their colonies. Within the silence they move erratically, at first with terror and then with something resembling curiosity. They turn without a signal. They inspect objects they have passed innumerable times. Predators fail to strike on cue because the prey reaches the expected point too early or too late.

Such animals cannot remain there indefinitely. The larger chord presses at the quiet boundary. Eventually one of its stable bands catches them, and they are drawn back into repetition.

But they do not always return unchanged.

A silver migrant that has entered silence may rejoin its school one body-length from its former position. A grazer may release an unfamiliar pulse. A crack-dweller may place a grain on the wrong side of a fracture. These deviations are tiny, and the whole ocean labors to absorb them. Bells collect the new sounds. Measures retune bone. Factories alter their emphasis without altering their sequence.

This may be why the inhabitants exist.

Not merely to preserve the chord, but to preserve it against the possibility—always arising in its own rare cancellations—that something here might begin to happen next.

Far below the flat lower boundary, the silent planet waits. Nothing in the suspended ocean is known to have crossed into its sky. Fish turn. Stones halt. Sound folds back into water.

Still, observers in the quietest places have reported seeing small armored creatures gather at the edge. They carry mineral grains in their mouths. One by one, they set the grains against the invisible boundary and listen.

They appear to be testing for a note that is not yet there.
