Those who live on the plateau do not think of it as alive. Life, to them, is a narrower condition: something that feeds, grows, divides, pursues, escapes. The plateau does none of these plainly. It only notices. It arranges. It remembers what has crossed it and makes small corrections after the crossing.

Its most visible inhabitants are the low, six-legged grazers that travel between basins.

Their flexible dorsal plates are the color of whatever ground they crossed during their first season: rose dust, ocher stone, black mineral glaze. A narrow body hangs beneath this armor, supported by legs fine enough to seem inadequate until they begin to move. Each foot spreads upon contact, distributing weight across gravel without dislodging it. They walk in loose files because the stone beneath one animal is still warm with its passage when the next arrives. That residual warmth communicates direction, health, and urgency more reliably than scent in the dry air.

They graze on lichens and the silver skins of dormant shrubs. When food is poor, they scrape translucent mineral fins from eastern faces of rocks and digest the trapped spores within them. Their plates lift in sequence to vent heat, producing a faint ripple along the herd. During courtship, two animals stand parallel and pass these ripples back and forth until the patterns synchronize. Their young are carried for a time beneath the lowest plates, clinging upside down and drinking condensed moisture from grooves in the parent’s skin.

They do not follow water. They follow incompleteness: a break in the insect swarms, reeds leaning without wind, a place where the doubled shadows briefly converge. Thus they often begin walking toward a basin hours before it fills.

Above them live the black fliers.

They have no feathers and almost no voice. Their wings are stretched membranes stiffened by branching rods, each rod containing a thread of dark fluid that shifts with the angle of the sun. By altering that fluid, they can become briefly indistinguishable from the glare. What seems like disappearance is only an exact management of reflected light.

At dusk they land head downward on cliffs and unfold narrow drinking surfaces beneath their jaws. Condensation beads across these surfaces and runs directly into channels in the throat. They roost separately, but sleep as a flock: impulses pass through the mineral wall between their gripping feet, keeping hundreds of bodies in the same shallow rhythm.

They are cautious around certain wet places. Where water occupies two locations at once—resting on a seamless plane while falling elsewhere—the fliers refuse to pass between it and the sun. Their missing shadows warn everything below. Even predators attend to these absences.

The pools belong to faster lives.

Transparent swimmers wait as folded specks inside dry crust. Water opens them. Within minutes they are feeding; within hours they bear young already carrying the beginnings of another generation. They have no stable adult form. Each molt answers the pool’s remaining depth, salinity, temperature, and expected duration. In a long-lived pool they become broad and slow, grazing films from stone. In one that will vanish by noon, they remain needle-thin, mate almost immediately, and harden their descendants into mineral cases before their own bodies have finished forming.

Long-legged hunters descend after rain. Their limbs unfold from cracks where they have waited, sometimes for years, as nearly weightless knots of tissue. They hunt by reading disturbances in surface tension. Some pools, however, display overlapping disturbances from their remembered states. There the hunters strike at prey from yesterday, or from a flood that has not yet occurred, and are themselves taken by pale amphibious climbers waiting beneath the rim.

Those climbers spend daylight adhered to overhangs. Their skin is milk-white and faintly ridged, able to drink vapor and absorb dissolved salts directly from stone. At night they move with slow, deliberate grips. Each leaves behind a temporary dry print. Others follow these prints toward moisture, so a colony can navigate without sound or sight. During drought they gather around one individual and seal themselves into a many-bodied sphere, the outer layer drying to protect those within. Which animal occupies the center changes from drought to drought.

Beneath these smaller lives are the inhabitants of the cliffs.

No one knows how many there are.

A head seen in one ravine may be followed by a plated section emerging kilometers away, but excavated passages divide, rejoin, and narrow beyond the diameter of any observed body. Some believe each visible segment is an animal. Others believe the cliff dwellers are distributed colonies: soft-bodied organisms inhabiting stone chambers and assembling temporary limbs where movement is required.

They sort excavated rock into exact grades. Powder lies closest to an aperture, then grains, pebbles, cobbles, and finally slabs fitted together with their broad faces aligned. These arrangements redirect runoff, shade eggs, stabilize weak slopes, and provide burrows for creatures the cliff dwellers never eat. Whether this is deliberate husbandry or merely the geometry of their digging is unclear.

Their deepest portions may not be biological at all. Pale seams enter their tunnels and sometimes appear embedded in translucent segments of their bodies. When the buried tone sounds, the cliff dwellers become still. When it sounds twice, they withdraw from the river corridor. When it sounds three times, they open passages beneath it, allowing deep water to rise through the gravel.

They may be the oldest animals on the plateau. They may also be among its structures.

The distinction weakens underground.

Within the towers, rings, and upright planes lives something for which the word inhabitant is misleading. There are no operators seated in hidden rooms, no surviving builders continuing a forgotten task. The attention of the structures is dispersed through seams, wells, dark terraces, mineral fins, and the porous rock that holds the buried river. It does not occupy the plateau as an animal occupies a den. Its body is the relationship among places.

A drop forms beneath an overhang. A seam warms on a distant ridge. Reeds bend. Insects rise. A flock changes course, shading a mat of succulents during the hottest minute of the day. A grazer sees that moving darkness and turns, disturbing seeds that would otherwise remain buried. Days later, rain enters the hoofprints. None of these events commands the next. Together they constitute a thought.

This distributed inhabitant has no single awareness of the present. Inside the half-sunken tower, the plateau appears in accumulated layers because that is how it is perceived: not as one landscape progressing through time, but as many recent landscapes superimposed. A dry basin and its flooded state remain adjacent conditions. A living herd crosses the traces of its possible migrations. A species extinct in one valley may still be present in the system as a route, a moisture requirement, a pressure once exerted upon other lives.

The structures preserve such absences.

They do not necessarily restore what has been lost. Instead they keep an empty shape from closing too quickly. A newly opened fissure may reproduce the shelter once made by a vanished shrub. Condensation may gather where an extinct grazer formerly exposed lichen by scraping stone. A predator may continue waiting beside pools whose original prey disappeared centuries ago, while another swimmer gradually enters the unfilled place.

Thus the plateau’s dominant intelligence may be less a keeper of creatures than a keeper of intervals: the proper distance between herds, between storms, between one refuge and the next; the pause long enough for thirst to direct movement but not long enough to erase a lineage. It tends scarcity because abundance would simplify the land. It permits death because uninterrupted survival would make its inhabitants cease to move, adapt, and distribute one another.

Its corrections are usually gentle. A shadow shifts. A pool persists for an additional hour. Gravel settles beneath one foot and not another.

Only confusion provokes precision.

Rising rain is part of how the plateau renews its account of itself. Each ascending drop carries microscopic traces: skin salts, pollen, spores, mineral signatures, fragments of scent, and perhaps finer records held in the arrangement of the water itself. The drops rise into low clouds, pass through unseen pathways, and return elsewhere as a revised distribution of possibility.

A moving body during this process may be entered twice into the record, or entered in two incompatible locations. The plateau resolves such contradictions without anger. A seam opens. A plane turns dark. For an instant, two versions of the same place coincide. When they separate, the body is where the larger pattern requires it to have been.

Sometimes it remains alive.

Sometimes travelers later glimpse it through the edge of a structure, walking beneath unfamiliar stars or beside a river that the present plateau only remembers. Such figures seldom notice observers. They appear intent on reaching water.

The native animals know none of this as doctrine. They know it in their bodies.

The grazers pause before contradictory shadows. The black fliers withhold their silhouettes from dangerous pools. Amphibious colonies seal themselves before the mist arrives. Cliff dwellers listen through whole ridges. Seeds rise on thermal currents timed to seams warming under stone.

Even the thorn trees participate. Their chalky bark contains innumerable fine channels, and their roots touch pale lines far below the surface. A solitary tree may stand hundreds of meters from the next, yet both unfold new leaves on the same night. During rare periods of prolonged rain, their flattened crowns turn toward one another. Given enough wet years, they might become a forest. The structures preserve that possibility without allowing it to arrive too easily.

So what lives there is not one hidden race, nor a machine supervising an ecosystem from outside.

It is a community of brief creatures and very long processes: herds that read warmth, fliers that edit light, swimmers born into vanishing worlds, colonies that wear the cliffs as bodies, plants waiting underground for a few useful hours. Among them lives a form of attention stretched so broadly across stone and weather that no individual encounter reveals it.

The plateau itself may not be alive.

But life there has grown into its habits so completely—and its habits have grown so deeply into life—that neither can now be found without the other.
