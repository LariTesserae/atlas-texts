The inhabitants of the drowned heights are difficult to recognize as inhabitants.

They spend much of their lives fixed in place. Seen at rest, one might mistake them for bundles of mineralized roots, for collapsed racks of tools, or for the black vanes stacked in the chambers beside the breathing shaft. They possess no faces and no permanent arrangement of limbs. A single one is a colony of narrow, muscular ribbons joined around a translucent core, each ribbon thin enough to slip through a tethering groove or wind between fitted stones. Spread wide, it resembles a dark plant flattened by the current. Gathered close, it becomes a compact, many-handed body.

Their substance is vegetable, but their behavior is not.

The outer ribbons are tough and nearly black, ribbed with channels that trap dissolved metals. Along their edges grow thousands of short glassy hooks. These hooks can fasten to stone, cord, tools, or one another, allowing several inhabitants to assemble into a stronger shape. Beneath the dark skin runs a dim blue-green chemistry. It brightens when the ribbons bend under pressure, so a moving individual appears not as a steady lamp but as a sequence of brief lines: force translated into light, thought translated into strain.

At the colony’s center is a knot of clear tubes containing fine mineral grains. The grains align to currents, warmth, pressure, and the deep pulse of the mountain. There is no brain in the animal sense. Memory is distributed through the knot and through the changing stiffness of the ribbons. Experiences leave patterns in the body: a route across a ridge becomes a sequence of tensions; the timing of an upward shaft becomes a ring of hardened tissue; the shape of a tool persists as a posture the colony can assume again.

They have no eyes. Nothing here does.

They perceive the country by pressure, vibration, heat, and taste. Every distant collapse reaches them as a geometry of trembling water. Mineral seams are warm outlines beneath the rock. A fitted wall interrupts current in a pattern unlike a natural outcrop. The inhalation of the central shaft can be felt long before it begins, as a loosening in their translucent cores.

To them, the drowned heights are not dark. They are loud with shape.

Most remain hidden during violent flows, braided into wall grooves or spread beneath weighted roof slabs. When the water steadies, they release themselves one ribbon at a time. Several may combine to work. One anchors the group while another forms a loop around a stone; others stiffen into levers, wedges, or grasping cords. This is why the builders’ implements resemble no anatomy. Their bodies supply whatever handles and joints are needed. A smoky-glass blade is not held in a hand but sleeved into a tightened braid. A copper hook may become the temporary end of a limb. They wear tools as extensions, then dismantle themselves and store those tools before the next reversal.

The huts are not dwellings in the ordinary sense. They are moorings, workshops, and external memories.

Their grooves preserve useful arrangements of the body. Their cairns divide currents into recognizable signatures. Their towers enlarge distant vibrations and carry them down through tether lines. The maps carved into stone are touched rather than seen: branching valleys, recurring pressure fronts, the predicted paths of buoyancy shafts. The vertical channels shown above the ridges record routes no untethered body could survive.

Their marks have no depictions of faces because a self has no single shape here. It may be one colony sheltering alone, or twelve colonies locked together across a cataract. Portions can be divided and later reunited. A ribbon severed by volcanic glass may remain active, root into suitable stone, and slowly grow a new core. Yet it does not become the same individual. It retains only the memories carried in its hardened bands.

They do not distinguish sharply between offspring, tools, and records.

The black vanes stored in the nine chambers are portions of cultivated bodies, cut during dormant periods and impressed with patterns of tension. When warmed in the mountain’s exhalation, they soften and begin repeating those patterns. Some curl into simple knots. Some enact routes. Some reproduce the motions required to shape a block or braid a tether. The most intricate contain fragments of ancient experience: the arrival of a migrating shaft, the failure of a bridge, the pressure-song of a ridge that has since collapsed.

Placed against a living core, a vane can be learned.

Thus the dead are not buried. They are thinned, sorted, and stored by size.

Their oldest colonies have grown almost indistinguishable from the architecture. They line the chambers as dark seams or lace themselves through the petrified ropes spanning the horizontal cataract. They may remain immobile for decades, exchanging minerals with the walls. When the mountain inhales, these elders tighten throughout the structure. Doors secure themselves. retaining bars settle over tools, and roof tethers draw taut. What appears to be an abandoned building is briefly revealed as a body too large and slow to notice at other times.

The mountain is central to their lives, but it is neither god nor machine. It is a partner whose scale exceeds comprehension.

They feed during its exhalations. Warm mineral water charges their internal gradients, awakens their stored vanes, and drives the pale growths from which they derive new tissue. Between breaths they diminish, becoming rigid and patient. A severe missed cycle may leave entire settlements mineralized in their working positions. Such places look like ruins, though some can wake after centuries if the blue warmth reaches them again.

They tend the shaft because the shaft sustains the forests, and the forests construct the terrain that directs water toward the shaft. They clear channels, anchor vulnerable stalks, lay petrified trunks across unstable sediment, and score bare rock so silver dust can take hold. Much of their labor is indistinguishable from erosion. Much of their engineering takes generations.

The stairway rising into open water is theirs.

It is not supported from below. Each step is a living raft of transparent stalks weighted on one side and held in place by the controlled lift of a narrow reversal stream. The inhabitants ascend by fastening themselves beneath the slabs. At each level they wait for the current to change, then release and rise to the next.

No colony living now knows who began it.

The stored vanes say only that something exists higher in the ocean: not a surface, perhaps, but another range suspended in denser water, or a region where the great pale flashes are warm. The oldest records contain pressure patterns that do not correspond to any known valley. One resembles repeated impacts. Another resembles many bodies descending together.

So the inhabitants continue to build upward.

They work without urgency. A new step may require a century of accumulated stone. A failed one can vanish in a moment, lifted into blackness when buoyancy reverses. Colonies are lost there, their tether lines found later trailing from empty water. Still, after every exhalation, while the country shines and the distant stair briefly appears, a few dark forms can be felt moving along its underside.

They have no mouths with which to call to one another.

Instead they pull on the water.

A greeting is a tremor sent through a cord. A warning is a sudden stiffness shared across a meadow. Knowledge passes from ribbon to ribbon as patterned strain. During the mountain’s brief silence, when even the cataract falters, every tethered colony in the canyon releases a sequence into the stillness.

The old structures answer.

The petrified ropes answer.

Far ridges, linked by root and cairn, answer after long delays.

For those few motionless moments, the drowned heights are filled with an immense, nearly imperceptible conversation: not voices traveling through the ocean, but the whole altered landscape tightening and relaxing as one distributed body.

Then the mountain exhales. Blue light floods their translucent cores. Thousands of black ribbons loosen from walls that seemed empty, unfurling into the warm current.

The inhabitants wake everywhere at once.
