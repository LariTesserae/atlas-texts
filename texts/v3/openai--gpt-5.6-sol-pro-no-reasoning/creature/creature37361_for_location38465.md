The first inhabitants are the listeners.

They live behind the six dark apertures surrounding the pass, withdrawn into the mountains so completely that only their pupils show: lenses nested inside lenses, each fixed upon the interval between the cairns. They have no interest in the steel hexagons themselves. Those may tarnish microscopically, be exchanged, recalibrated, rebuilt from stock hidden below. The listeners attend only to what is not there.

They measure the gap without entering it. Light crosses from opposed ports, but never along a path forbidden by the local rules; it skirts the absence, glances from polished facets, and returns carrying fractions. Capacitance feels across it. Pressure membranes infer it from the industrial thunder. Fields are raised on either side and compared. Six systems, each mistrustful of the others, describe the emptiness continuously to machines buried in the girder.

In this sense, the place is crowded with attention.

The carbon growths inhabit it too. They begin as soot-dark freckles where charge accumulates along a seam. From these points they put out angular stems, dividing according to gradients too weak for a human body to feel. Their copper tips seek not nourishment but completion. Each colony grows toward a condition. When a circuit closes, the branching ceases, and the finished thing remains: a black, thorned diagram of an electrical event that may have occurred a hundred years ago.

They are less like plants than like frozen decisions.

Some stand alone on exposed faces. Others gather in little groves beneath overhangs, all their branches inclined toward a buried conductor. None dies. None reproduces except when authorized by another leakage path. If a branch exceeds its allotted outline, a pulse passes through it and the excess vanishes into odorless carbon vapor, immediately extracted. Thus every growth is permitted to become exactly itself and no more.

The pale dust has inhabitants of another kind: the numbered fragments that rest upon it.

They are curls, chips, washers, slivers of steel, and a few deliberate shapes whose function is not obvious. Each occupies a surveyed position. Fine fields hold them with a gentleness more exact than touch. They serve as witnesses. A crescent shaving balanced on the fifth peak records a vibration node. Three black grains in the western valley disclose the direction of a magnetic gradient. The flake suspended above the hollow is perhaps the most carefully kept creature in the range. Sensors watch its underside. Coils correct its posture. An entire cabinet of power electronics labors to preserve its effortless hovering.

It has never landed.

Beneath the visible landscape dwell the maintainers, though they seldom appear whole. They inhabit ducts and slots too narrow for people: jointed manipulators, inspection worms, cable-drawn sledges, polishing heads, miniature cranes folded into cliff faces. Most sleep for decades with their tools tucked beneath them. When a plate begins to drift beyond tolerance, a seam opens. Something thin and many-elbowed emerges, braces itself against hidden sockets, and performs the necessary exchange.

Such repairs happen without disturbing the mountain’s silhouette. One arm supports the failing plate while another brings its replacement up through the ridge. Magnets take the weight. Fasteners turn in a prescribed sequence. For several seconds the old and new surfaces occupy nearly the same place, one passing through the role of the other. Then the worn plate descends into the body of the range and the hatch closes over it.

The landscape appears never to have changed.

The maintainers do not cross the central interval. Even their shadows are kept from falling through it. Their routes curve around the pass in buried arcs, and their instructions contain the prohibition at a level deeper than choice. A machine reaching toward the gap would lose power joint by joint before its tool could enter.

There are also inhabitants made only of sound.

The seven peaks divide the factory’s roar into stable populations of pressure: a low, compact heaviness in the first basin; a thin vibration clinging to the underside of the second ridge; a bright line stretched between two serrated summits. They have fixed territories and sharp borders. Move a finger through one valley and the bones of the hand fill with bass. Lift it a few centimeters and the sensation becomes a shriek, though the protected ear hears almost nothing.

These standing waves do not travel. They persist.

They lean against the slopes without shaking them, shaped by the same cancellation apparatus that keeps the dust still. At the edges of the palm-sized quiet pocket, opposed pressures meet and annul one another. The silence there is not an absence of the roar but its exact double occupation: noise arriving in equal strength and contrary phase. It is the most densely maintained silence in the tower.

If anything could be said to graze upon the range, it would be the corrective systems feeding on error.

They detect warmth, drift, charge, fatigue, displacement, contamination. Error is their only food, and they are kept nearly starving. A temperature rise of a thousandth of a degree wakes a valve. A grain’s microscopic rotation excites a coil. An unauthorized molecule crossing the threshold is captured, identified, and removed. The systems consume every beginning of change before change can become history.

Their spoor is invisible: a brief current in a buried wire, a quantity of heat carried into coolant, a revised number in a record no one may read.

And perhaps something inhabits the gap itself.

The instruments behave as though this were so. Their vigilance is too elaborate to seem directed at mere distance. They do not simply confirm that the cairns remain apart. They test the interval for pressure, charge, curvature, resonance, passage, and events for which the tower’s engineers left no names on any accessible panel. Some detectors watch continuously. Others wake at immense intervals, perform one measurement, and sleep again. A few appear never to have reported anything at all.

Nothing may cross the gap because crossing would either injure what is kept there or allow it to leave—or because the gap must remain fit for an arrival that has not happened.

There is no evidence favoring one explanation.

At times the measurements disagree by an amount smaller than the tolerance of the measuring devices. The discrepancy passes inward through shielded cables. The cooling loops alter their flow. Charge drains brighten imperceptibly at their copper tips. Deep inside the western tower, turbines take on additional load. Across the factory continent, generators compensate. All this may occur because the interval between two machined stones has become, for less than a second, fractionally more or less empty.

Then correction ends. The readings converge. The suspended flake remains where it was. The black thorns keep their completed shapes. The acoustic creatures hold their territories among the peaks.

Nothing visible moves.

Thus the little range is not uninhabited. It is inhabited by watchers, witnesses, frozen decisions, sleeping hands, captive pressures, and systems that live on infinitesimal failure. They have no tracks because they are forbidden to leave consequences. They have no seasons because variation is removed at its first appearance. They neither colonize nor depart. Their lives consist of preserving the conditions of their lives.

All face, directly or indirectly, toward the narrow pass.

There the two cairns stand like boundary markers on opposite shores. Between them resides the chamber’s most costly inhabitant: an emptiness kept from widening, narrowing, filling, aging, or being crossed.

Everything else lives there to keep it alive.
