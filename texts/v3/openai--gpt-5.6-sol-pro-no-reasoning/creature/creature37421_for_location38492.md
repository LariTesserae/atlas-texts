The beings within the leaf are not quite a community and not quite the organs of a single creature. They are the moving parts of an ecology that has forgotten how to proceed.

Nearest the glowing roof live the light-eaters: thin algal films spread across the inner membrane in green, gold, and occasional blood-red stains. They have no clear edges. Their cells divide, are grazed away, and divide again without either advancing or retreating. Yeasts bloom among them like pale dust. Stomata overhead admit measured breaths, and colonies clustered beneath each opening swell and flatten in rhythm with the valves, as though the leaf itself were breathing through them.

Mosses occupy the junctions of the lesser veins. They rise in widely spaced towers, each forest no larger than a bead of dew. Their stems are translucent below and dark above, burdened with black capsules that bend, tighten, and burst. Every discharge fills the air with spores; every capsule remains ready to burst again. Liverworts spread nearer the wet channels, arranging themselves into chains of green cups. They catch condensation, bow under its weight, tip it onward, and return upright before the transferred drop has finished trembling.

The fungi live deepest. Their pale cords enter the peat-soft floor and climb inside the old veins, where they pulse in coordinated sequences. Some are fine as smoke. Others have thickened into glossy, rootlike cables, stained amber by tannin. They digest the leaf continually but never consume it. Their fruiting bodies stand alone in the open spaces: pinhead mushrooms with milk-white caps, grey fans growing from rib walls, minute red cups holding beads of clear fluid. Each appears solitary, though all are joined beneath the surface in one vast, laboring mesh.

Among this vegetation move the springtails, quick and silver, crossing bare reaches of cuticle like sparks. They travel established routes without forming roads, leap over the same channels, and land in the same dim hollows. Their antennae test the air before every jump. Their impacts strike through the leaf chamber as small, hard knocks.

The mites are slower and more numerous. Rust-red herds graze along the veins with their heads lowered, combing algae from the channels. Some carry round eggs beneath their bodies, but the eggs neither hatch nor fail. Others are themselves newly emerged, their skins still pale, though no mite is ever seen to grow old. At intervals the herd divides around an obstacle—a mushroom stalk, a water lens, a waiting predator—and reunites beyond it with the same number of bodies.

Transparent worms occupy the flowing films. They anchor their tails in pits in the leaf skin and extend feathered crowns into the current. Their cilia revolve without tiring, brushing bacteria and fragments of tissue from the water. When pressure passes through the chamber, every worm bends in the same direction. Then all rise again, a field of delicate mechanisms resetting after a stroke.

Rotifers inhabit the reservoirs. Fixed to submerged veins, they open their crowns and turn the brown water into invisible spirals. Spores pass one way, bacteria another, green scraps a third. Their jaws work deep inside their glass-clear bodies with such speed that they seem to contain little mills. They feed perpetually and remain perpetually hungry.

The tardigrades move through the moss like ponderous livestock. Their eight blunt legs press whole groves aside. Their bodies are colorless except for the dark matter in their guts, and their claws grip each stem with solemn care. They pause to pierce moss cells and drink, leaving the cells full. Occasionally one folds inward, shriveling into a dry, barrel-shaped tun. Yet moisture gathers at once, and it opens again exactly as before. No drought requires this transformation. It is simply one station in its circuit.

Predators give the place its briefest impressions of decision. Beneath a vein’s torn lip waits the pseudoscorpion, bone-pale and broad-clawed. It can remain still through thousands of impacts, sensing each vibration through the floor. Then its pincers close around a passing mite or springtail. The struggle is violent, magnified by the taut membrane beneath them. The prey is dragged backward into shadow and eaten. Soon afterward, the same route carries a creature indistinguishable from the one taken.

In the tarn lies the midge larva. Its body resembles a length of clear tubing with a black head and a few internal threads. It rests beneath the surface, suspended between reflection and the cellular grid below. When a springtail lands to drink, the larva strikes. Waves run outward, touch every shore, and return in perfect rings. Sometimes the springtail escapes. Sometimes it does not. Neither outcome changes the next strike.

There are smaller lives still: bacteria glazing every damp surface; flagellates flickering in trapped droplets; amoebae deforming themselves through the peat; nematodes threading the fungal mesh; minute eggs fastened beneath hairs and within folds. None are merely hidden stages of future creatures. The egg remains on the verge of opening. The juvenile remains newly made. The mature feeder remains at full appetite. Birth, growth, death, and decay are present only as motions, never as passages.

No inhabitant appears to understand the condition of the chamber. They flee, hunt, feed, mate, molt, root, release spores, and seek moisture with complete urgency. Their bodies insist that each act matters to what comes next. Instinct repeatedly promises a future that the leaf does not permit.

They do, however, respond to the opening at the curled tip.

Whenever an outer gnat lands on the water seal, the creatures nearest it become restless. Springtails assemble on the converging veins. Mites lift their forelegs. Rotifers contract into themselves. Even the fungal cords alter their pulse, driving a faint amber surge toward the colorless aperture. The gnat’s feet stand beyond the membrane like black columns, attached to a life that can depart.

For a few moments, the whole plantworks seems to orient toward those legs.

Then the gnat rises. Its shadow slips away across the roof. The water seal relaxes, warm vapor recoils, and the gathered creatures disperse along their accustomed routes. None crosses the threshold. Perhaps none can. Perhaps passage would require the one thing absent from the chamber: an event that cannot be undone.

So they remain—grazer and hunter, spore and hypha, moss tower and turning wheel—separate organisms bound into the metabolism of the leaf. They are neither prisoners nor immortals in any ordinary sense, because imprisonment implies passing time, and immortality implies a life extended through it. These beings possess only recurrence.

Together they inhabit a single sustained instant of abundance: always feeding, always consumed, always germinating, always at work beneath the green-gold skin. Their innumerable mouths make the roar. Their bodies keep the conduits open. Their waste nourishes the films that nourish them. Every life is fuel for every other, yet nothing is depleted and nothing increases.

What lives inside the fallen leaf is an entire food web held at the moment of perfect function—a civilization without memory, a factory without product, and perhaps one diffuse organism whose countless parts still mistake themselves for individuals.
