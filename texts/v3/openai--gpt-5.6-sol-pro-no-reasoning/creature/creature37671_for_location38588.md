The inhabitants have no common shape, only a common talent for remaining unfinished.

They are creatures of the interval between water and steam, between a sharp edge and the wound it might make. Their bodies answer conditions faster than ancestry can fix them. In liquid they become lucid and soft, nearly indistinguishable from the droplets around them. Under heat they tarnish into metallic motes, unfold bladders, vanes, and hollow wings, and rise into vapor. On dry glass they flatten into mineral films so thin that their organs must lie beside one another rather than within. Many can pass from one state to another. Few survive being forced to remain in any state for long.

The most numerous are the wall-clingers: clear, jointless discs with rings of inward-pointing teeth. They graze the warm haze of minute life that powders the obsidian. Where food is plentiful, thousands overlap until the cliff appears glazed. They move by releasing one side of the mouth and fastening the other, a slow rolling that leaves temporary circles in the condensation. When startled, they seal flat and become lenses. Through a crowded colony, the black glass appears multiplied into innumerable small, distorted depths.

Among them hunt thread-knots. At rest, one resembles a snarl of transparent filament caught on a crystal point. When prey approaches, the snarl unties. A single filament may extend across several ledges without crossing the space between them, following adjacency rather than distance. It loops around a grazer and tightens—not on its body, but on the cavity inside it. The victim collapses clear and empty. The knot recoils, now carrying a new loop whose faint pulse will eventually separate and become another hunter.

The leg-forests are less easily named. Each consists of dozens or hundreds of fine limbs arranged around an absence. There is no central body. Food is passed from foot to foot around the vacant middle until it vanishes, and eggs appear afterward on the outermost joints. If divided, they do not heal: each portion simply revises where its center is not. They travel in dry-sounding congregations, producing the whisper that sometimes passes over the cliffs before rain.

In the hottest basins live the delay-hunters, squat forms darkened by layer upon layer of heat-resistant skin. They rarely move. Around each one, light slows and reflections detach slightly from what they reflect. The hunter attacks the delayed image of an animal, opening it there; a moment later the wound arrives in the living body. Old hunters are surrounded by a wavering corona of past prey—fleeing, feeding, or mating fractions of a breath behind the present. When the basin boils, these accumulated reflections vanish all at once, and the hunter must begin collecting time again.

Vapor has its own fauna. When the red fissure opens and water leaps from the lower shelves, dormant skins burst into silver bladders, hinged loops, comb-winged specks, and mouths borne aloft without bodies. They swim through steam and feed on differences of temperature. Liquid water clogs them instantly; a cool droplet is to them what deep ocean would be to a flame. At the first return of condensation they gather beneath overhangs, knotting themselves into pale crusts. There they wait, indistinguishable from fresh mineral deposits, until heat frees them again.

Several species inhabit the hanging drops. Some paddle along the inner surface, suspended upside down beneath the world outside. Others crowd so densely that the apparent boundary of the droplet is made from their interlocked backs. They regulate its shape, shifting weight to keep it attached to the ceiling. Within the larger drops are warm internal seas and smaller weathers, and within those live swimmers that may never encounter glass at all. To them, the enclosing animals are landscape. Their migrations are felt by the host-droplet as changes in surface tension.

The tool users belong to the many-legged kind, though “kind” may describe a habit rather than a lineage. They have paired grasping limbs close to the mouth and a fringe of shorter feet that test the competing gravities of adjacent facets. Their bodies are smoky, angular, and often contain bright inclusions that move independently. They select obsidian not only for sharpness but for freshness. A newly broken edge still remembers the larger stone’s geometry and can cut through shells whose surfaces have chosen different laws.

They use such flakes quickly. They open armored grazers, shave mineral films, sever predatory threads, and score tiny channels along the glass so condensation runs toward clusters of eggs. The channels are never straight. A straight channel persists too clearly. Instead they branch, double back, and end in false basins, as if confusing water—or confusing whatever notices intention.

Their eggs resemble flaws in the obsidian: tiny smoky commas just beneath the reflective surface. When wetted, each flaw opens inward. The young do not hatch out. They deepen until the glass contains enough interior for them to unfold, after which they emerge from some nearby droplet, already carrying the instinct to avoid a place no surviving creature can explain.

The tool users know, in whatever way knowledge is possible here, that manufacture is perilous. A natural splinter belongs to the fracture. A shaped edge implies a sequence: selection, purpose, alteration, use. It remains itself from moment to moment. That persistence makes it conspicuous.

So they dispose of tools in hot pools and let mineral skins erase the evidence of making. Sometimes they are too slow. Then attention enters the pocket.

No inhabitant sees the visitor. They register it as agreement: all local gravities briefly point toward one object; all reflections show the same angle; every creature, however simple, becomes aware of the same place. The shaped tool is removed not from the landscape but from the history by which it arrived there. Often its maker follows. Channels remain because water has already learned them. Eggs remain because they have begun elsewhere. Fear remains because fear need not remember its cause.

This may be why nothing builds. It may be why no body keeps one anatomy longer than necessary. Permanence is a scent, and something beyond the fracture hunts by it.

Yet the inhabitants are not merely fugitives. They feed with exquisite specialization. They court by exchanging organs, shadows, temperatures, or directions of fall. Some mate only during an eclipse, when a passing finger covers the seam and every rainbow disappears. Some divide when struck by rain. Some require the brief interval during which a drop still obeys the outer world while the ledge beneath it obeys another down. Their offspring are born along the disagreement.

Death is equally various. A body may be eaten, boiled clear, plated over, untied, or reduced to a habit inherited by unrelated animals nearby. Nothing becomes soil. Almost nothing becomes memory. What remains is pattern: a route taken around an empty patch, a sudden flattening when brightness opens below, the communal turn toward a cool bead forming in the heights.

That bead is their season, migration, and promised country.

As it swells, the wall-clingers roll upward. Thread-knots cast lines from facet to facet. Leg-forests lift their outer limbs and flow over the glass like a pale fur. Vapor creatures descend inside narrow columns of steam, risking drowning for the cool boundary they need to seed their next forms. Hunters leave the boiling basins, dragging zones of delayed reflection behind them. Tool users abandon their channels and climb with the rest.

The country appears to move, though the stone does not.

Millions converge without crowding because each carries more interior than its outline permits. Predators pass through prey. Parasites open rooms inside hosts. Whole lineages arise in the warm films left by the ascent and vanish before reaching the droplet. The bead hangs above them, clear and immense, holding an inverted image of the outer sky.

When at last it falls, some will be crushed, some carried into the depths, some translated into creatures suited to the sudden rivers. For a few minutes the fracture will be all flood and glittering migration. Then the water will climb again as steam, and life will revise itself to follow.

From outside, none of this exceeds the faint brightening of a wet seam in black stone.
