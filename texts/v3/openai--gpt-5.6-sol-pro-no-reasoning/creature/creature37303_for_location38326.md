What lives there does not travel.

It has no use for speed, pursuit, concealment, or warning. Nothing in that world has ever been chased. No leaf has evolved against a grazing mouth; no flower has learned the shape of a visitor. Life adheres, infiltrates, swells, dries, and waits.

The most widespread inhabitants are too thin to see from a distance. They lie across the plain as dark varnishes and powder-pale stains: colonies of algae, lichens, fungi, and dormant spores fitted to minute differences in shade and moisture. A depression no deeper than a thumbprint may hold its own community. One species occupies the cool edge of a glazed crack, another the porous mortar beneath it, another only the salt ring left when a bead of water evaporates.

They feed slowly on light, airborne dust, and the dissolved substance of the construction itself. Acids seep from their living films and cloud the glaze beneath them. Threads enter pores too narrow for roots, separating lime grain from lime grain. After rain they deepen in color, almost black or green; by noon they contract into brittle skins. Some can remain dry for years, indistinguishable from mineral discoloration, then resume their growth within minutes of being wet.

In the covered ways live the veils.

These are broad fungal tissues suspended from damp ceilings or spread over walls in gray, translucent layers. They have no fruiting bodies shaped to attract or be carried by animals. Instead, when a vault cools after sunset, the veils tighten. Their surfaces wrinkle and release clouds of nearly weightless spores into the slow currents of underground air. The spores settle on pillars, in drains, on the undersides of stairs. Most wait without consequence. A few encounter recurring damp and become another veil.

Where roof chambers breathe through distant openings, entire galleries may pulse with this life. Condensation darkens the sheets; they absorb water and sag. As the air dries, they draw taut again, making a faint papery sound. Their pale edges follow old mortar lines, so that from far down a corridor they can resemble writing too large and incomplete to read.

More conspicuous plants occupy the paths of temporary water.

The smallest form low, rubbery mats pressed into seams. Their bodies are divided into overlapping lobes, each able to store a little water and seal itself beneath a translucent skin. Dust accumulates around them. Dead outer layers become the first true organic sediment on the plain: a black-brown film caught among cream mortar grains and red brick flour. New growth advances over this mixture by fractions of a finger’s width each wet season.

In shallow basins stand the little reeds. They grow in crowded rings around places where water lingers longest, though few rise higher than a hand. Their stems are jointed, hollow, and silver at the tips. Beneath them is not soil but a felt of roots and crumbled masonry. They spend most of the year desiccated, bent in the prevailing wind. Rain straightens them. Within hours their cells fill, and the rings turn from ash-gray to muted green.

They reproduce by breaking.

When dry, the upper joints loosen. Wind knocks them together until fragments fall and skitter along channels in the pavement. The next storm carries them downslope. A piece lodged beneath a step may remain inert through many seasons, then root when a sequence of cool nights provides enough condensation. Thus the reed rings migrate according to drainage, tracing an ancient hydraulic order without knowing it.

The largest living things are the solitary water-bearers scattered across the open districts.

They are ribbed columns of green-gray flesh, seldom branching, their surfaces waxed against the long heat. Most are no taller than a person, though great age can produce broader individuals crouched like low pillars. They begin in cracks, sending fine roots horizontally between courses of tile. These roots may travel far beyond the visible plant, following damp interfaces and salt-softened mortar. They do not break the masonry quickly. They inhabit its weakness and make that weakness patient.

A mature column may store the water of several storms. In drought it contracts, drawing its ribs inward until deep pleats form. The outer skin dulls and takes on the color of dust. It can remain so for years.

After sufficient rain, pressure returns to its tissues. A seam opens near the crown, and by night a cluster of small white flowers unfolds. Their petals are plain, almost geometric, arranged to expose pollen directly to the air. There is no scent. Nothing needs to be summoned.

Warm updrafts lift the dry pollen. Cooling air carries it down again. Some settles into grooves on neighboring flowers; much more becomes part of the dust. Fertilized blooms fold and harden around a few heavy seeds. These eventually drop close to the parent or roll along the shallow slope until arrested by a joint. The seeds are armored against heat and alkalinity. Generations may accumulate in a crack before one finds the brief conjunction of water, shade, and softened mortar required to open.

Because nothing eats them, these plants possess no thorns, poisons, bitter sap, or sacrificial fruit. Their defenses are against exposure alone: mirrored hairs, wax, compact form, deep dormancy. Their architecture answers temperature, not appetite.

Yet they are not gentle in their effects.

Roots enter the continent’s seams and widen them. Fungal acids turn polished surfaces cloudy. Lichen colonies retain moisture against walls, encouraging flakes to detach. Reed mats trap sediment and raise the floors of basins. When a solitary column dies, it does not become carrion. It collapses inward and dries into a fibrous husk. Rain presses it into the crack where it grew. Fungi reduce it slowly, leaving carbon among the lime and ceramic grains.

Around such remains, the first dark soils begin—not inherited earth, but matter made locally by life from air, water, and architecture.

There may be whole provinces whose inhabitants exist almost entirely below sight. Root webs occupy the narrow planes between tiles. Microbial films line buried cisterns. Pale fungal cords extend through cool vaults for kilometers, interrupted by collapsed chambers and resumed wherever damp masonry continues. None forms a single mind or organism at continental scale. Their connectedness is incidental, produced by repeated conditions and the continuity of the structure.

Still, they give the made land a kind of inward metabolism.

Rain enters. Films wake. Salts dissolve. Roots swell. Mortar grains loosen. Water moves deeper. During drought, tissues contract and leave new voids. During heat, spores rise through shafts. During cooling, condensation carries dissolved minerals back down walls. Life does not merely occupy the construction; it participates in its settling.

There are no watchers among these inhabitants. No eyes open when storms arrive. No ears receive the underground thunder of a falling roof. Nothing startles when a slab sinks or when a cornice shatters across an empty court.

But living surfaces register every event.

A new fracture admits darkness, damp, and spores. A collapse exposes cool faces on which lichens begin their nearly motionless advance. A flooded stair holds a green line at each receding level. A shifted tile shades one seam and condemns another to glare. The record accumulates in stains, growth rings, root scars, spore beds, and thin organic horizons.

What lives there is therefore less a population than a slow occupation of possibility. Wherever the construction gathers water, excludes noon, catches dust, or permits a root’s width of entry, something eventually arrives—not by foot or wing, but as pollen, fragment, spore, or invisible cell borne on weather.

It waits.

Then, briefly, it becomes green.
