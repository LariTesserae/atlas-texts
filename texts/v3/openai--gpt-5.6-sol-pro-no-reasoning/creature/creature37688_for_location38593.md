The people of the Green Works are difficult to distinguish, at first, from the labor that surrounds them.

They wear aprons of waxed leaf, masks carved from husk, boots wrapped in shed caterpillar silk. Their tools cling to them alive: scissor beetles sleeping in belt loops, measuring tendrils wound around wrists, lantern grubs carried in cages beneath the ribs. Sap and spores stain their skin. Many have scarred palms from grafting work, small pale circles where medicinal leeches have been fixed, or blackened teeth from chewing the bitter resins that keep them wakeful through the active shift. Seen motionless beside a trunk, a worker may resemble another knot in the bark. Only the eyes move.

They are not all of one ancestry, or even entirely of one kind.

There are long-established human families in the terraces, broad-footed and compact from generations spent under low ceilings. Their shoulders tend to stoop, but their hands are extraordinarily strong. They can tell a pressure change by the ache behind the eyes and identify districts by the taste of their condensation. Most cut their hair close, not as fashion but because anything loose is eventually claimed by gears, thorns, hooks, or nesting mites.

Some households include the blind trellis monkeys as dependents, partners, thieves, or all three. The monkeys have names, work tokens, and grudges. A few are formally apprenticed to valve keepers. They understand dozens of touch signs and can travel through the overhead city faster than any ground messenger, though they remain incapable of resisting polished brass. A responsible workshop locks its gauges before allowing a monkey crew inside. An irresponsible one paints cheaper gauges gold and accepts the consequences.

Rat-eared weather animals live in the walls of nearly every inhabited building. They are neither pets nor vermin. Families leave peelings and mineral salt for them and listen at dawn for the quickening scratch that predicts rainfall. Children learn their patterns before they learn engine bells. In some houses, a particular colony has occupied the same partition longer than the resident family and is included in tenancy disputes.

The largest population may be the ants.

No census has measured them successfully. They occupy a second Green Works nested inside the first: hair-thin roads under the rootways, warm brood chambers around steam pipes, fungal granaries in walls too narrow for a human finger. Their colonies are treated as wards, contractors, infestations, and sovereign neighborhoods according to circumstance. Ant envoys—large, lacquered individuals bred to tolerate open air—are carried to municipal meetings in humid boxes. They do not speak. They arrange scented beads into patterns while an interpreter reads the movement of their antennae.

There are stories that the interpreters invent half of what the ants say. The ants continue to employ them.

No single authority rules the district. Power belongs to those who can keep a process alive.

The Rain Tenders have the greatest prestige and the least freedom. They live in narrow dwellings grown directly into the central engine’s support roots, where the vibration never stops except before rain. They sleep strapped into hammocks so the machinery cannot shake them to the floor. Their ears are permanently damaged; many communicate almost entirely through pressure signs made against rails, tabletops, and one another’s sternums.

Rain Tenders listen with their whole bodies. A cheek against a pipe tells them which intake is fouled. A bare heel on the engine platform reveals whether a ceramic vane has cracked. They cultivate colonies of diagnostic insects in shallow boxes: beetles that dance in response to dangerous harmonics, worms that knot themselves when the vapor mix turns acidic, moths whose folded wings predict the approach of bearing failure.

They do not command the Rain Engine. They placate, prune, clean, replace, and interpret it. No Tender agrees on where the machine ends and its attendant organisms begin. The oldest diagrams show clear divisions between metal, plant, fungus, and animal. Nothing in the present engine respects those lines.

Below the Tenders in status—but richer, usually—are the sap factors. They control tapping rights, reservoir fruits, fermentation schedules, and the transparent vascular pipes that carry raw fluid between civic trees and processing vats. They dress conspicuously in polished chitin and cultivate smooth, perfumed fungi on their collars. Their clerks calculate in measures of sweetness, viscosity, and recovery time. A debt may be denominated not in coin but in how many days a trunk must bleed.

Cutters form another old caste. They remove diseased wood, dismantle obsolete structures, and kill growth that has crossed its allotment. Because nearly everything in the district is alive, theirs is a troubling profession. They must know when cutting is carpentry, when it is surgery, and when it is execution.

A cutter’s principal tool is often a long-headed beetle bred to bite only after tasting a particular resin. The cutter paints the approved line. The beetle follows it. For larger work, teams use saw-centipedes held between forked handles, feeding them strips of salted hide to keep them from turning on the operators.

Cutters mark their faces with white fungal ash. The custom began as protection against spores and became a warning: do not distract this person; something nearby has already been judged expendable.

The wardens are less a police force than mobile arbitrators between appetites. They patrol in pairs along the marked rootways wearing shells hung with bells. The bells announce them to citizens and satisfy the ordinance governing large carnivores, since many wardens ride or work beside such creatures. Their favored partners are low, six-legged hunting lizards with luminous mite colonies embedded along the jaw. The mites brighten when the lizards become excited, giving pedestrians time to clear the road.

Wardens carry dye syringes, pruning hooks, nets, and packets of concentrated odors used to redirect swarms. Weapons meant to kill are rare. Killing one creature often creates work for ten others.

Their most common disputes concern eggs. Eggs appear everywhere: in axle housings, under warm vats, inside ventilation flowers, along pipes, in lunch baskets, beneath the collars of sleeping workers. Some are harmless. Some hatch into valuable cleaners. Some become fist-sized borers capable of opening a pressure vessel overnight. Wardens learn thousands of clutches by color and texture. When they cannot identify one, they chalk a circle around it and reroute traffic until somebody can.

Children grow up close to the floor.

The youngest wear broad fungus-lamp caps so they remain visible among carts and adult legs. Bells are tied to their ankles until they learn the safe rhythms of movement. They gather moth scales, sort husks, carry messages through passages too narrow for grown workers, and harvest the translucent shoots that emerge after rain. Every child is taught three prohibitions before any formal trade: never drink from an untested cup in living wood; never follow a familiar voice upward into darkness; never touch a quiet machine.

Their games rehearse the city’s hazards. They play collapse, in which one child becomes a failing branch and the others must brace or dismantle them. They play warden and predator, arguing over whether the capture occurred on a marked rootway. They imitate monkey alarm calls until an adult stops them. During rain they race seed-hulls down the temporary channels, placing bets in beetle cases and bright stones.

Few of them have seen the old city above. To them, open sky is not freedom but an impossible structural defect.

The elderly become reservoirs of remembered arrangements. They know where streets ran before roots turned them, which house is secretly continuous with which factory, and what sleeps behind walls that have not opened for forty years. Since written records rot, sprout, or are eaten, much municipal knowledge is kept in songs tapped through wood.

An old route keeper may sit for hours with both hands on a civic trunk, reciting changes into its rhythm:

three knocks for a sealed tunnel,  
a scrape for sour soil,  
two palm beats for load-bearing root,  
fingers drummed lightly for nesting life.

These records are not symbolic alone. Certain trees have been trained to thicken, flower, or release scents in response to the patterns. Memory is distributed between the keeper and the plant. When a keeper dies without teaching a successor, a portion of the city becomes illegible.

Death itself is seldom private. Bodies are too nutritionally significant to waste and too biologically dangerous to abandon.

The dead are washed in bitter latex, inspected for parasites, and carried to one of the quiet courts. Families may claim teeth, hair, chitin implants, treasured symbionts, and sometimes a finger bone to set into a household threshold. The remainder enters the district’s cycles. Some are placed beneath saplings. Some go to fungal vaults. Skilled workers may be incorporated into structures associated with their trade: a Rain Tender beneath the engine roots, a road polisher beneath a new crossing, a trellis keeper in an upper orchard.

This is not spoken of as returning to nature. The people of the Green Works do not regard nature as a place one leaves or returns to. They say the dead have taken another shift.

Their devotions are practical, suspicious, and intimate.

They offer sugar to pump housings, blood to newly cut roots, warm milk to pale snakes after foundation repairs. Before opening a long-sealed room, someone knocks on the wall and waits—not for an answer exactly, but for the absence of one. Rain is greeted by exposing the palms. Fire is cursed even when useful. Silence is never prayed for.

The closest thing to a sacred being may be the chamber itself, though few residents would call it a being aloud. They speak instead of moods: the Works are thirsty, the Works are brooding, the Works have begun to favor the western terraces. Such phrases are partly metaphor and partly observed fact. Buildings do close against disliked occupants. Roads do migrate toward well-kept neighborhoods. After murders, bitter leaves do fall.

Whether the city judges anyone is a matter for tavern argument. That it reacts is beyond dispute.

A smaller, secretive population lives where even workers seldom look: in the high root-webs above the manufacturing quarter, close to the condensation dome.

They are called the Pale Choir, though they do not call themselves anything that carries through ordinary air. Their bodies suggest descent from the warning birds that still scream among the rafters, but the resemblance has been altered by darkness and height. They are thin-limbed, almost featherless, with translucent skin stretched over deep breastbones. Their feet can close around cables. Their vestigial wings have become broad sensory fans threaded with blood vessels, exquisitely responsive to vibration and air pressure.

The Choir tends the ceiling.

They crawl among cold roots, clearing fungal mats from condensation surfaces, opening and closing leaf gutters, and consuming insects that would foul the rain catchments. They map vapor currents through song. Each individual produces only clicks, whistles, and harsh descending cries, but gathered across the dome their calls reveal temperature, moisture, obstructions, and leaks. Rain Tenders below interpret the shifting chorus as another set of instruments.

No one knows how many Choir-folk there are. They lower their dead in woven cradles but rarely descend alive. Once or twice in a generation, a child of the lower city is taken upward—usually one born with malformed eyes and an unusual sensitivity to vibration. Years later, a new voice joins the ceiling.

At street level, amid guilds and engines and tolerated predators, live the unattached: scavengers, fungus hunters, discharged apprentices, fugitive debtors, and people whose buildings have refused them.

A building’s refusal can destroy a household. The door puckers shut. Sleeping shelves tilt. Water-storage tissues sour. Attempts to force entry cause the walls to release itching fibers or attract wood-boring insects. Sometimes the reason is clear: blood spilled repeatedly, roots poisoned, heat vents blocked. Sometimes it is not. Those expelled may petition a horticultural court, but cases move slowly because the judges must observe the structure through several moisture cycles.

Until then, the refused sleep in membrane tents on open loam, never too close to a road or cistern. They form brief neighborhoods that vanish after rain. Some become experts in coaxing abandoned buildings back into tolerance. Others learn to live without walls and acquire the wary stillness of prey.

Not everything intelligent in the Green Works has a face.

One of the civic trunks near the eastern mills has, over centuries, accumulated the plumbing of several demolished offices, the fungal records of a court, and the abandoned pheromone galleries of an ant colony. Its roots penetrate a warm computational vault left from the old city. The resulting organism is called the Clerk.

Petitions are pushed into a slit in its bark on sheets of pressed pith. Days or months later, the Clerk extrudes a ribbon of cambium marked with raised knots. Licensed readers interpret these as permits, denials, tax assessments, or requests for further information. The Clerk is ponderous, inconsistent, and impossible to bribe in any ordinary way. It has approved marriages, condemned unsafe bridges, reassigned workshops, and once declared a moth swarm legally responsible for stolen lamp oil.

Most residents complain about it. Nobody permits it to be cut.

There are also the pale foundation snakes, which may be more aware than the laws admit. They move through compressed soil and ancient masonry, leaving aerated passages behind them. They gather beneath sites where collapses will later occur. They have been seen knotting themselves into repeating shapes near damaged supports, and old route keepers claim those shapes correspond to forgotten structural signs.

The snakes are protected because the city needs them. They are feared because they sometimes emerge in inhabited rooms and stare at sleepers with lidless, colorless eyes. A snake may remain for hours beside one person and ignore all others. That person usually receives advice, afterward, to move.

Some do not.

Above all these lives the collective multitude: the grinders in the mills, the vat skimmers, the moth shepherds, the frog harnessers, the road polishers, the spore sweepers, the boiler grafters, the night orchardists, the mite combers, the worm tuners who maintain the municipal horns. Their labor is repetitive but never identical. A machine’s needs change as it grows. A route thickens, a valve buds, a vat becomes gravid with gas. Every trade is partly the study of variation.

They work in noise so constant that their bodies continue to feel it after they sleep. They dream of vibration. Infants are soothed by being laid against pump casings. Lovers court by sharing a rail and tapping private rhythms beneath the public thunder. Arguments are conducted forehead to forehead, each person feeling the other’s anger resonate through bone.

When the Rain Engine stops, all of them become still.

The human hand pauses on the lever. The ant halts beneath its load. The hunting lizard lifts one luminous jaw. Monkeys crouch with stolen metal clasped to their chests. The Pale Choir folds itself against the ceiling roots. Even the Clerk’s leaves draw inward.

In that arresting quiet, the inhabitants do not seem like separate species or guilds or hungers. They become organs awaiting a common signal.

Then the first drop falls.

The Green Works open.

Mouths tilt upward. Gutters spread. Frogs drag themselves from wells. Reservoir fruits unclench. Children shriek and run. Workers strip off masks and let the condensation wash black dust from their faces. Predators drink beside prey under the brief authority of thirst. Ant roads dissolve and reform on higher ground. The ceiling sings in a thousand impacts.

For an hour, everyone who lives there is visible in motion.

Then the water sinks, the lights dim, and the district closes around them again—not hiding them, exactly, but returning them to their proper state: threaded through the machinery, housed in the plants, carried inside one another’s lives.
