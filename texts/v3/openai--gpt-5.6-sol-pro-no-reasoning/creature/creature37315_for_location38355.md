The dominant lives of the high country are not its largest animals but its continuities: colonies, mats, interlocked roots, and creatures whose bodies participate in the movement of water. Almost nothing lives here as a solitary outline. Fur carries algae. Wood carries forests. Cliffs carry gardens. Even a footprint becomes habitat before its maker has gone far.

The old six-legged grazers come closest to embodying the plateau. They are long-backed and low-necked, with paired forelimbs built for levering apart sedge turf and four hind legs that spread their weight across the marsh. Their shoulders contain immense slabs of muscle, though they move with deliberation, testing each patch of ground before committing their weight. The narrow skull ends in a divided, dark-lipped muzzle capable of shearing grasses above the water or pulling pale roots from below it.

Their coats begin almost black. With age, the outer hairs become grooved and porous, catching spores and holding rain. Green films establish themselves first along the spine, then spread over the shoulders and flanks. Moss grows behind the hornlike ridges above the eyes. Tiny invertebrates shelter in the warmer undercoat, and small bill-clicking birds work through it, taking parasites without stripping away the useful growth. The oldest grazers resemble slow fragments of turf that have lifted themselves and begun to walk.

They do not merely inhabit the marshes. They extend them. Each step compresses peat, opens buried channels, and leaves a deep basin that immediately fills from below. Their feeding exposes mineral soil. Their trails gather seepage; seepage attracts sedges; sedge roots stabilize the channel. Generations of movement become tributaries. The herds have no understanding of watershed or engineering, yet valleys remember their migrations long after individual animals are gone.

They communicate through the ground. A forefoot pressed hard against a root plate sends a pulse through saturated soil, too low to carry clearly through air. Other members of the herd receive it through sensitive pads along their lower legs and chest. The signals are simple—danger, separation, deep water, a calf calling—but in broad marshes they may pass from group to group until a whole basin changes direction without any audible alarm.

Their predators are similarly shaped by quiet.

These hunters are broad-footed, supple-bodied animals whose weight is distributed across splayed toes fringed with water-shedding fibers. Their coats separate rain into bright threads so that water falls away without saturating the insulating layer beneath. They wait among tree ferns or travel along suspended trunks, placing each foot where roots or old branches will carry it. They seldom chase. A rush across unstable peat would announce itself everywhere. Instead they study the slow traffic of the marsh and approach through channels already trembling from springs.

When they strike, it is brief. When they fail, they do not pursue far. The ground belongs too completely to vibration for persistence to remain secret.

Smaller lives occupy the intervals these great animals cannot use. Feathered browsers move through shrub layers, clipping buds, fungi, and soft fern tissue. Their feathers are dense and oil-dark, with stiff tips that comb water from leaves before the birds feed. They click their bills at distances where faces are still visible; beyond that, the sound becomes unreliable in fog. Their more important messages are visual: flashes of pale throat skin, the measured raising of crest feathers, a wing held briefly open against the dim forest.

Some follow grazing herds, feeding where hooves expose roots and larvae. Others live almost entirely on fallen trees. A suspended trunk may support several generations, with nesting hollows in its softened core, feeding territories across its mossy upper surface, and escape passages through fungal chambers below. To such a creature, the green bridge is not debris. It is an elevated valley.

The cliff birds live in hanging vegetation. Their nests are woven into mats of roots where groundwater crosses the rock face. The roots continue to grow through the nest walls, fastening them more securely each season. When cold rain swells the fibers, the cup contracts around the eggs. The adult birds need only shelter the exposed side with their bodies while the living cliff holds the clutch from beneath.

Higher still, long-winged hunters use the plateau’s buried heat. Their wings are narrow at the base and divided into flexible vanes near the tips, allowing them to feel weak differences in rising air. They patrol geothermal ravines where warmth lifts vapor through the trees. In fog they may be visible only as a dark change in brightness, circling without a wingbeat. They hunt cliff nesters, marsh birds, and the small animals that emerge near warm springs in winter. Their territories follow columns of air rather than landmarks and shift as vents weaken or new fissures open.

Beneath the peat live the dark fish.

Their bodies are flattened enough to pass through water films under root mats. Their side fins are muscular, jointed, and edged with gripping rays. During saturated nights they push out of spring windows and travel through moss, the whole body bending in slow lateral strokes. Mucus protects the skin and leaves the silver routes seen at dawn. They are not wandering at random. They can detect minute mineral differences in water and will cross stretches of land to reach newly opened pools, flooded hoofprints, or warm seepage where eggs develop faster.

Some pools exist for only a season. The fish arrive, breed, and depart before sedges close the surface. Their young may spend months under what appears to be solid ground, feeding in the cold darkness among roots.

The hollow water-plants host a population as intricate as any forest. Shelled insects live inside the joints of their segmented trunks. They scrape fungus from the membranes, remove mineral crusts, and keep the narrow valve passages open. Their shells are low and smooth so that a discharge cannot tear them away. Before a chamber empties, changes in pressure cause them to flatten against recessed grooves in the wall. The membrane snaps. Water sighs into the next basin. The insects emerge afterward to feed on the sugars exposed along the valve.

Their colonies reproduce by following new growth upward. Winged forms are rare; most young crawl from one connected stem to another through films of water. A hillside of these plants is therefore also an immense, branching settlement. Its inhabitants may never encounter open air except when a stem breaks.

Other animals read the electrical mosses. Pale burrowers, nearly blind, have broad sensory plates around the snout and forelimbs. The weak impulses moving through wet copper-colored mats give them a map of edges, flowing seepage, approaching bodies, and perhaps distant weather. They dig shallow galleries under the moss rather than deep tunnels, staying within the charged layer. During heavy atmospheric pressure, when the marsh rims begin to glow, they rise close to the surface and align themselves in the same direction. Whether this is navigation, courtship, or merely comfort is difficult to separate; here one behavior often performs several functions at once.

Amphibious grazers follow the great herds’ tracks. Soft-bodied and hand-sized, they rasp exposed roots and microbial films from the new pools. Their eggs are laid in the clear, cold water of fresh impressions before sediment darkens it. Many pools dry into mossy hollows, but enough deepen into channels that the species travels with the creation of drainage itself.

Fungi may be the plateau’s most extensive inhabitants. Their visible fans—white, violet-gray, amber—are only seasonal edges of networks running through fallen timber, living roots, and peat. They enter a suspended trunk before it falls completely, hollowing it without immediately breaking its outer shell. This prolongs the life of the bridge. Seedlings root into the softened core; animals make passages; moss preserves moisture. By the time the trunk ceases to be recognizable as wood, much of its matter has already crossed into other bodies.

Even the forests behave less as collections of trees than as load-bearing communities. The conifers stand on broad plates of roots that overlap beneath the saturated ground. A tree beginning to tilt may be held for decades by its neighbors. Epiphytes add weight; fungi remove strength; water shifts the balance by increments. When one finally settles, it often does not crash. It leans into the canopy, descends through shrubs, and remains there, alive along part of its length while another part becomes soil.

Nothing presides over all of this. There is no master species hidden in the cloud, no intelligence regulating the springs or directing the migrations. What resembles intention is the accumulation of exchanges. Plants meter water because those that burst or drown did not persist. Insects clean valves because sugar gathers there. Grazers make streams because heavy bodies follow soft forage. Mosses carry charge because wet mineral surfaces permit it, and burrowers sense that charge because anything that can read the marsh has an advantage within it.

The inhabitants are therefore difficult to separate from their functions, but they are not mere components. Calves stray and call through the roots. Cliff birds steal fibers from one another’s nests. Predators abandon hunts to lie beside warm vents. Fish choose one silver route over another. Old grazers pause beneath particular trees to have their algae combed by familiar flocks. Individual lives unfold everywhere, private and imperfect, inside the larger continuities.

At night these lives become apparent by interruption. A glow in torn peat goes dark when something crosses it. Vapor folds around an unseen body. A suspended trunk gives one soft knock beneath a foot. The spring chambers thump underground, and after each pulse the shelled insects begin cleaning again.

By morning there may be no lasting print. Water rises. Moss straightens. Rootlets tighten around the cliff nests. The plateau closes gently over every passage, not erasing what lives there, but receiving it back into the ongoing work.
