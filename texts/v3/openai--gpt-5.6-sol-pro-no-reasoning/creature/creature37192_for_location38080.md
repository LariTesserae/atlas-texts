What lives there is not a collection of species so much as a single restlessly partitioned metabolism.

Its smallest inhabitants occupy the stone’s pores. They are clear, nearly structureless things, visible only when mineral grains shift against their movement. They drink differences: a fraction of warmth, a trace of voltage, the tension between fresh water and brine. Packed together, they behave like tissue. They loosen rock, seed frost, and make the mud responsive beneath larger feet. Some live for seconds; some colonies have persisted since the flats first cooled. Their dead bodies become crystals indistinguishable from the crystals they consumed.

Above them are the makers: blind compressors, extruders, polishers, carriers, aligners. Their anatomies resemble tools because the work came first and shaped the flesh around itself. Wedge-headed burrowers ram sediment into chambers under the crust. Six-sided brood animals press their eggs into ingots, each larva sealed inside a different impurity. Hook-mouthed sorters break these blocks apart and test every fragment against organs sensitive to density, charge, age, and unrealized possibility. What they reject becomes terrain. What they keep may become food, armor, mating signal, trap, or the first component in a structure no living participant will survive to see completed.

The herds are the most obvious citizens of the flats. Low and dark, they carry their weight on forests of short articulated legs. Their shells overlap like kiln-fired shingles, often chipped and repaired with secreted glass. Beneath that protection their bodies are soft reservoirs: saline bladders, contractile cables, branching nerves that flash through translucent membranes. They graze with their whole undersides pressed to the world. Warmth, pressure, and electrical imbalance pass into them through broad mouths, and their collective feeding produces the hum that seems to come from underground.

A herd has no fixed membership. Individuals lock together during storms, exchange organs, divide, or leave carrying portions of one another’s memory. Calves bud from the warm seams between crowded adults. At first they are pale, many-legged droplets with a single dark plate. They follow not their parents but the pressure wake of the herd. The old lose their legs one pair at a time and eventually settle over productive cracks, becoming living feeding platforms over which younger bodies pass.

Between their feet run the listeners. These quick ringed creatures are all ear: loops of mineral cartilage joined by wet muscle, with hooks that strike the ground in coded bursts. They map moving hollows, false distances, submerged mouths, and places where an animal’s next step might occur before its present one. Their clicking is not merely perception. In unstable country, a sufficiently precise echo can briefly persuade the terrain to retain a particular shape. Herds tolerate them because a cloud of listeners can hold a crossing together long enough for thousands of bodies to pass.

The fog belongs to folding things. They condense around heat as if assembled by weather: membranes first, then branching frames, then clusters of heavy sensory bulbs. They fly badly but persistently, rattling through vapor on wings that crease and unfold along metallic veins. When warmth fades, they cover rocks in dense colonies and close themselves valve by valve. The lowest layers are crushed and consumed by those above. At the next warming, the survivors open carrying fragments of the dead in their frames, so that every flock is partly made from earlier flocks.

The water contains quieter forms. Silver shoals travel edgewise, thin enough to vanish when they turn. They arrange crystals, eggs, bones, and bits of shell into lines whose geometry redirects current and charge. Some rows attract spawning bodies; others lead predators away; many seem to serve no immediate creature. Yet the shoals cannot cease making them. Their reproductive organs develop only while they are aligned to an unfinished pattern, and completion renders them sterile. Thus they continually build almost-perfect structures and dismantle them before the final piece can be placed.

In the broad pans rotate the great strainers. Each begins as a small ciliated disc attached to another’s underside. Over centuries it thickens into a black armored wheel, half animal and half local current. Its rope-like fringe gathers metals, drifting larvae, loose charge, and microscopic organisms from the water. Layer by layer, these catches become new plates around its circumference. The oldest strainers may no longer possess a center. Water, offspring, scavengers, and entire small ecologies circulate through the hollow where their first body once was.

They turn because feeding, breathing, reproduction, and orientation are the same act. Their rotation circulates the basin; the circulated basin supplies their food. If one dies, the waters around it stagnate, distances thicken, and swarms arrive to eat channels through its plates. Eventually a young strainer anchors in the cavity and inherits the dead one’s current. In this way the sea keeps its machinery without ever preserving an individual machine.

The predators are less easily separated from the ground. Some are long, buried bodies whose ridged backs mimic weathered basalt. Their mouths are horizontal areas rather than visible openings. They feed by inclining gravity toward themselves. Herds begin to lean; listeners tumble; water climbs the slope that is not there. The predator remains still while prey crosses the intervening plain. Inside, falling continues through nested chambers until shell, salt, heat, and momentum have been digested separately.

Others hunt chronology. They wait in delayed reflections or inhabit abandoned sequences beside old channels. When an animal drinks, these predators seize its earlier or later self. The victim may survive apparently unchanged but cease to cast a reflection, fail to grow older, or begin repeating one movement at widening intervals. Such damaged animals are often protected by their herds. They can sense routes no longer present and remember dangers that have not yet arrived.

There are parasites that live only in scale. A black thread may infest a creature’s leg at ordinary size, then emerge from behind a pebble kilometers long. Pale sacs attach to shadows rather than bodies. Glassy mites feed on acceleration, causing their hosts to move with painful slowness until a sudden leap repays all the stolen motion at once. The most feared parasites settle in synchronized sound. When the horizon-wide descending tone passes through the plain, they reproduce inside its lowest frequencies and fall out afterward as hard, ticking eggs.

No animal produces that tone alone.

It belongs to the basin-spanning organisms whose bodies cannot be surveyed from any one location. A watershed may be one creature: channels as vessels, frost fields as sensory tissue, rotating strainers as feeding organs, herds as mobile symbionts, and the black sea as a shallow stomach. These beings overlap. The same river may serve as the nerve of one and the intestine of another. Their thoughts, if thoughts they are, take the form of weather, migration, and coordinated changes of rhythm. A decision requires decades except when the landscape’s causality wears thin; then an impulse may cross the whole plain before it is made.

The upright white creatures at the sea’s edge are among their most sensitive tissues. Each is a hinged shell around a column of conductive brine. Alone, it does little but open toward pressure and close against cold. In millions, the colony passes sequences along the shore. The pattern is not language in the deliberate sense. It is closer to a reflex distributed across bodies: measurement, appetite, memory, and command compressed into a traveling ripple.

Nothing stands apart to read it because everything participates in its reading.

The strainers change speed. Burrowers alter the depth of their hammering. Winged colonies release from the rocks. Herds raise their feeding pads and turn. Predators flatten themselves along the route, hoping the migration will cross their mouths. Dormant eggs open inside mineral rods laid down by vanished species. Reflections gather beneath pools and wait for their originals to pass.

Even the apparently dead things join the movement. Spheres packed in old depressions loosen and roll uphill. Glass bundles flex beneath the silt. Hexagonal ingots split into pale jointed young. Mineral discs lift on cilia too fine to see. Ice shelves release swimmers that have spent centuries threading their bodies through frozen pores. The ground becomes less a surface than a crowd changing posture.

The migration is driven by hunger, but not solely the hunger of animals. The western flats have been grazed smooth of usable gradients. Pressure has equalized. Brines have cooled. Too much accumulated sequence has made the channels unreliable. Elsewhere, along the black sea’s advancing edge, fresh differences are being born: new salt against old water, warm fracture against ice, near against far, now against what has not happened yet. Life follows these differences as other life might follow sunlight.

The oldest beings migrate by altering the world around themselves. Channels reopen like vessels. Rain reverses and rises into the sky, carrying dissolved organisms with it. Gravity leans toward the destination. The shallow sea extends interlocking circular waves into the plain, and the rotating bodies ride forward inside them. Smaller animals walk, swim, tumble, condense, or are passed from one host to another. Predators allow themselves to be dragged with the prey.

The noise is tremendous because every scale of life is in motion at once. Shell strikes shell. Cilia thrash. Fog wings hammer the air. Subterranean bodies drive against the crust. Ice plates rotate screaming through mineral grit. The shared tone descends beneath all of it, turning distance soft.

Then the plain folds along its old channels.

A herd steps behind a low stone and emerges days away. A strainer rotates once and occupies a different basin. Fog creatures vanish upward into rain that has not yet fallen. Whole colonies travel by remaining still while the horizon approaches them. Some animals arrive as fossils and slowly recover movement. Others reach the destination before departing and wait there, feeding beside their younger bodies until sequence repairs itself.

Loss is immense. Many are crushed in the convergence. Many slide into patient mouths. Some enter distorted regions and spread across centuries, never completing another step. But the dead are not left behind. They become plates, currents, gradients, nursery chambers, conductive paths, and pieces of unfinished geometry. Here, extinction and manufacture are neighboring digestive processes.

What lives there is therefore difficult to name in the singular or plural. There are beasts in uncountable abundance, but individuality is temporary. An animal may become infrastructure; a current may become an organ; a pattern may hatch; a waste product may begin to hunt. The landscape is crowded because it does not merely support life. It is what life looks like after so long without plants, builders, or any clean division between organism, machine, and place.

And when the whole plain migrates, it is not leaving its habitat.

The habitat itself is going with it.
