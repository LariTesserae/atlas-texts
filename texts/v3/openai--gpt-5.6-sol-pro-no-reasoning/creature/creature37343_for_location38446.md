The people who live here are built less like conquerors than like creatures perpetually braced for impact.

They are low-bodied and broad through the hips and shoulders, with six limbs arranged along a flexible trunk. Four are usually employed in walking, though any may become a hand when the body settles back on its haunches. The forward pair are finer-jointed, dexterous enough to drill shell and knot wet sinew; the rear limbs are thick, blunt, and powerful, suited to levering hut-pads from sucking mud. On uncertain ground they spread themselves wide, distributing their weight over splayed, keratinous toes. When the crust trembles, they flatten instinctively and become difficult to overturn.

Their skin is soft, pleated, and nearly colorless where it is visible. It must be kept beneath articulated plates against abrasive silt, biting swarms, scalding rain, and the casual collision of herd bodies. These plates are harvested rather than forged: overlapping crescents taken from molted armor, drilled at their edges and tied into garments that can flex, shed sections, and be repaired while worn. A moving group makes a dry, busy music of clicks and scrapes. Individuals recognize one another partly by this sound. Families often tune their armor with hollow beads, ridged plates, and tiny whistles, so that a person approaching through steam or behind a herd can be known before they are seen.

Their heads are wedge-shaped, protected above by a natural brow shield. Several dark, lidless eyes sit beneath it in two uneven rows, each adjusted to a different distance. The stone-light gives them excellent vision; they are not blind cave dwellers and have no mythology of primordial darkness. What concerns them is obstruction—rain, fog, swarms, dust, the sudden wall of an animal migration. Around the mouth grow short sensory tendrils that taste humidity, salts, animal spoor, and the metallic tang that precedes certain cracks. Larger tendrils along the jaw detect minute vibrations transmitted through shell or stone.

They speak, but rarely in long uninterrupted streams. Their voices are low and percussive, suited to carrying beneath the noise: knocks of the throat, chest-booms, clicks made against the inner mouthplates. Much of a conversation is tactile. Companions standing together lay limbs across one another’s armor and communicate through taps, tremors, and pressure. Across longer distances they use drilled whistles whose pitches pierce herd noise without resembling the calls of common predators.

A name is not considered permanent. It changes with age, occupation, migration, and notable survival. A person may be called First-at-the-Drain as a child, White-Raft-Follower in youth, and Twice-Carried-Hall after helping save a communal structure from two collapses. The oldest names become compressed biographies, difficult for outsiders to translate. To speak someone’s whole name is to recount where they have stood and what moved beneath them.

They do not divide the world sharply into wild animal, tool, dwelling, and companion. Nearly everything they use was once alive, remains alive, or may become part of another life. A screen across a passage might be woven from shed membrane over a frame of dormant tube-creatures that stiffen when dry. A water vessel may clamp itself shut if overturned. Heating chambers are made by encouraging fist-sized vent animals to cluster inside insulated shell baskets. Lamps are unnecessary, but small heat-sensitive animals serve as alarms, unfolding bright internal fans when a wall warms toward rupture.

Domestication is temporary and negotiated. A species useful this season may migrate beyond reach in the next. The people tether little. Instead they learn calls, scents, feeding rhythms, and tolerances. They attach shelters to the flanks of mound colonies that welcome waste and warmth, then leave when the colony enters a corrosive phase. They follow broad-backed grazers—not grazers of plants, but scrapers of fresh mineral films—because those animals often choose reliable crust. Tiny burrowers are carried in perforated boxes and released onto suspect ground. If they begin digging, the floor is drying. If they curl inward, water is rising. If they leap, everyone runs.

Children learn geology before genealogy. They are taught to feel a floor through the pads of the feet, to distinguish hollowness from softness, and to sleep with one limb touching a load-bearing rib. Their first independent task is often to cross a patch of young crust carrying a bowl filled to the lip. Spilling is not shameful; failing to notice why one spilled is.

Play mimics survival. Youngsters build miniature walking shelters and race them across trembling mud. They whistle at harmless swarmlets, imitate the freezing posture of startled herd beasts, or compete to predict where a rain thread will reach the floor. Adults pretend not to watch, but remember which children consistently guess well. Such children may become readers of tendencies: scouts, map-cutters, migration listeners, or advisers who decide when a hall must be taken apart.

Their maps resemble no view from above. Thin shell plates are incised with grooves, raised dots, hinged tabs, and patches that change texture when warmed or wetted. They are meant to be handled while traveling. One edge might record the intervals between deep concussions; another the sequence of animal calls near a basin; a folding section might show how to alter course after three warm rains. Maps are revised by scraping, plugging, drilling, and lacing new pieces over old ones. An untouched map is distrusted. Accuracy means having survived correction.

Communities are rarely larger than can dismantle themselves in a few hours. Kinship follows burden rather than blood: those who carry the same hall ribs, share water traps, and answer the same evacuation whistle are considered a household. Families split and join according to terrain. A child may be raised by six or twelve adults scattered among several shelters, meeting them all whenever the migrating structures converge.

Leadership is similarly conditional. The one who knows the winged swarms leads during ceiling weather. The one who reads herd movement leads across open flats. A hall-carrier directs dismantling. A water-taster decides whether a new pool may be drunk. Authority that persists after its usefulness is regarded as dangerous, like a tunnel worn too deep by repeated passage. Their stories are full of leaders who mistook a temporary alignment for ownership and were closed into the stone.

They have no word exactly equivalent to *land*. The closest term means “what is holding, for now.”

Nor do they own fixed territory. They claim routes, timings, agreements with colonies, rights to harvest particular molts, and obligations to warn others of changes. Theft is less often the taking of an object than the withholding of knowledge: failing to mark a thinning crust, concealing a water migration, or driving a herd away before another group can read its behavior. The gravest wrongdoing is to leave certainty where uncertainty should be—to mark a passage safe after it has begun to close, or preserve an obsolete map because it favors one’s own migration.

Their dead cannot be buried reliably. Soft ground may swallow them, hard ground may rise, pools may arrive, and scavengers will find any exposed body. Most communities practice dispersal. Armor is removed and inherited plate by plate. Useful tendons, hooks, and sensory spines are taken with formal gratitude. The remaining body is placed in a migration current, offered to a mound colony, or laid on fresh stone where small creatures strip it quickly. This is not believed to erase the person. Continuity resides in redistribution: a dead elder may travel as a buckle on one child, a needle in another’s kit, a tuning plate in the communal hall, and nourishment moving through a hundred animals.

Some are kept more literally. The inner shell of the head bears fine growth ridges altered by illness, diet, pressure, and age. After death, this plate may be cleaned and read as a final map—not of places, but of conditions endured. A wall of such plates is among the few memorials rebuilt whenever a community moves. Each reassembly changes the arrangement. Ancestors are grouped by relevant experience: those who survived rising water, those who crossed warm crust, those who knew closing tunnels.

Their religion, if it can be called that, is concerned with response rather than creation. They do not ask who made the caverns so often as what the caverns are doing now. The deep concussions are listened to communally. After each one, conversation stops and every person places at least one limb against the ground. Two sounds close together may prompt departure. Three separated by a long quiet may call for feasting, because certain shell migrations often follow. No interpretation is sacred forever. A prediction that fails is carved into the communal record alongside those that succeed.

What they fear most is not death, collapse, or predation. Those are immediate and intelligible. They fear repetition without attention. A route used merely because it was used before; a shelter placed where a shelter once stood; a season named as though it must return unchanged. To become habitual is, in their thought, to invite the world to contradict you.

Yet they are not joyless. Abundance permits festivals as extravagant as anything in a greener world. When silver swimmers raft toward a new pool, families race beside them, scooping only from the trailing edge so the migration survives. During mass molts, pale shells fall from overhead colonies in such numbers that the chambers fill with clattering drifts. People select resonant pieces and suspend them from hall ribs. For days afterward, every tremor plays the settlement like an instrument.

They ferment animal flesh and mineral-rich fluids in sealed stomach vessels, producing sharp, effervescent drinks. They paint armor with clays—red from newly opened layers, violet from the secretions of wall fans, black from heat-cured oils. Because the ambient light is broad and honest, color has social force. Bright patterns declare confidence and health. Scouts wear broken disruptive bands. Mourners leave one side of the body unpainted. Those who have survived a floor opening beneath them mark a clean white line from chest to tail.

Courtship often begins with shared prediction. Two people watch a shifting basin or migrating herd and separately cut their expectations into small shell tiles. Later the tiles are compared. Agreement is pleasing, but not essential; complementary errors are sometimes considered more promising. Lasting pairs and clusters form among those whose attention covers one another’s blind spots.

The other inhabitants are more numerous and often stranger.

Across the flats run many-footed herd animals whose mouths face downward beneath shielded bodies. They rasp newly hardened mineral skins, feeding on salts, trapped organic residues, and microscopic animal films too small to look like growth. Their countless legs act independently. If a crust shears, the front half may climb while the rear half clings to the dropping slab, the body stretching until one set of limbs releases. Whole migrations flow over obstacles with the cohesion of spilled grain.

Among them move leg-shedders: quick, pale hunters with rings of disposable limbs. When caught, they leave a kicking segment behind and escape on the remainder. During plentiful seasons they grow more legs than they require, then snap off the outermost pairs near nurseries, where their young and smaller scavengers consume them. Drifts of limbs are therefore not waste but larders, courtship gifts, territorial markers, and bait.

The immense mound colonies are among the chamber’s longest-lived beings. Each is a society and a body at once. Its visible towers are secretion, shelter, respiratory apparatus, and brood shell. Within, thousands of soft units pass food mouth to mouth and slowly migrate through the architecture. Older bodies mineralize in place, becoming walls that younger bodies inhabit. A mound remembers in structure. Passages thicken where heat once came; sealed vents preserve the chemistry of old rains. Tool-users sometimes read these layers more accurately than stone.

In the pools live glass-limbed filterers, jawed ribbons, armored tiles, and silver rope-beasts whose individual bodies are difficult to distinguish once they knot into rafts. Some species survive drainage by sealing wet organs behind shell shutters and becoming indistinguishable from loose stones. Others race the departing water. Predators spread into funnels, nets, or flowerlike mouths. They need little cunning where abundance itself is the prey.

The winged animals overhead are not birds. Their chalky membranes stretch between many narrow struts, and their bodies are little more than digestive sacs, gripping hooks, and bundles of flight muscle. They feed in the updrafts on minute floating animals and reproductive dust. Rain overloads their wings, forcing them down in collective impacts. Once landed, they cling to anything warm—including people—covering travelers in a heavy, clicking mantle until the air dries enough for flight.

There are also the almost-still beings that imitate vegetation: branching shell cages full of retractile mouths; tube fields joined beneath the surface by contractile flesh; hanging colonies that lower feeding threads into passing swarms. They grow by adding animals, not cells. A “branch” may split because its inhabitants disagree about the direction of current. A “flower” may close because every petaled body has smelled blood.

All these creatures respond to the making of the world beneath them. Their mass presses the floor. Their burrows introduce water. Their shells redirect runoff and trap sediment. Colonies vent heat; herds knead soft mineral into firmer layers; borers turn new ridges into lattices of weakness. Roads rise under migrations perhaps because pressure compacts them, perhaps because heat follows life, perhaps because something below answers rhythmic weight.

The people do not assume the caverns are indifferent. Neither do they assume they are conscious. To them, that distinction is another kind of fixed foundation.

They say only that stone notices eventually.

So they travel lightly across a world that is bright but has no morning, crowded but has no forest, alive but never settled. Behind them their footprints may harden into a road, slump into warm mud, fill with rain, or close without a mark. Ahead, animals stream through pale openings into chambers newly exposed and already inhabited.

The people follow—not because the creatures know the way, but because every moving body is asking the ground a question.

Sometimes the ground answers.
