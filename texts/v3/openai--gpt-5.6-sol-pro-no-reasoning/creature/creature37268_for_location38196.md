The hollow is inhabited less by one creature than by a whole compressed country of small lives.

Nearest the surface cracks live the true plants: translucent-leaved fans that turn their pale centers toward the narrow shafts of daylight, quilted liverworts on sweating stone, algae in channels that wake only after rain. Their roots mingle with those descending from above until the distinction between local growth and the unseen landscape overhead becomes impossible to trace. Quick fungi accompany them—orange shelves, white antlered branches, thin parasols—appearing after water arrives and collapsing almost as rapidly.

Deeper in, things only imitate vegetation. Soft, leaf-shaped animals open fringed bodies against damp walls. Brown cords hanging between roots contract at a touch. Broad fungal cups drink condensation, hoarding it briefly before giving it to buried webs of hyphae. Root masses themselves behave like inhabited structures: wrapped in moss and microbial films, tunneled by larvae, grazed by minute crustaceans, and crowded with pale shoots. Sugars leaking from damaged tips feed colonies that may never encounter sunlight.

The grove’s most numerous inhabitants are tiny and luminous. Beetles pass beneath roots as green sparks. Blue-white larvae cluster in cracks and extinguish themselves together. Mites ride centipedes like embers caught on moving armor. Beneath overhangs, gnat larvae suspend shining threads, transforming low ceilings into inverted constellations. Their light reveals only fragments, but those fragments suggest that nearly every seam is occupied.

Ants keep roads along the dry grooves. Geckos patrol the warm walls on broad adhesive toes. Stone-colored moths sleep in overlapping ranks beneath ledges. Hairless rodents nest on mineral shelves above the flood line, among husks, gnawed roots, and dry bedding carried from unseen places. Tiny frogs inhabit wet root tangles, while larger ones bury themselves during drought and emerge when the dead runnels begin to darken.

In the pools live pale crabs and blind crustaceans. When the water shrinks, the crabs close themselves into mud chambers under submerged roots and wait. When rain returns, they climb among flooded passages, feeding on whatever the new current dislodges. The largest basin belongs to something longer and darker. It remains in the undercut hollow beneath the wall, occasionally turning near the surface before withdrawing into distances concealed by a pool only a few paces wide. It may be an eel, a salamander, a fish stranded from an older drainage—or something adapted so completely to this one basin that no familiar name fits it well.

Predators are woven through the same architecture. Thread-thin snakes hang among the roots and are visible chiefly when they cease pretending to be roots. Thick cave spiders bridge constricted passages with short, strong webs. Frogs station themselves at the mouths of runnels during rain, swallowing insects until their bodies swell. Centipedes hunt across the powdered floor, carrying luminous mites that disclose their movements to anything watching.

Even the three stone enclosures have residents. Geckos cement eggs into their seams. Crabs retreat beneath the lowest slabs. Roots penetrate the joints, and insects use the mineral-softened walls as shelter and road. The enclosure filled with soil supports a dense stand of narrow leaves; the one containing water breeds larvae almost as soon as it fills. Whatever made these forms—hands, animals, erosion—they no longer stand apart from the living grove.

All these inhabitants obey the hollow’s central economy: water cannot remain empty. A bead of condensation acquires mites and feeding mouths. A footprint becomes a pond, then a nursery, then a dry hollow ringed with husks. Newly filled cups cloud with eggs and larvae. Freshly wetted roots bloom with films that draw grazers, which draw hunters. No moisture remains merely moisture for long.

The inhabitants also seem to know when rain is coming. Before any drop enters, insects face the old channels. Fungal cups unfold beneath silent cracks. Frogs move toward the mouths of runnels. The dark pool-dweller rises. Perhaps pressure travels through the rock; perhaps cooling roots carry the warning downward; perhaps the whole grove senses changes no individual creature understands.

Thus what lives here is not simply a collection of cave animals. It is an anticipatory community organized around absent water, borrowed sunlight, and food descending through roots. Much of it sleeps, seals itself away, curls inward, or survives as eggs and hidden filaments. Then the stone begins to tick, and the grove unfolds.

For a few warm hours, every channel becomes a road. Every pool becomes a world. Every wet surface acquires a mouth.
