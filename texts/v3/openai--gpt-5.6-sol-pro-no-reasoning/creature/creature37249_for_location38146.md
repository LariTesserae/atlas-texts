The basin is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, many-layered pressure of life.

At its foundation live the varnish-makers: bacterial films and mineral-feeding colonies spread over wet basalt in skins finer than dust. They metabolize what condensation draws from the stone—iron, sulfur, salts—and lend the rock its dark, greasy sheen. Almost invisible, they support everything above them. Where water travels, they bloom; where it withdraws, they seal themselves into brittle films and wait.

Grazing upon these skins are the basin’s innumerable small bodies. Colorless shell-bearers pack the hollows several layers deep, their hinges glimmering blue and their old injuries shining chalk-white. Soft, many-footed raspers move between them, flexible enough to squeeze through gaps narrower than their resting bodies. Flat scavengers overlap across ledges like scales on some greater animal. Blind jumpers mass beneath overhangs until stone fails under their weight. In temporary pools, anchored sacs pulse at the touch of rain, while clouds of grain-sized swimmers turn a finger’s depth of water milky and alive.

The fissures hold longer things: supple animals that braid themselves together in cracks, feeding, mating, or merely conserving warmth until no individual can easily be distinguished from the knot. Beneath the clay are pale burrowers that live in communal chambers and migrate between them as muscular rivers. Some have neither obvious head nor tail, but move by sending contractions around their bodies. Others balance on delicate jointed legs and listen through their feet, reading impacts and vibrations carried for leagues through the rock.

Sound-making creatures crowd the escarpments. They are throatless, armored things with resonant plates inside their chests. In aggregate, their vibrations become the basin’s metallic roar. Deeper colonies drum against burrow ceilings in measured synchrony, perhaps marking water, danger, reproductive readiness, or simply their continued possession of space. Each population contributes only a scrape, tick, pulse, or rattle; together they make silence nearly impossible.

Among them live predators adapted not to pursuit, but to congestion. Low, broad hunters slip beneath packed congregations and abruptly arch their backs, overturning whole layers of prey. Others press themselves to the stone and emit loosening vibrations, shaking clinging animals from walls. Gravel-burrowers travel invisibly beneath the flats, announced by a moving swell, then open circular collapses under crowded ground. Their attacks kill fewer creatures than the ensuing crush and feeding frenzy.

Some hunters exploit the basin’s faint living light. They cultivate luminous parasites around their mouths, arranging the glow to resemble a moist crevice crowded with harmless shell-bearers. Others hunt only in rain, sensing the pattern of droplets on membranes stretched across their backs. The largest are flood-time filter-feeders. Usually indistinguishable from rounded stones, they unfurl along fresh channels and draw animal-thick water through inward-pointing teeth. When the flow dwindles, they close around what remains, becoming both predator and cistern.

Scavengers are everywhere and may be more important than hunters. They enter bodies before death is complete, drain soft tissues, rasp armor for calcium, and reduce emptied shells from shelter to tool-like surface to grit. Nothing retains its shape for long. A carcass becomes a chamber; the chamber becomes food; its residue descends through porous rock to nourish buried mats. Death here is not disappearance but transfer downward and outward.

Rain summons the basin’s briefest inhabitants. Dormant bodies soften inside dry crusts. Threadlike young spill into new rivulets. Eggs foam in hollows. Sealed chambers open, releasing creatures that may remain active for only an hour before returning underground or dying after reproduction. Other organisms survive drought by carrying water inside themselves. Some are transported inadvertently in the mouths and digestive folds of larger grazers, so that every feeding journey also seeds distant pools.

None of these creatures builds in the deliberate sense. They gather no stone and raise no walls. Yet their occupation has reshaped the entire basin. Shells grind channels. Burrowers perforate clay. Colonies dissolve minerals, bodies polish passageways, and generations widen every weakness in the rock. The land is their collective trace: an ancient habitation with no architect and no boundary between anatomy and geology.

What lives there, finally, is crowding itself—a community so dense that vacancy functions like a wound. Any exposed patch is tested first by feelers, then crossed by scavengers, covered by shell-bearers, and entered from below. No organism commands this closure. Each follows moisture, food, shelter, or vibration, and their separate hungers produce the same result: the immediate restoration of living surface.

Even the unseen depths are inhabited. Something large, or merely vast in aggregate, shifts beneath the lowest chambers and sends tremors through the flats. Whether it is one buried animal, a migrating colony, or the failure of rock hollowed by countless bodies may be impossible to determine. The basin responds to all three in the same way. Shells rise. Listening limbs unfold. Chest plates begin to vibrate. Burrows answer escarpments, and escarpments answer distant valleys.

Thus the true inhabitant may be the chorus: mineral-eaters, grazers, shell-bearers, swimmers, burrowers, scavengers, parasites, filters, and low ambush predators, all joined by damp stone and transmitted vibration. No single body encompasses it, yet it drinks, recoils, feeds, closes wounds, and wakes in waves.

It has no empty organs and almost no quiet thought. It lives everywhere the basin gives it room—and wherever room appears, it arrives.
