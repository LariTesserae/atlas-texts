The city’s true inhabitant is not an animal, nor any people hidden in the folded rooms. It is the vacancy for which everything has been built.

This vacancy lives chiefly in the central court. It occupies the ankle-deep water without displacing it and feeds on the generous intervals between buildings, pillars, trees, and lights. Most of the time it appears passive, almost decorative: a breadth of black reflection left open so that the perimeter may regard itself. Yet when the low tone sounds three times, it reveals its nature. It becomes distance—not greater space, but the impossibility of arrival. It can place a few visible steps farther away than kilometers can reach. For less than a minute, the small chamber contains an elsewhere.

The city tends this inhabitant carefully.

The pale houses keep to the walls to leave it room. Bridges retension themselves around it. Doors close before it wakes. Platforms shepherd intention back to shore, and the trees fold their leaves as though avoiding notice. The porous crown drinks the upward rain; the walls return it; the floor gathers it again, maintaining a dark, shallow surface in which the vacancy can practice reflections. Sometimes it wears the ceiling. Sometimes unknown stars. Sometimes nothing at all.

Perhaps the impossible interiors of the houses are sheds or organs in which surplus distance is stored. Corridors longer than the cavern, windows facing one another from incompatible directions, rooms occupying volumes absent from the surrounding rock—all may be lesser expressions of the same being. The city does not violate space accidentally. It cultivates space in forms geology cannot contain.

Around this central presence lives a second, quieter population: plants severed from the ordinary agreements of life.

The moss is closest to native. It clings to the sweating basalt in dense, dark pads and enters each new fracture almost as soon as groundwater does. It seems less to grow than to record the chamber’s changes. Every cushion marks an old seep; every vivid green thread marks a crack born during a recent expansion. When the resonant tone travels around the cavern, the moss shivers before the stone does.

Ferns inhabit gutters, thresholds, and the undersides of bridges. Their young fronds are translucent in the amber guide light, uncurling like delicate instruments testing the air. Liverworts spread across unused entrances, softening doors that still open hopefully for visitors. They do not decay into soil. When they die, the city notices. Platforms come without passengers, gather the fallen matter, and carry it to warm apertures in the floor. There it is translated into moisture, mineral salts, and a scented exhalation resembling leaf mold—the odor of decomposition without the decomposers.

The trees are stranger. Thin and smooth-barked, they stand in sparse groves around the lower tier, never touching the floor with true roots. Beneath each trunk hangs a pale fringe of filaments that drinks directly from the saturated air. Their leaves are long, oval, and widely spaced, preserving darkness rather than competing for light. They respond not merely to water but to its direction: edge-on when rain rises, open when seepage descends. They have learned the city’s inverted seasons.

Their flowers answer electrical fields instead of sun or insects. They open beside active lintels, humming walls, and workshop apparatus. Their pollen travels by architecture, crawling grain by grain through conductive veins in the porcelain substance, passing under bridges and around doorframes until it reaches another blossom. The city itself serves as bee, wind, soil, and fungus. Whether the plants adapted to it or were designed as part of it is no longer a useful distinction.

There may once have been intended citizens. Their shape can be inferred only from courtesies.

They were expected to approach doors. They possessed intentions strong enough for platforms to read, yet perhaps contradictory enough to strand them. They required warm rooms, lifts, workshops, railings, marked edges, and warnings delivered in three tones. They could inhabit dimensions denied to stone. They made—or expected to use—rings without openings, sheets whose shadows are solid, and vessels that keep an interior colder than their surroundings. Nothing in the city suggests what bodies they had, only what accommodations awaited them.

No trace says they ever arrived.

In their absence, the systems have acquired the character of patient organisms. Workshops create objects and absorb them again, rehearsing usefulness. Lifts travel empty as if circulation itself must be kept alive. Rooms brighten at nearby motion, hopeful for an instant, then dim without resentment. Doors dilate like pupils. The ceiling crown receives rain through innumerable pores and answers with blue impulses through its inverted roots. Hidden functions pulse in the rock like a slow heart.

Thus the city may itself be alive, though not independently. It is more like a host body organized around a missing organ. The buildings are its ribs, the folded rooms its viscera, the amber lines its nerves, the water its circulating medium. Its foundations continuously feel the pressure of the earth and make tiny corrections. Its bridges wake to their own strain. Its mineral seams flare during the court’s transformations as if carrying pain—or information.

The young cavern also lives, but uneasily. It has been persuaded into a shape it would not naturally hold. The black walls press inward. New fractures report like gunfire. Groundwater enters every wound. Each cycle of impossible distance changes the load upon the stone, and each adjustment by the city asks the geology to accept another moment of contradiction. The chamber is not eroding toward age so much as negotiating its continued existence.

Nothing here is wholly wild, and nothing is entirely artificial. The vegetation depends on the architecture. The architecture responds like tissue. The stone is still recovering from having been convinced to move aside. The weather is manufactured, yet behaves with the intimacy of climate. Even emptiness performs a function.

So what lives there is a community of substitutes.

Machinery stands in for animals. Conductive walls stand in for pollinators. Warm slots stand in for decay. Platforms stand in for instinct, carrying bodies and dead leaves according to desires they somehow perceive. The gardens stand in for an ecosystem. The resonant tones stand in for fear. And at the center, cultivated with the greatest care, immeasurable distance stands in for the absent population.

The city waits around it, attentive and immaculate.

It may be waiting for inhabitants.

It may already have one.
