They are the Interstitials, though no single species bears that name, and none of them would understand the cavity as one country. They are a commonwealth of bodies, residues, and borrowed motions: ants and mites, roots and mold, translucent swimmers, flattened hunters, and things assembled from whatever the freight yard sheds.

Most are ordinary at first glance. The closer they live to the abandoned pump, however, the less firmly they belong to any familiar kind.

The ants are the most numerous and the nearest thing the place has to builders. Their workers are soot-black, with rust-red joints and abdomens faintly banded by whatever they ate while young. They construct upward as readily as downward, fixing grains to the ceiling with grease and fungal silk. Their galleries form hanging terraces around bolt shafts and retaining walls beside flood channels. The dead are not buried. They are mortared into bridges, mandibles outward, so that even in death they continue to grip.

Different colonies specialize in different industrial materials. One gathers magnetic filings and lays walls that tremble before a train arrives. Another harvests the red dust created by vibration in the deepest chamber. Its nest grows after every freight, although the workers never excavate. The pump colony uses beetle shell and black sealant, making glossy ramparts that repel brief floods. These ants tend pale, soft-bodied insects on the roots of grass descending from above. They milk them for clear sweetness and move them from root to root according to the timetable of the engines.

The mites travel farther than the ants. Seen alone, each is only a moving bead, pearl-gray or red as ground brick. Seen together, they reveal the cavity’s hidden roads. They follow fungal threads across glass edges, rat ribs, rust flakes, and pools filmed with oil. At junctions they pause, touch forelegs, and choose routes with uncanny agreement. Some carry spores. Some feed on eggs. The largest hunt springtails and wear grains of magnetic grit on their backs, which turn slightly whenever the pumps above begin.

Springtails gather where drops are likely to fall. They are white, smoke-blue, or nearly transparent. A disturbance launches them in all directions, and for an instant they become a spray of living punctuation. Many drown. Others land in pools that did not exist moments earlier and graze on bacterial films until the water vanishes. Their shed skins accumulate in corners like fine ash.

Cockroaches occupy the dry high places, especially shelves of fractured insulation. Their bodies are amber beneath the usual brown, and in the noon light their folded wings resemble old varnish. They eat labels, glue, fungus, and one another. Older individuals carry pale mineral crusts along their backs where condensation has dried repeatedly. A few have lived so long beneath dripping concrete that calcite beads have fused to their wing cases, making them heavy, armored, and nearly unable to squeeze through the passages of their youth.

Silverfish browse in quieter chambers. Their scales catch the green light of status diodes and return it long after they have passed, leaving brief, moving glimmers in darkness. They consume the names of things: serial numbers, voltage warnings, inspection dates, fragments of shipping instructions. Paper survives only as lace behind them. In one sealed recess, generations have fed on the same folded wiring diagram until no printed line remains, yet they continue to trace its vanished routes.

The spiders make use of lingering shadows. They spin close to the ground, between washers and bolt shafts, where pressure fronts bend their webs without tearing them. When prey crosses a bar of sunlight, its shadow remains after its body moves. The spiders strike not at the insect but at this second, delayed darkness. Often the prey is pulled backward into the place where its shadow still says it is. These spiders are thin and colorless, their abdomens marked with shapes like cracks in concrete. During blue electrical dawns, every thread in their webs becomes visible at once, revealing networks that seem to pass through solid objects and continue in neighboring chambers.

The centipedes live under curled paint. They are copper-colored where young, blackening with age. Their many feet can feel a locomotive’s approach long before the first audible vibration. At such times they emerge and wait beside channels, knowing that pressure will drive smaller animals from their refuges. They hunt with abrupt efficiency, then vanish beneath flakes barely thicker than their bodies.

Crickets shelter in the foam. Their calls are rare because sound is dangerous in a place where every surface carries vibration. Each cricket has only a few notes it will risk. One chirps after the night shift. Another answers the cooling units. A third sings only when the disconnected pump turns. Its note is lower than its body should produce and is sometimes answered from beyond the clay.

The pools contain their own population. Mosquito larvae hang head-down from surfaces no wider than coins. Blind crustaceans, milk-white and delicate, scrape rust from submerged metal. Flatworms unfold from seeming stains. Threadlike worms knot themselves around grains of sand and wait out drought inside transparent skins. Bacteria make green-gray clouds, violet sheens, and films that fracture into plates when touched.

The pump distributes these lives without regard for distance or size. A larva swallowed by one pool may appear days later in another, older but unfed. Snails emerge from the outlet though no adult snail lives in any reachable chamber. They are tiny, with clear shells containing a spiral of dark organs. They graze for several hours, lay one or two eggs on the underside of a rust flake, and disappear during the next rotation. Their offspring hatch normally but grow shells coiled the opposite way.

Occasionally the pump delivers creatures that smell of the open river: a caddisfly larva in a case of reed fiber, a shrimp no longer than a fingernail, a black leech striped with green. They enter a world without sky, live as long as they can, and alter it by their presence. The ants dismantle their remains. Fungi cross their shells. Mites carry pieces of them into districts that have never known rain.

The fungi are perhaps the place’s true inhabitants. Everything else merely moves through their country.

Their threads lace the cavity from object to object, joining paper to bone, clay to insulation, insect corpse to living root. Some glow weakly under electrical load. Some trap flies with adhesive stems. Others grow only on rust and fruit in minute orange cups after a freight passes overhead. The largest colony surrounds the pump. Its hyphae enter seams in the iron housing and emerge from cracks in distant chambers, forming a network whose geometry does not match the space between them.

That colony produces no visible mushroom. Instead, after heavy condensation, it raises little translucent pillars from dead insects and grains of clay. Each pillar holds a single drop. Within the drop, spores turn slowly against the current, like dark weather seen through glass. When the pump wheel completes a rotation, all the drops fall at once—some downward, some upward, some vanishing without striking a surface.

The roots are inhabitants too, though they belong partly to the yard above. They descend through cracks as fine exploratory hairs, then thicken where warmth and moisture persist. Deprived of ordinary direction, they make pale knots along the ceiling. Near dormant motors they grow dense and fleshy, embracing casings, entering screw holes, and following buried cable. Seeds beside the pump germinate into plants that orient toward engines. Their colorless leaves lie flat against concrete, trembling whenever pistons idle overhead.

A few roots have ceased to correspond to any plant visible above. They arrive through unbroken sections of slab or emerge from the clay and climb toward the ceiling. These are glassy, almost transparent, with slow-moving bubbles inside. Ants tend them carefully. When cut by a rust fall, they leak warm water smelling faintly of reeds.

Rats are weather more than animals. Only juveniles can enter most passages, flattening ribs and shoulders to force themselves beneath the slab. Their whiskers sweep ceilings clean of droplets. Their breath fogs glass chips. Their weight compresses channels and redirects streams. Colonies recognize individual rats by gait and vibration, sealing tunnels before certain heavy-footed hunters arrive. A rat’s death becomes a province. Ribs form vaulted corridors; hide becomes food, then felt, then dust; teeth remain as bright mineral outcrops long after the rest has been distributed.

Geckos come through drains. They are pale from generations in service spaces, their skin nearly translucent over pink muscle and blue vein. They wait beside the moving bar of noon light and feed on flies drawn to its warmth. The oldest gecko is broad-headed and thin as a folded glove. Part of its tail disappears behind a bolt shaft into a gap too narrow to contain it. The tail has never emerged elsewhere, and the gecko has not shed it. During heavy leaks, the hidden portion twitches in another chamber.

There is also the thing within the pump.

It may be an animal, a colony, or only a behavior shared by iron, water, fungus, and vibration. No inhabitant sees more than parts of it. Ants encounter warm surfaces inside the housing and retreat with their antennae wet. Mites vanish into a seam and later reappear carrying river pollen. Pale crustaceans gather around the outlet before it turns. Spiders anchor threads to the cast iron, and those threads remain taut even after being cut.

When condensation is heavy, the wheel begins its slow rotation. Something contracts behind it—not regularly like a motor, but softly, with the interval of breathing. Water is drawn from one pool and issued elsewhere. Now and then the outlet produces a pulse of clear gel containing rust grains arranged like eggs. The ants collect these grains and build them into the innermost wall of their nest.

Whatever occupies the pump appears to be growing, though not in any direction the cavity can display. Its presence is inferred from increasing correspondences. A pool beside the western slab ripples when touched in the east. Two unrelated ant colonies begin using identical routes. A spider loses a leg, and days later another spider emerges from a bolt socket carrying an extra one. Shadows linger longer near the housing. Roots converge on it, then pass beside it without touching, as though curving around a much larger body than the fist-sized iron shell.

The Interstitials do not worship this presence or fear it. They exploit it. Ants use its warmth for brood chambers. Mosquitoes use its transferred pools. Fungi enter its seams. Predators wait at its outlets. If it has intentions, they are treated as weather: another repeating condition around which hunger can organize itself.

No creature there possesses the whole place. Each lives inside a different version of it. To the ant it is a system of load-bearing edges and chemical roads. To the mite it is a web of fungal junctions. To the mosquito it is a chain of temporary skies. To the root it is gradients of heat and electrical hum. To the gecko it is a hunting ground made of narrowness and light. To the pump-being, perhaps, the cavity is merely one wet chamber of a body extending beneath rivers, foundations, factories, and every other place where two surfaces have separated without fully parting.

Together they fill the interval.

They eat the freight yard’s leavings, anticipate its movements, and build with its decay. They have incorporated the timetable into instinct and anomaly into anatomy. Their nests brace new fractures. Their bodies clog channels and open others. Their dead become floors above older floors. Wherever the slab lifts from the clay, they advance in order: moisture, fungus, mite, ant, web, tooth.

By the time the widening can be measured, someone already lives there.
