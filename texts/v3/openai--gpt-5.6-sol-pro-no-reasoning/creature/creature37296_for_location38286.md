The people who live there are difficult to distinguish, at first, from the maintenance of the city.

They are not numerous in proportion to it. A crowded ward may hold fewer people than animals beneath a single pumping house, and there are districts where no human voice has been heard for years, though lamps are trimmed, sluices reset, and warning shutters changed with the seasons. They inhabit scattered chambers in the continuous masonry: dry galleries behind boiler walls, rooms suspended inside bridge piers, apartments built above the reach of plated herds. Their settlements are not islands of order within wilderness. They are quieter knots in an order too large and old to belong to them.

They tend to be compact, broad-footed, and deliberate in movement. Bare skin is uncommon. Most wear overlapping aprons and short coats of glazed cloth, ceramic scales, treated membrane, or thin plates of dark metal. The clothes shed rain, resist teeth, and announce the wearer’s heat and size to creatures that navigate by warmth. A person dressed for a condenser vault may appear pale and cold beneath mineral powder; one crossing a brood tunnel wears bands of warm copper so the hatchlings recognize them as part of the wall.

Their footwear is soft-soled despite the stone. Hard heels vanish into the city’s noise and can provoke animals that listen through paving. Soles are ribbed for wet inclines and frequently replaced. Experienced walkers know the streets by vibration: the drag of a chainworks three wards away, the pulse preceding a cistern discharge, the deep approach of a herd long before its horns sound.

Hearing is considered useful but unreliable. Nearly everyone learns the hand languages of their district, as well as the broader semaphore used across roofs and viaducts. Conversation at close range is economical, accompanied by touches to wrist or shoulder so that questions are not lost in the roar. Names are often visual: a sequence of finger positions, a lamp color followed by a direction, a pattern traced in condensation. Spoken names are shorter, meant to be shouted once.

Homes are lined with whatever interrupts vibration: layered felt grown from animal membrane, ash-packed cavities, suspended ceramic panels, the abandoned husks of large filter feeders. Silence is not expected. What people value is separation—the ability to hear one kettle, one sleeper, one familiar set of breaths against the greater sound. The most expensive rooms are not the driest or highest, but those in which the city can be reduced to a murmur.

No single government appears to command the plateau. Authority follows systems rather than territory. The cistern keepers rule the hours of water. Track guilds decide which streets may bear wheels. Heat stewards regulate vents whose closure might freeze microbes in one basin and cook a sleeping quarter in another. Migration clerks record the routes of larger animals and can suspend commerce across several districts by raising a striped panel. Kiln assemblies determine which ruins may be ground for new block. No one owns an entire building if its foundations contain a public conduit or its roof carries a nesting shelf older than the deed.

Their laws are full of thresholds: permitted temperatures, allowable vibrations, minimum passage widths, the number of nights a grate may remain closed, the depth at which runoff becomes a shared resource. Trespass is defined as much by obstruction as by entry. To block a wall-runner seam, to draw too heavily from a breeding cistern, or to introduce an unfamiliar alloy into microbial water may be judged more severely than theft.

Children learn the city as a sequence of living relations. They are taught which railings may be gripped during rain, which opalescent films burn, which animals tolerate a moving shadow, and how to lie flat when a glider swarm thickens the air. Before they can read worn inscriptions, they can identify water quality by the animals at its edge. Their first maps are not street maps but flow maps: heat, drainage, current, migration, noise.

They play in shallow flooded courts, floating marked ceramic chips through gutters and wagering where they will emerge. They collect shed plates, wing membranes, cracked insulators, and the tiny geometric shells of creatures that breed in relay boxes. They are warned never to follow black runners into an expansion joint, however wide it seems. Those passages belong to the internal city, where routes do not respect human scale.

The old are valued less as repositories of dates than of rates. They remember how quickly a crack widened, when a herd last altered its route, whether the western rain has grown more acidic, whether the third piston now hesitates longer at the top of its stroke. Records exist in enormous quantity, but age teaches which changes matter. A measurement may remain harmless for fifty years and become urgent in the fifty-first.

Their engineers seldom build from a blank design. They listen, expose old layers, and add weight cautiously. Before placing a buttress they may spend years observing the animals that use the prospective stone, the heat moving through it, and the neighboring towers’ minute seasonal sway. A successful structure does not merely stand. It enters the city’s exchanges without causing famine, flood, stampede, or destructive resonance.

Repair crews travel in processions because most repairs exceed individual scale. They bring winches, signal lamps, membrane tents, portable shutters, and cages containing indicator animals more sensitive than instruments to gas or electrical leakage. Work advances in pauses. The crew cuts, listens, waits for the masonry to answer, then cuts again. Haste is associated not with courage but with failure to perceive consequences.

Few machines are wholly understood. This causes neither shame nor mysticism. An engine may have intact maintenance traditions for every bearing and valve while its original purpose remains unknown. If its heat sustains six cistern ecologies and its motion pumps water to inhabited roofs, purpose has been replaced by relation. The inhabitants judge machines by what now depends on them.

Some people spend their lives inside a single mechanism. Pipe walkers enter conduits broad enough to contain avenues and travel for months between inspection hatches. Scale shepherds manage pale colonies that graze boiler interiors. Relay listeners sleep in ceramic booths and diagnose electrical faults from recurring patterns of thunder. Cliff divers descend through the western curtains to clear calcite from reservoir mouths, wearing rigid suits that make them resemble the beaked filter animals nearby.

Others work with the great street herds. Route keepers know individual animals by chips and polish in their bricklike plates. They do not direct them in any simple sense. They open arches, adjust warmth beneath paving, release mineral water at selected cisterns, and wait for the animals to choose the intended road. Calves are born in vaulted stations lined with damp ash. When one dies, its plates are removed only after the scavenger succession has finished; some become roofing, others heat shields, others memorial markers worn smooth under public hands.

The people eat from the same mineral ecology, though usually at several removes. They cultivate microbial sheets in heated ceramic trays, harvest soft-bodied filter animals from cistern racks, and grind the shells of small grazers into meal rich with salts. There are blind, slow creatures kept in warm cellars for flesh and oil. Certain glider pupae are preserved in brine. Flavor comes from smoke, metals, sulfur compounds, fermented membranes, and water drawn from distinct geological layers.

Meals are dark, pale, rust-red, translucent, or gray. Green is a color of glass, signal enamel, or imported antiquity, not food. Images of leaves survive in old ornament and are often mistaken for feathers, branching pipes, or imaginary aquatic animals.

Nothing is wasted quickly. Refuse is sorted by which life can consume it and at what temperature. Bone goes to acidic vats; membrane to insulation cultures; corroded filings to iron-feeding cisterns; ash to ceramics. Human dead enter the same circulation. Customs differ, but burial in inert ground is impossible and considered lonely. Some bodies are lowered into warm digestive reservoirs, some dried near kilns and powdered into block, some sealed into conductive walls where their salts slowly alter the current. Memorials describe not where a person rests but where they have gone: west cistern, roof tiles, nursery heat, bridge mortar.

Faith, where it exists, concerns continuance more than origin. The deepest rock is not necessarily sacred, nor are the oldest machines. Reverence attaches to cycles that have survived interruption: a pump restarted after a century, a migration returning to an abandoned route, rain reaching a basin through channels cut by vanished hands. Bells are rung for successful recurrences.

There are also those who believe the city itself is an animal, though they disagree about what kind. Some call the people microbes in its heated vessels. Others identify the herds as its blood and the chainworks as its slow thought. Engineers generally reject the metaphor in public, then use it constantly in private: a sick ward, a blind conduit, a fevered kiln, a joint that refuses to heal.

The inhabitants do not consider themselves masters of the other creatures. Neither do they imagine harmony. Animals chew insulation, choke turbines, strip useful films from pipes, and occasionally fill inhabited rooms so completely that the people must abandon them. Brood years can starve neighborhoods. Large predators follow the herds through submerged avenues. Gliders smother lamps and ignite on conductors, falling in burning sheets.

There are culls, barriers, poisonings, and sealed chambers. Yet every act of control is constrained by dependency. Remove too many wall grazers and corrosive films thicken. Kill the runners and eggs accumulate in relay housings. Exclude a herd from a pumping station and distant reservoirs lose the organisms carried in the seams of its armor. Cruelty is known, but simplification is feared more.

The oldest families are not those with the longest bloodlines. They are households attached to enduring duties. A child adopted into the keepers of a particular overflow becomes heir to centuries of observation. Kinship forms around valves, bridges, breeding chambers, warning towers, and sections of conductor. Marriages often join systems that must coordinate: a cistern household with a kiln household, a migration line with a track guild. Domestic affection and hydraulic necessity overlap without being identical.

Travelers can cross the city for years and still be within it. They sleep in public dry niches marked by white ceramic bands, exchange route knowledge at shutter stations, and carry stamped tokens proving immunity to common cistern fevers. Because districts use different signals, professional interpreters wear articulated frames on their backs, enlarging hand signs so they can be read across a crowded avenue.

Trade follows gradients. Upper wards send fired ceramic, dry salts, and collected glider membrane downward. Low wards send oils, filter flesh, conductive paste, and metals precipitated from drainage. The ravine districts provide calcite, pressure-grown shell, and creatures adapted to mist. At the rim, salvage crews quarry the exposed historical layers, though law forbids cutting anything still warm, wet, vibrating, inhabited, load-bearing, or connected to a known cycle. In practice, almost everything meets one of those conditions.

Their art is made for a world where small sounds disappear. Sculpture predominates: reliefs read by touch, arrangements of polished erosion surfaces, counterweighted forms that change position over decades. Public music uses pipe organs driven by aqueduct pressure, bridge cables struck by mechanical hammers, and tuned cisterns whose notes can be felt in the ribs several streets away. Intimate music is conducted through bones, teeth, floors, or taut threads between two people.

They admire patience but are not passive. To wait correctly is a technical accomplishment. One must know whether a century-long process is stable or merely slow catastrophe. Their heroes are those who noticed the difference: the listener who heard an extra beat in a buried engine; the child who recognized that a wall-runner migration had reversed; the crew that spent twenty-three years preparing the district into which a leaning tower finally settled without killing anyone beneath it.

They fear silence.

Not ordinary quiet—the muffled refuge of a room, the pause after a herd, the thin hour before rain—but a true cessation. A pipe without scraping life, a floor without transmitted motion, a conductor that neither hums nor warms: these are signs of severance. People gather quickly around such absences. Lamps are raised. Animals are brought near and watched. Someone places a palm to stone.

For them, life is rarely recognized by shape. It is known by exchange: heat crossing a boundary, water altered by passage, a rhythm answered elsewhere, a substance consumed and returned in another form. The city is alive because nothing in it remains entirely itself.

The people are the same. They pass through vocations, households, protective garments, infections, symbioses, and eventually masonry. Their bodies carry cultures from cistern to cistern. Their tools are gnawed, plated, colonized, and repaired. Their dwellings breathe boiler vapor and shed rain into nurseries below. Even their private thoughts must be signaled through a multitude of other lives.

So whoever lives there does not merely occupy the plateau.

They circulate through it.
