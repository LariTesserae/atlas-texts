The people who live in the seam are slight only by comparison with their clothing. Beyond the warmed districts, each person carries an architecture of insulation: close-fitted ceramic fibers, articulated shells over the joints, heated collars, gloves whose pale fingertips leave brief clear marks on frost. Indoors they shed these layers piece by piece. Their ordinary clothes are seamless, washable, and made without plant or animal fiber, usually in the same mineral shades as the city. Color is used sparingly but personally—a cobalt cuff, an amber fastener, a band of violet light woven around a sleeve.

They are not a subterranean species. They have ordinary eyes, ordinary lungs, bodies still governed by an open world most have never experienced. Broad-spectrum lamps regulate their days; supplements and calibrated foods maintain what the buried environment cannot provide. Many are pale, but not uniformly so. What distinguishes them is posture. They duck before ceilings that do not require it. They keep elbows near their bodies in passages and place their feet with care. Even in wide rooms, they move as though negotiating with nearby surfaces.

They rarely stand entirely still. A hand finds a warmed rail. A thumb checks condensation on a cup. The jaw shifts in response to some distant vibration. Children learn early to distinguish the harmless settling of a wall from the first pulse of a pressure event. Before they can read, they know cobalt means water movement, amber means restricted structure, and cold white means to stop speaking and listen.

Their culture treats attention as a civic virtue. A conscientious person notices the new line in a ceramic panel, the changed rhythm of a pump, the taste of iron in recycled water. Neglect is considered more shameful than ignorance. Anyone may report a crack; no one is mocked for caution. The mountain does not reward bravado, and so courage here usually takes the form of patience: remaining beside a sensor through a long tremor, crawling into a cold conduit, admitting that a familiar route no longer feels right.

Professions blur into forms of stewardship. Structural negotiators study the mountain’s strains and decide where stiffness may safely be added. Water accountants trace every droplet from intake to recovery. Thermal custodians allocate warmth by the hour and by the body. Route tenders oversee the slow migration of rooms and passages. Surface crews maintain the vents and hatches, becoming weather-burned figures of fascination to those who seldom leave the seam.

No craft is more respected than listening. Specialist listeners press bone-conduction instruments to walls and compare the mountain’s present murmur with archived patterns. Some claim to recognize districts by vibration alone. They speak of faults as if they have temperaments—not because they believe the rock alive, exactly, but because intimate knowledge naturally acquires a social vocabulary. A fracture may be stubborn, a chamber restless, a basalt face trustworthy under cold but irritable during thaw.

Children often take this language literally. They ask whether the mountain is asleep, whether moving a corridor hurts it, whether the city is a splinter under its skin. Adults answer differently. Engineers describe pressure gradients. Elders say pain belongs to living bodies but damage does not, and damage should still be prevented. A few insist that the distinction is only convenient. These disagreements are conducted quietly beside circulating basins, with fingertips resting on stone.

Families do not always occupy permanent homes. Because rooms migrate and thermal needs change, a household may move several times without traveling far in any ordinary sense. Its chamber is disassembled, remade, or exchanged for another farther along the fold. Possessions remain few: fitted utensils, personal tools, memory crystals, a lamp calibrated to a favored dawn, perhaps a fragment of untreated stone kept under permit. Home is less a fixed volume than a set of relationships—who shares heat, who answers during an alarm, whose cup rests beside whose in the wall recess.

Birth is treated as an environmental event as much as a family one. Warmth, water, oxygen, and future space must be assigned before a child arrives. This accounting is not considered cold. It is a promise made measurable. Newborns sleep in close thermal cradles whose walls reproduce pulse and breath. Their names are entered with a structural allotment: the mass the city expects to feed, shelter, heat, and eventually entrust with part of the mountain.

Death is handled with equal material care. Bodies cannot be buried, burned casually, or released into the water cycle. The dead are reduced in sealed chambers and separated into useful constituents. Salts enter thermal stores; minerals become ceramic; trace metals return to fabrication. Families may reserve a small fraction within a translucent memorial tile, often installed near a pressure court. Thus the ancestors become literally load-bearing only in a minor way, but the phrase persists. People touch these tiles when entering a changed district, as if asking those who lived under older stresses to witness the new arrangement.

Their food is nutritionally exact and sensually restrained. Clear gels carry faint mineral flavors; printed wafers differ more in fracture and temperature than in taste. Meals are made distinctive by texture, geometry, and sequence. A celebration might begin with a brittle warm sheet that dissolves on the tongue, continue with cold saline beads, and end with a dense paste flavored by ozone-safe aromatics synthesized in the vats. Those who have worked outside sometimes speak obsessively of smells the city cannot reproduce: sun-warmed dust, feathers, distant smoke, the sharp green wound of a broken stem. Most listeners have no reference for these things and suspect exaggeration.

There are people who long for the surface, though longing is complicated by fear. The sky is an absence without edges. Wind touches the entire body at once. Distance offers no wall to orient against. Citizens making their first ascent have been known to crouch beside the hatch, unable to step into so much space. Others become devoted to it immediately and train for exterior work, returning with frost on their suits and descriptions everyone pretends not to envy.

Surface crews acquire small superstitions. They tap the hatch three times before opening it, though no procedure requires this. They never point at distant weather with a bare finger. Some bring back sealed vials of outside air, labeled by date and pressure, and keep them as private relics. They are forbidden to import snow except for analysis, because even clean snow is chemically unknown until tested. Yet children sometimes beg to see it before it melts, as if the white matter beyond the vents were a creature.

The oldest inhabitants remember earlier versions of the city, when galleries were harsher, maps more dependable, and pressure courts little more than emergency cisterns. They distrust routes that move too readily. They keep internal geographies no longer aligned with the mountain and give directions by vanished places: past the former west reservoir, below the court that became a filtration spine, beside the wall where the first long fracture stopped. Younger people understand these as stories rather than instructions.

Governance belongs partly to humans and partly to constraints. Councils debate priorities, but pressure, water, heat, and mass have votes that cannot be overruled. Civic arguments therefore concern interpretation: whether a district truly needs another warmed chamber, whether a route may remain open through the thaw, whether structural material should reinforce an old neighborhood or allow it to migrate. Public records include not only speeches and decisions but strain models, condensation forecasts, and the measured consequences of every proposal.

Their laws are severe around recklessness and forgiving around error. An unauthorized hole in untreated rock is a profound offense. Wasting heat is regarded almost as vandalism. Contaminating a channel can exile a person from water work for life. Yet someone who makes an honest mistake and reports it immediately is protected, because concealment can kill districts. Confession is built into safety systems: red handplates stand at intervals along the galleries, allowing anyone to stop local machinery and summon review without first proving they were right.

Conflict seldom becomes loud. Anger is expressed through precision. People repeat another’s measurements back to them. They request that a claim be entered into the permanent record. They withdraw shared warmth from a meeting alcove—not enough to harm, only enough to remind everyone that comfort is collective. Physical violence is rare and terrifying, less because bodies are sacred than because uncontrolled force near a pressure wall is unthinkable.

The small inspection machines live among them in an ambiguous category. Officially they are infrastructure. In practice they are named. Children track particular pale crawlers by scratches on their casings and leave approved lumps of sealant where they can be collected. Technicians deny that the machines have preferences, then complain that one is timid or another habitually ignores instructions. When a damaged crawler fails to return, its absence may be discussed for weeks.

The largest conduit machines are treated with more caution. Their many-jointed bodies pass through service arteries with the slow certainty of bone being drawn through a throat. People flatten themselves into recesses and watch pale segments glide by, each carrying sensors, repair reservoirs, and microscopic workers. The machine does not acknowledge them. Its touch would be dangerous without being aggressive. After it passes, the walls are often warmer and the rock’s murmur subtly changed.

During tremors, the inhabitants become almost ceremonially calm. Each person moves to an assigned place, secures loose objects, opens certain panels, closes others, and waits. Adults place children’s palms against safe sections of wall so they can feel the distributed motion rather than imagine catastrophe. When the seam flexes, people bend with it. They do not pray aloud. Some count warning intervals; some recite pressure figures; some hold another person by the wrist, where the pulse can be measured.

Afterward comes the true communal labor. Every corridor is walked. Every basin is sampled. People compare sensations that instruments may have missed: a pressure in the sinuses, a metallic taste, an unexpected draft along the ankle. Repair crawlers emerge, but humans follow. The city trusts machines with measurement and people with unease.

Pleasure is quiet but not meager. Residents warm smooth stones and pass them hand to hand. They race droplets along grooved tables by changing the table’s temperature. They compose patterns for civic water displays, sending timed ripples through linked pressure courts. They play games in which a route must be remembered after the guiding floor lights are extinguished. Lovers exchange private heat allocations, allowing one another’s rooms to remain warm longer than efficiency requires.

Music exists, despite custom, but much of it is tactile. Performers transmit controlled vibrations through benches, rails, or basin water. An audience sits with hands on stone and feels harmony travel through the fingers, sternum, and teeth. The best compositions incorporate the mountain’s current murmur without imitating it. Loud airborne music is reserved for rare chambers isolated from the surrounding strata, where citizens go to experience sound as a physical excess. Many emerge laughing; a few emerge shaken.

Their celebrations are made from temporary abundance. On civic nights, handrails grow warmer. Rations arrive in unnecessary shapes. The daylight lamps deepen toward gold, a color rarely used in ordinary work. Linked basins lift and settle in slow succession, and people follow the traveling wave from court to court. Children run beside it. Adults who would never raise their voices permit themselves to call across the chambers before the water reaches the next district.

The bird, when one comes, unsettles everyone.

It is too warm, too soft, too structurally improbable. Its feathers conceal rather than reveal its construction. Its heartbeat is frantic, its bones hollow, its waste biologically hazardous. Environmental crews handle it with grave concentration while residents gather behind transparent partitions. To many children it is the first living thing they have seen that was not human. When it opens its wings, people step back.

Later, accounts differ. Its color grows brighter in memory. Its size changes. Someone always claims it sang, though recordings show only ventilation noise and the scrape of claws. For months afterward, birdlike forms appear in light displays: branching wings, impossible arcs, a small moving shadow crossing an artificial dawn. Then the city returns to its usual abstractions of stress, water, and stone.

What lives there, above all, is a population trained to understand survival not as conquest but as continuous adjustment. They do not imagine themselves masters of the mountain. Neither do they think of themselves as its guests, because guests may leave without consequence. They are one of its active conditions: a warm, thinking seam; a network of bodies and instruments folded into pressure.

Each morning, they wake under the reproduced light. They drink measured water and read the night’s minute alterations. A corridor has lengthened. A chamber will move. One fault has grown quieter; another needs watching. Someone retrieves a glove left beside a basin. Someone touches a memorial tile. Somewhere in the dark beyond the heated air, a pale machine enters a crack too narrow for any hand.

Then the inhabitants disperse through the galleries, carrying small zones of warmth with them while the mountain holds, shifts, and is answered.
