Those who live beneath the surface are difficult to separate from the place itself.

At first they seem to be animals sparsely scattered through the vegetal galleries: the glass-skinned frogs in leaf cups, the wingless moths fixed to luminous petals, the blunt, bark-backed walkers among the roots. But prolonged attention makes their categories uncertain. The moth’s feet disappear into the flower as fine white filaments. Green veins branch inside the frogs where bones should be. The great shelled creatures have no mouths beneath their blunt faces, and buds crowd the seams of their carapaces. None appears trapped. Each seems caught at the point where one form of life is preparing to become another.

The inhabitants proper are rarer.

They occupy the bark shelters and pleated alcoves, though they cannot always be distinguished from them. A seated figure may resemble a bundle of pale stems until one notices the arrangement: two long limbs folded beneath a narrow trunk, a head bowed between leaflike shoulders, hands spread over the floor. Rootlets issue from the fingers. Where hair should be, there is a close growth of curled shoots, every tip arrested before unfurling.

They are roughly human in proportion, but only roughly, as if humanity were one shape the country had attempted among many. Some are tall and reed-thin, with jointed limbs enclosed in translucent skin. Light travels through them along branching veins. Others are broad and low, their backs plated with bark, their heads sunk almost entirely between their shoulders. A few appear soft and unfinished: white, folded beings with sealed eyes and feet still joined by a web of living tissue. They might be embryos enlarged to adult scale, or adults being patiently returned to an embryonic state.

No two are identical. One may have a face with recognizable lips and cheekbones, while another bears only a smooth oval crossed by the raised midrib of a leaf. Some have mouths lined with tiny petals. Some have hollow cups instead of ears, holding clear water that never spills. Their eyes, when visible, are glossy black seeds or pale green lenses without pupils. They do not blink.

They do not breathe in any perceptible rhythm. Instead their bodies remain slightly expanded, chests lifted as though each has just drawn a first breath and has not yet released it.

Their posture always suggests an interrupted intention.

One stands at the entrance to a shelter, a hand raised toward the luminous corridor beyond. One kneels beside a grown bowl, fingers almost touching its water. Several face one another in a circular clearing, hands extended but not yet joined. In the narrow passages, solitary figures lean forward with one foot planted and the other lifted, forever on the verge of taking a step. There is no strain in them. Tendons do not tremble; muscles do not tire. Like the shoots, the hanging drops, and the unopened flowers, they inhabit the preparation for action rather than action itself.

Yet they are not statues. Their surfaces are warm. A palm laid against one finds the yielding firmness of living flesh beneath a thin vegetal rind. Sap-colored fluid stands under the skin. Their bodies smell faintly individual: resin, cut melon, pepper leaf, bruised mint, green wood after rain. If touched, some produce a deep internal resonance, less like a heartbeat than the vibration of a trunk carrying pressure from somewhere impossibly distant.

Whether they are conscious cannot be answered by watching them. Their stillness has no vacancy in it. They seem attentive—sometimes intensely so—but their attention does not pass from object to object. It is fixed at its first instant. The kneeling one is forever discovering the water. The figures in the clearing are forever recognizing one another. The traveler at the threshold is forever about to depart.

Perhaps each experiences only that instant, infinitely deepened.

They leave no waste and require no food. The country has prepared nourishment for them everywhere: pulp under membranes, bright nectar in flowers, clear water cupped in leaves. Their mouths, where they possess mouths, remain closed. Nothing is consumed. Instead, rootlets bind many of them to the floor, entering at heel, fingertip, spine, or navel. Through these connections the greater plant may sustain them—or they may be specialized organs through which it senses its own interior.

The shelters suggest that they once lived differently. Sleeping hollows match their bodies. Bowls wait beside resting places. Bridges suit the span of their stride, and stairlike ridges fit the intervals of their feet. But there is no residue of occupation, no worn path, no stain left by repeated touch. It may be that the country formed these conveniences in anticipation of them. It may be that they were shaped at the same time as the alcoves, each inhabitant grown together with the room intended to receive it.

In the deepest galleries are smaller ones.

They lie inside split seed cases, curled tightly around themselves. Their knees touch their foreheads. Their arms cross their chests, and folded structures cling to their backs—perhaps leaves, perhaps wings, perhaps only organs for which the surrounding world has not yet provided a use. Fine roots pass through their navels into the luminous flesh of the pod.

They are not children. Their faces are ageless and incomplete. Features appear beneath translucent membranes but have not pressed fully through. Some pods contain two or three bodies intertwined so closely that they share limbs. Others hold only a dense braid of fingers, eyes, tendrils, and small white bones: not malformed, but undecided.

Near them, taller inhabitants keep watch. Or appear to. Their heads incline over the open pods, and their many-jointed hands hover just above the occupants. They might be attendants waiting to lift the newly emerged. They might be another stage of the same organism. They might simply be flower parts arranged around a reproductive chamber.

Nothing clarifies the relationship because nothing proceeds.

The greatest concentration of inhabitants is found in the central basin, among the millions of shoots pressing toward the upper membrane. There they stand in long, irregular ranks, all facing upward. Roots envelop their legs. Leaves lie folded along their arms. Their bodies grow progressively less human toward the heart of the basin: skin becomes sheath, ribs become parallel veins, raised faces taper into pale spearpoints.

Some still possess hands. Those hands press against the translucent ceiling from below.

Above them lies the blurred other world. Its brightness filters through the membrane and mingles with the light inside their bodies. Their expressions—on the faces complete enough to bear expressions—are neither fearful nor serene. They show the naked concentration of emergence: brows lifted, mouths barely open, every feature arranged around the expectation of a next moment.

Perhaps the buried country is not their home but their collective body. Perhaps every gallery is an organ, every pool a held secretion, every motionless creature an experiment in shape. The humanoid inhabitants may be thoughts the plant has grown for reaching upward: hands to part the membrane, feet to cross what lies beyond, eyes to encounter distance.

They have not failed. Failure would require an aftermath.

They remain perfectly equipped for departure. Their shelters wait behind them, unused or already relinquished. Their rootlets are taut. Their folded crowns point toward the surface. Millions of pale shoots press beside them, and among those shoots it becomes impossible to tell which are plants and which are people.

All are inhabitants.

All are offspring.

All are the one immense buried life, caught at the bright threshold of becoming plural.
