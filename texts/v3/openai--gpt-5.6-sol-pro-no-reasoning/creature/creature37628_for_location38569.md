The principal inhabitants are the kelp forests. They rise from drowned shelves in dense, many-tiered stands, thick-stemmed and long-lived, their crowns floating near the rain-dark surface while holdfasts grip polished rock far below. In calm water they form olive-gold canopies; in the passes they stream nearly horizontal, groaning and knocking against stone. Beneath them grow red weeds, fanlike algae, eelgrass, crusts, mossy mats, soft corals, and pale branching colonies. Together they turn each submerged mountainside into a vertical forest whose layers are determined by light.

Mussels occupy almost everything solid. They plate boulders, retaining walls, cables, pylons, ladders, and turbine housings in blue-black shells. Barnacles whiten the higher structures. Anemones open in drill holes and old root hollows, while sponges and corals blur the distinction between machinery and reef. Lobsters withdraw into fractures in the ancient rock. Octopuses favor the human-made cavities—pipes, culverts, collapsed masonry—and arrange shells and crab remains around their entrances. Crabs travel across the gray silt plains below, sometimes in loose processions that vanish when storms lift the bottom into clouds.

The upper water belongs to shoaling fish. Herring gather in such numbers that whole straits seem to flicker. They contract around kelp columns, split before diving birds, and turn silver when disturbed by mackerel, tuna, porpoises, or sharks. Large cod inhabit the colder drowned crags, moving slowly through trenches and old cirques. When deep water surges upward, unfamiliar pale fish may be carried into daylight with sediment and torn weed, exposed briefly before sinking or becoming prey.

Seals sleep on slanted summit slabs and hunt through the forests below, folding and turning between the stems with an ease the larger predators cannot match. Porpoises work the open basins. Whales travel the deepest submerged valleys, following trenches that once held rivers and glaciers. Their calls cross several basins at once, returning from the rounded cliffs as altered voices, so that a single animal may seem like a dispersed congregation.

Above water, the surviving peaks are governed by birds. Gannets, gulls, auks, and cormorants pack ledges, roofs, crane arms, ventilation housings, and patches of thin turf until the islands look snow-covered from afar. They nest wherever claws can find purchase. Their guano feeds the black summit soil and makes possible the grasses, sorrel, ferns, low shrubs, and rare twisted rowans crouching behind ribs of rock. These thickets shelter insects and small nesting birds, modest terrestrial communities marooned above an overwhelmingly marine world.

People live here too, though precariously and in small numbers. They inhabit compact industrial stations fixed to the highest peaks: weather-beaten rooms among tanks, winches, grating, cranes, and enclosed turbines. Their lives are organized by wind, tide, corrosion, and maintenance. They clear kelp from intakes, scrape shell from sensors, replace rusting bolts, watch cables for strain, and wait for narrow passages of safe weather. Their boats shelter behind summit-stone breakwaters. Every journey is conditional.

No structure belongs exclusively to them. Birds settle in housings still warm from operating machinery. Mussels thicken around pylons. Kelp winds through guards and railings. Seals haul out on access ramps. Octopuses occupy abandoned conduits, and loose nets gather colonies of their own. The workers do not so much exclude the sea as negotiate temporary clearances within it.

Farther below, life becomes sparse, slow, and difficult to distinguish from the terrain. Low crusts spread over black stone. Filter feeders open into currents too faint to stir the silt. Larvae drift through drowned passes and settle wherever the mountain, a cable, or a lost fragment of timber interrupts the flow. Dead kelp descends from above carrying shells, bones, and small scavengers, feeding communities beyond the reach of useful light.

Nothing living here possesses the range for long. Forests are torn out and regrown; colonies smother machinery and are scraped away; shoals occupy a basin for an hour; birds abandon one summit and whiten another. Even people remain only through constant repair. The mountains alone seem permanent. Around them, their inhabitants form a succession of temporary countries—kelp canopies, mussel reefs, nesting cliffs, silver shoals, work platforms—assembled by current and season, then broken apart and made elsewhere.
