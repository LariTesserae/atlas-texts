The people of the drowned summit are long-bodied, soft-jointed beings shaped less for swimming than for remaining exactly where they intend to be.

At rest, they hang nearly upright. Their torsos are narrow through the waist and deep through the chest, enclosing broad pressure organs that flex visibly beneath the skin. Four arms grow from the upper body in two unequal pairs. The higher pair is strong enough for hauling along causeways and holding position against a current; the lower is smaller, deft, and seldom still. It ties knots, signs speech, tests the warmth of walls, and works shell blades into new mineral crust. Their two legs are shorter than their arms and end in divided, gripping feet. They walk when a surface permits it, but walking is considered a cramped, local form of travel. Most movement is a sequence of reaches, anchors, and controlled drifts.

Their skin is smooth but not bare. Fine sensory ridges run along the ribs, forearms, jaw, and spine, reading pressure changes too slight for conscious hearing. In calm water these ridges lie flat and resemble pale scars. When a current shifts, they rise like the nap of velvet. A citizen entering an unfamiliar chamber may spread all four hands, close their eyes, and understand from the water’s minute movements where the exits, occupants, and hanging objects are.

They have no hair. From the scalp and shoulders grow flexible vanes, translucent and richly supplied with blood. These serve partly for respiration, partly for display, and partly for heat exchange. A relaxed person’s vanes drift loosely around the head like a soft crown. Fear draws them tight against the neck. Anger darkens them. During strenuous work they open wide, exposing branching vessels that glow red-black in amber light.

Color varies by depth and lineage: ivory mottled with wine, smoke-gray, muted violet, clay-red, and near-black with pale extremities. Green is rare and carries no special beauty; under the city’s weak illumination it can look unhealthy. Infants are almost transparent. Their organs show as dim shapes beneath the skin, and the meals in their digestive chambers can be followed as clouds of color. With age, mineral grains accumulate in the outer tissues. Elders become opaque, faintly lustrous, and cool-looking, as though beginning to turn to pearl.

Their faces are broad and quiet. The eyes are large but not dominant, adapted to register glows, movement, and broad contrasts rather than detail. Several clear lids can close over them. Around the mouth lies a ring of short tactile folds, used to taste currents and inspect surfaces. The mouth itself can seal tightly. Speech is made chiefly in the throat and chest: low pulses that travel efficiently through water, shaped and qualified by the hands.

A person’s complete name is therefore both sounded and signed. To know only the audible portion is to know them formally or imperfectly. Intimates may recognize one another by the smallest motion of the lower hands, or by the pressure rhythm produced when knuckles touch sternum. Across a crowded chamber, identity is often announced through posture rather than voice.

Silence among them is not absence. It has grammar.

One hand resting over another asks patience. Palms turned inward disclaim certainty. A slow opening of all fingers means that the speaker has finished but the matter remains unfinished. To point with a single finger is abrupt and sometimes insulting; direction is given with the whole hand, as currents give it. Children are noisy because they have not yet learned to prevent emotion from passing into their vanes and pressure ridges. Adults can appear impassive while conducting fierce arguments below the level of sound.

They are born live, usually one at a time, after a long pregnancy. A newborn cannot yet regulate buoyancy and must be tethered to a parent or enclosed in a nursery net. Families often sleep with infants floating at the center of a web of adult bodies. The first skill taught is not swimming but anchoring: how to grasp without tearing, how to release without drifting, how to find a handhold in darkness. A child’s earliest civic lessons are tactile journeys. Blindfolded, they are led from home to reservoir, reservoir to garden, garden to refuge chamber, until the city exists in their hands.

Kinship is broad and architectural. A household includes those who maintain the same growing rooms, whether or not they share ancestry. A person may be said to belong to a wall, a spring, or a root canopy. Houses absorb families and divide them: a chamber closes under mineral growth, a new passage is opened, siblings establish sleeping nets on opposite sides of what was once a single room. Genealogies are kept, but so are sequences of occupation. Someone may introduce herself as “third keeper of the warm alcove,” though the first keeper died centuries earlier and the alcove now lies buried six arm-lengths inside stone.

Food comes overwhelmingly from the gardens. They eat gas-leaf flesh after its buoyant bladders have been pierced and drained, strings of starchy bulbs, waxy cups filled with bitter gel, tender root tips, and dense cakes made from ground fronds fermented in ceramic jars. Warm seep-water is passed through beds of mineral-loving growths to make broths rich in salts. Tiny grazers are sometimes collected with fine nets and eaten whole, but this is seasonal and faintly luxurious.

Meals take place around tether rings. Bowls have lids with narrow valves, and diners draw out portions with flexible spoons or directly with their mouth folds. Fragrant substances are valued more than strong taste because scent spreads through the water and makes a meal communal. A single crushed bulb can fill an entire room with sweetness. Courtesy requires warning others before releasing a powerful scent.

Clothing does not chiefly conceal or warm. It prevents abrasion, carries tools, communicates work and affiliation, and controls drag. Most wear wrapped bands around the hips and chest, tight sleeves on the hauling arms, and belts woven with tactile maps. Loose fabric is ceremonial and difficult: beautiful because it must be managed. Officials and mourners may wear long root-fiber streamers that reveal every current crossing a chamber.

Ornament is attached carefully. Nothing merely rests on the body. Beads are knotted into cords; ceramic plaques are stitched through reinforced bands; native-metal flecks are hammered into thin scales and bound against brow or sternum. The rarest adornments are voids: intricate openwork collars designed to catch no water, displaying the maker’s command of line and tension.

Occupations shape the body. Causeway haulers develop massive upper arms and callused palm hooks. Gardeners bear small stains where plant secretions have entered the skin. Quarry workers often have cloudy eye-lids from mineral dust and missing tips on their sensory ridges. Seep-guides, who redirect the city’s building waters, cultivate extraordinary sensitivity to heat and salinity. They taste stone before touching it. Some can predict where a wall will thicken by holding their tongue in the current.

These guides are not quite architects. No single person commands a building. A new structure belongs to several kinds of keeper: lattice weavers determine its first skeleton; flow tenders place clay tubes and channels; pruners remove unwanted deposition; maturity readers judge how the structure will change over decades; abandonment witnesses decide when it can no longer safely be kept open. Their plans are braided cords containing knots for shape, temperature, rate of growth, and expected closure.

A plan that depicts only the finished building is considered ignorant. Buildings do not finish. They pass through useful ages.

The people themselves are understood similarly. Childhood is the age of attachment, youth the age of current, maturity the age of redirection, and old age the age of enclosure. Elders often relinquish wide travel and inhabit warm interior rooms, becoming repositories of maps, judgments, and remembered sound. Since their mineral-heavy bodies require less ballast, they are comfortable in still water and can remain almost motionless for hours. Younger citizens visit to learn forgotten routes by touching patterns tapped against an elder’s forearm.

Death changes the household physically. The dead do not float away. Their bodies are washed in filtered seep-water, folded into a resting posture, and tied within a lattice niche. Mineral flow is then admitted. Over years the niche seals, leaving the person inside a pale swelling of the wall.

This is not regarded as imprisonment. It is called taking depth.

A small textured mark remains outside, often no more than a distinctive arrangement of grooves. Families touch these marks when entering or leaving. In very old houses, dozens of dead lie at different depths behind the visible surface, along with lost rooms, abandoned tools, sealed doorways, and former walls. The house is both dwelling and lineage, though no one assumes the dead remain conscious. What remains is position: the fact that a life continues to alter how others must move.

Their healers know pressure injuries, mineral poisoning, root-burn, infections of the respiratory vanes, and the dangerous loss of buoyancy that follows damage to internal bladders. Splints are grown from fibrous plants around broken limbs. Wounds are closed with hooked ceramic needles and thread that slowly dissolves. Patients with ruptured buoyancy organs are suspended in adjustable nets and may spend months relearning balance.

Illness is diagnosed partly by listening. A healer places cheek or chest against the patient and feels for irregular internal pulses. Public clinics are lined with smooth ceramic scales because rough mineral walls retain contaminants. Their doors bear strong scent cords as well as tactile signs, so the injured can find them while disoriented.

Their scarcity of animals has shaped imagination. They do not divide nature readily into plant and beast. Movement is not, to them, the proof of a mind. Plants adjust, walls grow, currents remember obstructions, and the mountain changes its pressure over centuries. The question they ask is not “Is it alive?” but “What does it answer?”

A garden answers quickly. A mineral spring answers across generations. The mountain may answer too slowly to be understood.

The rare large visitors from above or below trouble these categories. Broad creatures crossing the summit are called the Passing Roofs. Abyssal beings are called Joint-Lights, though each recorded appearance differs from the last. No one claims they are gods. That would make them too familiar. Their arrival suspends ordinary life because their movements fill the city with pressures no wall was designed to bear. People retreat indoors, tie every loose object, damp all resonant surfaces, and wait.

Afterward, witnesses bring accounts to the archive. Since sight is unreliable, testimony includes current direction, taste, warmth, rhythm, and the timing of each light. Archivists weave these observations into enormous knot records. A single visitation may occupy a cord longer than a street.

The archive’s inhabitants are recognizable by the hardened pads along their fingers. They read walls and cords continuously, often in total darkness. Written marks exist on ceramic slips, but knots are more trusted: ceramics break, incisions fill with deposits, while a maintained cord can be recopied without requiring sight. Histories are performed by groups. One person voices dates, another signs relationships, another manipulates the map cord, and a fourth produces low pressure beats marking uncertainty, disagreement, or missing evidence.

Truth, in their tradition, is expected to have several simultaneous forms.

Their government reflects this. The council is not organized by territory alone but by depth, flow, and obligation. Warm-water keepers speak for springs. Garden tenders speak for cultivated bodies. Causeway crews speak for connections. House delegates speak for inhabited stone. A separate seat belongs to future closures: its holder considers those who will live with walls after present doors have narrowed.

Debate in the spiral-columned council house is slow. Speakers grip opposite grooves so listeners can locate them in darkness. Interruptions are made through a tug on shared cords rather than by shouting. Decisions affecting long-grown structures may take years, because haste can be more destructive than refusal.

Their law distinguishes sharply between damage and redirection. Breaking a wall through carelessness is serious. Altering its growth without communal consent is worse, because the harm may not become visible until the perpetrator is dead. Water theft is likewise treated as an offense against time. Diverting a seep from one district may starve reservoirs decades later or cause an ancestral room to seal prematurely.

The worst crime is false anchoring: placing a support, tether, route marker, or structural knot that appears sound but is not. In a city where every body entrusts itself to handholds, deliberate unreliability is nearly incomprehensible.

Their celebrations are acts of controlled release. During depth festivals, thousands of buoyant seed cases are freed into sorting towers and allowed to rise through successive nets. At the dedication of a causeway, citizens cross without pulling themselves, trusting the current to carry them from handhold to handhold. Courtship often begins with the exchange of route cords. To give someone a map of one’s home is intimate; to guide them there in darkness without once correcting their hand is more so.

Music is felt through architecture. Choirs press feet and palms against resonant lattices and pulse their throats in staggered patterns. The listeners hang from connected cords, receiving the composition through bone and skin. Some works take advantage of the city’s distances: a tone begun in an upper district arrives at the square several moments later, where another choir answers it. The whole mountain becomes an instrument too large for any performer to hear at once.

Their art is similarly distributed. Sculptors do not only carve figures; they arrange mineral flows so an image appears gradually, becomes legible for a generation, and then vanishes beneath further growth. The most admired works include their own burial. A famous civic relief may currently show twelve interlocking hands, though archival cords record that it once showed a face and will eventually become a blank pale bulge.

Children sometimes ask why artists make what will be covered.

Adults answer that covering is how the mountain remembers.

The Unfinished Square belongs to everyone and no one. Citizens cross it lightly, knowing the seep will record each pause in white crust. Lovers leave paired handprints. Petitioners place tools there. Children lie flat until their outlines begin to gather. Then, at the appointed scraping, all such traces are removed.

Those who scrape the square hold a peculiar office. They are not priests, exactly, though outsiders might mistake them for such. They wear no ornament and use shell blades shaped to fit the shallowest irregularities. Their work is to prevent meaning from hardening there. No memorial, market, council platform, grave, or dwelling may claim the space. Even accidental mineral ridges are leveled. The square must remain available to whatever the mountain would do without persuasion.

Above it lives the great canopy.

Some citizens insist the slow lights within its tissues correspond to temperature and nourishment. Others have spent lifetimes mapping the glows and claim they anticipate deaths, structural failures, or decisions not yet spoken aloud. Canopy listeners suspend themselves among the hanging roots for days, drinking through tubes and recording minute changes in brightness. They disagree among themselves about whether the organism thinks. Most consider the question crude. The canopy answers; the difficulty is determining whether the answer is addressed to anyone.

The people know the mountain is rising. Marker grooves prove it, though the movement is too slow for the body to feel. They possess words for upward and downward, warmward and coldward, inward and abyssward—but the direction toward the remote surface also has another name: the impossible shallows.

The shallows occupy religion, speculation, comedy, and dread. There, according to old calculations, water ends.

Scholars dispute what an ending of water could mean. Some imagine a hard ceiling. Some believe the ocean becomes too thin to hold a body and that anything entering it would be torn upward. Old prophetic cords describe a region where unsupported objects rush downward, where sound weakens, where plants cannot hang, and where heat becomes visible and hungry.

Fire is known only as a theoretical cousin of vent heat: warmth detached from water and made ravenous. Falling is harder to believe. Citizens can understand sinking, drifting, being dragged by current—but not the universal betrayal of every untethered object in a single direction.

There are groups who prepare for emergence. They breed denser garden strains, design frames that might support a body without buoyancy, and build sealed chambers in which water levels can be lowered by tiny amounts. Volunteers enter these chambers and endure the partial weight of themselves. Even a slight reduction causes pain, panic, and injury. Yet they return, convinced that some distant generation will need these brutal lessons.

Others reject the project. The summit remains thousands of fathoms from air; why deform the present for a catastrophe beyond counting? They argue that the mountain may stop, split, or sink. Some claim the rising markers show not ascent but swelling, and that the entire city is being carried on the inhalation of a sleeping world.

Most citizens live between these doctrines. They repair nets, cultivate bulbs, tend the dead in their walls, and cut back doorways as mineral rims close. They measure their lives by the structures they will not finish. They inherit errors made before their birth and begin works whose users they cannot name.

They do not call themselves a people of the deep. To them, the abyss below is the deep, and the brightness above is a distant danger. They inhabit the warm middle where stone is becoming, where roots and houses can be taught to share a shape.

Their closest word for civilization means, approximately:

**the arrangement that holds while it grows.**
