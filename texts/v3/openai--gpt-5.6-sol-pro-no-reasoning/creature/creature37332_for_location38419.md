The inhabitants of the high sea are creatures of suspension. None are built for a floor, because there is no floor; none for a true surface, because water becomes weather before it ends. Even the animals that cling to the reef understand attachment less as resting than as choosing what to orbit.

At the base of the living multitude are the **filings**: microscopic drifters whose transparent bodies contain articulated chains of iron-gray granules. In ordinary light they resemble dust caught in glass. Under lightning, the chains align, and whole clouds of them flash into ordered bands. They feed by holding themselves across the sea’s weak electrical gradients, using their metallic inclusions to force nourishment from dissolved gases and salts. Their populations are densest near the mist boundaries, where wind friction charges the water, and around freshly precipitated mineral, where chemical imbalances remain strong.

Filings do not swim. They turn themselves into favorable orientations and let the angled buoyancy carry them. Their migrations are therefore weather written at microscopic scale.

Grazing upon them are innumerable **slivers**, laterally flattened animals with mirrored flanks and almost no visible thickness when viewed head-on. They travel through cracks like thrown blades, though their motion is not fast: each advances by a continuous ripple passing from snout to tail. Their mouths form narrow combs along their leading edges. A feeding shoal opens and closes like a silver fan, straining filings from the water.

A sliver has no fixed upper side. Its organs occur in paired rows, and it can reverse direction without turning around. This is useful among the reef’s knife-edged chambers, where turning space is scarce and all apparent routes may end suddenly in stone. When alarmed, thousands align edge-on to the source of danger and seem to vanish. Lightning reveals them again as hair-thin scratches suspended throughout the water.

Among the slivers drift **redknots**, colonial animals resembling loose bundles of clear tubes. The crimson bodies visible inside are not organs but smaller partner organisms carried in protected channels. These partners hoard reactive metals; the transparent host pumps charged water over them and consumes the compounds they release. Neither survives long alone. Old redknots become elaborate tangles, with abandoned tubes trailing behind and new growth branching toward electrical currents. Shoals shelter among these glassy forests until a hunter tears them apart.

The reef’s branches are crowded with **radials**, chalk-pale armored creatures whose claws encircle the body like the spokes of a wheel. They fasten themselves to black mineral and face whichever way the current presses hardest. Between their plates are soft electrochemical brushes that harvest filings, fragments of shell, and dissolved residues. When the current changes, a radial does not pivot. It releases one claw at a time and walks around its own grip until its body has turned.

Generations may occupy the same spur. Their discarded armor plates cement together, making pale collars around the black glass. These collars are among the closest things in the sea to sediment.

The small knocking animals are **shutters**. Each consists principally of two asymmetrical shells joined around a muscular sac. They live in fissures where pulses of water pass between chambers. A shutter opens to admit a measured volume, strips it of edible particles, then snaps closed hard enough to propel the remainder onward. Neighboring shutters entrain one another. A colony may clap in elaborate sequence, sending pressure waves down a chimney and drawing fresh water behind them.

Their rhythms alter with temperature, hunger, electrical charge, and danger. To an observer they might sound purposeful, even coded, but the pattern is neither language nor mechanism. It is appetite passing from body to body.

The bell colonies occupy larger channels. Each **bellows-bell** is translucent, thick-rimmed, and tethered by a cord of contractile tissue. Hundreds line the walls of a chimney. They fill slowly, then collapse almost together, pulling cold metal-rich water from below and driving warmer water toward the sea’s crown. Their bodies are veined with dull mercury colors. Larval bells begin as soft blisters on mineral skins; when mature, they rupture free, swim briefly, and fasten deeper in the same branching system.

Entire lineages may never leave one chimney. They inherit its pulse from their elders through vibration.

Predators tend to be less visibly animal. The **seam-mouths** lie coiled in hollows as smooth, jointless cords, dark violet against black stone. Their bodies have no privileged front. A seam-mouth can split open along nearly its entire length, revealing inward-pointing mineral hooks and a lining that contracts in opposing waves. When a sliver shoal passes close, the predator unfurls through it, closes sideways, and becomes a sealed tube full of prey.

Digestion leaves the seam-mouth heavy with salts. It then winds around a warm spur and remains nearly motionless while scavengers clean its exterior. If disturbed, it releases its grip and descends—but because descent bends toward the reef, it usually spirals back to another branch rather than falling away.

The thin, piercing hunters are **needlers**. They resemble transparent rods with a dark sensory knot at either end. Needlers hunt by producing short, violent clicks from cavities along their bodies. Echoes return through the mineral and water, giving them a momentary map of every shell and moving surface nearby. They pursue weakness rather than shape: an irregular beat in a shutter colony, a gap opening in a shoal, the strained vibration of a molting radial.

Several needlers hunting in one chamber can deafen smaller animals and confuse the reef’s local rhythms. For this reason the shutters sometimes answer them collectively, clapping with enough force to flood the chamber with useless echoes.

Death belongs to the **pall**, a shifting population of scavengers so small that individual bodies are seldom seen. They appear as smoke around a carcass. Each is little more than a membrane, a mineral jaw, and a vesicle for carrying dissolved material. They work from the outside inward, releasing no large fragments. Even bone, tooth, and armor become mobile salts before gravity can take them through the lower mist.

The pall also attacks damaged tissue in living animals. Larger species survive by cultivating electrical patterns or surface vibrations that the scavengers avoid. A creature too weak to maintain its identifying pulse may begin to dissolve before it has ceased moving. There is no prolonged carrion in the high sea, and almost no waste.

Above all these are the **mills**.

A mature mill is broader than a person is tall and so dense that, outside the sea’s altered buoyancy, it could not support its own weight. Its wheel shape is genuine but imperfect. The rim undulates. The overlapping teeth differ in size and composition, replaced throughout life from mineral gathered by feeding. Beneath them runs a circular mouth, and within the mouth sits a muscular hub crossed by pale contractile spokes.

A mill lodges sideways in a reef socket and lets the current turn it. Muscle does not usually drive the rotation; muscle resists, regulates, and extracts work from it. Charged drifters, small grazers, and suspended shell enter between the teeth. Edible matter is combed inward. Excess mineral is ground to powder and expelled in a white wake. Some of that powder later precipitates on the reef, so that the mill slowly enlarges the structure that holds it.

Young mills are free-swimming rings no wider than a hand. They roll through the angled currents and gather teeth grain by grain. Most are eaten or lost through the lower boundary before finding a socket. Those that settle may live for centuries.

They sense one another through the reef. When one turns, its teeth transmit a pulse into the black mineral; nearby mills adjust their muscular resistance until their rotations fall into ratios. They do not all move at the same speed. One may turn twice while another turns three times, with a third producing a slow accent beneath them both. Across many chambers these coupled rhythms merge into the foundational drone of the sea.

No mill understands that its labor helps hold the water aloft. It turns because current brings food, because neighboring vibration settles its muscles, because prolonged silence triggers distress. Yet the consequence of all their feeding is atmospheric architecture. Their synchronized low frequencies press faintly against the sea’s boundaries, countering instabilities in the converging winds.

During the dim phase, nearly every creature seeks shelter. Slivers pack into fissures until they resemble metallic deposits. Seam-mouths close into inert cords. Bells pulse only often enough to keep their chimneys from stagnating. But the mills continue, and certain other animals maintain them.

These are the **tenders**, though they tend without intention. They are small many-legged grazers that live between a mill’s teeth, eating trapped flesh and polishing mineral faces. When a rotation falters, tenders swarm toward the uneven pressure and their combined movement often clears the obstruction. They breed in the warm recesses beside the hub and cannot survive elsewhere. To the mill they are irritation, cleaner, parasite, and necessary companion all at once.

The suspended sea may therefore be considered one animal only in the loosest sense: not a shared mind, but a shared dependence so complete that no boundary remains ecologically honest. Filings turn wind-charge into flesh. Slivers gather the filings. Hunters break the shoals. The pall returns bodies to solution. Bells and shutters circulate the dissolved remains. Mills grind shell into seeds for new mineral growth. The reef gives all of them chambers and transmits the sound that preserves the water containing them.

Even apparent emptiness is inhabited. In the outer mist live **veils**, animals spread across films only a few droplets thick. They have branching bodies measured more easily by area than volume. Veils catch fugitives approaching the boundary and contract around them, drawing both prey and water back into the sea. When a fragment tears away and begins to fall, veils lace across its surface. For a brief time they hold the little globe coherent, enclosing a sample of the entire ecology.

Then the pressure fails. The globe frays into rain. The veils become threads, the slivers separate into bright falling points, and the filings lose their ordered chains. Almost none reach the ground alive.

Within the parent sea, the injury fills with fog. Chemical skins spread over broken mineral. Shutters colonize the new cracks. Slivers test the altered passages, disappearing and returning until the shoal learns a new route. Last of all, the mills revise their coupled rhythm around the missing mass.

The correction may take days. One wheel slows. Another begins to answer it. A deep interval passes from chamber to chamber until the reef settles into a new pattern.

Then the inhabitants resume the only world they know: no earth, no horizon, no green growth and no reliable down—only cold water thinning into sky, black glass branching through omnidirectional light, and the unceasing communal thunder by which they feed, navigate, breed, and unknowingly keep their ocean from falling.
