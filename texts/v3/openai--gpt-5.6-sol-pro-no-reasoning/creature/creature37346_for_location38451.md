The beings of the plateau do not think of themselves as inhabitants. To inhabit implies a place that remains beneath you.

They are continuities: habits of flesh carried from one arrangement of the world into the next.

The small green-backed grazers are among the most numerous. Each has six slender legs and a blunt head held close to the stone, where mineral dew gathers in thin iridescent skins. What looks like fur is a dense growth of symbiotic filaments. In pale light the filaments spread, drinking what little reaches them through the cloud ceiling; near hot fissures they fold flat and shed excess heat as a dull green shimmer.

No grazer travels alone. Their columns wind through the wet groves like roots that have remembered how to move. The first animals test the ground with delicate forefeet. The rest step into the impressions before those impressions have necessarily been made. When distance lengthens unexpectedly, a column contracts into a nearly motionless knot and waits for its destination to draw near again.

They feed continuously and sleep in turns. At night—or during whatever local condition resembles night—they enter the root chambers of the turbine groves and cling together in layered masses. Condensation runs through their fur and pools beneath them. Their young form in these communal hollows, budding from translucent sacs spun among roots and shed fibers. For their first few days, they possess no fixed number of legs. The terrain teaches them how many they will need.

Gliders roost above them.

Folded, a glider resembles a wet strip of dark bark. Unfolded, it can span the width of a tree crown. Its membranes are streaked with shifting colors that do not advertise the animal so much as describe the currents around it: violet where gravity is steep, bronze where distance is thinning, oily green where time has begun to shear. Flocks read one another’s wings and turn as a single distributed sense organ.

They rarely beat the air. They climb invisible inclines, hook their narrow feet into the edges of cloud shadows, and descend through ravines that may not yet have opened. Their mouths are fringed with fine combs for straining spores, flying mold, and tiny animals from the mist. When frightened, they scatter not in space but in sequence—some vanishing into the instant before alarm, others appearing minutes later with wounds they have not yet received.

In warm suspended pools live the glass swimmers. Their bodies are almost entirely transparent except for chains of luminous organs arranged along the spine. A single flash means food. Two indicate danger. Longer sequences are memory.

A school can preserve patterns for generations, passing light from body to body even as individual swimmers die. Thus the oldest creature in many pools is not any one animal, but a message: an intricate migration of pulses that has outlived countless vessels. Some of these messages remember basins erased before the present plateau rose. When released by a collapsing pool, they continue flashing in falling droplets, trying to direct the rain toward vanished water.

Across the exposed shelves move the many-footed pilgrims.

They are broad, armored, and low enough to shelter in the thin boundary where gravity clings most reliably to the ground. Beneath each body hang dozens of pale, muscular feet. Before taking a step, every foot lifts a sensory filament and searches for down. A pilgrim crossing a disturbed region may stand upon five different gravitational slopes at once, its body bowed and twisted between them.

Their shells accumulate lightning crust: soft, nourishing glass formed where strikes have briefly fused the rock. The pilgrims scrape it up with rotating mouthplates. Inside those plates live colonies of heat-loving worms that digest minerals and sing electrically to the host’s nerves. During storm season, the worms grow restless and guide the pilgrim toward places lightning is about to strike. Old individuals are mapped with branching burns. Herds follow the scars as though reading weather chronicles.

The walking reefs are larger still.

From a distance one may mistake one for a mobile hill or a grove shifting against the wind. Beneath the carried forest is the carrier: a patient animal plated in porous shell, supported on pillars of jointed cartilage. Its true head is small and buried deep within the forward mass. It sees through hundreds of borrowed eyes belonging to creatures that nest in sockets along its shell.

A mature reef is never one life. Mosses hold its moisture. Bladder-fronds root in the grooves of its back. Small grazers shelter beneath its skirts. Needle-mouthed hunters patrol its foliage. Fungi lace its shell and carry chemical messages across distances too great for its own nerves to span. Parasites regulate its blood, and birds—or things temporarily shaped like birds—clean the parasites when they become too numerous.

The reef carries all of them between wet archipelagos. In exchange, they tell it where the world is.

When a reef reaches a region whose distance has swollen, the creatures on its back begin calling in relays. Sound passes forward through nests, root webs, shell chambers, and conductive molds until the direction becomes a bodily pressure. The reef turns. Sometimes the whole ecology disagrees with itself, and the animal stands for years at a fork invisible to anything else.

Reproduction begins when two reefs meet beneath a descending storm. They do not mate directly. Their forests mingle. Spores cross, nestlings wander, fungi exchange nuclei, and pieces of shell are loosened and carried between them. Months later each departs with a warm cavity growing beneath its vegetation. From that cavity eventually drops a soft, pale carrier no larger than a grazer, already seeded with fragments of both parent ecologies.

Not every large moving thing is an animal in the same sense.

Some forests walk. Their trees advance by rooting their forward branches, hollowing the trunks behind, and pulling the grove across the stone one slow tension at a time. In a century they may travel farther than a herd. The creatures living among them inherit the movement as climate. Generations are born and die believing the persistent backward wind beneath the leaves is a property of the world.

The intermeshed turbine groves possess another kind of life. Each stalk is simple, scarcely more aware than a tendon. Together, linked by touching leaves and buried root chambers, a grove can sense tremors across an entire ridge. Rotation transmits information. A shudder at one end becomes a sequence of minute turns, pauses, reversals, and heat pulses at the other.

Animals learn these sequences. Some indicate rain. Some indicate an opening fissure. Some are only the involuntary dreams of the grove.

Very old groves retain motions from landscapes that no longer exist. On still days they may begin transmitting the shape of a vanished mountain, turning its slopes and clefts from leaf to leaf. Gliders gather above these performances and circle empty air as though the mountain were present. Occasionally distance obeys the memory, and for a few hours the lost summit becomes reachable.

Among all this abundance move the predators.

One is known only by subtraction. Insects stop sounding. Spores cease drifting. The green fur on the grazers’ backs flattens all at once. A clear passage opens through swarms and mist, bending toward whatever has drawn the hunter’s attention.

Nothing visible occupies that passage.

The predator appears to be an organized exclusion, a moving condition in which small life cannot remain. It feeds when the absence closes around an animal and removes some essential relation: flesh from skeleton, warmth from blood, or destination from movement. Victims are often found intact but unable to arrive anywhere. They walk until they collapse.

The echo-hunter is more comprehensible, though only slightly. It exists first as acoustic possibility. Its body condenses wherever returning sound has been made sufficiently contradictory.

A click comes back from too near. A wingbeat repeats from beneath the ground. The noise of running feet arrives from ahead. Prey turns away from these false distances, unknowingly arranging the hollow in which the hunter can become physical.

When it does, it is long, colorless, and folded into more joints than its motion requires. Its skin bears pits like small mouths. Each opens onto an internal chamber where captured sounds circulate for days. The animal can release them in altered directions: thunder from a root hollow, mating calls from empty air, the reassuring footfalls of a herd already dead.

It does not roar. It hunts with remembered quiet.

There are also creatures born from mistakes in time.

They are usually encountered beside molting grounds after storms: pale, unfinished figures nosing among shed skins. Some resemble the local fauna. Others combine incompatible ages—a juvenile head on an exhausted body, newborn limbs beneath scar tissue, eyes clouded by centuries while the flesh around them is still wet from birth.

Most survive only briefly. A few stabilize.

These become the before-born, solitary animals that avoid herds because their tracks run ahead of them. They often reach water before thirst, flee before danger, and grieve before loss. Other creatures tolerate them uneasily, sometimes following when the before-born abruptly changes direction. Their foresight is not wisdom. They experience consequence as scent, immediate and overwhelming, without always knowing which action will produce it.

The deepest root chambers shelter another order of being.

Conductive fungi spread through powdered basalt and metallic salts, forming webs that can reach from one wet grove to the next. Most are mere colonies. But where several buried mechanisms intersect—where pressure, lightning, heat, and mineral flow repeat for long enough—the webs develop persistent patterns.

They notice through interruption.

The tread of a pilgrim dims one branch. Rain brightens another. A landslide severs a thought. A forest rooting into new soil adds a slow, green sense to the whole. Such a mind may extend across hundreds of kilometers and take months to complete a response. It has no image of the plateau, only a vast inward weather of conductivity.

These buried minds remember longer than the terrain. When a mountain is deleted, the fungal web may retain its mineral signature and continue routing signals around its absent mass. Roots grow according to those signals. Herds follow the roots. In this way an erased mountain persists as behavior: a detour, a migration, a ring of vegetation surrounding nothing.

Some networks reach the natural machinery.

They enter the pounding shafts and basalt wheels as filaments, are burned away, return, and enter again. Over generations they learn the cycles. No one built the systems, and the systems have no purpose, yet the fungi respond to their recurrence as animals respond to seasons.

A few networks have begun to alter them.

Only slightly: retaining moisture in a pressure seam, encouraging mineral growth beneath a descending piston, shifting conductive mats so lightning enters one chamber rather than another. These interventions require centuries and resemble instinct more than design. The result may be a warmer valley, a delayed thunder season, or a new route by which spores can cross a barren region.

There are still no engineers on the plateau.

But something under the forests has begun to anticipate.

Near the unfinished interior live the newest beings. They arrive with the immaculate forests, fully formed and entirely without history. Their bodies function. Their instincts direct them toward foods they have never eaten and enemies that may not yet exist. They carry scars with no injuries behind them. Some wake already searching for offspring that were not created.

For the first few days they move with terrible certainty.

Then the plateau enters them. Insects bite. Fungi colonize their skin. They choose paths, fail, learn, and acquire genuine scars over the inherited ones. Their impossible memories fade or attach themselves to real objects. An animal born remembering a blue lake may adopt a warm spring as that lake. One born mourning an unknown companion may bond to the first creature that approaches.

Soon they are no longer new. Nothing remains pure for long.

When deletion comes, survival depends less on speed than on attachment. Grazers knot themselves into roots. Gliders spread across several gravitational currents. Reefs press their broad bodies flat and let their symbionts anchor them in a thousand places. The fungal minds repeat the local pattern of the world through every conductive strand, as though insisting upon it.

Sometimes this works.

Sometimes a habitat vanishes while its inhabitants remain. For a few breaths they continue according to absent conditions. Fish swim through air along the contours of a deleted pool. Arboreal creatures climb invisible trunks. Burrowers curl inside volumes of remembered soil. Then other laws take hold.

The survivors do not understand catastrophe as punishment or accident. They possess no single word for it, where they possess anything resembling words. To them, loss is one of the directions in which the world can move.

Afterward they emerge.

The grazers find new mineral films. Gliders map the altered currents on their wings. Reefs redistribute water across the nurseries on their backs. Echo-hunters release the sounds of ravines that no longer exist. Beneath everything, fungal webs grow into fresh cracks and encounter stone with no memory in it.

At the rim live the fallers.

They are narrow-bodied, hook-limbed animals that cling to the youngest scarps and feed on salts flowering from exposed seams. Their eggs must fall in order to hatch. The adults cement long chains of them to stones just before those stones break loose.

Egg and rock descend together through amber haze.

During the long fall, the shells open. The young feed on mineral dust, cloud organisms, and sometimes one another. Generations may pass before the stone completes its descent. The colony spreads over it, matures, dies, and is replaced. By the time gravity bends and the rock begins to fall upward, an entire ecology may cover its surface.

When it returns to the plateau, the descendants are unlike those that departed. They arrive from above in showers of dark stone and living threads, carrying spores from lower weather and memories encoded in inherited flashes of color.

The creatures of the rim gather to watch.

They do not regard the returning stones as messages, though in a sense they are. They do not ask what lies below, or why below should eventually become above.

They wait for the impact.

Then, while the stone is still hot, they begin to feed.
