The highlands are inhabited by a biosphere of reservoirs: creatures that gather what the country offers only briefly—water, charge, heat, vibration—and carry it through the long intervals between abundance.

Nothing here lives entirely alone. What appears to be an individual is often a moving convergence of animal, microbial colony, mineral deposit, and borrowed current. Bodies are not sealed against the landscape. They admit it carefully.

The most visible of the large inhabitants are the long-file herds.

They are called nothing, but one might think of them as **pleatbacks**. Their height exaggerates their thinness. Four long load-bearing limbs rise to a narrow trunk, with two smaller balancing limbs folded close beneath the chest. Each foot spreads into a ring of soft, pressure-sensitive lobes. These lobes do not merely feel the ground. They listen through it. A pleatback can distinguish floodwater from a predator’s imitation, though only by comparing the signal with those received by other members of the herd.

This is why they travel in such widely separated files. The distance between animals is part of a living instrument. Each body samples the plateau at a different point, then answers with a deliberate footfall or a momentary halt. Information passes backward and forward through stone. A herd stretched over fifty miles functions as one long, discontinuous sense organ.

Their heads hang low, but not to graze. The mouth is a narrow mineral rasp used on salt crusts and the swollen bases of spindle shrubs. Most nourishment comes from the pleated organs along their flanks. These organs are warm, moist recesses inhabited by dark symbiotic flyers. The flyers remove parasites, carry spores between hosts, and feed on salts secreted by the herd. In return they leave nutrient-rich secretions and maintain colonies of heat-producing microbes inside the folds. During cold atmospheric shifts, a pleatback opens its flanks and seems to breathe through its entire body.

Young are born in wet years. They begin as squat, six-footed animals that remain among the condensation grooves, feeding on black-green films. As they grow, the middle pair of limbs becomes lighter and folds upward against the chest. For several seasons these limbs help the young cling to steep channels during floods. In maturity they become resonating paddles, pressed against the ground when the herd listens.

Pleatbacks seldom sleep completely. Rest passes through the herd as a slow wave: one animal lowers itself, then another many miles away, while those between remain upright. A herd can therefore pause for days without ever becoming wholly insensible.

They are followed by the **false waters**.

A false water has no fixed outline when at rest. Its skeleton consists of hundreds of jointed mineral rods embedded in a wide muscular sheet. Spread across warm stone, it can become scarcely thicker than a hand. Pigment cells match not only color but sheen, porosity, and the direction of tiny scratches in the rock. Dust adheres to the upper surface. Condensation gathers there. Small grazers cross it unharmed, and seeds sometimes begin to root before being absorbed.

Its mouth is distributed around its margins. There is no single biting aperture. When prey steps near its center, the sheet contracts by degrees, subtly altering the slope beneath the animal. The victim is guided inward rather than seized. By the time it recognizes the movement, the apparent ledge has become a shallow bowl whose edges are rising.

False waters can wait through entire dry intervals. Their metabolism becomes almost geological. Conductive nodules along the underside draw faint currents from the stone, enough to maintain the nerves and preserve tension in the muscular sheet. Some individuals occupy the same ledge for centuries, growing so slowly that generations of herd animals learn the place as part of the terrain.

Yet they must migrate when the plateau’s deep pressures change. Then the ledge lifts itself.

Rods unfold. The broad sheet gathers into a many-legged, high-arched body and crosses the upland at surprising speed, leaving behind an area of stone cleaner and paler than its surroundings. For a few hours it is unmistakably an animal. At another channel it opens, settles, darkens, and disappears again.

Other predators practice the opposite deception. These are the **rain-callers**, spare-bodied hunters with deep resonating chambers in the chest and jaw. They station themselves near dry spillways and drive low vibrations into the bedrock. The signal resembles the first distant impact of water entering an ancient channel.

A rain-caller cannot precisely imitate a flood. It imitates thirst’s expectation of one.

Small grazers turn first. Burrowers emerge. Sometimes an entire branch of a pleatback file alters course. The hunter waits beside a pool-shaped depression, reading the approaching footfalls through whiskerlike bones laid against the ground.

Older prey know many individual rain-callers by rhythm. This has produced an ancient contest of variation and memory. Hunters add irregularities learned from real floods. Herds compare signals over wider distances. During genuine rain, the distinction collapses, and rain-callers cease hunting. They crouch in channels with their resonating mouths shut while the plateau produces sounds more intricate than anything alive can counterfeit.

Beneath nearly every visible community are the **valveworms**.

They inhabit fractures too hot and narrow for surface animals. Their bodies are armored tubes, widest at the head and tapered behind, with rings of crystalline teeth that do not bite food but grind cavity walls. Each worm enlarges a passage only slightly over its lifetime. In great numbers, however, they redirect vapor, expose ore seams, and prevent mineral conduits from sealing completely.

They feed by refining what passes through them. Water enters hot and chemically burdened. Layered stomachs precipitate iron, sulfur, and trace metals into distinct chambers. Useful compounds are absorbed by symbiotic microbes; the rest is expelled as dense, regular cakes. The cakes accumulate in stacks because the worms brace against previous deposits when excreting the next. Their geometric appearance is incidental, though uncannily exact.

Valveworms communicate by pressing armored throats to stone and grinding. Deep colonies produce continuous structured resonance: warnings of pressure changes, mating signals, territorial boundaries, and perhaps information about the timing of distant discharges. They are not conscious engineers of the plateau, but their lives have become inseparable from its machinery. Where they vanish, conduits clog. Pressure migrates elsewhere. Springs fail, then appear in unexpected valleys.

The creatures most responsible for the highlands’ hidden fertility are much smaller.

**Film grazers** move in thousands along every damp seam. Their broad bodies are enclosed in porous shells that collect condensation. Microscopic channels direct droplets toward the mouth and storage sacs. Their many padded feet exert so little pressure that they can scrape the upper growth from a living crust without damaging the buried reproductive layer.

A dry film grazer resembles a pebble. It anchors itself in shade, retracts every limb, and allows its tissues to lose most of their free water. During this state its shell remains active. Minerals expand and contract with temperature, pumping tiny quantities of air through internal chambers. The animal may persist this way for years.

When wetness returns, whole slopes begin to move.

The grazers emerge almost simultaneously, not because they see or smell the water, but because the first runoff changes the electrical potential of the rock. Their shells detect the difference. Within minutes, what seemed a bare channel becomes a flowing population. They feed, divide, mate, lay mineral-coated eggs in the deeper film, and retreat before the water is gone.

Their predators are needle-thin runners that hunt between their legs, transparent sacs that drop from ribbon colonies, and minute burrowers that erupt from pores in the stone. Much of the highlands’ animal abundance exists at this scale: brief, dark, and close to the surface.

Above them drift the largest inhabitants, the **cloud combers**.

A cloud comber is not truly a single airborne body. It is a layered colonial animal organized around a warm central bladder. Hundreds of translucent lobes overlap around this bladder, each capable of muscular adjustment but incapable of independent life. The colony inflates itself with gases produced by internal microbes and rides the escarpment currents with little expenditure of energy.

Its apparent emptiness is camouflage. The tissues transmit most of the dim atmospheric light, but not perfectly. Seen from below, a comber looks like a distortion in the clouds: a moving region where darkness has been thinned.

Along the leading edges hang millions of fine filaments. These comb water from mist, trap airborne organisms, and collect metallic dust released by the geothermal throats. Droplets move inward through branching vessels. Excess water is shed in a fine, warm rain beneath the animal, creating temporary traveling habitats across the ground.

At condensation time, symbiotic flyers rise from the pleatback herds and enter openings along a comber’s underside. Some feed there; some lay eggs; some carry spores and microbes from one great animal to another. Thus the herds and the cloud combers are linked despite rarely touching.

When a comber descends, its scale becomes difficult to understand. The gas bladder collapses into folded chambers. Peripheral lobes wrap around the center, then around one another, compressing a span as wide as a valley into a dense crouched mass. Settled beside a pool, it resembles a stack of wet glass or an enormous translucent flower closing against the ground.

It drinks deeply, exchanges minerals with the pool, and sometimes buds. The young detach as small, heavy crawlers that climb the nearest escarpment over several years. Only when they reach persistent updrafts do they inflate and become creatures of the air.

Not all inhabitants possess such recognizable bodies.

The conductive root grids beneath the dark plains may be the most extensive living things in the country. A grid begins with a spindle shrub or a breathing cup sending a filament into a metal-bearing crack. It encounters other filaments, fuses with some, rejects others, and gradually becomes a mesh of roots, fungi-like tissues, bacteria, and deposited ore.

No center governs it. Parts die constantly. Connections form elsewhere. Nevertheless, the grid stores electrical gradients, directs moisture, and changes its conductivity in response to damage. During wet periods it awakens across hundreds of miles.

Blue corona gathers above exposed tips. Seed membranes charge and lift from the rock. Buried eggs receive the impulse to hatch. Film grazers open. Valveworms alter their grinding. Even distant herds become restless as the stone begins to hum.

Whether the grid communicates or merely conducts is impossible to separate. In this country, those may be the same act.

The oldest grids develop structures called **listening knots**: dense, black tangles around piezoelectric crystals. Every footfall, flood, collapse, and subterranean discharge strains these crystals and changes the growth of the surrounding tissue. Their layered mineral deposits preserve centuries of vibration. The knots do not remember as an animal remembers, but the past physically alters what signals they transmit next.

A herd crossing an ancient grid therefore walks over the accumulated consequences of earlier herds. A predator tapping into the stone may receive a current shaped by floods that occurred before its species existed. The landscape’s memory has no image of events, only changed pathways through which future events must pass.

In the central rift, life becomes stranger still.

There the geothermal throats inhale and exhale in sequence. Around each opening grows a ring of **vent mantles**, sessile animals whose layered bodies resemble mineral lips. They open during inhalation and expose corrugated inner surfaces to the cool descending air. During exhalation they close, trapping vapor and metallic dust in deep chambers.

Their tissues tolerate temperatures that would cook the plateau herds. Colonies of microbes within them extract energy from sulfur and iron. The mantles grow in overlapping generations, new animals settling on the mineralized remains of old ones, until the rim of a throat is partly geological and partly alive.

Between throats move pale, sightless **rift walkers**. They have no lungs. Rows of chambers along their backs fill and empty with the rhythm of the vents, circulating gases through blood and symbiont beds. A walker caught away from the rift’s pulse suffocates, even in breathable air; its body requires pressure changes rather than simple oxygen.

They graze on vent mantles, but carefully. A walker that strips too much growth alters the throat’s airflow and may destroy the rhythm on which it depends. Natural selection has made their feeding precise. Their jaws remove only the swollen outer layers, leaving the structural rings intact.

During synchronization events, thousands of rift walkers stand facing the same direction. Their dorsal chambers open and close together. From the rim, the whole population appears to be one articulated surface laid along the rift, breathing in time with the stone.

The rare electrical releases transform every inhabitant.

Pleatbacks halt and spread their resonating limbs against the ground. False waters draw themselves into compact forms, unable to maintain camouflage amid rapidly changing charge. Rain-callers become silent. Film grazers pour from their shelters. Cloud combers assemble over the uplands, their translucent lobes shining at the edges.

The root grids brighten first.

Then current moves through the cracks in immense horizontal branches. For an instant, the plateau’s separate lives are joined by a common stimulus. Muscles contract. Eggs divide. spores open. Mineral valves shift. Creatures separated by hundreds of miles begin the same behavior at nearly the same moment.

This is the nearest the highlands come to a season.

Afterward comes the brief multiplication. Breathing cups inflate across salt flats. Ribbon colonies lower amber fronds. Spindle shrubs turn their metallic leaves upward and release skating seeds. Grazers cover the wet stone so densely that the plains seem to flow. Predators feed until their bodies distort with stored nourishment. Pleatback young are born into warm runoff and stand within minutes, listening before they can properly walk.

The abundance does not last. It is converted.

Water becomes tissue, eggs, spores, swollen roots, internal gels, and filled shell chambers. Electrical energy becomes chemical reserve. Mineral dust becomes armor. Every organism takes some portion of the pulse and seals it away.

Then the country empties again.

The cloud combers rise. The herds lengthen into files. Predators settle into the stone. Film grazers close like pebbles. Vent creatures withdraw behind mineral shutters. Roots contract around buried moisture. Nothing departs the highlands; life merely increases the distance between its visible moments.

In the dimness, an observer might find the uplands vacant.

But a foot placed on the warm black rock sends pressure outward. Nearby grazers seal their shells. A buried throat stops grinding. Far away, a pleatback raises one balancing limb and waits. The members behind it pause in sequence. A false water subtly tightens across its ledge. Conductive filaments bend around crystals and alter their current.

The disturbance passes from body to body, from root to ore, from ore to water, and from water into the chambers beneath the plateau.

What lives here is not simply a collection of animals and plants.

It is a dispersed, listening congregation, forever assembling itself from darkness, stone, and the promise of the next pulse.
