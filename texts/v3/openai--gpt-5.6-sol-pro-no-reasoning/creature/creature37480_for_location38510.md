They call themselves by touch rather than sound.

A hand laid briefly to another’s breast, throat, or brow conveys identity through a pattern of taps and pressure: quick-quick-pause, a knuckle drawn downward, the faint scrape of a nail. Spoken names would travel too far in the bright chamber. They would rebound from the bowls in the floor, enter the uncertain fissures, and perhaps return changed. So each inhabitant carries a small, private rhythm that can be given only at arm’s length.

They are little taller than the broadest ferns. Their bodies are fine-boned and upright, with long feet adapted to limestone slickness and toes that spread when weighted. The soles are soft, ridged, and nearly silent. Their skin is pale but not colorless. Warmth, fear, exertion, and desire move visibly through it in stains of rose, violet, and muted amber. In stillness they resemble flesh-toned figures carved from the chamber’s old ivory rock; in alarm they blanch so completely that predators and companions alike may lose sight of them.

Their eyes are large, dark, and protected by a translucent inner lid. They do not see the radiance as humans might. To them, each wall has grain and weather: old stone burns with a dense, steady whiteness, while younger patches appear smoky and porous. Moisture lies over everything as a second landscape. A thumbprint of condensation, invisible to another creature, may shine to them like a path.

They sleep in the shoulder-wide passages, gathered in knots where the stone holds warmth. No nest is permanent. Root-fiber cords are looped through natural holes; skins of dried bladder hold a mouthful of water; bundles of threadbird filament provide scant insulation. When a sleeping place fouls or becomes noisy with mites, they loosen the cords and move. Within days the abandoned hollow contains little evidence that anyone rested there.

Their lives are organized around rain they cannot see.

Before water reaches the chamber, they feel the change in their teeth and inner ears. Old injuries ache. Hair-fine sensory folds along their necks lift in the thickening air. One individual begins the careful tapping heard across the chamber—not an announcement so much as a question addressed to the stone. Others answer from beyond the walls, sometimes nearby, sometimes with delays that suggest impossible distances.

Then they emerge.

They come singly because groups make confused reflections in the radiance and too much vibration underfoot. Each carries a dark flake of fresh stone, a fiber loop, and one or two collapsed water skins. They collect without stripping. From every unfurling fern they take no more than one bitter tip. They gather filament only where threadbirds have shed it, and beetle cases only after checking for mites. Even water is drawn from several pools rather than emptied from one.

This restraint is practical, but it is also the center of what they understand about the world: anything made conspicuously useful becomes fragile.

They do not possess the chamber, and they do not imagine that it was made for them. The luminous rock is alive only in the broadest sense—not an animal, not a spirit, but something that closes injury and remembers exposure. They believe brightness is the stone’s healed condition. Darkness is not evil; it is newness, severance, or a wound not yet taught to shine.

For this reason, the dark handprints are not decorations.

An inhabitant makes one only a few times in life. The youngest press a palm when they have learned to cross the chamber alone. Adults may leave another after surviving sickness, losing a companion, or returning from a passage believed impassable. The dead receive no print of their own. Instead, someone who knew them places a hand over an older fading mark, never touching the wall, and waits until the outline can no longer be distinguished beneath the hovering fingers.

The acidic sap is gathered from root-knots after heavy rain. It stings the skin and leaves the maker’s palm numb for days. When pressed to stone, the radiance goes out soundlessly. For a moment the hand seems to open a hole into night. Then the sap dries, and the shape becomes matte gray.

No one watches a print for long. To see one’s own hand made absent from the wall is considered dangerous—not because the stone punishes, but because the mind may begin to prefer the absence.

Children are told that every mark must be allowed to heal. A print retraced to keep it dark would cease to be memory and become a claim.

They have no chiefs in the ordinary sense. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever best understands the present condition: one who knows mites during an infestation, passages during migration, roots during drought, births during the warm season. The most respected are listeners called, in touch-language, by a slow pressure beneath the jaw. They lie with an ear against the floor and distinguish distant water, moving colonies, settling rock, and the deep response that follows hammering.

They teach that this low vibration is not anger. Anger belongs to quick creatures. It is closer to adjustment—the chamber redistributing strain through distances that do not behave correctly.

The uncertain fissures shape their thought more than the open room does. To them, a passage is not necessarily a way between places. It may be a place folded too narrowly to inhabit. Knotted cords are fed into cracks not merely to map them but to test the disposition of that folding. Knots indicate arm lengths, sleep intervals, births, and remembered rains. Yet measurements rarely agree. A cord may return shorter, wetter, warmer, or bearing mineral dust unlike any stone nearby. Once in several generations, one comes back with an extra knot.

Such cords are never cut.

They are coiled around the torso of a listener, who carries them until death. Afterward they are fed back into the fissure from which they emerged. Sometimes the crack accepts the entire length. Sometimes it refuses the final span, leaving a loop that slowly stiffens beneath mineral deposits. These pale loops can be found in hidden passages, older than any remembered inhabitant.

Their dead are not buried. There is too little loose earth, and breaking the floor would darken it. The body is washed with condensation, wrapped in threadbird filament, and carried into a narrow warm fissure where soft-shelled grazers and mites can reach it. Bone is recovered later, though never all of it. Long bones become awls; small bones become toggles and drinking tubes. The skull is left within the passage.

They do not consider this a failure of tenderness. What can still serve should move among the living. What held sight and touch should remain where distances loosen.

Birth takes place beside the most dependable pools. Newborns are darker than adults, almost plum-colored, and cannot tolerate the oldest stone’s brilliance. Their eyes remain sealed behind pearly membranes. For the first weeks they are carried in slings lined with shed filament and kept beneath ledges where shadows gather blue and cool.

A child first enters the open chamber with its face covered. The covering is lifted gradually: first beside a dim waterline, later near a young surface, and only at last before the brightest wall. Many cry without sound. Some reach for the radiance. Some turn immediately toward the dark hand-shapes.

They learn silence before toolmaking. Games are played through stillness: remaining unseen among mineral ridges, crossing a wet basin without disturbing its reflection, passing a beetle from hand to hand without making it unfold its legs. Their merriment appears as rapid color flickering beneath the skin and trembling shoulders held rigid against laughter.

Yet they are not solemn creatures.

They imitate threadbirds by stretching fiber membranes between arm and flank and leaping from low shelves. They arrange empty beetle cases into unstable towers, then sit around them waiting for a condensation drop to strike. They tease sleeping companions with moth wings laid over the eyelids. During abundant rain they slide down the slick runnels on their heels, risking noise and bruises for the brief speed of it.

Their courtship is similarly visual. Two inhabitants stand far apart on the glowing floor and answer one another with posture, flush, and the unfurling of braided cords worn at the waist. They approach only if each sequence is remembered correctly. A mistake ends the exchange without humiliation. Accuracy matters because desire, like sound, can travel farther than intended.

Pairing is often brief, but bonds between water-sharing companions may last a lifetime. To give someone the last swallow in a bladder is among their most serious vows.

Food is sparse and intensely flavored: bitter fern tips, soft grazers, mites ground into paste, beetle flesh, translucent eggs taken from only the largest clutches, mineral film scraped from damp shelves. They eat little at a time. Feasts occur after rain, when dormant life opens all at once. Even then, a portion is carried into the cracks for those too old, injured, or light-sick to enter the chamber.

The oldest inhabitants often become unable to bear the radiance. Their inner eyelids cloud, and bright surfaces lose detail. They retreat into dark young fractures, where newly broken stone has not begun to whiten. There they chip tools by touch. The tapping that crosses the chamber is frequently theirs.

Some taps report water. Some warn of a shifting shelf. Some are lessons repeated for children in distant hollows.

And some have no known maker.

Those begin beneath the floor rather than beyond the walls. They come in threes or fours, careful and dry, exactly like tool blows but too deep to be felt through the air. At such times every visible animal becomes still. Moths flatten their wings. Threadbirds fold into cracks. Pool insects lift their feet from the water. The small people crouch and press their palms to the luminous floor.

Occasionally, something answers from inside the fissures: not another knock, but the low hum of a taut cord where no cord has been laid.

The inhabitants do not widen the cracks. They do not dig downward. Their stories offer many explanations, but no certainty. Perhaps the chamber is only the exposed surface of a far greater life. Perhaps the missing lengths of cord cross its interior. Perhaps all bright stone is slowly closing around a darkness that was there first.

Or perhaps distance itself lives in the narrow places, feeding on whatever tries to measure it.

They are content not to decide.

When the rain ends, they withdraw with filled bladders, bundles of filament, fern bitterness wrapped in membrane, and dark tools held close to the body. Their color fades into the stone. One by one they turn sideways and pass through openings that seem too narrow to admit them.

The chamber empties again.

Only their hand-shapes remain: generations of deliberate darkness, slowly invaded by ivory light. Along each palm and finger, brightness returns first as pinpricks, then as a cloudy grain, then as a continuous glow. The stone does not erase them quickly. It holds their absence for years.

By the time a hand has healed completely, the one who placed it may be bone, tool, memory, or a rhythm still tapped softly from one body to another in the dark.
