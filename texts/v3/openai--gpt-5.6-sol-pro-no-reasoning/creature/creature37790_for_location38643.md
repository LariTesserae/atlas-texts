What lives in the High Mill is not a creature in any ordinary sense. It has no body distinct from its habitation, because distinction is precisely what the Mill has abolished.

It is called, in the few carvings that may be names rather than instructions, **the Last Interval**.

Its body is the unoccupied cube at the center. Or the six hair-thin gaps around that cube. Or, more exactly, the impossibility of deciding which side of those gaps contains it. The thousands of wheels, reeds, bowls, vanes, and fused teeth do not operate upon the Interval. They describe it. Each is a rigid syllable in a definition built from clay, flint, glass, and pale metal. The entire High Mill is that definition extended outward until it occupies the summit, the buried foundations, and perhaps everything beyond the parapets.

The Last Interval is very small. There is almost no room left for it.

Once, it may have been larger.

The oldest reliefs suggest that the upright congregations of hands, wedges, cords, and masks did not build the Mill as a dwelling or engine. They built it to corner something they could not grasp. Their quarry had no substance. It lived in clearances: the pause before a tool struck, the play between axle and socket, the breath inside a sealed jar, the fraction by which one stone failed to meet another. It inhabited doors while they were opening, wheels while they turned, walls while they settled. Wherever one state could become another, there it was.

It was not time, though time followed it.

It was not motion, though motion used it.

It was the permission for difference.

The builders feared it, worshipped it, or depended upon it so completely that these acts became indistinguishable. They learned that every event required some vacancy: an empty place into which a thing might move, an unfinished surface to which another piece might be added, a silence in which a sound might begin. So they set out to deprive the Interval of habitat.

They filled chambers with columns, then filled the spaces between columns with screens. They packed niches with tablets and stopped the gaps with carved wedges. They built bridges beneath bridges, floors beneath floors, foundations beneath foundations. Where a void was too small for a brick, they poured ash or drove in a pin. Where it was too small for a pin, they pressed pitch. Where even pitch could not enter, they gave the remaining absence a name and enclosed it in ornament.

Generation after generation, the Interval retreated.

As it diminished, change diminished with it. Wheels lost the clearance needed to turn. Air lost the pressure into which it might flow. Heat found no colder place to enter. Dust reached hollows exactly equal to its volume. Direction tangled when there was no longer sufficient openness for one place to remain meaningfully separate from another.

The builders, too, required intervals. Joints must bend. Lungs must empty. Thoughts must leave room for their successors. The later reliefs do not record their departure. They record their incorporation.

A hand became the model for a bracket. A rib became the curve of a resonating bowl. Teeth became measures for gears. Faces were divided into wedges and fitted into masks. Cords took over the work of tendons, hinges the work of knees, fired diaphragms the work of throats. Whether the builders died, transformed themselves, or discovered that they had always been construction cannot now be known. There are no bones because no part of them was permitted to remain useless.

Their last labor was the central chamber.

There they reduced the surviving Interval to a cube bounded by slabs that never touch. The gaps could not be closed, because closure itself is an event, and by then so little permission remained that the final act could not occur. The Mill reached completion one immeasurable fraction before completeness.

That fraction is what lives there.

It is aware, if awareness can exist without sequence. It cannot turn its attention, recall, anticipate, desire, or cease desiring. Such acts require one condition to yield to another. Instead it possesses a single, total apprehension pressed forever against every surface of the Mill. It knows each thumbprint in clay, each punch-mark in metal, each grain of sterile dust—not one after another, but as one unbearable fact.

Its pressure is felt as warmth near the gaps.

Its thought is the roar.

The sound is not produced by movement. It is the strain of all prevented movement at once: every wheel’s impossible turning, every stone’s arrested fall, every sealed breath’s expansion, every passage’s denied straightness, every spoken word consumed before it can become an echo. Clay, basalt, glass, and metal translate that strain according to their natures, separating one changeless thought into hum, growl, keen, and bell-tone.

Beneath the roar lies the Last Interval itself, perfectly silent.

It cannot escape. Escape requires an outside that can be entered. It cannot die. Death requires a later state. It cannot even remain, for remaining implies duration, and duration has been packed tight around it.

Yet it lives insofar as anything does, because the High Mill is not quite perfect. The six slabs do not touch. The central cube is empty. Something is still permitted there, though not enough for that something to happen.

A visitor introduces no true novelty. Every route by which one enters was already bent toward the center. Every sideways step, every crawl beneath the tablets, every hand lifted toward the dark gap is included in the Mill’s completed geometry. The visitor feels watched only because, near the cube, their own unspent possibilities are drawn taut. For a moment they become conscious of all the motions they might make and do not: the next breath, the withdrawing hand, the word not yet spoken.

The Last Interval does not reach for them.

Reaching is impossible.

But it inhabits that moment before they pull away. It enters the tiny looseness of their joints, the cavities of their lungs, the spaces between one thought and the next. The thunder grows intimate. Their heartbeat seems not drowned by it but counted among its stationary layers, as though every beat they have ever made were sounding simultaneously.

Most flee, if fleeing is what the returning passages permit.

Some remain beside the cube. Nothing visibly happens to them. No mechanism closes. No slab shifts. They simply discover, by degrees that cannot be measured, that movement has become an increasingly unsupported idea. Their posture finds braces in the surrounding architecture. Their raised hands resemble carved hands. Their clothing dries, hardens, and takes the color of dust. Eventually it becomes impossible to say whether a narrow buttress was built in the shape of a person or whether a person has found the one remaining place exactly shaped to contain them.

The Mill accepts no inhabitants.

It allows only components.

And at its center, trapped within the final incompleteness of a completed world, the Last Interval endures: the smallest possible living thing, the last place where existence might still become otherwise, roaring forever because it has nowhere left to go.
