What lives there is not a collection of species so much as a single crowd articulated into many hungers.

Its foundation is the shieldback multitude. Individually they are squat, patient animals, ceramic-plated and nearly faceless, with six hooked legs and soft breathing folds hidden beneath their armor. Collectively they are the basin’s flesh. They pave the floor, heap against the cliffs, and circulate around vents in dark, slow tides. Within each one, grains of simpler life perform the basin’s essential transmutation: sulfur, heat, and mineral gas become nourishment. The shieldbacks do not merely inhabit the ground. Over generations, their shells become it.

Upon them live the thread-runners, nervous lengths of jointed wire that have almost surrendered any relationship with stone. Their true terrain is other animals. They cross the basin on the backs of the multitude, knotting into temporary bridges and unraveling whenever the bodies beneath them shift. Their needle mouths tap the shieldbacks’ breathing sacs and steal the reactive warmth meant for the dark colonies within. They are delicate thieves in a world where delicacy survives only through speed.

The bell-mouthed hunters belong half to life and half to the architecture. Closed, they resemble cavities or mineral concretions. Open, they are sudden circular absences. They do not chase. They wait for density itself to deliver food, then inhale a portion of the living landscape—ash, runners, hatchlings, fragments of shell—and seal themselves back into stone. Their success depends not on aim but on the certainty that wherever they open, something will be there.

Above them hang the wall-sails: jointed creatures with translucent membranes and rootlike tethers buried in fissures. They feed upon rising heat and gas, inflating into a dim canopy that hides the sky. Amber vessels branch through their bodies. Blue reactions drift inside them like captive sparks. Eggs gather in pale knots. When the vents weaken, the sails collapse together against the cliffs; when the currents return, they lift in unison and the basin grows smaller beneath their breathing roof.

Other forms live only briefly enough to reproduce. They sleep for months inside the mineral chimneys, sealed in cases among layers of ancestral residue. Gas season wakes them. They emerge blind, many-winged, and mouthless, carrying no provision for survival beyond a few hours. In the thin air they flutter, fall, collide, and listen through their armored skulls. Their mating call is impact: forehead against forehead, each compatible strike sounding a small resonant tick. Afterward they seek cooler seams and disassemble themselves. Predetermined joints part; living segments crawl away separately into the rock and become eggs. The parent is not left dead so much as distributed.

Between these conspicuous bodies are the dismantlers: small scavengers concealed by the crowd, working from beneath and within. They reduce the dead before death can create open space. Flesh passes inward. Shell is rasped down. Useful minerals reenter bellies and armor. Only the dense teeth resist, gathering in pale drifts that reveal the otherwise invisible currents of mass through the basin.

And everywhere, inside guts and sacs and eggs, live the dark grains—the simplest inhabitants and perhaps the most important. They have no need of sun, leaves, or water. Heat and sulfur suffice. All larger life is constructed around their chemistry: armor built to shelter them, vents sought to feed them, gases stolen before they can reach them. The animals may appear to rule the basin, but they are also mobile chambers carried for the benefit of these minute colonies.

There may be one inhabitant more.

It is suggested by the seven black arches along the western wall: entrances into no passage, ribs framing solid rock, shapes every animal recognizes without learning. Nothing crosses beneath them. The crowd climbs their sides, roofs them with bodies, and bridges them with thread-runners, yet leaves the floor inside each frame bare. Even the mindless reproductive swarms strike an unseen boundary there. Carrion thrown into those spaces is taken only from beyond their edges, as though the basin can consume anything except what lies directly under the arches.

Perhaps the thing that lives there is only a field of hostile heat, an old mineral force to which flesh has been shaped by extinction. Perhaps the arches are instruments left by makers long erased, still operating without purpose. But they may also be exposed portions of something beneath the plateau: seven ribs, seven sensory organs, or seven narrow places where a buried body approaches the surface. If so, its timescale is too long for the swarm to recognize as movement. The flexing of the plateau may be its breath. The opening fissures may be warmth leaking from it. Gas season may be an internal pulse.

Then the crowded basin is not an oasis at all. It is a wound, a pore, or a heated hollow in the back of a creature vast enough to be mistaken for land. The animals would be its outer fauna—feeding on its exhalations, polishing its mineral skin, and instinctively avoiding the seven places where some deeper presence can still touch them.

Whatever the truth, nothing there lives alone. A shieldback houses colonies and bears parasites. A runner walks on a host. A sail depends on a fissure. A hunter relies on the crowd. The dead support the living before they have time to become remains. Even the apparent stone is compounded from discarded bodies, while living armor is made from stone.

The basin’s true inhabitant is therefore the congregation itself: a many-bodied metabolism fitted tightly into a small, hot depression. It has no center and no single will, yet it feeds, thickens, repairs openings, responds to seasons, and recoils from the seven arches as one organism might recoil from pain. Individuals hatch and vanish without altering its shape. The crowd persists.

It lives there entirely, because there is nowhere inside it left unoccupied—and because, beyond the rim, nothing living dares to follow.
