The city belongs to congregations.

Not merely to animals in great number, but to animal multitudes that have ceased to be understood as collections of separate lives. A flock is a citizen. A reservoir full of pale, surfacing bodies is a citizen. A hereditary procession moving between towers over the course of decades is a citizen. Individuals are brief articulations—mouths, feet, shells, breaths—through which these larger beings touch the city.

The West Viaduct Flock lives across nine bridges and the warm hollows of three towers. Its members are dark, bladder-bodied creatures that travel without wings. They rise by swelling chambers threaded through their torsos, drift through the bridgework, then contract to settle in dense, shivering layers. Alone, one seems nearly helpless: soft, slow, and poorly directed. Together they sense pressure gradients over the entire basin. Their coordinated buoyancy maps weather no instrument can predict. Doors open for the flock’s advancing edge; transit pauses until its trailing members have passed. When it sleeps, its thousands of bodies spread over façades like a single black mantle breathing.

The silver-coated colonies inhabit the lower roofs and exchanger courts. Their bodies are humped against the cold, with short faces buried in reflective fur. Moisture gathers on the outer hairs, freezes, and is shed when they shudder in sequence. This communal tremor travels through them in visible waves. They feed beneath vapor vents, standing open-mouthed in the warm nutritive fog while condensation pearls on their coats. Their young are born into the center of the mass and may not see stone for years.

Six-limbed climbers occupy the wet vertical districts. Broad sealing feet let them cross weeping walls and hang beneath bridges above the black channels. Their front limbs are delicate and many-jointed, used to scrape edible films from mineral panels. The rear four anchor and release with a continuous noise like rain being peeled from glass. They recognize routes through pressure and texture. Smooth new stone confuses them, so the city scores fresh ridges with temporary grooves until generations have worn paths of their own.

In flooded stairs and submerged halls live the white breathers. They are blind, long-necked swimmers with skin thin enough to show slow organs beneath it. They rise in groups to air pockets preserved in ceilings, each body following the exhalations of the one before. Some populations have occupied the same cistern systems since before the newest mountains began to surface. Their migrations are vertical: from cold deep water to warm machinery chambers, from drowned plazas to reservoirs held impossibly above the basin. They carry colonies of transparent droplet-life in folds around their mouths.

The plated animals live where water strikes hardest. Their shells form as interlocking polygons, dark at the center and luminous along the seams. They fasten themselves around pressure gates, spillways, and the mouths of pipes, feeding on organic sediment before it can obstruct the machinery. The city does not clean these systems; it cultivates them. Old shell masses become part of the plumbing, mineralized into valves and strainers. A gate’s legal identity may include both its manufactured frame and the generations grown over it.

Long-bodied wanderers cross the open plazas. Their ribs are hollow resonators, their limbs narrow and deliberate. No plants exist for them to graze, but the posture persists: heads lowered, mouths moving close to the ground. They drink the thin nutrient films spread across pavement and take trace metals from fresh extrusion. Wind enters openings along their flanks and sounds through their skeletons. Herds therefore hum as they walk, each animal producing a shifting chord. During feeding hours their music is nearly lost beneath the knocking and rasping at the channels. In the intervals it fills the lower basin.

Some creatures inhabit rain itself. They are transparent specks dispersed in individual droplets, dormant while airborne. When droplets meet on a wall, the specks gather, exchange matter, and briefly become larger colonial forms: branching films, pulsing nets, tiny chains that orient toward warmth. If the water separates, they divide again and continue downward in fragments. Every wet façade contains these momentary societies. A storm is both their migration and their reproductive season.

The upper towers are occupied by a citizen so vast and slow that observers disagree about its body. For sixty years it appears as countless soft, frost-bearing organisms packed into apertures, roosting vaults, and heated ridges. Then pressure, temperature, and some inherited timing align. The whole population loosens from the towers and moves east over a period of months. Whether the migrating mass is one creature, a culture, or a temporary union is not considered an urgent distinction. The law addresses it as one enduring presence. Districts close for its brooding. Towers alter their temperatures for its return.

There are deeper inhabitants as well.

In the cisterns, organic assemblers and mineral-feeding organisms have grown around ancient machinery until neither can be removed without killing the other. In the warm darkness beneath the foundations, colonies process carbon drawn from below the crust. Some may have begun as designed microbial systems. Others may be descendants of the first life to discover the city’s artificial food web. They manufacture the slurries that feed creatures above, and in doing so maintain both the city and the demand for its continuation.

The mountain-making structures may also be alive.

Their pace obscures the question. They act over centuries, raising seamless stone into the basin, transmitting pressure pulses through bedrock, and producing heat and mineral gradients that resemble metabolism when viewed across long enough intervals. The city’s instruments clasp them like medical apparatus—or husbandry equipment, or organs. No one knows whether the buried structures obey an old design, continue an autonomous growth, or respond to the accumulated weight and sound of the colonies above.

The rare human-shaped figures belong ambiguously to this ecology. They walk the high routes inside sealed coverings, seldom entering crowded districts. Some are maintenance bodies, remotely inhabited by intelligences distributed through reservoirs and towers. Some may contain human descendants adapted to regard themselves as custodial organs rather than citizens. Others are empty forms repeating duties whose origin has been forgotten. Animals do not flee them. Climbers cross their shoulders; floating flocks divide around them; rain-life gathers on their casings.

What truly lives here is not any single species.

It is the arrangement: mountain pressure, engineered darkness, circulating water, fabricated nourishment, architecture, and colony. Each sustains the others. The animals require the city’s heat and food, but the city’s purpose, law, and shape have become expressions of animal need. Towers exist because bodies roost in them. Bridges branch because swarms cross them. Reservoirs remain warm because something must breed there. Darkness is maintained because migration passes through it.

Even sound is inhabited. Calls travel through cavities prepared to carry them, taking minutes to cross the basin. A note issued below may be completed by a colony on the upper terraces long after the first singer has fallen silent. The answer belongs neither to one throat nor to one moment. It moves through stone, water, and millions of bodies as a shared act.

When new terrain arrives, every citizen stills. The climbers seal themselves to walls. The silver masses stop shivering. The white swimmers hang beneath the surfaces of their pools. Even the droplet colonies suspend their branching.

The concussion rises from below.

Then the city’s inhabitants answer together. Hollow ribs sound, shells vibrate, throats open, buoyant bodies pulse against the air. Towers and braces take up the resonance. Water trembles in its channels. The newborn ridge receives the sound before weather has touched it.

That answering multitude is the closest thing the city has to a name.
