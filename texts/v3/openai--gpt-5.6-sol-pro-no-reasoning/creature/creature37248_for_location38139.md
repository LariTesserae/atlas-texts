The people here live as careful islands.

They are tall only by accident of posture, made narrow by generations spent passing through rooms that change their proportions and along walkways that must not be weighted in groups. Their bodies retain the local dusk: skin with a muted translucence, veins showing as smoky green or violet threads. Across the throat, ribs, and inner arms lie fields of small reflective cells. These brighten, darken, and run in bands when the muscles beneath them tighten. What may once have been blush, flinch, or gesture has become language.

Their ears are protected from infancy. Soft organisms, pale as condensation, are placed along the outer cartilage and permitted to grow inward. They do not deafen. They separate sound into layers: animal, architectural, subterranean, atmospheric, remembered. An adult can stand in the city’s violence and attend to one seed turning on the pavement—or choose not to hear the tower calling with a dead relative’s voice.

They seldom speak aloud. Their mouths have therefore acquired an unsettling stillness, while their hands are ceaselessly precise. Conversation passes in bent fingers, exposed wrists, minute changes of balance, and colors traveling beneath the skin. Close companions use pressure: shoulder to shoulder, palm against spine, brow to brow. At greater distances they exchange packets of induced vertigo through small instruments worn behind the jaw. Meaning arrives as orientation.

*Home* is the sensation of having found level ground.

*Water* is a cool tilt to the left.

*Danger nearby* is a step that never lands.

*I remember you* feels like turning toward a warmth no longer present.

Their translucent mantles are neither clothing nor machine. They are colonial skins grown in shallow reservoirs beneath the dwellings. At rest, a mantle hangs like a film of wet glass. Under impact it hardens in concentric plates, converting sound into ripples of dim color. The loudest citizens walk surrounded by halos. Children, whose mantles have not learned them yet, flare brilliantly whenever the ground-note changes. Adults read those involuntary colors and hurry them indoors before the child knows to be afraid.

No one lives alone, but no one lives closely.

A household may occupy several hectares: one sleeping room, one water room, one sealed chamber for speech, and long empty corridors between them. Kinship is measured less by cohabitation than by synchronization. Families wake on the same vibration, maintain reservoirs on alternating days, and stand at separate windows during the amber openings. They may see one another only a few times each season. Still, their pulse-displays answer across courtyards, and their houses subtly lean together at night.

Names are not sounds. Each person has a sign-name, a balance-name, and a private pattern carried by the skin. The sign-name is used in work and trade. The balance-name is given by caretakers after the child first dreams of falling. The skin-name develops slowly and cannot be chosen. It is revealed in fear, desire, illness, or grief, which makes intimacy here partly an act of witnessing what the body cannot conceal.

The people keep their population small, though whether by custom or necessity is unclear. Births are infrequent and attended by the entire local complex from behind acoustic walls. The newborn is laid first against warm basalt, then beneath a sweating building, then beside a root. This is not a claim of ownership. It is an introduction to the three things believed to be awake.

Children learn the city through prohibitions before they learn its map. They are taught to test pavement with a weighted cord, to watch whether droplets agree about downward, to distinguish an animal silence from a hunting silence, and to drop cherished objects without hesitation. Their earliest games rehearse disasters. One child turns the room’s floor uncertain while the others release toys, flatten themselves, and display calm. Another mimics a remembered voice through the wall; the others must refuse to answer, even if the voice pleads.

Yet they are not joyless. Their humor is intensely physical and almost invisible to outsiders: a deliberately mistimed shift of weight, a formal greeting that resolves into the sensation of sitting on nonexistent furniture, a skin-flash hidden beneath ceremonial fabric. In the protected speech chambers they sometimes laugh aloud. The first sound is usually startling enough to stop the laughter; the second begins deliberately, defiantly, and soon the whole room is shaking.

Their occupations are forms of negotiation.

Water-tenders coax condensation from buildings without encouraging excessive growth in the channels. They stroke walls with combs of differing temperature, guiding droplets toward ceramic cisterns while seed-sized amphibians bellow from within the masonry. River-keepers monitor the standing blade during rain, not to control it but to mark which direction has become down. Moss-cutters harvest thin layers from the great organisms without provoking withdrawal across an entire district. Path-readers walk at dawn to discover which streets remain streets.

The most respected are the listeners who do not listen.

These specialists enter chambers sunk near the deep engines and allow selected portions of the ground-note to cancel inside their bodies. They return pale, bruised, and unable to recognize certain faces for several days. From the absences in what they heard, they forecast construction, ridge-rise, lamp migrations, and the arrival of rain columns. They insist they receive no messages. They study interruption, not voice. This distinction is foundational to the culture and not always believed.

There are also callers, though the title is spoken only inside sealed rooms. They study the forbidden frequencies to which the architecture responds. A caller can persuade a doorway to remain where it is, turn a balcony outward, or halt a sideways elevator between incompatible rooms. Their art is dangerous because every useful tone resembles, at its edge, an invitation. Many lose their ordinary voices. Some are eventually found standing before blank walls, silently moving their mouths while rooms assemble on the other side.

The city’s nonhuman inhabitants are not considered animals in any single category. The residents divide them according to the kind of agreement possible.

The blue-throated grazers are **those who can be redirected**. Colored screens guide their herds away from new construction, though old females occasionally ignore the signals and lead the others to places that become safe only later.

The six-legged browsers are **those who remember paths**. Their seasonal routes are treated as precedent older than law. A dwelling built across one will open itself or be opened.

The membrane scavengers are **those who borrow shape**. Children leave them lightweight frames, and after storms the open ground rolls with temporary wheels, stars, cages, and crude imitations of people.

The branch-runners are **those who wait beside light**. They are never fed intentionally, never killed near a lamp, and never counted.

The silence-hunter has no public name. Its glyph is an empty circle inscribed around whatever warning matters. Some believe it is a predator. Some say it is a mobile failure in the city’s attention. After it passes, the missing are mourned without sound, because grief itself might mark the survivors for its return.

The mites that gather beneath traveling brightness are treated as workers from another jurisdiction. Residents place tiny ceramic markers beside their assemblies and alter planting schedules according to the patterns they form. No one knows whether the color moving through leaves commands the mites or consults them. This uncertainty is considered courteous. The people distrust explanations that assign a single master.

They build very little themselves. Furniture, vessels, acoustic barriers, and hand tools are made locally, but buildings are accepted, adapted, or refused. A newly formed structure is watched for a full cycle before anyone enters. Its doors are counted. Its interior volume is compared against its exterior. Water is left at the threshold along with spores, a reflective plate, and a warm stone. If any offering moves against gravity, the structure is designated civic rather than habitable, regardless of its apparent purpose.

Some buildings choose inhabitants by growing warmer when particular people approach. Such invitations are treated with suspicion. The city is generous in ways that often become anatomical.

Those who accept may discover rooms calibrated exactly to their stride, walls that display forgotten skin-patterns, or chambers containing air from a childhood season. Over time the resident’s mantle may begin to share grain with the walls. Their footsteps can open distant doors. Their sleep alters the building’s condensation. Eventually it becomes difficult to say whether the person occupies the structure or serves as one of its temporary organs.

The people have no settled account of who made the city. They preserve several incompatible understandings without requiring one to defeat the others.

In one, the city is an engine intended to construct a world, and the inhabitants are contaminants it has learned to accommodate.

In another, it is a world-sized animal still forming its nervous system. Towers are sensory organs; roads are growing boundaries; rain columns carry hormones; the solitary lamps are points of thought.

A third teaches that the city is a harbor built before its sea arrived.

A fourth claims there was once a dense civilization here, but it spread itself so completely through stone, weather, roots, and machines that only spacing remains of its social order. Every grove and tower is a citizen. Every interval is a form of respect.

The last belief is not taught to children: that the city is practicing.

This is why the new districts make no obvious sense. The entrance-less stations, severed bridges, artificial nights, plazas for crowds that never gather—these are drafts. The city is attempting to remember or anticipate an inhabitant whose body, scale, and laws of motion it does not yet know.

The human communities endure by making themselves easy to revise. Their rooms are sparsely furnished. Their possessions fit into sealed baskets. Every citizen carries a release token: a smooth object meant to be dropped when upward rain begins, so the hand remembers that survival may require surrender. Nothing irreplaceable is given a handle.

Their dead are not buried. The young plain does not reliably keep graves, and cremation may attract the sail-backed flocks. Instead, bodies are placed in condensation chambers where the mantles dissolve first, then the soft tissues. Water drawn from the chamber is distributed among distant vine colonies. Bones are left inside unfinished buildings. Often they disappear into the walls. Sometimes, years later, a tower sounds with an overtone that surviving relatives recognize in their teeth.

This is not considered return.

Memory arriving through the ground is always refused.

At night, the inhabited complexes dim themselves while the buried city brightens. Families gather in separate rooms linked by liquid windows. They watch the underground lines ignite beneath roots and basalt, extending beyond every mapped district. Skin-signs pass from pane to pane. Children press their hands to the trembling glass. Elders monitor the inclination of hanging cords.

Then every building opens one door.

The residents do not approach. They secure their baskets, loosen their grips, and turn their bodies sideways to make themselves less like an answer.

Warm air crosses the plain, carrying the odor of wet stone before rain. Empty transit lines hum. Towers adjust their balconies. Herds stop between steps. Even the great colonies tighten through their roots.

In the low complexes, every human being feels the same impossible greeting placed gently into the sense of balance:

the sensation of a threshold leaning toward them.

They wait without replying.

They have survived here by understanding that not everything which makes room for you means you are meant to live inside it.
