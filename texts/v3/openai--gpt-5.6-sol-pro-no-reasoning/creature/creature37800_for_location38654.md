The basin is inhabited less by separate species than by arrangements of thirst, heat, and mineral exchange.

Its first citizens cannot be seen. They occupy the pores of stone and the bright interiors of springs, living wherever unlike substances meet: sulfur against oxygen, hot brine against cooling air, dissolved iron against porous carbonate. They make no green stain, no velvet film. A colony is known by a faint ambering of water, a blue cast on a submerged ledge, or the delicate husks it leaves sealed beneath transparent mineral. These invisible chemists are the basin’s pasture.

Under the ledges graze glass-backs, small many-footed animals with clear shells and amber-lit channels inside them. They scrape the newest deposits from stone, leaving clean tracks like writing. In cisterns, coin-sized sieves drift and unfold, their bodies opening into latticed flowers to strain minute life from the water. Pale runners patrol drainage cuts on four needle legs, moving in loose companies from crack to crack and lowering hinged mouths into evaporating brine.

The air has its own grazers. Membrane kites rise on the basin’s heat: enormous, nearly weightless bodies tensioned between jointed spars. They consume dust, spores, and the ballooning young of spring creatures. At noon they become almost invisible in the glare, betrayed only by cool shadows crossing the ground. At dawn they settle over factory roofs and condenser meshes to drink, and the machinery waits for them.

Predators imitate what the basin has in abundance. Some resemble pumice rafts and float motionless in hot pools. Others flatten into the exact shadows of pipes. The long hunters of the aqueducts read pressure through water and strike beneath an unbroken surface. Cistern keepers tolerate them—even maintain marked pools for them—because they keep the glass-backs from choking municipal intakes. Here, husbandry often means making room for something dangerous.

Humans live here too, though the city conceals them. Most dwell below the terraces, gathered around cool shafts and chambers where breath briefly clouds. Aboveground they appear singly: reflective mantles moving across vacant plazas, dark faceplates turned against the white sky. They work among machines designed to waste neither pressure nor sound. Their doors exhale; their freight platforms glide; their pumps announce themselves only through the soles.

They are ceramicists, brine engineers, cistern keepers, thermal surveyors, membrane tenders, animal stewards, and readers of black-glass records. Their civilization has no inherited intimacy with timber, cloth, paper, or oil. They sit on fired stone, pass through doors balanced on mineral bearings, shelter beneath mica plates, and weave with metal. Even their idea of softness must come from water, cooled air, articulated surfaces, and the yielding tension of membranes.

They do not rule the basin by imposing order on it. They negotiate. Gates open for migrations. Spillways are roughened for eggs and clinging feet. Waste heat warms breeding chambers. Factories pause while kites descend. Streets rise as travertine engulfs their lower courses. A dangerous pool receives no fortress wall, only a curb and the respectful bend of every nearby route.

At the city’s center lives—or perhaps occurs—the one inhabitant no taxonomy contains: the still reservoir. It neither falls nor evaporates. Its water supplies half the city without diminishing. Creatures placed within it return through remote springs carrying minerals from nowhere known. Flyers refuse the air above it. The oldest channels appear to climb toward it, as though water remembers a direction other than downhill.

The people leave it open.

That may be their deepest custom. They mark peril not with barriers but with accommodation, acknowledging that some presences become more dangerous when challenged or named too confidently. Their city’s true architecture is therefore made of allowances: hours of silence, unused widths of street, empty shelves between districts, marked basins, raised steps, and the low unobtrusive curb around an impossible abundance.

When evening condensation begins, all these inhabitants emerge into relation. Invisible colonies quicken in the channels. Glass-backs graze. Runners unfold from cracks. Kites descend through manufactured rain. Aqueduct hunters loosen from stone. People adjust gates and retreat beneath cooling terraces while silent systems pulse underfoot.

Nothing here is rooted. Life does not rise from soil or seek the shape of a tree. It clings, drifts, precipitates, filters, condenses, circulates, and returns.

The basin is inhabited by things that have learned not to grow, but to follow.
