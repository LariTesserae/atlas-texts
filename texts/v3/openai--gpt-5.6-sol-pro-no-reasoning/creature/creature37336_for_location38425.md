What lives there is difficult to separate from what continues.

The distinction may once have been obvious. There were custodians, presumably: builders, machinists, quarry crews, clerks who recorded each transfer of heat and mass, and residents who occupied the sealed blocks around the central mound. But the city has worn away their outlines more thoroughly than it has worn its own. No bones remain. No names persist except as labels for pressure tolerances, rail gradients, kiln intervals. If the inhabitants made images of themselves, those images have either been returned to raw material or altered until they resemble diagrams of machinery.

It is possible that they left.

It is also possible that leaving was the one operation the city could not balance.

The most conspicuous living things now are the wire animals, though even they seem less like creatures than loose parts of a system whose casing has become the whole plateau. A mature specimen stands no higher than a worker’s knee. Its limbs are mismatched in length and number; some have six, others seven, and one set of tracks suggests an animal with nine. The joints are simple loops nested inside loops, polished on their inner surfaces by immense repetition. Each limb ends in a narrow hooked foot that settles naturally into cracks, rail grooves, drainage channels, and the seams between paving slabs.

Their bodies are ceramic nodules the color of old teeth. Hairline fissures cover these cores, sometimes packed with yellow lubricant crystals. Fine wires enter the cracks and emerge elsewhere, so that an animal seen against furnace light resembles a bundle of roots carrying a stone.

They move with grave precision. A forelimb is placed, tested, withdrawn, and placed again. The body advances only when every contact is secure. Crossing a plaza may take months. During the long intervals between steps they are indistinguishable from debris, and survey teams of the former city occasionally marked dormant animals as fixed features. Certain old maps identify them as bollards, valve housings, or unexplained deposits of scrap.

They do not appear to perceive by light. When a lamp is restored along their route, they halt at the edge of its radiance and remain there until it fails, even if this requires decades. Electrical gradients attract them. So do minute strains in buried metal. Before a rail buckles, wire animals gather along it and touch it at intervals, each extending one limb to the steel. Whether they predict the deformation, cause it, or merely participate in it is unknown.

Sometimes they repair things.

The repairs are not improvements. A broken conductor may be bridged by one of their own limbs, detached without ceremony. A fractured insulator may be held in alignment by an animal standing motionless beneath it. When the surrounding mechanism eventually returns to tolerance, the animal retrieves what it can and moves away. Those that cannot free themselves remain incorporated. Many substations contain ceramic cores mortared into switchgear, their remaining limbs still tightening and releasing in cycles too slight to see.

Sometimes, instead, they assist a failure. They gather around a functioning transformer, touch it, and wait until it burns out. Then they disperse just before another transformer elsewhere resumes operation. Their behavior makes sense only across distances and spans of time that no observer can hold together.

They may be maintenance organisms.

They may be the surviving citizens.

Or they may be the city’s smallest accounting marks, walking from one side of an equation to the other.

The silicate colonies in the conduits are even less decisive. They inhabit pipes that carry warm exhaust, especially where sulfur salts accumulate around slow bends. Each colony is made of translucent wafers thinner than paper and fitted edge to edge in overlapping fans. When current passes nearby, the wafers incline toward it. When a section is removed, neighboring plates grow along the exposed boundary until the old contour is restored.

No individual wafer does much. It thickens. It clouds. It splits along a line of internal stress. Yet entire colonies migrate against the direction of gas flow, requiring centuries to cross a valve chamber. Two colonies meeting in a pipe may stop, interlock, and remain joined. Afterward their splitting rhythms synchronize, even when long lengths of conduit separate them.

In some sealed mains, they have arranged themselves into repeated branching figures identical to the warning glyph for water.

This could be coincidence. The city produces coincidence with industrial regularity.

The colonies respond most strongly to the eighty-one-hour pressure pulse. Moments before the eastern gate opens, every wafer turns edge-on, presenting its smallest surface to the coming gas. The adjustment passes through the pipe network faster than any known mechanical signal. Once the pulse has gone, they relax. A faint ticking follows as millions of new fractures propagate through them.

If this is metabolism, the pulse is their season.

If this is thought, it is thought performed as stress management.

There are other presences, but they are known chiefly by their effects.

Something occupies the lowest rail tunnels. It never comes into the lamps. Maintenance records describe sections of track polished along both outer faces, as though a soft, immense body passed between the rails without touching the ground. Pressure gauges fall as it approaches and rise after it has gone. Wire animals flatten themselves into cable trenches. The silicate wafers close.

The thing leaves no residue. It removes none either. Scratches counted before and after its passage are identical, although their positions may change.

In older records it is called a transit.

Not a passenger. Not a vehicle. Simply a transit, as though the passage itself had acquired anatomy.

There are also the inhabitants of rooms.

Most residential cells are empty in the straightforward sense. Their recesses contain nothing; their ceiling loops hang without load. But the temperature inside some rooms differs from the corridor by a fraction of a degree. The difference moves. It begins in the floor recess, rises gradually to the height of the loop, remains there, then returns to the floor. One cycle takes roughly the length of a former work shift.

Dust is too scarce to show footprints. Sensitive instruments record a displacement comparable to a body crossing the room, but no mass enters or leaves. When several adjacent cells are observed, the warm displacements seem to keep separate schedules. In one block they gather along the sealed windows at the hour when the western furnaces brighten. In another they cluster near the doors during the deep subterranean tone, then retreat.

These may be residual patterns in the building’s heat.

They may be occupants reduced to routine.

The city allows passage. It does not guarantee that what passes will retain a body.

The shaft at the center has its own population, if returned objects can be called inhabitants. Many have circulated so long that origin is impossible to establish. A bent iron tag emerges, is catalogued, corrodes for eleven years, and is dropped according to an instruction engraved on its reverse. Centuries later it returns unbent, bearing the same instruction in a different script. A ceramic gear may arrive warm and unworn, operate in a kiln for six thousand revolutions, break, and descend into the shaft as fragments. The fragments later appear in separate factories, where they are ground into compounds used to manufacture the original gear.

Some objects seem aware of their obligations. Latches open before the mechanism reaches them. Carts roll a few millimetres uphill to receive a block. Empty collection trays flex under an expected weight hours before anything appears.

The custodians may have learned to read these small anticipations. Perhaps this was their principal skill: not invention, but obedience to events already partly accomplished. They would have lived among arrivals that had causes in the future and departures whose consequences were ancient. Their records could not have distinguished prediction from memory.

A city like that would produce a peculiar citizen.

Patient. Exact. Suspicious of novelty. Careful never to mistake possession for temporary custody.

One can imagine them sleeping in the floor recesses, tethered by the ceiling loops against some pressure condition that no longer occurs. One can imagine their bodies made for heat and dryness: layered mineral skins, lungs that sifted useful salts from gas, joints lubricated with crystalline sulfur. Perhaps they drank electrical charge from wall contacts. Perhaps their young began as soft ceramic forms cured gradually beside domestic furnaces.

But the surviving rooms do not confirm any body. The recesses fit a curled animal, a machine casing, a ritual vessel, or nothing at all. The loops could restrain, suspend, ground, measure, or complete a circuit. Every interpretation explains one feature and makes the others stranger.

Only the rule is clear:

**NOTHING MAY BE ADDED.  
NOTHING MAY BE REMOVED.  
PASSAGE IS PERMITTED.**

The remaining life obeys it.

The wire animals borrow current and return failure. The wafers borrow exhaust and return crystal. The unseen transit borrows the shape of the tunnels. The warm room-forms borrow the dimensions of vanished residents. Even the distant signals bring no message, only energy that the factories later compensate for with minute movements.

Nothing here eats in the usual sense. Eating would create an inside and an outside, a possession and a loss. Instead, things participate. They redirect. They delay. They hold materials briefly in altered arrangements and then surrender them.

Death, likewise, is hard to identify. A wire animal may stop for centuries and then resume its crossing. A silicate colony crushed in a valve appears later as a matching growth upstream. Machines dismantle themselves slowly enough to be mistaken for erosion, while disconnected components elsewhere edge toward assembly.

Perhaps the city contains no living organisms.

Perhaps the city has eliminated the difference between organism and environment so completely that every surviving process is one organ of the same being. The factories are its digestion without consumption. The roads are nerves along which almost nothing travels. The pressure pulse is a heartbeat separated from any blood. The central shaft is memory, womb, mouth, and wound, though it may be none of these at the same time.

If the city itself lives, it does not seek growth. Growth would violate its law. It does not reproduce, unless the repeated factories are copies of some older internal form. It does not adapt, except by bending every disruption back toward what was already present.

Its impulse is not survival as other creatures understand it.

It survives because change cannot finish happening there.

And within that vast, arrested life, the little lives continue: the ceramic-centered walkers poised over seams, the glassy colonies turning before the pulse, the warmth rising obediently from empty beds, the transit descending through unlit rails. They inhabit the city as thoughts inhabit an old mind—not freely, not separately, but recurrently, each returning by obscure routes to a place prepared for it.

Nothing stays gone.

Nothing entirely arrives.
