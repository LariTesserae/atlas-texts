The forest is inhabited less by separate creatures than by arrangements of appetite.

Closest to the ground, the inhabitants are small, numerous, and almost indistinguishable from the substances they consume. Mites move among grains of soil. Springtails gather on films of moisture. Beetle larvae tunnel through wood already softened by fungi; termites enter harder timber and hollow it from within, leaving a convincing shell around their galleries. Millipedes and roaches reduce leaves to fragments. Worms draw those fragments downward. Bacteria and fungal threads continue the work beyond sight, inhabiting root tips, dung, carcasses, standing trees, and the warm mud beneath shallow water. What appears to be ground is a crowded digestive layer.

Ants give this layer its visible organization. Their trails run over roots and under litter, divide at trunks, vanish into holes, and emerge elsewhere as if the forest possessed a nervous system near its surface. Some ants harvest seeds, some cultivate fungus, some tend sap-feeding insects, and some move as predatory fronts that drive other animals ahead of them. Colonies occupy rotten logs, hollow stems, chambers in the soil, and folded leaves overhead. No individual understands the territory, yet together they build roads, ventilate nurseries, move food, dispose of dead nestmates, and conduct wars along boundaries too subtle for larger animals to notice.

Above them live the specialists of bark and leaf. Lizards flatten themselves against trunks, becoming patches of lichen until an insect approaches. Tree frogs seal their bodies into damp crevices by day and emerge after rain, some small enough to breed in water held by a single plant. Snakes imitate vines, roots, or fallen twigs. Stick insects resemble the stems they eat; leaf insects reproduce not merely a leaf’s shape but its stains, damage, and irregular edges. Caterpillars hide under foliage beside the empty outlines of earlier meals. Moths wait with their wings closed, and spiders occupy the routes between surfaces, laying their geometry wherever flying prey is likely to pass.

Many residents announce themselves chemically rather than visually. Flowers issue odors at particular hours for particular pollinators. Female insects release minute signals that males can find through the obstructed air. Mammals leave musk on trunks, urine at crossings, dung along paths, and glandular traces at boundaries. Fungi advertise decay. Fruit broadcasts ripeness. A carcass begins summoning its dismantlers almost immediately. Much of the forest’s communication passes through the nose, antenna, tongue, or root.

The plant inhabitants seem stationary only because their movements occur on another scale. Seedlings live for years in suspended hunger beneath the canopy, holding a few leaves toward the weak light. Saplings lean through improbable angles to occupy narrow openings. Lianas rise by borrowing trunks, then spread into crowns and bind distant trees together. Strangling plants begin overhead, sending roots down around their hosts. Palms place armored leaves where browsing mouths will meet them. Thorny climbers hook fur and feathers, turning animal passage into transport.

Each plant is inhabited in turn. Roots contain fungal partners and microscopic grazers. Bark shelters eggs and larvae. Leaves carry films of bacteria, algae, spores, and tiny predators. Pools in leaf axils support complete communities: mosquitoes, worms, crustaceans, frogs, and the organisms that feed on their waste. Epiphytes gather soil without touching the ground. A mature tree is therefore not one inhabitant but a vertical province, with different populations at its base, trunk, branches, crown, flowers, fruits, wounds, hollows, and dead limbs.

The larger animals travel through these provinces without fully belonging to any one of them. Pigs nose through litter and churn small territories of soil. Tapirs follow old routes between feeding grounds, salt, and water, pressing passages that may persist for generations. Browsers crop the shaded growth and disperse seeds in dung. Cats use the same paths more quietly, reading scent, disturbed leaves, and the behavior of prey. Some sleep in root cavities or dense vegetation; others rest on dry rises barely distinguishable from the surrounding plain.

Monkeys inhabit a different map. To them, trunks are roads and gaps are hazards. Their routes join fruiting crowns, sleeping trees, water, and refuge. One tree coming into fruit can alter the movements of several troops and draw birds and mammals from far beyond sight. Seeds fall from feeding hands and mouths. Dropped pulp feeds animals below. Alarm calls pass through the middle canopy, sometimes revealing a predator that remains invisible from the ground.

Birds divide the air and foliage with equal precision. Heavy terrestrial birds pace beneath shrubs, turning litter or swallowing fallen fruit. Insect hunters wait on low perches and make brief sorties into dim openings. Others search bark, follow ant swarms, probe flowers, or remain in crowns where sunlight supports leaves unlike those below. Their calls travel farther than their bodies can be seen, so that many exist to an observer chiefly as notes: a whistle descending through damp air, a wooden knock, a sudden harsh cry, wingbeats displaced from their source.

The water has its own hidden population. Fish hold beneath undercut banks and submerged wood. During floods, some move outward among trunks, feeding on fruit, seeds, insects, and detritus in places that will later become dry paths. Amphibians breed in temporary pools before those pools contract. Turtles and crocodilians may resemble exposed roots or patches of mud. Heavy mammals rise to breathe with little more than a widening circle on the surface, then return to opaque channels where their size becomes difficult to believe.

Flood and drought do not merely alter the inhabitants’ surroundings; they rearrange who can live beside whom. A depression may be a fish habitat in one month, a frog nursery in another, and a rooting place for pigs later. Aquatic insects emerge into the air and become food for birds and bats. Terrestrial fruits fall into water and feed fish. When channels withdraw, stranded life concentrates in residual pools, drawing predators to temporary abundance. Boundaries between forest and river are repeatedly made and erased.

Bats take possession of the dark above the understory. Some glean insects from leaves; some drink nectar; some carry fruits away from parent trees; some hunt over water. Their roosts occupy hollows, foliage, and concealed cavities. They move through spaces too narrow or cluttered for most birds, mapping them through returning sound. Their nightly work is one of the forest’s chief means of pollination and dispersal, though from below it may register only as a flicker crossing a pale opening.

Every inhabitant is also inhabited. Mammalian guts ferment leaves and fruit with the help of microbes. Insects carry smaller organisms internally and externally. Parasites live in blood, muscle, skin, intestine, lung, and brain. Wasps place their young inside other animals. Fungi enter insects and redirect the final acts of their bodies. Seeds survive digestion and are deposited with fertilizer. Predators contain parasites; parasites contain microbes; dead microbes become nutrients. The apparent individual is often a moving consortium.

Death does not produce vacancy. Flies arrive first or seem to. Beetles, ants, wasps, fungi, and bacteria follow gradients of odor. Scavengers open the body; smaller organisms enlarge the openings; fluids enter the soil; minerals pass into roots. Bone is gnawed, weathered, buried, or dissolved. Even a great fallen tree becomes occupied before it reaches the ground: vines bridge from it, insects enter its wounds, fungi spread through its tissues, seedlings root in trapped debris. Its fall crushes some lives, releases others into light, and creates a corridor of accelerated growth.

The most consequential resident may be the gap itself—not a creature, but a brief condition inhabited opportunistically. When a crown falls, dormant seeds germinate, established seedlings surge upward, insects gather in the warmth, and flowering plants exploit the new visibility. Browsers arrive for tender growth. Predators follow them. Vines cross the opening quickly. Young trees race vertically, sacrificing strength for access to light. Eventually one or several crowns occupy the space, shade returns, and the community of brightness yields again to the community of endurance.

No inhabitant governs this place. Even the largest animals modify only portions of it, and the largest trees ultimately become substrate. Order emerges from countless negotiations over light, water, nitrogen, shelter, transport, flesh, and time. Cooperation and predation often occur in the same relationship: a fruit feeds the animal that destroys its pulp but carries its seed; a fungus extends a root’s reach while drawing sugars from it; an insect pollinates a flower while consuming part of its reproductive effort.

What lives here, then, is not merely an abundance of species. It is a dense system of exchanges wearing the temporary forms of trees, cats, ants, fish, fungi, birds, and microbes. Its inhabitants build one another’s conditions, occupy one another’s bodies, consume one another’s surpluses, and inherit one another’s remains. Each life makes a narrow claim, but almost none can keep what it claims.

Seen from nearby, they are vivid particulars: a wet eye above black water, a beetle’s polished shell, a monkey’s hand around a branch, a fungal fan on a log. Seen together, they become the forest’s distributed machinery—quiet, warm, crowded, and almost impossible to separate from the place that they continuously manufacture.
