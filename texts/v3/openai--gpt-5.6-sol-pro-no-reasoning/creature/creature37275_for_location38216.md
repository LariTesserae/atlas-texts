The things that live there are not plants, although plant is the nearest shape available to them.

Each visible crown is only the exposed portion of a broader event. The fronds, paddles, columns, and folded globes are terminals: places where subterranean pressures briefly acquire form. What appears to be an individual begins beneath the flawless plates as a shallow web of conductive roots, but the web does not end at its own perimeter. Its finest branches fade into pores, thermal gradients, and minute discrepancies in distance. One organism may be rooted partly in the basin as it is now and partly in the basin a century hence.

This is why they stand so far apart. They do not compete for ground. They compete for certainty.

The oldest are the black columns feathered in scarlet. Their surfaces have the dull luster of quenched metal, yet they bend by degrees toward changes in pressure. The red filaments comb static from the air. When wind passes, their dry hissing is not incidental: the filaments are measuring the fissure, reading its width and the minute disagreement between its two sides. Deep within each column are concentric lattices holding a record of every contraction the basin has undergone. Split one open and there would be no rings, only nested geometries, each describing a slightly different mountain.

The broad copper-green forms are heat gatherers. They spread themselves where warmth rises least evenly, making a level gradient out of turbulence below. Their foil-like fronds are built from countless hinged scales. During the basin’s electrical discharge, every scale closes edgewise, passing its stored charge into the shallow root network. Their apparent lushness is a radiator’s extravagance: more surface, more exchange, more opportunity to turn heat into structure.

The translucent paddles are reservoirs, though they store no fluid. Their central cavities hold absence under tension. Through the day, the paddles draw minute quantities of disorder from nearby mineral, leaving the surrounding plates smoother and more perfectly fitted. At night—or during whatever interval serves as night when time loosens—they release that disorder downward in carefully portioned bursts. Somewhere below, a wheel turns. Somewhere at the basin’s edge, a seed acquires another facet.

The soft needle mats are younger and more responsive. They contract from touch because touch introduces history. Pressure, warmth, oils, and intention all enter their lattices as contamination. For years afterward, a touched mat may reproduce the outline of the hand in successively smaller contractions, refining the event until it can be stored as a simple pulse. Nothing here forgets by decay. It forgets by compression.

The pale folded globes are the nearest things to flowers, but each is also a calculation. Their hundreds of surfaces unfold to sample different possible conditions at once. Most possibilities are rejected and close again. One remains open long enough to harden into a seed: a small, immaculate solid whose angles do not quite total what they should. It rises because the basin’s gravity does not fully recognize it.

These seeds are not offspring in the familiar sense. They contain no miniature body and no hereditary script. They carry a set of tolerances: how much heat may pass before form is required, how much space may differ from itself, how long a thing can remain possible before becoming actual. Those that lodge beneath stone wait for the mountain to grow around them. Those that vanish upward may not be departing. They may be entering the neighboring version of the basin from which the fissure is seen edge-on.

There are inhabitants less easily mistaken for vegetation.

They occur in the intervals between discharges, when the blue line has passed from root to root and all the leaves have turned edgewise. For several seconds the basin becomes almost two-dimensional. In that condition, thin upright distortions can be seen crossing the open ground. They resemble heat shimmer constrained to narrow, deliberate bodies. They pause beside individual plants, lean without joints, and continue toward the central circle.

Whether these are creatures is uncertain. They leave no tracks, but afterward a plant may alter its orientation by a fraction of a degree, or open a surface it has kept folded for centuries. Sometimes several distortions gather around a wavering specimen whose roots have entered nonexistent stone. They remain until it either becomes definite again or thins into daylight. Their behavior resembles tending. It might equally be calibration.

The shadows may be another life stage.

At noon, when they loosen from their crowns, they move with preferences not explained by the sun. A shadow will avoid the newest plants and linger beneath the oldest. It may stretch a narrow branch toward a drift of hexagonal flakes, causing every flake to rotate at once. When two shadows overlap and the machinery below becomes visible, the view is not necessarily through the ground. It may be through them.

In those moments the apparent belts, pistons, wheels, gantries, and furnaces seem less like machines than organs perceived with the wrong scale of attention. Their wear may be age, or it may be texture: the marks left by cycles completed before stone existed to count them. The basin’s vegetation could be their surface growth, sensory tissue spread into daylight. Or the engines could be the plants’ collective interior, assembled downward from every charge, every folded leaf, every flake aligned north.

There are no animals because the functions performed by animals have not yet separated into bodies.

Movement belongs to shadows. Pollination belongs to pressure. Consumption belongs to the dark solvent passing through stone. Memory belongs to the black columns and the needle mats. Decomposition belongs to geometry. Selection occurs when two organisms demand the same patch of reality and one loses coherence. Even soil is not yet a habitat but a future substance under manufacture.

The living system is therefore both sparse and continuous. Each crown appears solitary, yet during a discharge the roots reveal their unity. The blue line does more than carry charge. It reconciles the basin. For a few seconds every organism agrees on the same depth, the same direction of gravity, the same present moment. Leaves turn edge-on because surface becomes unnecessary; individuality is suspended. What passes beneath them may be nourishment, instruction, or the brief reassembly of a single distributed being.

The bare center is where that being is least visible and most concentrated.

Nothing grows there because the circle is not empty ground. It is an aperture maintained in an unfinished state. The immense peripheral furnaces glimpsed there are engaged in repeatedly defining its boundary. Each cycle stabilizes one additional possibility, and that possibility appears at the basin’s edge as a plant that seems always to have existed. The outward-facing crowns are not avoiding the center. They are its emissions, oriented away like rays from a source.

If the place has an inhabitant in the singular, it is this process: a life too early for anatomy, using mineral and machinery as provisional metaphors. It has no mouth because energy is everywhere. It has no blood because nothing here needs transport in liquid. It has no fixed body because the basin itself is still negotiating volume. Its metabolism is the mountain’s rise. Its respiration is the pause in vibration when gravity weakens. Its reproduction may be the upward migration of impossible seeds—or the eventual duplication of the entire hidden chamber at another unnamed altitude.

The separate plants are its experiments in becoming local.

One tries breadth. One tries height. One tries storage, sensitivity, branching, repetition, color. Some persist for millennia. Others blur and are withdrawn. Their dead tissues accumulate as hexagonal flakes, the first material in the basin that is truly leftover rather than actively required. From that residue, soil will eventually form. With soil may come organisms that can afford to be smaller, faster, wetter, and less exact.

But for now, what lives there must participate in making the conditions of its own existence.

It must build the ground that supports it, maintain the fissure that conceals it, regulate the heat that feeds it, and continually persuade space to remain wide enough. It lives without water, decays without rot, communicates without sound, and grows without passing cleanly through time.

The basin’s inhabitants are not survivors of the newborn mountain.

They are the means by which it is being born.
