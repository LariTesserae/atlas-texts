The city itself is the principal inhabitant.

It is not alive in the manner of an animal, nor merely active in the manner of geology. Its life is architectural: vapor is its breath, faults its buried vessels, and the pale mineral districts are a body continually producing finer versions of its own enclosure. It does not spread across the waste. Instead, it turns inward, digesting vacancies into rooms, passages, ribs, and walls. Anything that dies within it becomes instruction. A stalk teaches it the shape of a buttress; a spore redirects a window; a discarded sheath is remembered as ornament. The city feeds less on matter than on form.

Its smaller inhabitants appear to participate in this metabolism.

The black fans are the city’s thinnest and most patient life. Fixed to cold seams, they open like scraps of charred metal and draw sustenance from the passage between mineral and vapor. Their days are measured by a slow curling of their edges. A colony may occupy no more space than a handprint, yet persist through the raising and sealing of whole nearby streets. Eventually it is grazed into perfect circles, buried beneath glaze, or incorporated as a dark line inside a new wall.

The spindle groves are sparse congregations of ivory stalks. They root where the mineral is youngest, as if freshness matters more than soil. Their translucent tips swell with each subterranean exhalation, turning courts into assemblies of dim, unlit lanterns. They cannot rely on wind, so they reproduce by violence: a bladder abruptly contracts and fires one heavy grain into the city. The seed’s tiny impact is both dispersal and announcement. Some grains find fissures and grow. Others are entombed, leaving their shapes to influence whatever the wall becomes next.

Among the cornices live pale, many-jointed grazers. Broad and flat, they imitate chips of newly formed masonry so closely that only feeding reveals them. They lower threadlike mouths onto the black fans and remove exact discs of tissue, as though punching holes in paper. They do not run when danger approaches. They release themselves. Buoyed by the heavy vapor, they fall with ceremonial slowness, rotating through the dark of an arch before alighting on another ledge. Their defense depends on becoming, for a few moments, indistinguishable from drifting debris in a place where almost nothing drifts.

Their hunter is less easily described because it seems to live between appearances. Its body is inferred from paired incisions in soft tiles and from transparent cast-off tubes filled with folded joints. It travels under cover of thresholds, vents, and sealed apertures, perhaps entering passages before they open and leaving after they close. It may be long enough to bridge an alley, yet narrow enough to inhabit a crack. Whether it hides from the open court or obeys it is unclear. When the central paving begins to pulse, the hunter becomes wholly still.

The open court may contain the city’s deepest inhabitant—or may itself be that inhabitant’s exposed surface.

Beneath its dark pavement, something immense enough to organize the entire basin lifts and settles by a finger’s breadth. The patterns crossing it are not random contractions. They resemble plans: spirals, branches, districts viewed from an impossible distance. Around the circle, the city continuously buds from rings of fine mineral teeth, as though the hidden thing were extruding interpretations of those plans into the world. Yet it preserves the central emptiness with absolute care. No vapor crosses it. No organism colonizes it. Even the surrounding towers recoil.

Perhaps the court is an eye that does not require light, and the city is the elaborate socket growing around it. Perhaps it is a mouth held shut, with every avenue and chamber serving as an organ of anticipation. Perhaps what appears empty is occupied by something whose substance cannot coexist with vapor, life, or stone.

Whatever lies there, all other inhabitants respond to its cycles. The fans spread. The spindle bladders fill. The grazers flatten themselves into cornices. The unseen hunter withdraws. Then thousands of apertures open, and the city enters its silent frenzy of construction.

Thus nothing here lives entirely by itself. The organisms feed on the city’s gradients and exhalations; the city adopts their bodies and accidents; the central presence governs both without emerging. Together they form a single sparse ecology devoted not to filling the basin, but to elaborating its enclosure.

The salt waste holds nothing.

The city holds absence—and lives by giving that absence ever more precise walls.
