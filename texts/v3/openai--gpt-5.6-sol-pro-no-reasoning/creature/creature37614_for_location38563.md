The beings that live in the high ocean do not reckon themselves by age.

Age would be useless there. Nothing persists by passing from one condition into another; it persists by completing its circuit. A creature is not young because it is small, nor old because it bears scars. Smallness and scarring are stations, inhabited as permanently as the upper waters or the root terraces. One animal hatches forever. Another is forever being eaten. A third carries eggs that are always on the verge of opening. Taken separately, each life appears interrupted. Taken together, they form a complete animal stretched across distance.

The most numerous inhabitants are almost invisible.

They live in the filtering veils and on the translucent hairs of submerged leaves: glassy specks, ciliated threads, soft mineral stars. Some consume the green dust released where grazers crop the foliage. Some gather salts precipitated by the sorting membranes. Some wake only during the passage of an electrical discharge, flare blue for an instant, and fold back into clear capsules. Their swarms give the fixed sunbeams their grain. Every mote that seems to sink is swimming, feeding, dividing, or being digested, but the pattern of motes remains exact.

The leaf-grazers are long and low, their bodies shaped to lie against surfaces broader than countries. They have many gripping fins arranged along their bellies and blunt mouths reinforced with self-sharpening plates. Their hides are green-brown, mottled like damaged foliage, and furrowed by pale seams where parasites dwell. They travel in straight, dark lanes. In front of them, the leaf is unbroken. Beneath them, it is being consumed. Behind them, new tissue seals into place, complete with old punctures, mineral stains, and the imprints of former bites.

They never encounter the beginning or end of a leaf.

Around the electrocyte fronds live the silver shoals. Each animal is narrow as a blade and polished enough to return the flash that stuns its food. Before a discharge, the shoal gathers into branching figures resembling veins. When the pulse comes, microbes stiffen throughout the water and the silver bodies break formation. They feed in the brief electrical silence, then reassemble before the next pulse. Individuals vanish into mouths or pump channels, but the shoal retains the same number, the same outline, even the same small gaps where no animal swims.

They possess no leader. The empty places lead them.

Soft-winged animals inhabit the vascular mouths. Their bodies are pale, boneless, and pleated, with broad fins that close around them like folded cloth. They drift toward the pumping chambers, spread themselves at the lip, and are seized by the inward pressure. For a time they pass through darkness: conduits hot with friction, salt-separating galleries, chambers where woody valves strike shut behind them. Their skins absorb trace metals and shed colonies of microorganisms useful to the plant. Eventually they are expelled into distant open water, wings spread and unharmed.

To an observer, this resembles migration. To the animals, it may be the nearest thing to a landscape: not places joined by travel, but a recurring sequence of pressures inside the body of the world.

The great filter feeders occupy the widest chambers.

They are almost entirely transparent. Their flesh has the faint opacity of old ice, and through it can be seen a branching blue circulation, slow organs turning, and chambers filled with captured plankton. Some are longer than trunks are broad. Their mouths are circular curtains held permanently open. Their tails taper for kilometers but never strike the water. Currents carry them around circuits so extensive that different portions of a single route pass through daylight, blue obscurity, pollen clouds, and the thunderous vibration near the rim.

Small animals live inside them. Shoals shelter between their ribs. Pale scavengers walk along their circulatory nets, removing clots that are always forming. Entire gardens of algae grow in the clear outer tissues and are cropped by creatures no larger than spores. A filter feeder is therefore not one inhabitant but a moving chamber of inhabitants, though its movement returns it perpetually to the same water.

Predators are fewer and darker.

The ribbon hunters span current passes where trunks and root arches constrict the flow. Their bodies are black on the upper surface and faintly reflective below. They have no need to chase. Each braces its tail around a rib of bark and lets the rest of itself hang in the stream, jaw unfastened into a narrow gate. When the silver shoals arrive, the hunter flexes once. Water folds around it. Several animals disappear.

Its digestion lasts exactly until the shoal returns.

Other predators resemble wounds in the vegetation. They flatten themselves against bark, matching its ribs and translucent leaves, and open only when a grazer passes. Some live inside the flowers, hidden among gold-heavy anthers. Their mouths mimic passages between petals. Pollinators enter, pollen erupts, and the petals briefly close. Yet the population of pollinators neither dwindles nor learns. Knowledge, like flesh, is restored to its prior distribution.

The pollinators themselves are dense, powerful things, clothed in vibrating filaments. They swim rather than fly, forcing their way through the giant blossoms with slow strokes of six paddle limbs. Their droning is felt first in the teeth and bones. Pollen cakes their faces and packs the grooves of their limbs, but each golden burden remains constant no matter how many flowers they enter. Certain small organisms live entirely in that pollen: eyeless burrowers passing through a yellow atmosphere, never touching water directly.

On the root terraces stand the shelled colonies.

From a distance they resemble white or rust-red undergrowth. Each shell contains many mouths connected to one contractile base. At the arrival of the great pressure pulse, forests of jointed feeding limbs rise together. They strain fragments from the rushing water, display fans striped with sensory color, and seize the minute inhabitants dislodged from the roots. As the pulse passes, every limb folds away. The terraces become bare stone again, though there is no stone anywhere.

Some colonies are continually being buried by living root fiber. Others are continually emerging. Neither completes the process.

The split trunk supports creatures found nowhere else. Sap drinkers cluster along the amber wound, their bodies swollen and dark. Threadlike scavengers hang from the loose fibers. Broad-finned animals remain caught in the act of fleeing, swimming steadily against a disturbance that never overtakes them. Within the sap cloud live opaque microorganisms that would poison the clear water if they escaped. Filtering veils surround the wound and capture them at exactly the rate they are produced.

The wound is an ecosystem of alarm.

Every animal there responds as though catastrophe has just occurred. Chemical warnings flood their tissues. Defensive spines remain raised. Broods are held back from hatching. Muscles strain toward safety. Yet generations—if generations can be said to exist—inhabit that single instant without exhaustion. Fear is not an episode there but a habitat.

Near the surface live creatures built for two media.

They rest in pores among the floating blades, breathing water through folded throat chambers. When the wind presses the canopy into valleys, they rise into the air on wet, narrow wings. Their calls are sharp enough to carry above rain. They skim the upper ocean, take tiny animals from the leaf edges, and plunge through the same openings from which they emerged. Beneath the surface their wings close into fins.

From below, their crossings appear as brief black strokes through the daylight. From above, they resemble ordinary birds, though none flies beyond the horizon or chooses a different pore. Nests exist in pockets of interwoven foliage: cups lined with fibers, holding speckled eggs warm beneath damp bodies. The eggs are always nearly ready. Cracks web their shells. Small beaks press from within. No chick wholly emerges.

Deeper down, where sound replaces light, dwell the listeners.

They are compact, heavy animals with little vision and broad chambers running through their skulls. They attach themselves to trunks and root buttresses, reading the organism’s labor through vibration. Each arterial contraction, valve concussion, electrical discharge, and distant fall reaches them by a different route. Their skins shift color according to the pressure pattern, producing slow bands of gray, violet, and white.

Other animals gather to watch these changes. Thus the listeners make the condition of the invisible machinery visible. A darkening flank announces the approach of pump pressure. A pale ring marks a valve opening many kilometers below. A ripple of violet corresponds to some disturbance near the rim that has always just occurred.

Whether this is communication is uncertain. Nothing they announce is unexpected.

There are also creatures that seem less like animals than mobile functions of the great plant.

Flat cleaners crawl through the salt membranes and eat obstructions before the obstructions can alter the flow. Armored borers open channels in dead bark while secretions from their bodies stimulate new bark beneath them. Needle-mouthed swimmers transfer compounds between tissues that never touch. Tiny electrical animals bridge breaks between electrocyte fronds, aligning head to tail until current passes through their bodies.

The plant feeds them, houses them, transports them, and restores the conditions in which they act. They prune, clear, pollinate, conduct, and circulate. None could live apart from the organism, but it is equally unclear whether the organism could remain what it is without them. The boundary between resident and organ has worn away.

Even the immense trunks are inhabited internally.

Their vascular mains contain blind swimmers with bodies shaped like valves. Their woody chambers hold pale colonies that digest one layer of lignin while secreting another. In gas-bladder groves, buoyant larvae drift beneath taut membranes and feed on condensed oils. Along the capillary columns climb chains of soft-bodied creatures, each gripping the one above, their combined contractions assisting the rise of water.

Somewhere in every trunk, bark is peeling. Beneath each suspended strip is a narrow country of darkness populated by flattened things: translucent crawlers, filament worms, slow mouths grazing new tissue. They live forever between detachment and exposure.

No bones lie on the root floor because death is intercepted.

Bodies sinking through the open chambers are met by delicate scavengers that arrive in a fixed succession. First come the clear biters that remove the outer skin. Then red threads enter the vessels and drink what circulation remains. Mineral-shelled forms take the harder tissues. The last residue falls as fine pale grains, only to be gathered by filtering roots before it reaches the floor. Elsewhere, births return precisely what has been dismantled.

Yet no single animal is necessarily restored. The balance does not require mercy.

There may be intelligence in the high ocean, but if so it is not concentrated in a face or voice.

The root floor carries chemical records of every feeding, wound, discharge, and pressure change. Trunks relay them upward through sap and electricity. Leaves alter their pores in response. Animals answer with movement; their movement changes the current; the current returns information to the roots. The entire ocean may therefore sense itself continuously.

If it thinks, its thought has no sequence.

A signal has always just been sent and always just arrived. A wound is recognized while remaining forever fresh. A correction is issued to a condition that never worsens. Memory and perception lie on top of one another until there is no distinction between what the organism knows and what it is.

The inhabitants live inside that possible thought.

They graze its surfaces, breathe its water, cross its nerves, clean its channels, and are hunted in the fixed folds of its attention. Each appears to possess a life full of effort: feeding, fleeing, mating, hatching, healing, navigating by thunder and pressure. But no effort carries them into a future. Their lives are verbs without tense.

They are not statues. They are not illusions. They hunger and are fed. They strike and are wounded. They feel the electrical storm pass through the water and turn their silver bodies toward it. They labor against currents. They call above the rain. They tremble at the open trunk.

What lives there is everything that can happen without becoming history.
