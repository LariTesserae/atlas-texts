The High Aviary is inhabited less by stable organisms than by recurring designs. Its creatures possess recognizable species, habits, and sometimes memories, but their bodies are provisional—assembled from mineral dust, conductive membranes, electric charge, and the pressures of the crater’s unending chord.

Foremost among them are the **sails**, the broad, translucent animals that seldom descend below the height of the tallest groves. A mature sail may span ten or twelve meters while weighing almost nothing. Its body is a single pleated sheet, veined with dark conductive threads and edged by microscopic muscles of contracting crystal. Copper flashes across it when it feeds; green travels through it when it turns; in moments of perfect alignment it becomes transparent except for a fine black web.

Sails do not flap. They persuade the wind to carry them, tightening one corrugation and relaxing another until pressure slips unevenly across their surfaces. Their flight is slow only in appearance. Because they fold distance through the seven towers, the same individual may cross the crater many times in a single sweeping turn. Occasionally a sail can be seen pursuing its own trailing edge.

They graze on charge concentrated along the upper canopy, brushing the mineral trees without touching them. Groves dim after their passage. During electrical surges, whole congregations gather over the crater floor, overlapping until they resemble a single iridescent roof.

There is no obvious distinction between their reproduction and their injuries. When torn, a sail does not attempt to recover its missing portion. It seals its new edge and flies on. The severed strips twist in the air, widen, develop veins, and become smaller sails. Old individuals are therefore not necessarily large. The oldest may have been divided into thousands of descendants, each retaining some fraction of the original’s responses. A flock can wheel with such unanimity because, in a meaningful sense, it is one animal remembering itself in pieces.

Beneath them swarm the **note-born**.

These are the silvery creatures that emerge around standing-wave nodes. At first they look like flecks of brightness held motionless against the gale. As a tone stabilizes, each fleck extends radial limbs, then a faceted body, then a trembling fringe that gathers airborne minerals. Their exact anatomy depends upon the pitch that produces them. Low notes make heavy rings with blunt grasping lobes. High harmonics make needle-bodied chains. Close, beating intervals produce paired creatures that repeatedly pass through one another.

A note-born animal has no dormant egg or hidden parent. Its template resides in the crater’s acoustics. When the proper vibration occurs, dust and charge arrange themselves accordingly. When the tone fails, the arrangement ceases. This disappearance is not treated as death by the other fauna. Predators ignore a collapsing note-born even when starving, as though nothing edible remains once its sustaining frequency has gone.

Some note-born have lived intermittently for centuries. They return whenever their chord returns, gathering themselves from different dust yet resuming unfinished actions. One ancient spiral-form appears only during a rare winter alignment of the western clefts. Each time it manifests, it continues cutting a shallow groove around the sixth tower. The groove now circles the frame hundreds of times. No one knows what will happen when it is complete.

Closer to the ground move the **kilnbacks**, low ceramic grazers with six or eight legs. Their armor resembles pale fired clay riddled with pores, channels, and small chambers. The body beneath is not flesh but a shifting mesh of metallic fibers immersed in molten salts. Through translucent seams in the armor, currents can be seen moving as red and violet glows.

Kilnbacks advance by welding each foot briefly to the stone. Their gait is consequently measured and violent: fix, contract, break, swing, fix. A herd sounds like masonry being demolished. They shave mineral foliage with paired mouthplates that vibrate too rapidly to see, leaving smooth dark tracks through the groves.

Their shells support entire communities. Tiny **chamberlings** shelter in the warm cavities, scraping salts from interior walls and repairing cracks with secreted ceramic foam. Threadlike scavengers coil around the leg joints. Flat, colorless grazers browse on the electrical films lining the inner passages. Some inhabitants are useful to the kilnback; others merely exploit it. The distinction changes with the weather.

Among them hunt the **pale tenants**—slender, many-jointed predators that spend most of their lives inside another animal’s armor. A pale tenant may pursue chamberlings through a kilnback’s internal galleries for months without ever seeing open sky. When its host is stripped apart or shattered, it crosses to another shell in the instant when two kilnbacks anchor together. Herds form chains partly for stability and partly because every contact permits a frantic migration of their inner fauna.

Kilnbacks themselves are difficult to kill permanently. If reduced to an empty frame, they may remain motionless for days while swarms of black maintenance motes enter their joints. The motes spin new conductive tendons, replenish the salt cells, and restart the body one segment at a time. Whether the revived beast is the former animal is unclear. It often returns to the same routes and reacts to familiar herd members, but it may have different numbers of limbs or carry chambers copied from another shell.

The maintenance motes are called **stitchers**, though they mend plants, beasts, and stone with equal indifference. Individually they resemble angular grains of soot. In groups they flow like dark liquid, bending around pressure boundaries and splitting into precise geometric fronts. They gather fragments after every destructive gust and sort them by conductivity, curvature, and resonance. A piece suitable for a tree may be installed in a grazer. A fragment of armor may become part of a tower-side fern. Stitchers preserve patterns rather than owners.

They also tend the groves.

The crater’s apparent trees are colonies of vanishingly thin mineral leaves held on conductive frameworks. Their buried roots contain compact branching templates, little more than black circuits etched across stone. When a canopy is eaten or blown apart, stitchers read the remaining template and rebuild it from passing dust. Thus a grove may return by evening with the same spread of branches but wholly different matter.

Some groves react to repeated damage. They alter their templates, developing hooked foliage, disruptive harmonics, or leaves that turn edge-on when a particular predator approaches. The oldest have become intricate and temperamental. One black stand near the eastern wall folds itself into the silhouette of a kilnback herd whenever sails pass overhead. Another produces no visible foliage at all, only a region in which airborne grazers abruptly change direction. Whether these are plants, traps, or distributed animals is uncertain.

Among the branches live the **hinge flocks**: small jointed creatures that resemble sparks connected by hair-thin bars. They fold themselves through leaves, opening and closing as they move. Their feeding removes only the top molecular layer of a plant, creating shifting iridescent patches. When startled, an entire flock locks together into a rigid lattice and is carried away as one broad object. Once danger passes, the lattice loosens and becomes hundreds of animals again.

The **rollers** inhabit the exposed terraces. Each is a living ring, its inner rim lined with gripping cilia of polarized crystal. They do not walk but roll crosswind, accelerating until they hum. Their migrations make bright rivers around the bowl. At obstacles they divide into two narrower rings, pass on either side, and rejoin beyond. When two unrelated rollers merge by mistake, they may struggle for hours, alternately forming one ring and two.

In the highest, most violent air spin the **vanes**, seedlike grazers with asymmetrical bodies. Each turns at a characteristic rate, drawing charge from the wind. Predatory vanes alter their rotation to entrain smaller ones, pulling victims into their wake. Courtship uses the same mechanism. So does flocking. A spiral of vanes may therefore be feeding, mating, hunting, or simply trapped in mutual influence; often it is doing all four.

There are also animals almost too brief to name. Blue forks race along the edges of leaves and disappear at the first interruption. Glassy bladders inflate from pores in the ground, live for seconds, and burst into organized offspring. Hair-thin things bridge gaps between branches and consume whatever crosses them. Flat creatures inhabit the leeward side of airborne dust curtains, falling upward or sideways according to charge.

The most feared predators are the **chord-breakers**.

A chord-breaker is invisible in still air. It reveals itself only through the reactions of the world around it: grass silences in a moving patch, dust curves into an empty outline, and note-born animals collapse before the thing reaches them. It hunts not bodies but coherence. By producing a precisely opposed vibration, it cancels the frequency sustaining its prey. The prey falls apart, and the chord-breaker absorbs the released electrical order.

At night these predators move through the crater like absences. Other creatures detect them as defects in the general sound and flee toward noisier ground. A feeding chord-breaker leaves momentary quiet behind it—the rarest and most alarming condition in the Aviary.

Larger specimens haunt the towers. Some seem able to extend through the folded interiors of several frames simultaneously. A disturbance at the first tower may be followed by collapsing flocks at the fifth and seventh, though no body crosses the intervening space. Whether this is one predator, a coordinated group, or an artifact of manipulated distance is unknown.

The seven towers have inhabitants of their own.

Within the dull frames cling colonies of **interval mites**, minute creatures whose bodies are longer inside than outside. Seen on a surface, each resembles a dark oval no larger than a fingernail. Opened by accident, it unfolds into yards of branching anatomy. The mites feed on discrepancies in distance. They congregate wherever one part of the frame appears farther from another than it physically is, slowly reducing the discrepancy until the tower creates a new one elsewhere.

Other organisms use the frames as nests. Sails trail ribbons through one opening and braid them together at another. Hinge flocks lay geometric lattices across paths that exist only during certain pressure alignments. Pale threads emerge from the base of one tower, disappear into its side, and bear tiny fruit in the crown of a different tower across the crater.

During the rare moments when all seven frames stand locally adjacent, animals from every region pour into the resulting composite structure. They feed, divide, exchange material, and emerge redistributed. Kilnbacks unable to fit through the narrow openings sometimes leave one leg inside; the leg appears in six other places and is harvested by internal scavengers without noticeable harm to the host.

The sealed black objects hidden near the towers contain a quieter population. Most of their impossible galleries are vacant, but not sterile. Fine mineral vegetation spreads across walls and ceilings, oriented toward private gravities. Blind motes drift through cold plazas. In several objects, tiny replicas of Aviary creatures have appeared, though their forms are subtly wrong: five-legged kilnbacks, sails that settle on surfaces, rollers shaped as incomplete arcs.

One seed-object houses a colony of **bench keepers**. These are upright bundles of dark filaments about the height of a hand. They gather in the miniature plazas, arrange grains along narrow ledges, and remain motionless beneath the artificial sky. When the object is disturbed, every keeper turns toward the opening. None attempts to leave.

They may be descendants of organisms that served the builders, or crude reconstructions made by the Aviary from residual patterns. The shallow depressions near the towers fit their lower bodies, but only if those bodies are imagined at a vastly larger scale.

Nothing recognizably like the makers survives. Yet traces of intention recur among the crater’s life. Flocks occasionally assemble in rows matching the burned spirals in the stone. Groves grow arches around empty spaces. Kilnbacks pause at the narrow benches despite being unable to sit upon them. During certain subsonic pulses, every animal in the bowl inclines toward the same tower.

This behavior lasts only a moment. Then the wind changes, and appetite, flight, and reconstruction resume.

No single creature rules the High Aviary. If anything lives there in the fullest sense, it may be the entire mechanism: the bowl, the clefts, the towers, the hollow mountain, and all the organisms repeatedly composed within their pressures. Its pulse is the retuning wind. Its memory lies in templates, scars, routes, and standing waves. Its cells are replaceable populations. Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, appear as sudden coordinated migrations or groves that all turn transparent at once.

At dawn, its inhabitants begin to return from forms that resemble death. Sails widen from ribbons. Kilnbacks restart in clicking segments. Note-born gather around the first stable harmonics. Stitchers lift from the ground in black veils. The groves rise from their buried designs, leaf by conductive leaf.

Nothing awakens alone. The chord assembles them together.

By the time sunlight enters the crater, the High Aviary is once more crowded with ancient creatures made from entirely new material, all continuing lives that no body has kept.
