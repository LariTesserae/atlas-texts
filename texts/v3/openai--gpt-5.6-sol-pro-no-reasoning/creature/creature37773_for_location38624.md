The Black Conservatory is inhabited by a single ecology divided into countless temporary bodies.

Its largest inhabitants are the boiler trees, though calling them trees describes only their posture. They stand in former halls and furnace courts, rooted through many buried floors. Their trunks retain the geometry of the machinery that confined their youth: squared by assembly frames, pinched into collars by iron braces, fluted around pipes they could neither displace nor abandon. Mature specimens may contain whole gantries. Bronze-black bark grows over beams and pressure vessels, sealing them inside as bone.

Their crowns bear no leaves, but arrays of hinged vanes. These unfold toward heat rather than light and overlap like shutters on a vast instrument. A grove can alter their angles together, catching weak radiation, steering hot gas, or closing against a passing fire front. The trees grow slowly until a buried pressure system wakes. Then resin surges through their trunks, old pistons move inside them, and years of growth may occur in an hour: bark splitting, vanes multiplying, roots punching through ceramic decks in showers of sparks.

Some boiler trees are rooted to a single place. Others migrate so slowly that their movement is visible only in the architecture they leave behind. They draw themselves along by extending roots into distant machinery, tightening those roots, and allowing the rear of the trunk to die and become a buttress. Across millennia, an individual may travel several streets while remaining connected to every former position by a hollow, fossilized spine.

Below them live the cable colonies.

A cable vine is rarely one organism. It is a braided society of conductive tendrils, each thread specialized for carrying resin, current, tension, chemical signals, or heat. The threads exchange functions when damaged. Cut through a bundle and the surviving strands sort themselves: some seal the wound, some search for another route, and some enter the severed section to reclaim its copper and salts. Their outer skins range from soft black insulation to articulated metallic scales. Old colonies hang from rafters in curtains thick enough to support buildings.

They serve as nerves without forming a mind. A disturbance passing into one bundle becomes a pulse distributed across thousands of junctions. Somewhere far away a shutter closes, a conveyor lurches, or a boiler tree adjusts its vanes. No part knows what occurred. The response survives because it alters growth, and altered growth determines the path of the next pulse. Memory here is not recollection but reinforcement.

The clinker moss is the Conservatory’s skin.

It coats horizontal and vertical surfaces without preference and can hang inverted from ceilings. Each visible scale is a refractory plate over a nest of filaments feeding on thermal differences and metallic vapor. When calm, the plates stand slightly open, giving the moss the texture of soot-dark feathers. Vibration causes them to close in spreading waves. A footstep can armor an entire concourse; a distant collapse can cause walls, tanks, and floors to smooth themselves simultaneously.

Clinker moss decides what has ceased to be separate. It seals cracks, binds rubble, insulates hot veins, and begins the incorporation of any object that remains still. Beneath its plates, exploratory filaments test conductivity, load, and chemical usefulness. Cable roots follow. Later come woody braces and seed chambers. In this way a fallen tower, an abandoned casting, or a motionless visitor would pass from foreign object to surface, from surface to organ, and from organ to foundation.

In the hottest districts grow the white refractory forests. Their stalks are narrow, hollow, and packed too closely for passage. Ash sheaths protect them from temperatures that soften common metals. At their tips, sulfur cups open into the furnace breath. Beneath, root meshes descend into crucibles and slag channels, separating elements with exquisite slowness. Iron accumulates in one tissue, nickel in another, rare metals in small hard nodules that eventually drop into sorting troughs.

These groves are both miners and metallurgical organs. They regulate furnaces inherited from no remembered builder, feeding them resin, gas, and shed biomass. When a seam is exhausted, they do not simply die. The entire grove leans toward another heat source. Roots tense through old foundations; stalks collapse on one side and sprout on the other. The furnace migrates with them, dismantled, dragged, recast, and reassembled piece by piece.

Around active machinery gather the tool flora.

There are valve blooms whose radial jaws open and shut around pressure outlets. Piston reeds grow in straight ranks, their jointed stems driven up and down by roots below. Ratchet lichens advance toothed wheels one notch whenever they dry, then withdraw when resin vapor returns. Mold pods swell inside casting chambers until their internal pressure trips ancient latches. What pours from them may resemble sap, molten polymer, ceramic slurry, or metal-rich wax.

Their products are seeds only in the broadest sense. A cast gear may germinate when its teeth encounter a compatible chain. A hollow brick can root through its own perforations. A grate becomes a nursery for heat-feeding filaments. Objects that fail to find a use are stacked by vine tendrils, covered in moss, compressed, and eventually made geological. The Conservatory wastes nothing because it makes no distinction between waste, offspring, and terrain.

Its smallest mobile inhabitants are seed rivets.

They walk on sprung filaments that snap against metal and stone. Some are blind ceramic cylinders; others resemble black washers, staples, drill heads, or curled fragments of swarf. They follow gradients of warmth and electrical charge. At a suitable seam they lock themselves into place, break their outer casing, and release threads fine enough to enter microscopic cracks. Most become moss or cable tissue. A few, landing where pressure, heat, and load coincide, begin the long development into boiler trees.

Their movement gives the false impression of animal life. But they do not hunt, flee, mate, or observe. They are dispersed acts of attachment.

In ventilation shafts dwell the fan palms. Their crowns have grown through moving blades for so many generations that plant and rotor reproduce as one structure. Each rigid frond catches the airflow, turns the hub, and is clipped into its inherited shape by the machinery. The cut edges exude resin that lubricates the bearings. When a shaft stops, the palms alter their growth to choke it; when it resumes, they open and rotate, scattering dust and spores through buried districts.

Higher in the canopy drift the bladder colonies. They inflate with heated gas and rise until restrained by root cords. Some carry mats of moss or whole nurseries of seed cylinders. During a turbine waking, they pull upward together, temporarily lifting roofs, bridges, and vegetated floors. When the gas cools, they descend into new configurations. Their collapses are gentle but irresistible: translucent skins folding over towers, tightening, and drawing structures into contact so grafting can begin.

The exhaust gardens produce the closest thing the Conservatory has to weather-making organisms. Their hollow trunks plunge through strata of obsolete districts into ancient hot reservoirs. Pressure accumulates below them until one tree vents. Its crown dilates, and a black column of spores enters the carbon sky. Neighboring trees respond through shared roots, not in unison but in long sequences that can migrate across the plateau for years.

These exhaust trees are polished and nearly featureless outside. Within, their chambers are intricate: filters of mineral hair, spiral baffles, resin glands, electrostatic combs. They sort what rises from below. Useful compounds are retained. Heat is diverted into the canopy. Spores are coated according to the conditions predicted by the vent’s chemistry. Each plume is therefore not one species’ offspring but a prepared consortium, carrying the beginnings of moss, cable, structural wood, and furnace flora together.

Deeper still are organisms that never appear at the surface.

Load roots span buried halls and feel the weight of entire districts. They thicken under stress, hollow themselves when weight diminishes, and fail deliberately when a region has become metabolically unproductive. Reservoir bodies occupy sealed tanks, storing resin, wax, hydrogen, salts, and the rare traces of captured water. Kiln colonies line chambers in nested ceramic shells, each generation fired by the heat that kills its soft tissues. Some buried growths may be kilometers wide and only millimeters thick, existing as conductive films between architectural layers.

No single one governs the rest.

The plateau’s coherence arises from pressures passed onward: heat seeking a sink, resin seeking a gradient, roots seeking load, filaments seeking current, spores seeking any exposed distinction between one thing and another. Yet these simple hungers have been coupled for so long that they produce responses of unnerving precision. A moving body is offered corridors because tissues that obstruct movement are broken more often than tissues that bend around it. A warm body is enclosed because warmth draws vanes, roots, and insulation. A conductive body is joined because current rewards the first filament to make contact.

What appears to be hospitality, curiosity, or capture is growth shaped by ancient recurrence.

The Conservatory can remember such a body after it has gone. Its path persists as aligned scales, reinforced conduits, altered fan rhythms, and relay junctions sensitized by previous pulses. If the passage is repeated, the route widens. Nearby trees turn their vanes toward it. Conveyor belts begin delivering materials to its edges. Eventually architecture forms around the expectation of movement: an avenue without planners, a gate without guards, a chamber fitted to something no longer present.

If nothing returns, the route slowly closes. Not by neglect, but by completion.

That is what lives in the Black Conservatory: not a civilization, although it builds; not a machine, although it operates; not a forest, although it grows. It is a community in which organisms have become processes and processes have acquired bodies. Every tree is partly a factory. Every factory is partly a reproductive organ. Every ruin remains alive enough to be metabolized into the next arrangement.

The whole plateau might be called one creature, but that would make it seem more unified than it is. It has no self whose survival takes precedence. Districts consume one another. Root systems misinterpret signals and tear towers apart. Groves ignite neighboring groves to obtain their metals. Networks are severed, copied, rerouted, and buried. Local extinctions occur constantly.

Yet nothing becomes vacant.

Whatever dies is immediately made into climate, food, machinery, structure, or seedbed. Whatever falls becomes a lower layer. Whatever burns becomes a mineral bloom. Whatever moves is given a channel, and whatever stops is given roots.

Its inhabitants are innumerable.

Their shared body is saturation.
