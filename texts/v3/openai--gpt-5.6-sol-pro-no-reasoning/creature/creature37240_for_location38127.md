What lives in the High Conservatory is not a single community but a chain of small nations suspended above one another, each founded on a cup of water, a seam of compost, or a degree of warmth.

Among them moves the **railkeeper**.

It is the largest permanent inhabitant of the pinnacle, though a person might look directly at one and mistake it for part of the machinery. Full-grown, it is perhaps the length of a maintenance cage: low-bodied, many-jointed, and plated in overlapping scales the color of wet iron. Moss grows along the ridges of its back. Fern spores lodge beneath its shoulders. Old individuals carry entire mats of vegetation, so that when they rest against a wall they resemble gardens sagging from corroded brackets.

The railkeeper has twelve limbs, but seldom uses more than six at once. Each ends in a divided pad: one half claw, one half magnetic hoof. It can cling to warm plating, descend an upright pipe, or hang beneath the gantry while the pinnacle bends around it. Along its belly are rows of softer gripping feet that close around cable, grating, and conduit. In this way it always holds several structures at once. It trusts no single surface.

It has no eyes in the ordinary sense. The Conservatory is too deep, too hazed, and too violently lit for sight to be dependable. Instead, the railkeeper’s narrow head is crowned with a fan of hollow ceramic horns. These receive vibration. Through them it perceives the chamber as a composition of stresses: the slow labor of flywheels, water moving inside walls, macaques striking their family signatures, the minute tearing of roots beneath iron scales, the approach of the seven-hour discharge.

To a railkeeper, every platform has a voice. Every pipe has weather inside it.

Its name comes from its habit of traveling the old maintenance ways. It follows toothed elevator rails, cable runs, ladder spines, and the engraved drainage lines cut by the builders. Yet it does not merely use these routes. It tends them.

Railkeepers graze on corrosion.

They peel loose oxide from bolts with broad, chisel-like mouthparts and digest it with the help of bacteria housed in hot chambers along the gut. They eat rust fungus, mineral crusts, old resin, and the metal-rich roots that would otherwise split plates apart. Their droppings are dark, fine, and chemically mild. Moss colonizes them quickly. Much of what passes for soil in the upper gardens has gone through a railkeeper at least once.

Where plating has become dangerously thin, they behave differently. Glands beneath the jaw produce a black vitreous paste compounded from silicates, carbon, and metals concentrated from their food. They press this material into pits and seams, then polish it with the flat bones of their forelimbs. The paste hardens in engine heat. Builders’ reports, when they still existed, may have called these deposits contamination. The maintenance parties now know them as patches, though no one is certain whether the animals understand what they repair.

They are not domestic, and there is no evidence that anyone designed them. Perhaps their ancestors were small scavengers living in slag quarries or furnace drains. Perhaps they arrived as larvae in ceramic aggregate. The Conservatory has enlarged their function over centuries, selecting for bodies that can withstand vibration, metal-rich diets, long drought, and falls that never quite become falls.

A railkeeper seldom leaps. When forced from a surface, it opens the membranes folded between its limbs. These are too small for true flight, but broad enough to catch the tower’s updrafts. The animal descends slowly, rotating, until a cable or branch interrupts it. Sail-backed lizards often ride the air around it, snatching insects shaken from its moss.

Its life is synchronized with the machinery.

Several minutes before a pressure release, the horns along its head fold flat. It clamps itself across at least three anchor points and seals its breathing slits. Smaller animals have learned to shelter beneath it. Geckos enter the warm spaces between its armor. Ants retreat into old scars. Beetles wedge themselves around the magnetic feet. Then the pipes discharge, the vines stream horizontally, and the railkeeper becomes immovable beneath its trembling garden.

When the blast ends, it unfolds one limb at a time.

Its feeding journeys last for years. An individual may begin on the upper gantry while one wall is green, descend through the vertical gardens as the resurfacing carts approach, and return generations of swifts later to find the same wall bare and resin-dark. Railkeepers do not appear to recognize the chamber by foliage. They recognize it by structural song. Even after carts grind a habitat away, the plates beneath retain their characteristic vibration.

They breed rarely. At long intervals, an adult enters one of the drained cisterns and scrapes out a bed in the accumulated compost. There it lays a single object like a rivet head, smooth and black, cemented directly to the metal. The egg may remain dormant for decades. It develops only when three conditions coincide: sustained cloud moisture, the low resonance of turbines entering phase, and heat conducted through the cistern wall.

The hatchling is pale and soft, with oversized magnetic pads. For its first months it eats condensation algae and the shed skins of geckos. Translucent ants often build temporary mold gardens around the egg, and the young railkeeper consumes these too. Few survive their first pressure season. Those that do may live for centuries.

No one knows how many there are.

Maintenance parties have catalogued seven. Macaques distinguish at least nineteen, each with its own warning gesture. It is possible that these counts refer to the same animals at different ages, burdened with different gardens. It is also possible that ancient individuals occupy the lower shaft, where inspection lamps reveal, on rare passes, shapes too broad to be platforms and too slow to be machinery.

The oldest visible railkeeper is called **the Verdant Brace** in maintenance notes. It rests for decades at a time across a junction between two immense pipes and a disused bridge. A fig tree grows from a split in its dorsal armor. The tree’s roots pass beneath several plates, emerge along the animal’s flank, and enter a ceramic gutter whenever the railkeeper lowers itself to drink. Bromeliads occupy the hollows behind its horns. Silver insects hatch in their cups during cloud immersion.

The Brace moves so infrequently that macaques use its back as a crossing. They do not groom it or offer it food. Before stepping onto its vegetation, however, they strike the railing in a short pattern not used elsewhere. The railkeeper answers by flexing its armor, producing a deep sequence of clicks.

Whether this is communication, warning, or merely pressure shifting through its plates is impossible to determine.

Farther up, where the roof slit shines and the walls become dangerously hot, lives something less substantial.

These are called **veil colonies**: pale, ribbon-bodied creatures that occupy the wet underside of leaves. Individually they are scarcely thicker than threads. In dry weather they contract into waxy knots and may remain so for years. When cloud enters the chamber, thousands unfold at once and link themselves edge to edge, becoming translucent sheets stretched between fern fans, cables, and rivets.

The sheets drink fog directly.

Light passing through them takes on a faint green-gold tint. Tiny silver insects become trapped, but the colonies do not consume them immediately. Instead, the prey is carried through slow muscular ripples toward dark digestive rosettes shared by hundreds of bodies. A veil colony is therefore neither one animal nor simply many. It is an arrangement continually assembled by moisture.

During pressure discharge, the veils tear loose and stream across the open chamber. Fragments adhere to distant walls and found new colonies. Some are caught on gliding lizards, which tolerate them because the veils retain water against the skin. Older lizards trail translucent banners from their sails.

The macaques harvest dried veil knots and press them into wounds. Railkeepers occasionally bear the same pale material packed beneath damaged plates. Whether they apply it themselves or acquire it by brushing through gardens is another unanswered question.

Lower down, beyond the warm switchboards and below the last territories of the macaques, the **bell-throats** nest.

They are flightless descendants of birds, black and narrow, with feet capable of locking around vertical cable. Their feathers are reduced to oily filaments that do not hold condensation. Because engine noise overwhelms ordinary calls, their chests have become resonating chambers. A bell-throat communicates by placing its breastbone against metal and pulsing the muscles around it.

One cannot hear the resulting signal clearly in the air. It travels through the structure.

Other bell-throats feel it through their feet. A courtship may cross half the pinnacle as a sequence of faint knocks in ladders and platforms. Mated pairs answer one another through walls. When the tower bends, whole colonies fall silent, listening to the changing strain.

They feed on blind geckos, beetles, and the miniature shrimp left behind as temporary pools shrink. Their beaks are long enough to probe drainage holes, but soft at the tip so they do not damage eggs. Around each nest they arrange metal objects—washers, relay caps, snapped bits of chain—not for brightness, since they see poorly, but for resonance. A desirable object is one that sustains a clear vibration after being struck.

Thus bell-throat nests become crude instruments. Some contain components taken from machinery over many generations. Maintenance workers are forbidden to recover anything from an active nest, even when the object bears an inventory number.

In the deepest illuminated levels there may be another inhabitant, though it is known only by its effects.

Something moves through the enclosed reservoirs.

It leaves no body, track, or shed skin. Water levels change without corresponding flow. Sealed tanks develop rings of pale residue on their inner surfaces. Pipes that have been blocked for centuries are occasionally found cleared. Once every several years, usually after the irregular hammer below has fallen many times in close succession, frogs gather at dry valves and wait.

Then warm water issues from them.

The frogs enter. Blind shrimp appear soon afterward. Moss brightens along drainage lines. For several weeks, the lower gardens expand.

Workers call the presumed creature the **cistern animal**, but the name may be an error. It may be a colonial fungus living in the water system, an automated cleaning apparatus, or merely pressure wandering through forgotten connections. The railkeepers sometimes pause at reservoir walls and spread their ceramic horns against the tiles. They may remain that way for days.

Whatever they hear, they do not graze there.

Together these beings inhabit the Conservatory without filling it. They remain separated by enormous distances, by inaccessible temperatures, by the timing of clouds and engines. A veil colony may live and dry and live again above a bell-throat lineage that never sees daylight. A railkeeper may spend a century crossing a space a swift traverses in seconds. Macaques know the upper machinery as paths and drums; geckos know it as warmth; ants know only the mold-lined interiors of its cables.

None possesses the whole place.

The closest thing to a master of the High Conservatory is therefore not a species but a relationship: between root and rust, animal and railing, fog and ceramic, flesh and the continuous note rising from below. Everything living there has entered the mechanisms without conquering them. Everything mechanical has acquired passengers, grazers, parasites, gardeners, and interpreters.

The pinnacle sustains them almost incidentally.

In return, they keep its seams open, its corrosion bounded, its channels breathing, and its slow processes from reaching catastrophe. They inhabit it the way organs inhabit a body whose mind is elsewhere—or gone.

And through all of them passes the same immense vibration, so that even the softest creatures tremble with the labor of the tower.
