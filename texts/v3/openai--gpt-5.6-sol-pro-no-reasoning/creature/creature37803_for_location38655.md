The district is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, distributed community of water, machinery, and flesh.

Its human residents are not truly residents. They descend by service stairs at the beginning of a shift and return smelling of iron, lamp oil, wet felt, and the mineral cold of enclosed water. Most are slight, patient people who have learned to pass through narrow spaces without brushing the pipes. They wear dark rubber capes cut short at the wrist, felt-soled boots, and belts of cloth-wrapped tools. Each carries a slate tag and a grease pencil. A few keep watches, though time below is more reliably measured by pump cycles, lamp dimmings, and the arrival of rain.

They speak rarely. Instead they leave messages on valves, doors, bridge rails, and one another’s hooks:

**SUMP 4 WARMING.**  
**MOTHS IN EAST RELAY.**  
**DO NOT DRAIN UNTIL SECOND DARK.**  
**BELLBACK UNDER LOWER WALK.**

The oldest workers can read subtler notices. They know from the angle of sleeping moths which ventilation shaft is drawing; from the taste of condensation whether rain has entered the northern wards; from a bellback’s tone whether its mineral crown has changed or the corridor around it has lengthened. They do not consider this mystical. To them, attention is simply another instrument—less exact than a gauge, but easier to carry.

Some families have served below for generations. Their inherited knowledge is full of contradictions because the district itself does not remain consistent. A grandmother’s shortcut may now end at a sealed pier. A valve once reached in forty steps may require fifty-three. Instructions are therefore preserved with dates, water conditions, and reference sounds:

**From Pump House, follow the warm main until the third belt can no longer be heard. Recorded in a dry autumn.**

Surveyors visit too, usually in pairs. They arrive with tripods, chains, plumb lines, acoustic instruments, and confidence. They leave with damp notebooks and several mutually impossible measurements. The workers are courteous to them. They add the new figures to the archives and alter no routine until the animals confirm the change.

The smallest inhabitants occupy every wet boundary. Silver insects comb the tide marks, reducing films of algae and grease to clean, wavering lines. They scatter at shadows but not at vibration, so a passing worker may set an entire wall flowing like mercury. Rust-shelled snails follow behind them, scraping what remains. White gasket worms live deep in felt and braided packing, their soft bodies stained according to the lubricant of the machine they inhabit: amber near the pumps, blue-grey under the old lifts, faintly green around copper relays.

The gutter crabs are practical, territorial creatures. They shelter beneath washers, broken tiles, valve plates, and coins too light to serve as calibration weights. Each crab collects fragments matching the mineral composition of its shell. In one street they build red iron barricades; in another, pale ceramic circles; near the condenser hall, little walls of black glass. When water changes course, they dismantle these structures and carry them piece by piece to the next favorable eddy.

Blind minnows inhabit the cisterns. They gather under dripping points and turn upward whenever a lamp brightens, sensing heat rather than light. Their bodies are almost transparent except for the dark threads of their spines. During pressure alterations they descend together and vanish into depths the cistern plans do not show.

The eels are bolder. Slender and milk-pale, they travel through pipework that appears too narrow to admit them. They sleep wound around warm motor housings and lay beadlike eggs in grease traps. Some return from newly opened intervals with amber organs along their flanks. These lights blink in municipal rhythm even when the creatures are removed from electrical equipment. A dead specimen may continue blinking for hours, slowing only when the district’s generators slow.

No one has discovered what the lights are for. The workers use them to judge grid irregularities.

Moths crowd the ceilings in ordered ranks. Each generation emerges already responsive to the district’s airflow and power cycles. Before a lamp fails, they loosen from the brick and depart one by one. Before a pressure change, they rotate together, making a pale compass of their folded wings. Spores cling to their bodies, as does mineral dust, so that they carry both life and a fine record of distant chambers. Workers sometimes trap a moth beneath a glass and identify where it has been by the colors along its abdomen.

The rats travel during the lamps’ slow dimming. They are sleek, dark, and almost noiseless, with whiskers often tipped white by lime. Their paths link workshops that have grown acoustically remote from one another. Black mussels ride in their fur, closing tight during dry crossings and opening in culverts to feed. In return, the mussels seem to mark safe submerged routes: rats bearing large colonies rarely drown, even when a familiar channel has deepened overnight.

The bellbacks are the district’s most conspicuous inhabitants and perhaps its oldest animals.

A young bellback is no longer than a hand. Its skin is smooth, bronze-brown, and faintly warm. Mineral buds form along its spine wherever drips strike repeatedly. Over decades these buds lengthen into hollow knobs, cups, and crooked tubes. No two crowns are alike. Their composition records the waters they have lived beneath: iron gives a low dull note, calcite a brittle chime, copper a muted green bell. Very old animals carry layered carillons whose tones belong to pipes and springs no longer accessible.

They move with extreme deliberation. One may take a day to cross a gutter and a month to turn around. Insects graze their backs. Snails rest in the hollows between their mineral growths. Some bellbacks permit pale reeds to root in the damp creases of their skin, becoming slow gardens as well as animals.

Their ringing is usually incidental—a single touch of mineral against mineral as they breathe, settle, or receive a drop. Repeated ringing without movement means that pressure is shifting somewhere inside the masonry. Whether the creatures predict this change or cause it is unresolved. The district does not distinguish sharply between warning and mechanism.

Older workers address them by location rather than by name: Under-West-Valve, Warm-Pipe, Three-Steps, Condenser Bend. When a bellback finally leaves a long-held place, its absence exposes a clean silhouette on the brick and often a narrow crack behind it. These cracks lead into the newest spaces.

Life in those intervals is related to the life of the known district, but not always comfortably.

Crabs come back glazed in minerals absent from local stone. Moths carry pollen from plants with no visible leaves or roots. Eels return illuminated. Occasionally the rats leave tracks that begin at a wet seam in a wall and end there again, with no turning marks between. From certain openings comes air that smells of rain before rain has fallen above.

The workers do not enter immediately. They set down a shallow dish of water, a strip of copper, a square of felt, and a lamp with a measured charge. After several cycles they inspect what has changed. If silver insects have found the dish, if the copper darkens normally, if moths approach the lamp and depart before it dies, then the interval is considered connected enough to map.

Mapping does not mean conquering it. A plank may be laid across the gap. A pipe may receive an extra joint. A new valve number may be added, always with enough blank space after it for revisions. In time the opening acquires a gutter, a lamp, a fungus trough, rat tracks, and a bellback sitting under the most dependable drip. It becomes another street without anyone having built it.

Thus the district’s true inhabitant may be the buried river itself—not as a ghostly body of water, but as a habit the place cannot relinquish.

It lives in gradients, delays, and repeated routes. Pumps serve as its organs. Cisterns are its pauses. Pipes and gutters imitate tributaries. Animals carry its moisture across dry thresholds. Workers maintain its circulation while believing they maintain the city above. Even the widening distances resemble a river’s labor: separating one bank from another, making passage necessary, leaving damp ground where adjacency used to be.

It has no face and perhaps no awareness. Yet it responds. A stopped machine is compensated for elsewhere. A sealed chamber develops condensation on the outer side of its door. A corridor that grows too dry becomes shorter in measurements until seepage returns. Sound travels along useful routes and abandons obsolete ones. The quarter preserves function while allowing location to loosen.

Everything living there participates in that preservation.

The fungi digest old spills. The crabs clear gutters. The worms soften neglected seals. Mussels filter culvert water. Eels move through the hidden mains. Moths reveal changes in air and current. Bellbacks register pressures too slow for gauges. Rats stitch distant rooms together. Human workers make repairs, but their repairs weather into habitat, and their rules become another form of animal caution.

Nothing below owns the district. Nothing fully understands it.

They inhabit one another: moss in mortar, worms in gaskets, mussels in fur, insects in the mineral gardens of bellbacks, workers inside the schedules of machines, and machines inside the remembered course of the river.

Together they form a patient urban organism whose growth is measured not by expansion into land, but by the slow creation of distance between things that were once touching.
