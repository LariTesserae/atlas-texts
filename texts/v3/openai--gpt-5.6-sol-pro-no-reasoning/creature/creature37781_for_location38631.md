They live beneath the skin.

Not far beneath. The basin affords no true depth, no private underworld sealed away from weather and engines. Their chambers lie in the warm interval between the clay crust and the older deposits: flattened rooms in black silt, cavities under concrete slabs, hollows opened by settling pipes and kept open by roots, scrap, woven stems, and whatever else will bear a little weight. A person may pass within the length of an arm without knowing. A bootfall dimples their ceilings. Pump vibrations travel through their walls. Rain arrives as a million soft impacts overhead.

They are small, though not uniformly so. Most, standing upright, would reach no higher than a person’s shin. The oldest are broader and lower, with shoulders that slope into long forelimbs and torsos shaped by crawling through saturated passages. Their young are narrow as reeds. All of them have the basin’s pallor: clay gray, limestone white, the faint pink of worms brought suddenly into air. Along their backs and limbs, darker bands show through the skin, mottled like organic layers in a core sample.

Their eyes are neither blind nor useful in the ordinary way. They are dark, lidless-looking, protected by a clear inner membrane against grit and oily water. They discern brightness, movement, and the enormous passing shadows of birds. More important are the delicate pits along their jaws and wrists, which register pressure traveling through mud. By these they know the state of the world: rain beginning at the western ponds, a dredge turning, a flock landing, a valve opening two levees away. They feel a pump start long before warning lamps show aboveground.

Their hands have four long fingers joined almost to the tips by translucent webbing. The nails are black, blunt, and continually regrown, suitable for parting mud rather than digging it. They do not excavate so much as persuade. A tunnel is made by pressing water ahead, stiffening the walls with snail slime, shredded fiber, and mineral paste, then waiting for the basin’s own weight to consolidate it. Their constructions are temporary because everything here is temporary. When a chamber slumps, they abandon it or reshape it around the collapse. A fallen ceiling becomes a bench. A flooded sleeping hollow becomes an egg pool. Nothing is wasted by resisting what it has become.

They breathe air, but can remain underwater for a long time. At intervals, one surfaces beneath a corrugated sheet, inside a drowned cabinet, or under the inverted hull of a skiff where an air pocket persists. The breath is taken slowly, without a splash. Bubbles released elsewhere draw attention, so they thread them through reeds, cracks in concrete, and the hollow stems of drowned plants. Many of the unexplained strings rising from the mud are theirs.

They eat what the basin concentrates. Snail flesh, midge larvae, beetle eggs, soft-bodied crabs, fish stranded in narrowing trenches. They harvest bacterial films from submerged surfaces with ridged tongues. They gather pale fungal growth from cable conduits and the undersides of pallets. Around warm pipes they cultivate dense knots of worms, not in ordered beds but by adjusting leaks, arranging stones, and laying down mats of decaying vegetation. Their husbandry resembles neglect so closely that no worker has recognized it.

Birds are both danger and calendar. The basin people know species by the tremor of their steps and the rhythm of bills entering mud. Terns mean exposed water. Stilts mean a crust that may support a light body. Ibises mean the upper layers are rich and newly disturbed. Gulls mean machinery, refuse, or death. When thousands rise at once, those below flatten themselves against chamber floors and wait for the pressure release, the engine backfire, or the unseen movement that startled them.

They also use birds. Loose feathers are drawn down through breathing holes and worked into insulation. Eggshell is ground into strengthening paste. Nests abandoned on mud islands are dismantled strand by strand. In return—though no bird understands this—they maintain certain shallow feeding grounds, loosening the silt from below so larvae gather near the surface. Places that seem naturally favored by waders are sometimes gardens.

Their settlements form around industrial heat. Pump houses are especially desirable. Beneath each, the pilings hold open durable channels, condensation provides fresh water, and the motor’s vibration disguises smaller movements. Yet no settlement occupies the space directly under a pump. Machines fail, foundations sink, outlets reverse. They live instead in crescents around them, linked by narrow submerged ways to concrete chambers where silence collects.

Silence is valuable because they have so little of it.

Their hearing is tuned to low frequencies and conducted vibration. The basin’s continuous roar does not deafen them; it fills their lives with weather-like information. Each motor has a texture. Each fan makes a stable boundary in sound. Valves announce themselves by changes in distant harmonics. They navigate not by landmarks but by interference: where two pumps cancel each other, where a culvert amplifies a diesel pulse, where the conveyor’s chain makes a slow metallic beat through the sediment.

They name places accordingly. Not “western pool” or “road three,” but Between-Hammers, Long Rattle, Under the Uneven Fan, Where the Chain Returns. A failed engine can erase a district more completely than flood. When machinery is replaced, guides spend months learning the altered soundscape, pressing palms and jawbones to walls, rebuilding routes in the dark.

Their own speech is quiet and partly tactile. They touch wrist to wrist, throat to shoulder, fingers to the thin bones behind the jaw. Spoken sounds are soft clicks, wet trills, and pulses too low to separate from frogs. Across distance they communicate by tapping pipes and marker posts. Some of the gauges that tremble in locked cabinets are responding to messages sent through the connected metal: warnings, counts, requests for passage, notice of a birth or collapse.

They have no single settlement and no ruler. The basin will not permit either. Groups form around stable structures—an old footing, a stranded hull, a culvert that has not shifted in several seasons—and separate when the ground changes. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever best understands the immediate condition: the one who knows birds during nesting, pipes during discharge, crusts during dry weather, or deep mud when levees begin to fail.

Age is measured not in years but in cycles remembered. One may be described as having known the black pond before it turned black, or the straight road before its middle sank, or the conveyor when its mound lay north rather than south. The oldest remember open sky where there is now a settling tank. They carry maps that cannot be drawn because the routes are made of pressure, temperature, salinity, and sound.

Their young hatch in warm pools sealed from fish. The eggs are laid in clear, overlapping sheets beneath stone, steel, or concrete, indistinguishable from the spawn and insect masses covering the basin’s undersides. Adults move the sheets as water chemistry changes, sometimes many times in a night. The hatchlings begin aquatic, fine-limbed and gilled, feeding among larvae. For a season they are scarcely separable from the basin’s countless small lives. Later their gills close, their fingers strengthen, and they are taught to read surfaces from below.

The first lesson is weight.

A smooth pressure passing quickly overhead may be a bird. A repeated narrow puncture is a wader’s bill. A broad compression with a hard central edge is a boot. Tires roll differently when loaded. Dredges announce themselves far in advance. Rain complicates everything, covering all signals with static, so the young are forbidden near the crust during heavy weather.

The second lesson is not to trust permanence. A wall may become a floor. A pipe may begin carrying hot water, caustic water, oil, or nothing. A familiar chamber may fill slowly from below without any breach. A road is only gravel descending through mud. A levee is water held temporarily at two heights. The world is not made of things but of tendencies.

The third lesson concerns the surface people.

They do not regard the workers as gods, invaders, or prey. Workers are powerful animals with poor senses and repetitive habits. They bring aggregate, metal, heat, vibration, food waste, poisons, and sudden death. They open the basin and try to close it. They drain water from one place and cause it to rise in another. Their machines are dangerous, but their schedules can be learned.

The basin people salvage from them constantly. Wire becomes braces and fish hooks. Plastic sheeting becomes waterproof partitions. Grease seals air pockets. Fragments of reflective vest mark hazardous tunnels because they remain visible in the faintest light. Warning lamps are watched carefully: not understood as commands, but trusted as signs of imminent force.

Sometimes a worker glimpses one.

Usually it is only a pale hand withdrawing beneath a slab, a narrow head breaking water where no fish should rise, or eyes reflecting from a culvert too small to enter. Such sightings are blamed on frogs, crabs, exhaustion, fumes, or the distortions of warm rain. Footprints are never convincing. In the basin all prints broaden, lose their toes, and become alike.

The inhabitants prefer it this way. Concealment is not maintained by elaborate law. It is simply the safest use of the surface people’s incuriosity. Humans look for what is large, upright, dry, and separate from its surroundings. The basin people are none of these for long.

Their dead are placed in active sediment. The body is folded into a shallow hollow with shell, iron flakes, bird feathers, and a piece of material from the place where the person lived longest. No marker is raised. Instead, water is encouraged across the grave so silt settles quickly. Within a season the place may be a feeding flat. Within several, a reed clump may hold there. They do not speak of returning to the basin, because they never considered themselves outside it.

They fear deep black water, sudden fresh water, and silence where machinery should be. They fear smooth mud without birds. They fear new concrete, whose weight has not yet declared where it will sink. Most of all they fear successful drainage. Flood, contamination, collapse, and burial can be survived by movement. Dryness exposes every passage, hardens nursery pools, silences the carrying mud, and divides the world into disconnected holes.

Yet they know the pumps will never entirely win. This is not faith. It is observation. The basin accepts work without yielding to it. Every trench fills. Every foundation tilts. Every road descends. The machinery removes water and makes hollows for more water. It separates mud only to stack it where rain can return it. The inhabitants help when useful: narrowing an unseen outflow, loosening aggregate beneath a barrier, packing a sensor housing with snail shells. But mostly they wait.

Their largest gathering place lies beneath the stranded barge.

Its hull presses down like a second sky, creating a broad region of dependable shade and trapped air. Mud packed around the keel has hardened with rust. Nesting birds crowd the upper surface, while below, chambers extend through sediment caught among old ribs and broken decking. Warm water enters from the east. A pipe of unknown original purpose carries sound from distant pump houses. Here the young are brought during pressure releases, the injured are treated, and long memories are exchanged.

On certain nights, when low cloud erases the horizon and rain smooths the whole basin into one trembling surface, they emerge there in numbers.

They climb onto the mudflat among sleeping birds. Damp skin whitens in diffuse light. Long hands test the rain. Some stand; some crouch; some remain half submerged, carrying hatchlings against their throats. They do not perform a rite exactly. They listen together.

Through water, hull, pipes, clay, pilings, and their own touching bodies comes the total basin: pumps hammering below hearing, conveyor links advancing one by one, frogs calling in flooded tracks, wings shifting overhead, culverts running, levees settling grain by grain. Beneath it all is a slower movement—the soft displacement of saturated earth, the basin continuing to lower and gather.

They listen for changes that no single body could detect.

Then, before dawn whitens the haze, they descend again through openings the mud closes behind them.

From the rim there is only the barge, the birds, and the flat gray water.

Below, wrist touching wrist in the warm sediment, the basin is awake.
