The city is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, distributed economy of breath, dust, oil, vibration, and patiently recycled flesh.

Its smallest citizens live upon the stone itself. They form gray-green films over limestone faces, colonies so thin they resemble tarnish. Where mineral salts concentrate, the films deepen to olive or black; where grazing is heavy, they survive only in cracks and pores. They feed on the faint chemical differences between one layer of rock and another, and their muted radiance supplies most of the city’s light. Each colony glows weakly, but together they turn whole courts visible, as if remembered daylight had soaked into the walls.

Among them crawl the shell-backed grazers. They are scarcely larger than a thumb, broad and flat, with overlapping plates like chips of old porcelain. Their many short legs end in hooked pads, allowing them to cling to walls, ceilings, herd animals, even the papery wings of resting gliders. Beneath the shell, paired rasping combs scrape microbial film and softened mineral into the mouth. Individually they make almost no sound. In their millions, they furnish the city’s constant metallic whisper.

They travel in fronts. A wall may be dark with growth one year and pale the next, the colony moving across it like a tide without water. Behind them they leave clean limestone stippled with eggs: tiny wax-sealed beads pressed into shallow pits. The adults carry their own internal solvent in dense sacs beneath the shell and can remain motionless for years when food fails. Such dormant multitudes lie under apparently empty pavements, waiting for dust, warmth, or the chemistry of passing dung.

The low forests belong to organisms that are neither quite plants nor quite colonies. Their visible fans are only collection surfaces. Beneath the stone runs a much older body: cords sunk into salt seams, clay lenses, and porous limestone. A single grove may be one individual spread across several chambers, its separated trunks joined through cracks too narrow for any animal.

The fan growths turn slowly toward traffic rather than light. Warm breath, shed skin, and disturbed dust nourish them. Their leathery surfaces are pleated to catch whatever falls, and their waxed margins curl shut when damaged. The column forms of the deeper plazas are their reproductive towers. Over decades, each crown accumulates debris between overlapping plates. There the organism sorts the city’s refuse, dissolving what it can and compressing the rest into dry capsules. When the plateau begins to inhale, the plates lift. Capsules roll into the avenues or are carried away in the furrows of passing bodies.

Many columns bear the scars of herds. Some have grown into bent shapes around habitual routes, forming gates through which generations pass. Others have been hollowed without dying. Their outer ribs remain active while their centers become warm, sheltered tenements for smaller creatures.

The most numerous of these tenants are seamlings: translucent, many-jointed animals no longer than a finger. Their bodies coil tightly to conserve vapor, and their skins are layered with wax that they renew by eating the inner walls of their host. They emerge during changes in pressure to gather spores and grazer eggs. When threatened, an entire colony snaps its body segments against the trunk. The resulting brittle chatter travels through the grove, causing nearby columns to fold their crowns and seal their openings.

Above them sleep the gliders.

They hang in dense ranks from ceiling seams, blind heads tucked between their shoulders. Their bodies are warm because their chemistry runs continuously; stopping would allow their internal solvent to thicken beyond recovery. The heat of a large roost can support whole hanging gardens of pale ropes and microbial velvet.

A glider has four limbs, but the rear pair and flanks are joined by broad, stiff membranes. These wings do not flap with grace. They crack open, catch the cavern’s slight currents, and are hauled through the air by powerful strokes of the forelimbs. Their flight is a sequence of controlled falls and abrupt corrections. They navigate through sound and pressure, sensing pillars as alterations in the returning thunder of the city.

Their faces are circular and ridged, built to collect vibration. There are no eyes, only heat-sensitive pits around the mouth. They feed by passing low over groves and migration trails, combing drifting capsules, seamlings, and loose grazers from the air with fringed jaws. Some follow herds for weeks, feeding on the clouds stirred by their feet. Their young remain in ceiling crèches, sealed inside leathery pouches supplied with enough fluid to survive until their first flight.

When a roost launches, it does so collectively. One animal unfolds; its neighbors feel the strain enter the rock; within moments acres of ceiling come alive. The flock pours down in sheets, papery wings striking and folding, briefly extinguishing the dimness beneath it.

The avenue herds are the city’s great carriers and gardeners. Adults stand low enough to pass beneath most ledges, though the largest have bodies as long as a wagon. Six padded legs support a barrel-shaped trunk covered in overlapping ribs of flexible hide. Dust collects between these ribs, where symbiotic growths feed and produce oils that protect the animal from drying. Older herd beasts carry miniature ecosystems on their flanks: grazers, pale crusts, seed capsules, and tiny scavengers that never touch the ground.

Their heads are blunt wedges armored with mineralized plates. They use them to part column groves, overturn brittle fans, and expose nutrient-rich growth beneath. The mouth is on the underside, shielded from dust by folds of waxed tissue. They eat slowly and almost continuously, crushing plant plates and mineral crust together. Stones swallowed with food remain in an internal grinding chamber until worn smooth, then pass out in compact pellets on which new microbial gardens bloom.

They perceive the world through their feet. Each broad pad contains concentric membranes tuned to different frequencies. Near stamping conveys identity, agitation, and direction. Deeper, slower impacts carry farther: warnings, migration calls, the state of distant feeding grounds. The animals often stand motionless with one forefoot raised, listening through the other five.

A herd is led not by the strongest animal but by one that remembers the oldest routes. These route-keepers are usually scarred, slow, and heavy with mineral accretions. They know where galleries have narrowed, where a ceiling has fallen, where a nursery seam has cooled. Yet even their knowledge is partly external. The migration tracks themselves guide the herd. Centuries of feet have polished the stone differently along safe paths, and the pads recognize that texture. Young animals follow the satin bands long after every living guide has forgotten how they began.

Their courtship turns architecture into an instrument. Males seek pillars whose buried roots match the resonance of their bodies. Bracing three legs and one ribbed flank against the stone, they contract deep muscles in measured pulses. A suitable pillar answers through the floor. Females compare not merely loudness but the shape of the returning note, choosing males able to find and sustain a clear resonance amid the cavern’s noise. Some courtship pillars have been used for tens of thousands of years and are polished dark at the height of the animals’ flanks.

The predators are built around interruption.

A common one, the hushback, is long and low, with eight narrow limbs and a loose mantle stretched from shoulder to tail. Its feet are divided into soft brushes that spread its weight and muffle contact with the ground. Along its sides run pits sensitive to minute air movements. It does not hunt by sight, scent, or even ordinary hearing. It maps the absence created when other creatures cease their noise.

As a hushback enters a district, nearby grazers stop rasping. Gliders flatten themselves. Seamlings withdraw. These silences outline walls and obstacles more precisely than echoes would. The predator advances into the growing quiet, reading each new boundary. Often it freezes for hours beside a migration track, indistinguishable from a fallen strip of hide. When prey finally moves, the mantle snaps rigid, and stored tension throws the animal forward.

Hushbacks seldom kill the largest herd adults. They take calves, sick animals, gliders forced to ground, and dense mats of grazers scraped up with a comb-like tongue. After feeding, they seal the remains beneath a secreted membrane. The packet can be dragged to a blind arcade and consumed over months without losing its internal fluids.

Older predators become territorial around resonant spaces. Their dens are littered not with fresh refuse but with meticulously dried parcels, shed foot-brushes, and bones arranged by accident into ridges where the predator repeatedly turns. Grazers eventually polish these remains into the floor. A long-occupied den develops its own pavement.

Below all this, the buried quarters have another population.

Dust-swimmers move through compacted drifts without opening permanent tunnels. Their heads are narrow and plated; their bodies widen behind them into muscular wedges. They swallow dust at the front, strip it of microbial spores and organic grains, then press it outward along their sides. The displaced material closes behind them, leaving only a raised line on the surface. Beneath a large dune, thousands may be moving at once, their intersecting trails forming a hidden street plan that changes by the hour.

Some dust-swimmers follow the warmth of carcasses. Others gather beneath nesting chambers to feed on fragments of eggshell and leaked provisions. Their own young grow inside dry brood nodules carried under the parent’s outer plates. During the plateau’s inward breath, the nodules are buried separately. Each contains solvent, food, symbiotic microbes, and a sleeping juvenile—a complete pocket of life awaiting the correct pressure.

On warm fault faces live the sealed egg colonies. The adults are seldom seen. They are thin, ribbon-bodied creatures that spend most of their lives inside hairline fractures, feeding on mineral-loving microbes. Only to breed do they emerge. Hundreds knot together against the warm rock and deposit communal clusters, coating them in alternating layers of lime and wax. The resulting masses resemble pale masonry. Heat spreads through them slowly, and the young develop over years.

Hatching is communal and violent. The innermost juveniles release gas into the sealed chambers; pressure builds until the lime casing fractures. Hundreds emerge in a burst of shell flakes and oily mist. Gliders gather for these events. So do predators. Most of the hatchlings vanish within minutes, but enough reach the cracks to replace the hidden adults.

Death supports the city’s slowest inhabitants.

Colonial undertakers spread as thin sheets beneath carcasses. They are not fungi, though their raised feeding edges resemble fans. The colony first seals the body to the floor, trapping every volatile substance. Then it sends hairlike filaments upward through skin and organ, dissolving the dead from within. Nutrients are stored in waxy bladders or exchanged with nearby root cords. Digestion may take decades. Throughout it, the carcass retains its outward form, becoming lighter and more hollow until a passing foot breaks it.

Specialist grazers harvest the exterior. Scavengers enter through joints and leave chambers inside the dried tissues. Plant cords invade from below. Eventually the body ceases to be an individual thing and becomes a temporary district: roof, food source, nursery, and landmark. Herds route around it. Gliders roost in its cavities. Predators listen from behind its ribs.

The oldest remains are unrecognizable as remains. Shell plates overlap with teeth, compressed stems, eggshell lime, and mineralized skins. Generations walk over generations until biological history becomes pavement.

Even the barren squares are inhabited, though mostly by listeners. Along their edges crouch stone-colored animals with broad, dishlike backs. They feed almost entirely on vibration. Not on vibration itself, but on microscopic flakes shaken loose where resonant stone meets the walls. Their bodies funnel each tremor toward rows of adhesive grooves. A distant stamp produces a faint harvest; a courtship drone can feed a colony for days. Too many gatherings, however, shake the thin floors dangerously, so these creatures space themselves at near-perfect intervals around the plazas.

In the central basin, life is most visible as motion at a distance. Herds move along inherited tracks. Gliders cross between pillars. Dust fronts creep from one quarter to another. Dark growth skirts the bases of the towers, nourished by everything the stone catches and returns.

There are said—if anything here can be said to say—to be animals that never leave the basin’s central dark. Their presence is inferred from pauses in migrations, from tracks that divide around apparently empty ground, and from low pulses that do not resemble courtship or warning. Perhaps they are immense. Perhaps they are buried. Perhaps the basin itself, layered with countless linked colonies and traversed by root cords older than species, has become responsive enough to resemble an animal.

No inhabitant could perceive the whole city. Each knows a version shaped by its senses: chemical gradients in stone, warmth along faults, air pressure in arches, resonances underfoot, interruptions in sound. To the grazers it is an endless feeding surface. To the gliders, a volume of currents and obstructions. To the herds, a web of remembered roads. To the predators, a field of silences. To the rooted colonies, it is not a city at all but a single slowly changing body of mineral and nourishment.

They live crowded together, yet much of the cavern appears empty. They move constantly, yet its great patterns scarcely change. Individuals hatch, feed, migrate, mate, dry, and are incorporated into the roads. Species replace one another. Routes outlast anatomy. Groves move from court to court. Pillars answer bodies whose ancestors had different numbers of limbs.

What lives beneath the plateau is therefore neither a hidden civilization nor a wilderness in the usual sense. It is an occupancy so old that habitation and geology have become difficult to separate. The creatures use the city, make the city audible, polish and nourish it, and finally enter its floors.

The stone provides the plan.

Everything alive becomes part of the architecture.
