The Bright Sump is inhabited less by a population than by a process.

Its most obvious life is sparse and specialized. Colorless skaters travel a finger’s breadth above the luminous pool, held aloft by whatever local law keeps the hair-thin channels in the rock empty. Their six legs never touch the surface, yet each produces a fine deformation in the sky reflected below, so that they seem to walk on light rather than water. They halt at the chamber’s deepest concussions and remain perfectly still until the circuit of clicks has passed around the walls. Whether this is instinct or obedience is impossible to tell.

In the crevices live pale, soft-bodied things within flexible shells. The shocks call them out. After a shelf collapses or a stone is freshly rounded, they unfold against the wet rock and graze on the new mineral bloom. They have no visible eyes and little need of speed. Nothing in the chamber seems to hunt them, although they retract whenever the pool grows suddenly quiet.

There is also the black shape below the pool.

It is seen too rarely and incompletely to be named. It passes beneath the brilliant surface without dimming it, long and narrow, sometimes bending where no body should bend. Its apparent size exceeds the pool’s dimensions. This may mean it inhabits another body of water glimpsed through the Sump’s surface, or that the chamber enlarges around it, or that it is not an animal at all but a moving boundary in the mechanism below. The skaters stop when it passes. The shell creatures withdraw. Even the falls occasionally reverse, as though displaced by its motion.

The chamber’s plants—or things most like plants—live in isolated communities. Dark red fans spread over corroding metal and draw sustenance from warm seepage. Fine mosses write slow lines along protected cracks. Translucent reeds on the western silt bar feed upon vibration as much as heat or mineral. Their hollow stems retain fragments of the Sump’s roar and hum them back during moments of quiet. In this way the reeds preserve vanished sounds for a few seconds, making the chamber hear its own immediate past.

Yet these are only the smaller inhabitants.

The true resident may be the engineered absence threaded through the limestone: an immaterial lattice of sealed gaps, pressure paths, exclusions, and directional permissions. It has no single body. Its organs are the chamber’s falls, terraces, fissures, heat fronts, reflections, and collapses. The pool is perhaps its exposed sensory surface. The stone is memory. Erosion is thought.

If so, it thinks by changing the place in which thinking occurs. A channel deepens and becomes a decision; a shelf collapses and revises it. Water enters one groove, returns elsewhere with a different chemistry, and alters the next stage of the calculation. Distances lengthen or contract. The arch migrates without moving. Gravity is reassigned locally. The chamber’s apparent violence is not disorder but cognition conducted through pressure and dissolution.

Its speed of awareness varies. Much of the time it is geological: slow polishing, deposition, the gradual narrowing of a pillar. At other moments it wakes with terrifying quickness. The deep report passes through the chamber like a pulse. The subsequent clicks may be measurement, error-checking, or the confirmation that every moving thing has assumed a known position. The simultaneous turning of all water toward the exterior sky resembles an aperture opening. For those few silent seconds, the Bright Sump may be looking out.

Someone once lived beside this intelligence, or tried to.

Their shelter remains near the southern wall, small enough for two people but perhaps occupied by only one at a time. They left rules rather than explanations, scratched into vitreous panels: how to judge safe stone, how to cross reflections, when not to answer, when not to move. The seven ceramic stakes and solitary arch may be instruments, warnings, or attempts to give fixed coordinates to a place that refuses them. The handrail descending into the sealed fissure suggests repeated entry. Its occasional warmth suggests that entry may still occur.

No human figure is ordinarily seen. Nevertheless, the marks are renewed, the rail gathers ascending droplets, and the arch sometimes frames mist disturbed by something that has already passed. Whoever maintained the shelter may have died, departed through the fissure, or become distributed through the chamber’s changing geometry. They may survive only as procedures absorbed into the Sump: the pauses after concussion, the counted clicks, the distinction between roof-water and sky-water.

There is one further possibility. The rules may not have been written for visitors. They may be instructions left for the chamber itself.

Thus what lives beneath the old sea floor is a layered ecology: mineral grazers and hovering skaters; resonant reeds and metal-feeding fans; an impossible swimmer in a water larger than its basin; absent or transformed custodians; and beneath them all a patient hydraulic mind. It is warm, bright, and perpetually rebuilding its own body. It neither preserves nor destroys the chamber. It inhabits the equilibrium between those acts.

When the Sump opens and thousands of tiny suns appear in its droplets, every inhabitant becomes still except the stones beneath the pool. They shift softly in the imposed silence, selecting new positions.

Then the opening closes, the roar returns, and the thing below resumes thinking.
