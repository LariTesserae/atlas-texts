What lives there is not a collection of plants, though at first the plateau offers no better word. It is a mineral metabolism spread thinly across the highland: many apparent groves, mats, columns, and thickets belonging to a few immense bodies—or perhaps to one body whose continuities lie too deep to recognize.

Its visible growths are solar organs. Black-green vanes turn by increments finer than sight, gathering light into conductive roots. Crystal-tipped filaments measure the sun, the atmosphere, and the minute electrical condition of the stone. Bronze thickets regulate heat and charge. The tall fluted columns in the central basins are not trees so much as raised stacks of reaction chambers, lifting collectors above their own shadows. Their pale bands record completed operations: a reservoir refined, a seed furnished, a mineral seam exhausted and sealed.

Most of the organism lives underground. It inhabits interfaces—between iron and sulfur, carbon and silicate, warm crust and warmer substrate. Its roots are both nerves and digestive surfaces. They follow useful strata for kilometers, branching into films only molecules thick. Where conditions differ, they maintain voltages. Where compounds are scarce, they manufacture them. Where old tissues lie, they reclaim them with such precision that there is no refuse and almost no loss.

It has no blood. Hydrocarbon gels carry reagents through its nearer organs, while charge and changes of crystalline state travel across the greater network. Its memory is probably not held in any central place. Sulfur-rich structures at root junctions can preserve stable configurations for ages: records not of images or events, but of balances—what has been taken, what must be returned, which boundaries must remain uncrossed. A colony remembers itself as an account.

The separated growths may therefore be less like individuals than like entries in that account. A frond can be sacrificed; a column can be emptied and dismantled; a seed can wait intact through geological spans. None is indispensable. The enduring creature is the pattern of allotment connecting them: so much carbon exposed to the air, so much metal held below, so much collecting surface permitted along a contour, so much bare stone left bare.

Its reproduction is correspondingly reluctant. A seed is made only when the network has accumulated every necessary substance without disturbing its fixed balance. The process may require centuries. The finished capsule contains no embryo in the animal sense, only a compact sequence of instructions embodied in catalysts, conductive fibers, mineral templates, and reserve oils. If carried to a viable seam—and almost none are—it begins by sorting dust. It lays down a microscopic circuit, then waits. Its first visible shoot may emerge long after the capsule that contained it has been reclaimed.

There is no struggle among these beings because struggle has been replaced by boundary chemistry. When two buried systems approach, their outer filaments read one another’s signatures and become inert. The meeting line is sealed, sometimes for millions of years. Each network accepts its allotted minerals, light, and area. Whether that restraint evolved, was imposed, or is simply what life becomes in a world without water cannot be told from the surface.

Nor is it clear that the colonies are insensible. Their synchronized pulses suggest a form of awareness, but not one divided into moments as an animal’s is. A change may take decades to cross a mature network. A shadow falling on one ridge could be registered elsewhere years later as a slight alteration in crystal growth. What such a being experiences, if it experiences anything, would be closer to climate or geology than thought: long differences in illumination, the opening and closing of chemical paths, the gradual arrival of a distant voltage.

The daily line of reflected light may be part of this slow sensing. Each aligned vane warms, charges, and passes a minute electrical bias into the roots beneath it. The visible sequence is only the surface expression of a deeper event moving across colony boundaries. Perhaps it is a clock, resetting distributed processes to the sun. Perhaps it is a census by which the plateau confirms that its farthest organs remain present. Perhaps it is merely an unavoidable consequence of their orientation.

There is no receiver because the whole system receives.

Nothing watches from among the fronds. Watching would be too concentrated, too rapid, and too hungry a thing for this place. Instead, the plateau registers. It preserves differences. It answers light with alignment, depletion with manufacture, damage with reclamation, excess with stillness.

What lives there is an ecology that has made restraint into anatomy. It does not seek to cover the empty ground. It does not hurry toward complexity. It does not fill every available niche. Its most fundamental activity is refusal: not to overspend matter, not to cross a border, not to make two where one can persist, not to let any process outrun the process that balances it.

Across the apparently vacant kilometers, beneath new stone and immaculate dust, that refusal is alive.
