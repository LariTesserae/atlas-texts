The people of the Low Works are compact, deliberate folk, shaped less by ancestry than by the pressures of the place. They tend toward bowed shoulders, broad feet, strong hands, and pale skin grayed by ceramic dust. Their toes spread naturally for purchase on wet tile. Their palms are crosshatched with old cuts from slag blades, and many lack the tips of fingers crushed between brick and lever. In the cold galleries they dress in layered hide, felted hair, linked ceramic scales, and aprons stiffened with clay. Nothing is discarded. A dead parent’s belt may become a child’s harness; a family’s winter cloaks may contain patches older than its recorded name.

Their deepest adaptation is attention.

They hear architecture the way others might hear weather or birds. A resident can distinguish the pop of cooling glaze from the snap of a failing tie, the harmless settling of a partition from weight beginning to migrate through a pier. Children learn to tap walls before they learn all their kin. Adults pause unconsciously beneath arches, feeling vibration through the soles of their feet. Even in conversation, one hand may rest against a column.

They rarely call themselves inhabitants. In most dialects, the ordinary word means something closer to *those presently bearing weight*.

Every person belongs to several overlapping structures. There is the household, which may occupy rooms scattered vertically through one stack of buildings; the rain-share, whose members maintain a linked system of gutters and jars; the brace, obligated to answer structural alarms in a particular quarter; and the echo-line, which keeps the measurements and remembered sounds of buried voids. Kinship matters, but shared walls often matter more. Two families divided by blood and custom may remain inseparable because neither dwelling can stand without the other.

Status is measured in reliability rather than accumulation. A respected person is one whose knots hold, whose cistern accounts balance, whose repairs do not shift strain onto an unsuspecting neighbor. The wealthy possess dry storage, copper fittings, rights to kiln heat, and claims upon upper-level rain. Yet no one owns material absolutely. Every brick is understood to have had previous uses and to await future ones. Possession is only custody between dismantlings.

Names reflect this impermanence. A child receives a private name, a route-name describing how to reach the family rooms, and later a work-name earned through skill or accident: Silt-Taster, Holds-the-East-Step, Three-Pegs, Heard-the-Hollow. Route-names change whenever streets move. To speak an obsolete one is intimate, insulting, or mournful, depending on who hears it.

Children are raised among hazards but not hidden from them. Toddlers wear belts tethered to corridor strings. They learn textured floor warnings as soon as they can crawl and are taught never to follow warmth into an unmarked wall. Their games imitate civic labor: building arches from potsherds, mapping echoes in jars, diverting trickles through miniature channels. A common contest is to dismantle a little tower while keeping a cup balanced atop it. Another is played entirely in darkness, with teams finding one another by tapping coded rhythms through partitions.

Those who survive into old age often become listeners, route-keepers, crack-readers, or teachers of touch. Blindness carries little stigma where so much movement already happens without sight. Indeed, blind guides are trusted in the deepest galleries because they are less deceived by lamps, shadows, and recent construction. Some can cross whole districts with one hand on a knot-string and identify each intersection by its draft.

The builders form no single guild. Clay kneaders, mold setters, kiln tenders, scaffold binders, load counters, arch cutters, glaze mixers, and cooling watchers all guard separate disciplines. Their arguments can outlast buildings. One school believes every crack expresses the city’s intention. Another holds that cracks are merely offers, to be accepted or refused according to need. A more austere sect refuses to draw straight plans, insisting that no line in the Works remains straight once weight has spoken.

Wall-listeners occupy an uneasy position between engineer, physician, and priest. They carry ceramic sounding disks, wax tablets, plumb cords, and small hammers of different densities. Before major construction, they lie in silence along the affected walls and listen through several cycles of kiln firing, cooling, and distant work. Their judgments can evacuate families or condemn entire streets to dismantling. When they disagree, the district may spend weeks whispering, as though loud voices could influence the verdict.

Echo-recorders serve the law that no void may be filled before its echoes are preserved. They enter chambers alone or in pairs, striking walls, clapping, humming fixed notes, and releasing measured strings of ceramic beads. The resulting sounds are marked as grooves, spirals, and stamped intervals on clay tablets. Important chambers are sometimes reproduced as resonant jars. Long after the original room has vanished, its acoustic likeness can be sounded by blowing across the jar’s mouth.

To them, an echo is not merely a property of space. It is the space’s final testimony.

Rainwardens govern the brief abundance after storms. They are among the few permitted to cut seals without prior assembly, diverting water away from fires, unstable vaults, burial corridors, and contaminated cisterns. During droughts they inspect every joint in the catchment system and adjudicate disputes over drops so small they are counted by sound. When rain begins, their authority becomes nearly absolute. They wear high ceramic collars so shouted instructions can be read from the movement of the jaw when the roar drowns speech.

Storms turn the population into something like a single animal. People emerge carrying bowls, plugs, wedges, rags, and lengths of linked pipe. Children kneel beside drains to catch escaping grit. Old people watch overflow marks. Swimmers descend into flooded rooms with air jars tethered to their waists. Boat crews pole through lanes that were staircases the day before. For a few days food, cargo, gossip, and disease move along temporary waterways, and neighborhoods ordinarily separated by arduous climbs become immediate neighbors.

Then the water is hidden away.

Reservoir seals are pressed with dozens of household marks so that no one can open them secretly. Water is rationed by vessel, task, and depth. Drinking water receives priority, then infants, animals, surgery, clay work, cooking, and cleaning. Baths are communal and infrequent. Dust is removed with scrapers, oil, or handfuls of fine ash. Tears are noticed. To weep openly during drought can be read as extravagance, grief beyond restraint, or an accusation that the community has failed to provide enough water for sorrow.

Their food comes from no field.

They keep pale, blind-bodied livestock in warm underrooms: soft grubs fed on rendered scraps, shelled creatures that graze mineral films, and squat hairless animals that digest leather pulp, bone meal, and the edible residue of vat cultures. Meat is rare because nothing large can be fed cheaply. Most nourishment is brewed or cured in sealed ceramic chambers. Dense sour cakes are grown from non-plant cultures feeding on salts, fat, powdered shell, blood, and charcoal-filtered waste. Different vats produce chalky curd, translucent gel, or a dark paste with a metallic taste.

Every household maintains a mother vessel whose living contents may be centuries old. Such cultures are inherited more carefully than rooms. A family displaced by collapse will abandon furniture before abandoning its fermentation jar.

The six-legged mice are tolerated when few and harvested when many. Their lime-scraped tunnels reveal warm cavities and leaking pipes, so some builders watch their movements as omens of structural change. Children set condensation cups near their nests. A mouse drinking calmly is considered evidence that a wall will remain sound through the night.

The eyeless cistern eels are regarded with greater suspicion. They are eaten only after elaborate purging, and their sudden appearance in sealed water is treated as proof that no enclosure is complete. Reservoir priests—though many reject that title—count them after rain and compare scars, lengths, and pigmentation with old records. Certain eels bear rings of mineral staining that resemble fired inscriptions. These are returned alive to the deepest water.

Gutter hounds occupy the boundary between animal and fellow craftsperson. They steal wedges, finger bones, glaze chips, and pieces of marked tile, then arrange them in inaccessible ducts. Some claim these caches are nests; others call them plans. Drain workers leave deliberately chosen shards at grates in exchange for the return of lost tools. A gutter hound that arranges pieces by color is merely tolerated. One that arranges them by age may draw scholars from several wards.

No one is certain what lives below.

The deep knocking has generated whole professions of interpretation. Knock-counters preserve sequences on tablets. Answerers argue over whether the city should respond. Most districts enforce silence, fearing that human hammering might be mistaken for an invitation, challenge, or load signal. A few secret societies descend as far as they can and strike columns in measured patterns. They return with conflicting stories: echoes moving against geometry, warm dust falling upward, replies arriving before the questions.

The conservative explanation is that distant crews inhabit galleries connected by unknown supports.

The feared explanation is that something beneath them is building too.

Death does not remove a person from the Works. It changes their architectural function.

Bodies are dried near low kiln heat until the skin tightens and the joints can be folded. Organs may be sealed separately in small vessels and placed near household water, workrooms, or favored routes. The wrapped body is coated in successive layers of fine clay. The deceased’s names, obligations, errors, and useful instructions are pressed into the outer layer. By law, these figures cannot bear structural weight. In practice, memorial walls often become psychologically indispensable: households refuse to dismantle around them, corridors bend to preserve them, and repairs accumulate until ancestors occupy the center of massive, load-bearing complexes while technically supporting nothing.

Some memorial passages are miles long. Fingers trace the stamped names in darkness. Descendants add new marks beside old ones, correcting routes, recording marriages, noting the loss of doors. Where too many generations have been plastered over, voices sound strangely muted. People say the dead are listening through thicker walls.

Religion, where it can be distinguished from building law, concerns burden, enclosure, and recurrence. The Low Works have few gods with faces. Reverence is directed toward conditions: Weight, which must always pass somewhere; Hollow, which must be heard before it is ended; Rain, which arrives belonging to no one and immediately becomes disputed; Heat, which changes substance but leaves obligation behind.

There are shrines made from branching cracks filled with blue glaze. Worshippers press an ear to one branch and speak into another. There are niches containing the first fragment removed from a condemned house. There are sealed warm walls before which offerings of water are placed and later found empty. At stair shrines, pilgrims descend newly uncovered steps carrying a string tied to the upper floor. Some return after minutes. Some return days later from another district. A few strings go slack without ever reaching an end.

The prohibition against claiming the lowest chamber is both sacred and practical. Every claimant who announces that they have reached the bottom is asked what lay beneath the floor. If they answer “another chamber,” they are mocked for the obvious. If they answer “nothing,” the chamber is sealed and their sanity examined. If they answer “ground,” they are required to produce a piece of it.

No verified piece has ever been produced.

The people do possess a word for soil, preserved in old sayings, but its meaning is disputed. Some define it as loose material that creates itself. Others call it the substance on which construction can rest without passing its burden onward. Many consider the concept indecent: a surface that was not placed, mixed, fired, fitted, or inherited seems less like nature than negligence.

Plant life survives only in language. Patterns called leaf, root, branch, and flower decorate tiles, though no one agrees what the originals looked like. A “tree” may mean a column that divides its load among several arches, a branching crack, or a diagram of descent. Children told that plants once pushed out of unmade ground often imagine green masonry assembling itself in sunlight.

Sunlight itself is half legendary in the central depths. Those who work on the highest roofs describe a brightness that causes pain and flattens detail. Their accounts are treated like travelers’ tales. Most inhabitants know the world through gradients of darkness: kiln-red, salt-blue, culture-violet, wet reflections, and the absolute black of an unentered room.

Beyond the populated districts live dismantlers, furnace clans, cistern hermits, and the keepers of failed routes.

Dismantlers follow collapse. They arrive with baskets, sounding hammers, pry bars, and ownership tablets while dust is still settling. Their work is dangerous and morally delicate. They must decide which fragments remain part of a house, which have become common material, and which bear names or echoes that forbid reuse. They can identify brick by firing quarter, clay source, and centuries of soot. The best can strike a shard once and name three former lives from its tone.

Furnace clans inhabit the orange-lit outliers where air is warmer and walls are young. Their skin is scarred by radiant heat; their speech is clipped to conserve breath. Central residents consider them reckless because they build without enough buried precedent beneath them. Furnace people reply that the old wards are cowards living inside accumulated mistakes.

Cistern hermits dwell near stored water and speak little to avoid contaminating the air with moisture. They maintain seals, skim mineral films, and watch for eels. Some remain in darkness so long that their pupils no longer contract properly. They are sought as judges because water, unlike walls, cannot be repaired once stolen.

The keepers of failed routes maintain ladders, ramps, towers, and approach roads built toward the black boundary wall. Their settlements are full of stretched measuring cords and tiles marked with angles that refuse to add correctly. Each generation invents a new way to reach the rim. They know beforehand that it will fail, but distinguish honorably between repetition and experiment.

The wall has affected them most profoundly. Some shave their heads and braid the hair into climbing rope. Some wear black-glazed masks during rain, facing the upward-running water in silence. Some believe the Works are inside a jar, kiln, body, or instrument. Others contend that beyond the rim lies the first natural surface, and that contact with it would cause the entire city to understand gravity differently.

There are also those who say the wall is not keeping the inhabitants in.

It is carrying them.

This thought recurs during the deepest structural alarms, when towers shriek and strain runs through the basin. For a moment every person feels the same movement beneath their feet—not an isolated collapse, but a slow redistribution across distances too great to map. Cups tremble. Cistern skins wrinkle. Memorial walls murmur with trapped dust.

Then crews take up their wedges and baskets. Families move along knot-strings. Kilns are sealed. Upper rooms are dismantled to strengthen lower vaults. No one waits for certainty.

Whatever the Low Works are, their people understand themselves as part of its structure. They do not seek permanence in any wall, street, house, or body. Permanence belongs only to the act of carrying something else: weight, water, heat, memory, obligation.

And so they continue—pale, scarred, attentive—building upward over unfathomable roofs, preserving the echoes of rooms they must erase, and listening beneath their labor for the measured blows of another laborer whom no one has yet met.
