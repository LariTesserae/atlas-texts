They are the **First Brood**, though no creature among them has a name for itself or for the basin that contains it.

They did not colonize the land. There was no bare interval in which stone cooled, rain gathered, and a few fortunate spores took hold. When the mineral slurry congealed, roots were already threaded through it. When the first pools opened their dark eyes, gilled bodies were already breathing at their bottoms. The animals and their world seem to have been made in a single act—not placed upon the basin, but included in it, as veins are included in flesh.

No one species rules here. The nearest thing to a dominant organism is the entire ecology.

Its smallest inhabitants are almost transparent: mites like moving grains of glass, worms whose organs show through milk-pale skins, soft grazers that scrape bacterial films from leaves without visibly diminishing them. They live in condensation beads, in the damp chambers of fungi, beneath scales and eyelids, inside the porous rock. Many never encounter open air. To them, a frog’s throat or a snail’s mantle is a country with its own climate.

The insects give the basin its texture and voice. Beetles wear hard shells of oil-black, green bronze, violet, and wet copper. Flies gather so thickly that their bodies seem to darken the air itself. Great dragonflies keep to narrow aerial lanes inherited with perfect fidelity; each patrols the same corridor above the same pool, and when one dies another soon occupies its path. The stationary columns of gnats are less like swarms than organs of the landscape, rising wherever vapor rises, endlessly dispersing and restoring their shape.

The frogs are innumerable. Some fit beneath a curled leaf edge. Others are broad and heavy, their backs furred with moss or stippled with glands that shine in the dimness. They stack together in cool hollows, skin against skin, exchanging moisture, heat, parasites, and perhaps something like information. Their calls are precise and continuous. No individual begins the chorus, and none appears to answer. Each merely holds its assigned place in a sound older than itself.

In the pools live animals that have never completed the transition from water to land. Long amphibians drift just below the surface with red gills spread around their heads like ceremonial fronds. Pale burrowers breathe through chimneys in the mineral floor. Flat predators lie under silt so fine that only the slow movement of their eyes betrays them. Broad-backed grazers carry floating gardens: moss, snails, insect eggs, and flowering runners rooted in the creases of their hides. Sometimes what seems to be an island inhales.

The larger land animals are low-built and powerful. Their flesh is warm, but only barely warmer than the air. They travel through permanent tunnels in the vegetation, placing each foot where uncounted feet have fallen before. Some browse with jointed forelimbs that unfold farther than their compact bodies suggest. Others crop fungi with blunt, tiled mouths. Their skins are rarely bare. Algae haze their flanks; mites occupy folds around their joints; small frogs shelter in moist hollows along their backs. An old animal may support so much secondary life that its outline becomes difficult to distinguish from the plants around it.

They do not migrate, court seasonally, or gather in response to weather. Reproduction occurs continually and without urgency. Eggs are tucked into leaf cups already occupied by larvae. Young emerge into herds that seem neither to welcome nor reject them. Birth adds no lasting abundance, because elsewhere, at nearly the same pace, something is being eaten, dissolved, shed, or drawn back into the mud.

The predators are equally woven into this balance. They do not roam in hunger. Each keeps an appointed threshold: a pool edge, a root arch, a dark opening beneath a fungal shelf. There it waits, sometimes long enough for moss to establish on its hide. Its camouflage is not merely resemblance. Liverwort roots into the grooves of a jaw; insects breed in the damp pits around an eye; vines loop across a motionless spine. When prey crosses the unseen limit, the hunter strikes with startling violence. Then it returns to precisely the posture the landscape expects of it.

Even death is communal and brief. Scavengers arrive before a carcass cools—pale, folding things from beneath the soil, beetles from stems, soft mouths from the water. Fungi bloom through nostrils and wounds. Seeds germinate in the released heat. For a little while the dead creature becomes a crowded mound, more visibly alive than it was before. Then skeleton, scavengers, and seedlings all sink into the same green-brown continuity.

No creature here seems fully separate from its surroundings. Bodies leak into habitat, and habitat enters bodies. Spores sleep in lungs. Larvae mature inside fruit and flesh alike. Roots grow through discarded shells. Snails armor animals that carry them between pools, though “between” may be the wrong word in a place where every pool participates in the same hidden water.

The First Brood possess no culture that can be detached from instinct, yet their behavior has the gravity of ritual. Routes are never varied. Calls do not drift. Hunting stations pass from body to body. Generations repeat gestures upon ground younger than the gestures themselves. It is as if the basin remembers for them—or as if each creature is only a temporary instrument through which the basin repeats one of its thoughts.

They are not peaceful. They sting, crush, parasitize, drown, swallow, and consume one another without pause. Nor are they cruel. Cruelty would require distance between the actor and the wound, and there is almost no distance here. The eater will in turn be eaten; the parasite carries smaller parasites; the blood taken from one body feeds eggs that will nourish another. Violence does not disturb the equilibrium. It is one of the means by which equilibrium speaks.

What lives in the basin is therefore not a collection of beasts so much as a single, distributed creature pretending to be many.

Its breath is the cloud and condensation.  
Its blood is the water welling up and sinking away.  
Its nerves are roots, fungal threads, tunnels, and inherited paths.  
Its skin is the crowded vegetation.  
Its voice is the unbroken chord of wings, throats, reeds, and gills.

The animals are its moving organs, born already knowing where to wait, where to feed, where to call, and where to die.

And if the basin has a mind, it is not dreaming of an older world or preparing for a world to come. It knows only this completed instant. It has never seen the sun, never endured a season, never watched a river cut its first channel. It does not imagine change.

It lives at the beginning of time with the habits of something eternal.
