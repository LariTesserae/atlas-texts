The basin belongs first to the **mist**.

It is not merely weather passing through, but the oldest inhabitant: a vast, diffuse creature forever climbing from the valleys, breaking itself against the cliffs, and gathering again from the water flung over the brink. It has no single body. Its breath is the wind in the stone teeth; its tracks are black trails beneath the seeps; its brief, innumerable eyes are the droplets that hold the inverted peaks. Everything else lives inside its circulation.

Closest to the ground is a dense, low nation of plants. Mosses lie over the basin floor in saturated quilts, thick enough to hide running water and swallow fallen stone. Sedges stitch the wet places together. Cushion plants build hard green islands against frost, sheltering beetles, larvae, and seeds in their warm interiors. Creeping willows, ancient despite their ankle height, travel sideways through the turf. Their trunks are hidden under moss, so that what appears to be a patch of separate shrubs may be one organism spread across generations.

The flowers bloom close to this hidden world: blue bells sealed with wax, yellow stars pressed low against the wind, tiny white blossoms occupying the bogs in boulder cups, and red chalices that collect whatever the air relinquishes. None can afford height. Their abundance is horizontal—overlapping leaves, braided roots, stems that crawl rather than rise.

Within that vegetation live the basin’s smallest keepers. Copper-backed beetles move through moss tunnels where the air remains damp and comparatively warm. Translucent gnats sleep beneath leaves, then rise in shining columns when sun reaches the floor. Minute insects complete their lives inside the water held by a single stone. Some pools contain whole lineages isolated from neighboring pools by only a few feet of drying rock.

Speckled amphibians inhabit the gentler seeps. They are small and dark, with pale markings like mineral flecks. During cold periods they wedge themselves under saturated moss or into cracks warmed by the stored heat of stone. When the basin clouds over, they emerge to hunt. Their eggs hang beneath submerged leaves in clear beads, indistinguishable at first from the condensation covering everything else.

Long-eared rodents occupy burrows under the rooted boulders. They are woolly, compact, and almost absurdly busy, feeding whenever the weather permits. They shear cushion plants with tiny incisors, gather fallen seed cases, and carry clipped grasses underground to dry in chambers lined with fur. Their ears turn independently toward water, wings, and cracking stone. At dusk they appear all across the floor, yet each remains close to an escape hole. The basin may seem suddenly crowded with them, then empty at one passing shadow.

Night brings larger visitors from the ridges. Mountain grazers descend by routes invisible to human eyes, placing narrow hooves among wet stones. They lick salt from the weeping walls and scrape mineral crusts from beneath ledges. Their weight opens the moss, redirects rivulets, and makes shallow basins in which insects and amphibians later breed. They do not stay. Before dawn they climb back into the bare heights, leaving only prints full of reflected sky.

The air belongs to birds.

At first light, pale swifts spill through the open side as if poured upward from the ravine. They turn inside the hollow without losing speed, taking gnats from above the sedges and drinking droplets in flight. Their nesting cracks are marked by feathers caught to wet stone. Later come smaller flocking birds that forage among the seed heads, their cries magnified until a few dozen sound like thousands. Broad-winged scavengers patrol the rim, almost motionless, waiting for cold, hunger, or a missed foothold to yield them something.

All of these recognize the basin’s quiet hunter.

The gray cat lives mainly in the walls. Thick fur blurs its outline against lichen and frost-paled rock; broad paws distribute its weight over slick shelves. It moves through cracks too narrow to appear passable and waits beside the routes between burrow and feeding ground. Rarely does it cross the open green. When it does, every bird sees it at once. The basin erupts, and the cat becomes the still center of a storm of wings and warning cries.

Humans do not truly live here now, though they once came often enough to leave habits embedded in stone.

The shelter against the inner wall remembers many sleepers. Its low entrance excludes wind better than any upright door, and its soot records fires laid centuries apart in the same shallow hearth. People climbed to the basin for water, refuge, hunting, gathering, or rites whose meanings have worn away. They repaired the catchment gutter with timber hauled from below. They reset the roof after frost shifted its slabs. They sharpened blades, hung meat or clothing from bone pegs, and crawled out before storms sealed the ridges.

They also observed the basin’s central courtesy: what enters the water must be fit to return.

For that reason ash was buried rather than scattered. Blood was covered. Waste was carried beyond the mist’s circuit when possible. The short cairns leading to the brink may have guided travelers through cloud, but the white pebbles beneath their capstones suggest something more deliberate. Perhaps each marks a visit, a death, a promise, or a thing surrendered without being thrown away. Whatever the practice was, the path ends where water rises instead of falling.

Now the shelter serves other bodies. Rodents nest in old bedding hollows. Beetles inhabit the soot. A bird may roost on the bone pegs. Moss slowly seals the roof while meltwater searches for the same gaps human hands once repaired. The trough still fills, and animals drink from a basin cut for people who are no longer there to claim it.

No single creature rules this place. The basin is inhabited by a **circulation**: mist becoming drops, drops becoming hidden streams, streams torn into mist again; plants becoming grazers, grazers becoming hunters and scavengers, bones becoming mineral and moss. Even the mountain participates, feeding iron, chalk, and salt into the water while frost loosens it grain by grain.

Here, to live is not merely to endure exposure. It is to become part of the basin’s return.

At evening, the flying things withdraw, the woolly grazers surface, and the gray cat begins to move. The moss releases the day’s warmth. Amphibians wait beside the pools. Old willow branches flex beneath the turf. In the dark shelter, condensation gathers on soot left by forgotten fires.

Above them all, the mist-creature climbs once more from the unseen valley.

It reaches the brink, is broken by the wind, and comes home across the walls in a thousand bright bodies.
