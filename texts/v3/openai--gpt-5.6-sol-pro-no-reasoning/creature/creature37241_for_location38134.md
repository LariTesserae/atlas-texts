What lives there is not a population but a set of evasions.

The colorless worms are the simplest. They inhabit those fissures cool enough not to seal around them, fastening their soft rear segments deep in the basalt and raising glassy crowns into the water. Their fans are neither gills nor mouths alone. They sift heat, mineral, vibration, and perhaps the unfinished intentions of the rock. When the basin’s deepest note begins, every worm retracts at once. For a few breaths the cracks appear empty. Afterward the fans emerge altered: more branches, fewer branches, or branches describing shapes like tiny doorways.

The blind crustaceans live under ledges in tight, separate congregations. They do not feed while watched. Their forelimbs continually test the stone, tapping out the approach of disturbances that have not yet occurred. Just before a quake they fold themselves flat and wedge into hairline fractures. Sometimes the earthquake fails to happen. Sometimes one happened hours earlier. The crustaceans flee it all the same. Their translucent shells contain fine black lines like maps of vanished trenches. No two maps agree.

Higher up drift the knot-creatures, clear bodies loosely looped around a single green filament. The filament is older than the flesh enclosing it and seems to direct the whole animal by bending toward whatever part of the water is least illuminated. They do not swim. Space gathers on one side of them and loosens on the other.

Alone, a knot-creature may persist for years. Two touching become one larger and more intricate knot, their green filaments splicing seamlessly together. Three cannot coexist. At the instant of contact, bodies and filaments vanish, leaving a square absence with edges too straight for water. The square lasts only seconds, but inside it the sea can be seen without distortion. Those who have looked report not the basin, but a green, sunless expanse filled with forests of the same filament, rooted in darkness and awaiting bodies.

The upside-down cups beneath the dry ledges may be related. Their surfaces resemble plants, but when cut they bleed warm brine and draw their wounds closed. They collect darkness as ordinary flowers collect sunlight, compacting it until their centers become deep enough to conceal the finger inserted into them. When full, they open and release a pulse of cold.

These pulses are their offspring—or their predators.

Each moves as a small region in which warmth has ceased to be true. Currents shear around it. Worms withdraw. Knot-creatures loosen themselves and drift aside. Wherever a pulse lingers, frostlike mineral branches form despite the heated water, and for a short time tiny black buds appear on them. Most dissolve when the warmth returns. A few detach and climb against the current toward the ledges, becoming cup-plants on arrival.

Something much greater also inhabits the sea.

It is not clear whether there is one such being, several, or only a single body encountered at different points along a length the basin cannot contain. What is seen is always a pale middle: smooth, unmarked, without fin, scale, scar, or discernible anatomy. It passes beyond the apparent far wall for an hour at a time, although its motion is neither swimming nor falling. Water bends around it before it arrives. The continuous roar deepens. The seven arches incline toward its passage.

No head has entered the basin. No tail has left it.

It may be threaded through the summit as a needle is threaded through cloth, with only one small portion crossing the sea at any moment. It may be the creature whose ribs the arches expose. It may live outside and regard the suspended water as a pore in some boundary, pressing its body against the opening to listen.

Or the long pale body may be the basin’s sole mature animal, while everything else—worms, cups, knots, arches, storms—is its larval anatomy assembling and disassembling around it.

The seven arches themselves behave enough like living things to deserve inclusion. Their basalt uprights have a faint grain, as if grown in layers. Their limestone spans are porous and warm. The red mineral coating them thickens after concussions and peels away in translucent sheets that curl briefly like tongues before dissolving. When an arch appears on the outer slope, the lichens nearest it pale, and the salt grass flattens toward it.

They do not move while perceived. They alter their arrangement in the unowned intervals between perceptions: a blink, a shock, the transit of a silence-pocket. Their changing formations may be locomotion too slow to occur within continuous time. A circle around the central vent could be rest. A scattered arrangement may be feeding. An arch standing alone on the dry ridge, dripping uphill, may be an attempt to leave.

The shallow steps and narrow shafts nearby are not their constructions. They are impressions of use without users. The basin produces them as flesh produces scars. Some correspond briefly to human proportions, but others suit bodies with strides longer than the hollow or feet no wider than a grain of sand. Their inconsistency suggests that the place is not remembering one vanished inhabitant. It is trying on the accommodations of many.

The moving chambers of silence are inhabited as well, though not necessarily by creatures.

Within them, the absence of sound has pressure and behavior. It recoils from vent-light, divides around the arches, and sometimes follows a swimmer after the transparent boundary has passed. A large chamber may contain minute motions: dim folds turning in empty water, visible only because nothing roars across them. On rare occasions, a person standing inside one sees impressions appear on the chamber wall from the outer side, as if soft, numerous mouths were pressed against glass.

When the pocket collapses, these impressions become sound.

The other figure on the rim is the most recognizable inhabitant and therefore perhaps the least trustworthy. It is shaped like the observer because the basin seems able to stabilize life only by borrowing an existing outline. Yet it is no mirror. It bears scars from accidents that did not occur, favors the opposite hand, and pauses before features that have not formed in the observer’s present. Sometimes it kneels where an escaped doorway will later stand. Sometimes it turns sharply toward a noise still sealed inside the mountain.

If approached, it remains across the basin no matter how far around the circumference one walks. If greeted, it may answer days earlier through vibrations in the rock. If both figures enter the water, only one is known to return, but the returning one cannot prove which summit they began on.

Most life here survives by refusing a fixed account of itself. Bodies merge, vanish, recur as mineral, or occupy only a segment of their own extent. Plants root in dry stone and feed on darkness. Animals anticipate events that may belong to another version of the basin. Structures grow, migrate, and arrange themselves around an unseen anatomy. Even fossils on the outer slopes may be emigrants: creatures that escaped not by moving outward, but by becoming their own remains.

Yet there may be a deeper resident beneath all of these—the thing for which the warm sea is not habitat but expression.

Its circulation appears as currents. Its fever rises from the vents. Its nerves are the crawling blue lightning. The low note felt in the teeth may be its voice, or merely a joint moving under the mountain. The inward-breaking waves at the dome’s summit resemble breath drawn into a lung. The expanding distances, reversed depths, and leaking chronology may be the failures of a vast life trying to fit its experience inside a hamlet-sized hollow.

The mountain’s bare peaks lean away because they are not walls. They are splinters.

And the sea remains because it is not being held there.

It is holding something in.
