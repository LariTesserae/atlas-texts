What lives here is difficult to divide into inhabitants and habitation. The high country supports few creatures in the ordinary sense. Its principal life is the country itself.

The root pavement, colonnades, courts, bridges, wetlands, and seed-towers appear to be differentiated organs of one vast vascular being. It has no central trunk or visible body plan. Instead, it expresses itself architecturally. Buttressed halls may serve as lungs or resonating chambers; circular pools as exposed bladders; hollow towers as reservoirs; causeways as veins or nerves. Its water is sap only in the broadest sense, bearing nutrients, heat, pressure, and vibration through every structure.

It is alive, but not developing. Every tissue remains perpetually new: seams knit without closing, crowns bud without lengthening, leaves flex without aging, and embryonic ravines hold their first crease forever. Its metabolism consists of immense circulation without transfer, labor without result. The thunder beneath the pavement may be the sound of this arrested physiology—the whole organism straining to complete a single pulse.

Within it live a few smaller beings, though none seems wholly independent.

Pearl-gray amphibians occupy the geometric pools. They are tall, smooth, and nearly featureless, with limbs fine enough to resemble pale reeds. A raised foot may remain raised for years. They neither hunt nor flee, and perhaps do not feed. Moisture passes continuously through their skin, suggesting that they are less animals than permeable valves: places where the plateau’s internal water briefly assumes an animal shape.

Black winged things inhabit the ribs of the great halls. From below they resemble birds, but their bodies also have the hard simplicity of seeds. Each travels endlessly between two fixed perches. They never nest, forage, or explore. Their repeated flight may pump air through the vaults, stitch separated tissues together, or measure a distance the larger organism needs to remember.

Beneath translucent sections of floor move clear grazing forms. Each contains a green growth along its central axis, like a sapling enclosed in flesh. It is impossible to tell whether an animal carries a plant, a plant wears an animal, or both are temporary organs of the same system. They follow closed paths through amber water, tending submerged root surfaces without visibly consuming them.

Certain white flowers generate perfect circles of silence. They may be sensory organs, suppressors, or mouths held permanently shut. Unopened buds contain the same dense quiet. Other plants produce tones instead: widely spaced reeds boom across the courts, seed-vaults crack without splitting, and broad leaves clap without contact. In this place, sound may perform the work that growth and movement perform elsewhere. The plateau communicates internally by resonance, pressure, and exclusion.

Even its apparent debris remains alive. Fallen leaves are tethered by invisible filaments. Loosened wood separates into finer organisms rather than decaying. The brown matter in seams is not soil but a community—or tissue—of root hairs, viable pollen, spores, and contracting nodules. Nothing becomes waste. Nothing is released from participation.

Thus there may be no true predator, prey, scavenger, parasite, or corpse here. Life does not pass from one body into another by eating. It circulates through a hierarchy of forms whose boundaries are provisional. An amphibian, a pool, a reed, and the buried root beneath them may be separate creatures, castes of one colonial organism, or successive surfaces of a single process.

And somewhere below the visible shelf is the greater inhabitant: the mass whose upper tissues resemble a country.

Its true scale cannot be known. The warm basins may be folds in its skin. Towers may descend farther than they rise. The inverted arches glimpsed through cloud suggest roots that are also limbs, repeated in orientations for which “up” has little meaning. What appears to be a mountain range may instead be one organism’s body emerging into the sky.

It is embryonic without being young, finished without being mature. All the plateau’s lesser lives inhabit the held instant of its formation. Perhaps they, too, are unfinished gestures: an animal about to take a step, a seed about to fly, a grazer about to depart its circuit. None can complete the act.

The being that lives here may therefore be described most accurately not as a plant, a city, or a landscape, but as **a beginning large enough to have inhabitants**—a beginning that circulates, breathes, sounds, and dreams of motion, while never passing into what comes next.
