The High Basin belongs to creatures for whom permanence would be fatal.

Its smallest inhabitants are the gray films on the masonry. They lie so close to the fitted slabs that they can be mistaken for discoloration: many-sided patches divided by darker seams, each patch no thicker than damp breath on glass. They do not grow toward light. They grow toward pressure—over the upstream faces of blocks, into the constricted throats of arches, along the undersides of thresholds where water is forced thin and fast.

A single film may cover only a handspan, but neighboring films join without quite merging. Together they make colonies extending across entire plazas. When currents slacken, their surfaces loosen into microscopic cilia and strain powdered ceramic, oxide, and organic debris from the water. When a shadow falls, they contract. The response passes outward polygon by polygon, slowly enough to watch: a dim tightening that leaves the stone apparently bare, followed minutes later by its return.

They are eaten by the chalk-mouths.

These pale nodules stud walls like mineral disease. Each contains a ring of tiny apertures, though only one opens at a time. They graze without moving, dissolving narrow circles in the films beneath them. When the colony has exhausted the stone around it, its interior softens and divides. The halves detach during a strong current, tumble through the basin, and adhere wherever they strike a suitable surface. Thus their migrations resemble weathering. A wall may whiten over a century, lose its coat in a single storm, and seed another district with what looks like a fall of hail.

The translucent crusts are more elusive. They spread across polished masonry in sheets clear enough to reveal the stone beneath. No mouth or organ is visible within them. Yet when animals pass overhead, the sheets pucker into shallow lenses, briefly gathering what little light exists. During rare calms, whole floors shine with these contractions, one after another, as though the drowned city were opening a multitude of blind eyes.

Above the terraces move the beadspines.

They are nearly invisible except for the chain of black vertebral knots running from skull to tail. Their flesh has the refractive quality of cold water; their fins are thin pleats that fold into the body when they turn. Each carries a small iron-rich sac beside the brain, and through it senses changes in the masonry below—the knock of a shifting tile, the tremor of a loosening arch, the faint agitation preceding an upwelling.

A shoal does not merely flee danger. It draws the invisible structure of the water. Thousands turn along the margins of a vortex, forming momentary bowls and spirals. They stream through passages before those passages are obvious to the eye. When a district begins to rearrange, beadspines gather above it in pale layers, feeding on organisms shaken loose from the stones. Their black spinal marks make them resemble writing suspended in water, a script that continually erases and rewrites itself.

They spawn in the hollow towers. During certain storm pressures, adults enter through the highest perforations and circle downward, leaving strands of eggs along the inner walls. The tower’s moaning vibrates the strands and keeps sediment from settling on them. Newly hatched beadspines emerge when the note deepens. A silent tower is abandoned, however intact it appears.

The scavengers of the galleries are heavier creatures, broad across the joints and low to the ground. Their shells incorporate whatever mineral surrounds them. Those from tiled districts are smooth and cream-colored; those from iron-dark walls carry rust-red ridges; some dwelling among collapsed reliefs become so irregular that, at rest, they are indistinguishable from broken ornament.

Each has eight load-bearing limbs and two finer appendages kept folded beneath the head. In calm water they walk with painstaking delicacy, testing every slab before trusting it. Their tapping travels farther through the masonry than through the water. Individuals answer one another across walls and floors, producing sparse exchanges of paired and tripled knocks.

When a current rises, they seek sockets cut—or worn—into the architecture. They insert their wedge-shaped bodies and extend their limbs against opposing surfaces. The harder the water pulls, the more tightly the joints lock. Many sockets contain pale shell fragments from creatures that misjudged the force.

Afterward, the scavengers emerge into the silt clouds. They feed on crushed beadspines, shed membranes, chalk-mouth colonies, and the soft bodies exposed when blocks grind together. They also collect small objects. In deep galleries there are caches of ceramic chips sorted by curvature, oxide nodules arranged from dark to light, and transparent shell plates stacked with improbable care. Whether these are stores, mating displays, or the residue of compulsive handling is unclear. When the chamber itself comes apart, the scavengers follow their collections as long as they can, retrieving pieces from among moving stones.

The broad swimmers of the open basin are called mantles only because their bodies seem worn rather than inhabited. Their upper surfaces are blue-black, their undersides the color of old porcelain. They have no visible eyes. A fringe of sensory pits runs around the entire body, allowing them to feel pressure from every direction at once.

Mantles spend days nearly motionless in the central water, supported by layers of differing density. Then, without obvious cause, they begin to travel. Their fins undulate so slowly that the motion is lost against their forward glide. Shoals part around them. Smaller animals sometimes shelter beneath their white undersides, making each mantle carry a moving constellation.

They feed where currents intersect. Opening long folds along the body’s leading edge, they sieve out films, larvae, mineral-rich detritus, and the tiny transparent organisms that bloom after walls collapse. Their feeding leaves clean corridors through suspended silt. From above, a migrating group can look like holes moving through the dark.

They avoid the deepest avenues.

There live the callers.

No complete caller has been seen in clear water. What is known comes in portions: a pale flank beyond an archway; a row of depressions large enough to be eyes but sealed with translucent tissue; a jointed edge passing behind towers; scars on polished floors where something heavy has rested. Their bodies appear long and soft, yet armored internally by plates that grind together when they turn.

Their voices enter the basin through contact. A caller settles some part of itself against a floor or wall and sends a descending vibration into the masonry. Because every slab touches others, and because the architecture is always being tightened or loosened by pressure, the note may travel for miles. It can surface from beneath a distant terrace or tremble through a tower long after the animal itself has moved on.

Callers seem to use the changing structure as a sensory organ. By listening to the return of their own notes, they learn which chambers are hollow, which causeways are under strain, and where new corridors have assembled. A fresh wall may look ancient to every eye, but it answers differently from one that has borne weight for centuries.

Their silences carry equal meaning. When one caller stops midway through a sequence, beadspines compress into tight spheres, scavengers lock into the nearest sockets, and mantles tilt their bodies vertically. Then something passes through the deep.

This is the floor-shadow glimpsed during calm.

It may not be one species, or one organism. Observers have described an extent too broad to reconcile with any stable anatomy: a dark margin advancing across tiled courts; low arches rising briefly beneath it like knuckles; clouds of scavengers fleeing ahead. At times it seems flexible, pouring through avenues. At others it moves as a rigid mass, and towers disappear behind it one by one.

The oldest mantles bear circular scars matching the mouths of chambers in the lowest courses. Caller-notes cease around those same openings. The gray films withdraw hours before the shadow arrives, exposing lanes of clean masonry along its path.

Some believe the thing feeds upon the basin’s life. Yet carcasses are not found after it passes. Instead, the architecture changes. Walls loosen in its wake. Blocks rise, roll, and lodge elsewhere. Courts constrict. Trenches acquire ribs. Doorways appear where solid courses stood before.

It may be an animal that uses the architecture as a shell.

It may be a colony threaded through the spaces beneath the visible floor, extending soft pressure into sockets and seams. The vast form seen moving could be only the coordinated darkening of many buried bodies. It may even be responsible for the basin’s preference for corridors and towers—not by planning them, but because water flowing around its hidden anatomy sorts the loosened blocks into shapes that resemble structures.

Whatever it is, the smaller inhabitants have adapted to its passage. Beadspines follow hours later to feed in the stirred water. Scavengers inspect newly opened cracks. Chalk-mouths settle on fresh surfaces. Callers return and sound the altered districts, giving long notes whose echoes have never existed before.

None of these creatures treats the High Basin as a drowned place. They possess no memory of dryness, no distinction between architecture and habitat. A tower is a spawning throat. A gallery is shelter from current. A polished wall is pasture. A staircase is a sequence of pressure shadows. To the callers, the whole basin may be one vast instrument, temporarily arranged.

Even death rarely stays with the dead. Soft bodies vanish into filters and mouths. Shells are ground into the gray suspension. Mineral plates lodge between blocks and alter their fit. A scavenger’s armor may become the shim that holds an arch in place; generations later, when the arch loosens, that same fragment may drift free and be built into another animal.

So the basin’s life participates in its rebuilding without becoming its builder. It clings, grazes, wedges, listens, vibrates, and migrates. Its inhabitants do not command the moving masonry, but neither are they merely carried by it. Their habits bias the water. Their bodies obstruct apertures, strengthen joints, open seams, and seed surfaces. Across ages, such slight pressures accumulate.

During the brief calms, when the drowned country can be seen descending in layers, the creatures appear sparse and separate: shoals over roads, mantles in the middle dark, scavengers among the chambers, callers hidden below.

Then the rain resumes. Currents cross. The apparent separations dissolve.

The High Basin moves through them, and they through it, until it is difficult to say whether the place contains a living world or has, by immeasurable patience, become one.
