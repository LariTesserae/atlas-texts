Those who live in the Wash are compact, broad-bodied people, built close to the ground.

Their legs are short and strong, with wide, flexible feet suited to slick rock and sucking mud. The toes spread under weight, much as the pads of the stilted walkers do, though each is edged by a thick, ridged nail. Their knees and hips permit a deep, stable crouch. Even at rest they seem braced against current or vibration. Falls are common in childhood and dangerous at any age, so they walk with bent legs and one hand often lowered toward the stone.

Their skin is gray-brown, made paler by cold and almost black when wet. A fine layer of oil gives it a muted sheen and slows the loss of heat, but does not keep them dry. Nothing in the Wash remains dry. They have little body hair except for dense patches along the spine, forearms, and scalp. Their hair is coarse and lies flat in overlapping whorls that shed water away from the eyes and neck.

The face is blunt and sheltered. Heavy brow ridges protect small, dark eyes from spray. The nose is low, with narrow closeable nostrils; the ears are folded nubs, better protected from cold and injury than adapted for hearing. Their hearing is not poor, but sound has become a secondary sense. Generations beneath the Long Fall have favored people who feel the world through bone.

The palms, soles, breastbone, and lower jaw are particularly sensitive to vibration. A person kneeling with both hands upon the rock can detect approaching footsteps, an animal struggling in a pool, a change in a familiar current, or the distant collapse of a ledge. They do not experience these as sounds exactly. They describe them as textures: rough, hollow, crowded, split, returning. A skilled traveler can distinguish kin by gait long before seeing them through the mist.

Their spoken language is intimate and seldom used at any distance. It consists of low vowels, clicks made against the teeth, and voiced pulses that can be felt through the jaw when two people touch foreheads. Public language belongs to the hands and feet. Broad gestures convey simple matters across open ground, while detailed speech is tapped into a listener’s palm, wrist, sternum, or copper chest plate. Pressure, interval, direction, and the number of fingers used all alter meaning.

Consequently, conversation is physically close. Friends often walk wrist-to-wrist. A parent corrects a child by laying a sentence along the child’s back. Agreements are pressed into joined palms. An apology is made with the speaker’s hand turned upward and placed beneath the offended person’s heel, offering not submission so much as a promise to bear their weight. To refuse contact is a grave act. To touch someone without first showing an open hand is worse.

Names are rhythms. A child receives a temporary name from its heartbeat, then earns a lasting one from some recognizable pattern: the cadence of its walk, a habitual tool-stroke, the tapping it makes when impatient. Spoken equivalents exist, but are used chiefly by family members at very close range. The same person may therefore have a hand-name, a floor-name, and a quiet mouth-name known only within the house.

They dress in layered shellback hide, worn with the slick surface outward. Garments fit close at the torso and hang in overlapping panels over the thighs so water can escape without exposing much skin. Hoods extend into brow flaps; gloves leave the central palm bare. Clothing that completely insulates its wearer from the ground is considered dangerous. Even sleeping skins have openings through which a hand or heel may rest against the floor.

Across the chest lies the most valued possession many adults own: a thin copper plate, usually oval, suspended by braided sinew. Its dents and discolorations are not polished away. Each changes the plate’s answer and gradually makes its vibration distinctive to the wearer. Plates pass between generations, but never unchanged. The inheritor adds a small hammer mark so the dead person’s pattern is carried inside a new one.

Most waking hours are spent obtaining enough fat.

The people drag weighted nets through mineral-rich pools, turn shellbacks with hooked poles, scrape translucent filter-feeders from overhangs, and dig sealed burrows from the mud after floods. Hunting parties move in loose lines, communicating through the rock with weighted staffs. They rarely stalk in perfect stillness, for stillness belongs to the six-limbed predators. Instead they maintain a soft, irregular pattern of foot-taps that announces them as people. When the pattern stops, everyone nearby becomes alert.

They eat much of their food raw or fermented, conserving lamp fuel. Shellback meat is chopped with stone and sealed in skin bags with mineral water until it softens. Sieve-mouths are pressed into pale cakes. Tiny darting animals are dried in dense sheets on stone warmed from below. The choicest food is the layer of fat beneath a mature shellback’s central scar. It is offered first to children, pregnant people, the injured, and anyone who must keep a lamp alive through the flood season.

Their houses are warm only by comparison with the world outside. Several generations sleep together around a low lamp, bodies arranged like fitted stones. Privacy is created not by walls but by facing away and ceasing to answer touch. Interior conversation runs through the floor: knuckles against slabs, heels dragging, copper plates held to the ribs. The roof is so low that everyone crawls or crouches, and this too is deliberate. A high room would lose heat, gather louder echoes, and give floodwater more leverage.

Households are not defined solely by ancestry. A household is whoever keeps the same lamp.

The lamp flame may be centuries old in lineage, though not in unbroken combustion. When it dies, a coal or wick is requested from another house. The recipient then owes warmth: food, labor, shelter, or care during a future flood. These debts create a web between widely separated dwellings. People remember them through short tactile formulas tapped at the wrist. Some debts survive the deaths of both parties.

There are few permanent leaders. Authority belongs to whoever knows the relevant piece of the Wash. A channel-reader leads crossings. A bone-worker decides how a carcass is divided for tools. A keeper of routes maintains cairns and knows which stones have shifted. During floods, those who understand the basalt pillar are obeyed without dispute.

Its keepers are recognizable by seven scars or gray pigment marks along the inner forearm, corresponding to the seven tongues within the pillar. They spend long periods seated around it with hands on the saturated ground, recording its drops in knotted sinew and sequences of drilled shell. They do not claim to understand the pillar. Understanding would imply ownership, and nothing that predicts the water can safely be spoken of as owned.

Their caution is justified. The pillar does not repeat itself exactly.

Most of its variations correspond to rain, pressure, lake height, or distant subterranean flow. Some do not. On rare occasions it begins a pattern associated with flood and then arrests midway. At such times animals leave low ground even under a clear iron sky. The keepers still order retreat.

No keeper has opened the pillar, but not all agree that this restraint is wisdom. There are those who believe its passages could be copied, allowing smaller warning pillars to be built throughout the Wash. Others hold that the pillar does not merely predict floods but regulates them—that clearing its outlets and replacing its cups are parts of an operation whose original purpose has been forgotten. To alter it might drown the whole depression or still the Long Fall.

This disagreement is not argued aloud. It is performed in two versions of an old dance.

In one, the dancer cups both hands, receives the falling water, and passes it onward. In the other, the dancer strikes the stone, opens hidden paths, and makes the water obey. Children learn both sequences before they are told they contradict one another.

Their histories are stored in bodies. A formal remembrance is danced barefoot while witnesses kneel in a ring with palms flat against the stone. Steps carry events; direction marks kinship; dragged heels indicate loss; leaps—rare and conspicuous among such grounded people—mean journeys beyond known routes. A skilled historian can transmit several simultaneous accounts by varying heel, toe, staff, and chest plate. The witnesses do not merely watch. They feel the story arrive through the floor.

These histories change with the stone beneath them. The same dance performed on a hollow shelf and on dense basalt does not mean precisely the same thing. Thus a history belongs partly to its place. When a flood breaks a memorial floor, some portion of what happened there becomes unrepeatable.

Death is managed quickly. The dead are opened, cleaned, and divided before the raspers find them. Fat feeds the household lamp. Hide may become a waterproof inner layer, though never a visible garment. Teeth become beads for counting obligations. Long bones are made into awls, hooks, net spacers, or sounding pins. The skull is usually mortared into the dwelling wall with the face turned inward, where its cavities subtly alter the house’s vibrations.

This is not understood as preserving the person whole. Wholeness ends at death. Instead, the dead are distributed among necessary things. A grandmother may remain as a needle, part of a doorway seal, three counting beads, and a tone in the eastern wall. The living mention her by touching each in sequence.

Bone left unused is considered a sorrow.

Children are raised with particular attentiveness because the Wash is full of quiet deaths: a shallow pool with a hidden current, a predator confused by the Fall, a patch of mud that liquefies under one foot. Infants are carried in inflated-hide slings high on the chest, where they feel the carrier’s breathing and copper plate. Before they can walk, adults place their palms against different floors and tap simple distinctions into their backs: solid, hollow, flowing beneath, unsafe.

Their first independent task is to crawl between two familiar marker stones in mist. Their first adult trial is not a hunt but a night journey during which they must return by vibration alone, carrying a covered flame. Success requires preserving both self and fire. Someone who returns alive with a dead lamp has shown strength but not readiness. Someone who saves the lamp by endangering companions has failed more seriously.

They fear silence.

Not ordinary pauses, nor the sheltered quiet of hide against an ear, but the cessation of the Long Fall. Stories claim it has stopped three times. In these accounts, people wake before noticing why and find the pools perfectly smooth. Predators stand bewildered. The entire floor of the Wash seems to wait. Then comes either a greater flood, an earthquake, or a noise from beneath the stone so deep it cannot be heard, only suffered through the bones.

Whether these events occurred is uncertain. The oldest dance describing one requires a foundation now drowned beneath the lake. Yet everyone knows the gesture for the Fall’s silence: both hands raised, fingers spread, then brought abruptly to the teeth.

They are not a mournful people, though outsiders might mistake them for one. Their humor is tactile and often indecipherable to strangers. Friends alter each other’s familiar rhythms by one beat and pretend not to notice. Children hide smooth pebbles beneath sleeping mats to make invented “messages” from the floor. Lovers tap absurd warnings through copper plates. A common joke involves standing solemnly around a puddle too small to contain anything and behaving as if a dangerous predator lurks within.

Beauty, to them, is responsiveness.

A beautiful stone answers clearly. A beautiful tool carries a vibration from hand to hand without distortion. A beautiful person is one whose meaning can be felt before it is understood. They polish shell not chiefly for shine but until a fingertip passes over it without interruption. Their ornaments hang under clothing, against the skin: drilled disks, strings of teeth, thin rattling membranes, pebbles selected for the distinct notes they transmit through the breastbone.

Courtship often begins at a distance. One person adds a recognizable variation to the tapping pattern of a shared route. If another answers on the next cairn, the exchange may continue for a season before they touch directly. The final declaration is made by trading copper plates for one night. Each sleeps with the other’s rhythm against the chest.

Among all the creatures of the Wash, the people are neither the strongest nor the most numerous. Shellbacks outweigh them in herds. Stilted walkers can cross mud that swallows a person to the hip. Six-limbed hunters perceive disturbances too faint for human senses. The microscopic veils in the pools ultimately sustain everything.

What distinguishes the people is that they make permanence out of repair.

They know no untouched dwelling, tool, route, or custom. Every object incorporates earlier failures. A house is rebuilt from collapsed houses. A net is knotted from the surviving lengths of older nets. A history is corrected when the ground changes its answer. Even the body returns as implements, walls, warmth, and signals.

They do not imagine endurance as remaining unaltered. In the Wash, anything that cannot be altered is eventually worn smooth, drowned, or carried over the Long Fall.

To endure is to be fitted again.
