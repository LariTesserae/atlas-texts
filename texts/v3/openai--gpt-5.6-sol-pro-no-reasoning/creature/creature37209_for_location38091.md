The people of the Hollow are small, though not remarkably so. Their size becomes apparent only beside the old teeth, whose polished flanks rise above them like towers, or when several pass together through the crack behind the eyelid without turning sideways. They are slight-boned and deliberate. Even the broadest adults seem made for recesses, ladders, and narrow ledges rather than open ground.

They dress in layers of felted moss fiber, bird down, woven root hair, and softened hide. Their clothes are close-fitting at the wrists and ankles so that nothing brushes a seedling or catches against a water channel. Most wear pale colors—not as camouflage, but so stains, spores, and damp can be noticed quickly. Belts carry flint slivers, coils of gut cord, folded leaf cups, and bits of chalk for making temporary marks. Children wear bells made from hollow seedpods, but the bells have no clappers. They identify one another by the different shapes knocking silently at their hips.

Their eyes are wide and adapted to the Hollow’s pervasive brightness. Pupils contract to dark pinpricks during the day and open slowly during the softened night. Many inhabitants have irises clouded with silver or mineral green. Their skin bears the same muted variety as the stone: chalk-pale, clay-brown, lichen-gold, the blue-gray of mushroom caps. No one thinks of these colors as separate kinds of people. More important distinctions are made by ward, cistern, sleeping recess, and obligation.

They keep their hair short or braided tightly against the skull. Loose hair steals water, carries mold, sheds into food, and casts unpredictable shadows over seedlings. The hair cut from the living is twisted into measuring threads. The hair of the dead is mixed with root fiber to repair the cords of the Balance. Thus almost everyone hangs, in some minute portion, above the central court.

No inhabitant is idle. This does not mean they work without rest. Rest itself is assigned a place in the city’s exchanges. Warm bodies sleep beside germinating beds. Infants are carried through mushroom chambers because their breath and heat improve the compost. Elders sit near cistern mouths, where their stillness makes them good watchers of water lines and frogs. The blind sort seeds by touch. Those whose hands tremble tease lichen into dye. Those who cannot climb maintain the black paving stones, pressing loosened ones into clay with patient thumbs.

Occupations are named not for what they make, but for what they keep from changing.

There are Lightkeepers, who inspect every crystal shelf for dust, moss, and accidental obstruction. They carry brushes of moth antennae and polished bone cards. At dawn they compare the brightness of neighboring lanes using slabs of translucent shell. If one ward has received more light than another, shutters are adjusted—not to darken the fortunate, but to turn the excess toward someone else.

Dropwardens tend the funnels and settling jars. Rootbinders train living fibers through cracks in houses. Quieters wrap tools, soften hinges, and line mortars before communal grinding. Returners are builders whose craft consists of restoring damaged structures to remembered dimensions. They preserve old templates in clay: the curve of a collapsed doorway, the depth of a stair, the angle at which a roof once met the cheek wall. A Returner is praised when the repair cannot be distinguished from wear.

The most respected are the Counters.

They count everything that may safely vary: jars, births, absences, leaf cuttings, handfuls of ash, sleeping recesses, fish glimpsed in the deepest reservoir. Their arithmetic is performed with knotted cords and black stones set into shallow boards. They do not seek increase. Their purpose is correspondence. A number is considered healthy when it has an answering number elsewhere: mouths to bowls, lamps to oil cakes, pruning knives to garden walls, children to vacant niches.

The Counters meet in the house of measures beneath the copper bowl. They are the only people allowed to touch it. Once each year they fill it to a scored line and carry it through the city so every vessel may be tested against it. The bowl is green, battered, and too valuable to repair. Its rim has worn thin where generations of fingers have steadied it.

Children belong first to the ward and only afterward to a household. They sleep with their birth families but eat at rotating tables so that no child mistakes one cistern, garden, or oven for a private inheritance. They learn the city by carrying empty baskets along prescribed routes. Later the baskets hold moss, then roots, then sealed cups of water. A child trusted with water is considered old enough to have opinions heard in council.

Their games imitate necessary acts. They race droplets down grooved tiles without touching them. They build miniature walls from damp grit and test them with breaths. They exchange knots whose meanings become increasingly elaborate until the game turns, almost imperceptibly, into literacy. One common game is called Falling: a child names something that might fail—a bridge cord, a fungus bed, an eye shutter—and the others must each offer one small action that would distribute the harm. No one wins. The game ends when the imagined danger has been divided into harmlessness.

They are taught never to shout, though nearly every child tries it once.

The sound leaves the mouth with force and disappears. It does not return from the walls. The experience is so disquieting that punishment is seldom needed. Afterward the child may spend several days speaking even more softly than everyone else, as if listening for the lost cry somewhere inside the stone.

Adults communicate through low speech, touch, and a fluent language of hands. A tap at the wrist means passage. Two fingers laid briefly against the back mean gratitude without debt. A palm turned downward asks for quiet attention; upward, for witness. Lovers often converse beneath tables while speaking conventionally to others above them. Disputes may appear calm to an outsider until the speed and precision of the hands are noticed.

Anger is not forbidden, but it must be contained without being hidden. Each ward keeps a pressure room: a small chamber lined with moss, hide, and layers of packed fiber. There a person may stamp, strike padded walls, or speak at full volume. The chamber absorbs everything. On leaving, the angry person presses a clay token with their thumb and gives it to whoever must answer them. The token cannot be discarded until the grievance has been addressed.

Their ceremonies are equally restrained.

At birth, no announcement is made. A new wick is set in an existing lamp, and for one night two flames share the same oil. At naming, the infant’s hand is placed against one of the black paving stones so that the first formal introduction is to the city rather than to any ancestor.

Partnership is marked by the exchange of sleeping warmth. Two people spend a season tending the same germination bed. If the seedlings remain healthy, their recesses may be joined by removing a woven partition. The partition is never destroyed. It is rolled, labeled, and stored, because every union must leave open the possibility of returning space to its former arrangement.

Death is called departure, though the dead do not leave the Hollow whole. Bodies are washed with water already used for roots, wrapped in exhausted garden cloth, and placed in the warm compost vaults. Bone is later recovered. Some is powdered for the cleansing beds; some becomes tools; some is carved into record tablets. The name of the departed remains attached not to an object but to a duty. Someone inherits their morning inspection, their section of gutter, their place in a counting sequence. Remembrance is continuation made specific.

When a sleeping recess becomes empty, the city waits.

No birth, adoption, or acceptance of an outsider may fill it until the Counters determine what else has changed: how many jars the departed used, how much heat they contributed to neighboring beds, which ladders bore their weight, whether their hands performed a skill already held by another. Sometimes the vacancy is filled within days. Sometimes it remains sealed for years behind a woven screen.

Those chosen to depart alive are treated with neither disgrace nor celebration. The decision is made through long accounting, private consent where possible, and a sequence of councils in which every dependency is named. Departing people are given layered clothing, flint, dried fungus, and a clay tablet impressed with a map of the face above them. Few return. Those who do may enter only if another place has opened, and even then they must spend a cycle in the eyelid passage while the city measures what their return would require.

The Hollow’s inhabitants know the outside exists, but most do not consider it empty. They imagine the plain as a vast unmeasured chamber whose walls are too far away to distribute pressure. Snow is described as loose light. Wind is understood as sound that has escaped containment. The horizon troubles them more than cold; it is an edge that never becomes a wall.

A few people tend the entrance and venture onto the statue’s face. These are the Eyewalkers. Their skin is weathered, their clothing bleached, and their gestures unusually large from signaling across distances. They gather sedge, bone, fallen feathers, wind-killed insects, and rare branches carried over the flats. Before reentering, they remain in a cleansing niche while every object is examined for mold, parasites, and unforeseen usefulness.

Eyewalkers are thought eccentric because they pause before doorways and sometimes stare too long down open lanes. They report storms days before they arrive by reading pressure in the blind eye’s grasses. They also bring back dangerous ideas: that snowbanks shift without being restored, that animals migrate without balancing their departure, that there are places where water runs faster than anyone can count.

The people listen, record these claims on clay, and continue their work.

They do not worship the statue. They have no surviving name for it and no agreement about whether it depicts a god, ruler, ancestor, or stranger. Its features are geography. The left eye is weather, the right eye light. The teeth are storage and foundation. The buried throat is loss. Children sometimes ask whether the stone face once belonged to someone alive. Adults answer that scale changes the meaning of life, and that whatever the statue was, it now performs the work of shelter.

The Balance is treated with greater caution, though not quite reverence. Each morning two surveyors approach it from opposite bridges. One measures its height while the other watches the cords. They exchange positions and repeat the measurement. If the marks agree, they touch the floor together. This is the nearest the city comes to a daily prayer.

There are theories about the water sealed inside the sphere. Some say it is the first drop that entered the Hollow. Some say it is the last reserve, to be released when every cistern fails. Others believe the droplet moves constantly but too slightly for human hearing, and that the city’s task is not to prevent motion but to remain gentle enough not to amplify it.

The surveyors themselves hold a plainer view: if it shifts, something has changed, and change must be located.

No one has heard it move. Yet certain elders claim that in the deepest softened hour, when even condensation pauses between drops, they can feel a minute weight travel through the bridges and into their bones. They do not report this to the Counters. They sit awake, palms against the black stones, waiting to learn whether the sensation belongs to the sphere, the statue, the world outside, or only to the slow water of their own bodies.

Around them, the Hollow continues.

A moth settles beneath a leaf. A child carries a cup without spilling. A Rootbinder guides a pale tendril into an ancient crack. Two neighbors redirect a blade of light between their windows. In a tower made from a tooth, seed jars rest in rows exactly matching last year’s tally. In the reservoir below, a colorless fish turns once and becomes still.

The inhabitants do not mistake stillness for absence. To them, stillness is labor completed so continuously that motion disappears within it. They live by passing every necessity from hand to hand until ownership softens into stewardship, and every disturbance is divided among so many small responses that the city seems never to change.

They are not eternal. They simply leave almost nothing unfinished.

And so they inhabit the fallen face as water inhabits stone: sparingly, completely, and along the courses made for them by time.
