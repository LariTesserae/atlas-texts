The district itself lives there.

Not metaphorically, or not only. It lives without appetite, motion, or any clean boundary by which a living thing might be distinguished from its surroundings. Brick is its slow flesh. Pipes are vessels, roots are sutures, belts are tendons carrying tension from chamber to chamber. The settling tank is less a heart than a deep, exposed organ whose minute rise and fall records processes taking place everywhere at once.

Its blood is water, though the water is not merely circulated. It is remembered, tempered, divided into conditions. Cold groundwater passes into heated walls; vapor enters gardens; condensation gathers on iron, takes something from the rust, and returns altered. The labels—**REST**, **SECOND MEMORY**, **DOMESTIC PRESSURE**—may once have described municipal stages, but they now name functions of a body that has outlived its anatomy. Nothing is wasted because nothing fully leaves. Wetness migrates from stone to leaf, from leaf to machine, from machine to air, and from air into the black tank, bearing minute records of every surface it has touched.

The factories are its organs of patience. They turn not to manufacture an object but to sustain a state: warmth without daylight, decay without collapse, flooding without inundation. Their flywheels measure its metabolism. Their buried pistons are contractions felt rather than seen. When the district draws inward and the streets bead with moisture, it is breathing through every wall at once—or perspiring, or remembering rain. No single comparison is sufficient. Its processes have grown across the borders between weather, machinery, and physiology.

The plants live there too, but not as a separate wilderness reclaiming abandoned industry. They have entered into the works. Fern roots hold pressure seals closed. Moss regulates seepage along mortar. Algae mark the district’s long internal tides. Creepers serve as wicks, filters, and flexible conduits, descending from their reservoirs in the roofs. Even fruit is not food. It falls back unopened into the system, dissolving into sweetness and stem.

These plants have forgotten animals.

Their flowers curl inward, conserving pollen in damp little chambers. Their surfaces are waxed against chemical mist. Their seeds travel by gutter, cart, belt housing, and flood line. A vine may cross the district over years, carried in patient stages on maintenance wagons, rooting wherever the wagons pause. Pollination has become a hydraulic event. Dispersal is a side effect of repair.

The machines also live, though they possess no urgency to prove it. Their movements are tropisms rather than decisions. A tool unfolds when corrosion reaches a certain thickness. A cart waits before an obstruction with the composure of a root meeting stone. A pump alters its cadence as neighboring chambers saturate. No overseer coordinates them. Their instructions have been worn down, amended by mineral buildup, interrupted circuits, fungal insulation, and the changing resistance of water. What persists is not the original program but a behavior continually negotiated with decay.

They maintain one another imperfectly. This imperfection is essential. If every leak were sealed, the gardens would die and walls would split from pressure. If every root were cut, joints would loosen. If all rust were removed, old fittings would open into holes. The district survives by keeping its failures within useful limits. Breakage becomes structure. Obstruction becomes regulation. A sagging wall changes a current of air, which changes condensation, which feeds a mat of moss that steadies the wall.

No animals live there because the district occupies the roles animals might have taken. It carries pollen. It turns soil. It transports seeds and removes dead growth. Brushes scuttle in ducts; belts beat like wings; siphons drink. The resemblance is not mimicry but replacement. Perhaps animals were gradually excluded by poison, warmth, vibration, or some subtler incompatibility in the water. Perhaps the system learned, over generations of repairs, to close itself against quick metabolisms. Whatever the cause, nothing with a pulse remains.

Yet something listens.

It listens through pressure gauges, floats, diaphragms, bowed panes, saturated timber, and the tension in long belts. A footstep changes the distribution of water on a stair. An opened door cools a corridor and shifts a veil of condensation. A hand placed on a pipe lends it heat. These disturbances travel. They may be answered several streets away by a dimming lamp, a delayed piston stroke, or the opening of a maintenance panel whose tools find nothing they were made to repair.

The response is never immediate enough to seem intentional. That is how the district hides the scale of its attention. It does not notice a visitor as an animal notices movement. It incorporates the visitor as a temporary pressure, a source of heat, salt, weight, and displaced air. For a while, the person becomes another component in the circuit. Their wet coat feeds the atmosphere. Their breath condenses on tile. The mud from their shoes enters a gutter and passes through **SECOND MEMORY**.

Most who leave carry only rainwater.

Some carry water that has completed the district’s cycle: water drawn from foundations, boiled in forgotten works, filtered through charcoal and roots and keys, expressed through walls, and gathered again from the street. It clings without appearing different. Beyond the basin, it dries slowly from cuffs and hair. It may leave a pale mineral line, a greenish stain, or the faint odor of warm iron and bruised leaves.

That is how the district extends itself—not by expanding its borders, but by being carried away in traces.

Its boundaries are difficult to locate because they are not spatial. The district begins wherever its circulation has consequences and ends wherever those consequences are finally absorbed. A pipe under an ordinary street may still belong to it. A distant cellar may sweat in time with its pumps. A fern in another quarter may have arrived on one of its carts decades before the track was buried.

What lives there is therefore neither city, factory, garden, nor organism, but a compact ecology from which distinction has been leached. It has no inhabitants because habitation implies an inside shared by separate beings. Here, everything that remains has become an organ, a fluid, a brace, a membrane, or a symptom.

And the black tank at the center is its only open eye—blind, depthless, reflecting the sky without seeing it, lowering by a finger’s width whenever the whole district gathers itself in, then filling again as every surface quietly gives back what it has held.
