The people who live in the basin are slight, deliberate creatures, though not especially small. Their narrowness is an adaptation to doors that may contract overnight, to passages that close by degrees, to a town where occupying too much space invites the walls to study one’s shape. They move with the attentive posture of those crossing a sleeping animal. Even at home they place each foot firmly, announcing weight to the floor.

They wear close-fitted layers of felted fiber, waxed at the shoulders and cuffs. Their coats smell of rain, smoke, and bruised leaves. Most carry pruning knives, chalk, a naming punch, and a small copper chime. Their boots have broad, flexible soles that spread pressure across the root roads. Children’s shoes are made deliberately heavy so that the streets will learn them early.

Their skin tends toward the pallor of people raised beneath leaves. Hair is cropped or tightly bound; anything loose catches on buds, belts, bark seams, and the grasping threads of neglected corners. Their hands are broad-knuckled and darkened by tannin. Fine scars cross their palms where young grafts have closed too quickly around a finger. Older inhabitants often lack a fingertip or have a wrist stiffened by some long-ago negotiation with a door.

They do not think of the town as alive in the manner of an animal. That would be too simple and, to them, faintly vulgar. They say it is *responsive*. It possesses habits, thresholds, appetites, and a kind of distributed memory. No single trunk knows the settlement, but pressure passes through roots, chemistry through sap, vibration through grafted beams. A house may recognize its keeper without knowing what a keeper is.

The inhabitants cultivate recognition. On returning home, each presses a bare palm to the lintel and waits for the seam to loosen. Families develop private sequences of touch: knuckle, thumb, heel of the hand; two warm breaths against the bark; a whispered name. A sick person may find their own door hesitant. A child returning after years away may have to stand outside until a parent admits them and reintroduces their weight.

Names are therefore not merely words. A proper municipal name consists of a spoken form, a stamped plaque, a place on the current survey, and repeated evidence of use. Important objects are also given a rhythm that can be struck on wood. The town’s oldest pump has seven names, because several generations feared that one might be forgotten. A cracked bench beside the pulp house bears dozens of enamel labels, each added by someone who wanted it to remain.

The naming clerks are among the busiest residents. They keep their records on resin-treated sheets stored in cabinets whose drawers must be opened daily. Their script is large, spare, and designed to survive stains. Every morning they compare reports from surveyors, householders, factory crews, and walkers. They decide whether a softened corner is still a corner, whether a blocked passage remains a lane, whether an object under roots is lost or merely misplaced.

These judgments can become grave. To remove a name is to permit reclamation. Before a condemned building is abandoned, its rooms are emptied, measured, and sounded one final time. The inhabitants gather outside while a clerk takes down its plaque. No one says that the building dies. It does the opposite: it ceases to hold a human-shaped absence and returns to uninterrupted growth.

Their dead are handled with comparable care. A corpse cannot simply be buried within the basin; roots would take it too quickly, and the distinction between person and town would become troublingly brief. Instead, bodies are dried in the warm galleries above the furnaces, wrapped in named cloth, and carried beyond the final roots to the open plain. There, where the soil does not answer, they are interred beneath low stones.

The deceased person’s belongings remain named for one year. During that time relatives use them, touch them, move them from shelf to shelf. Afterward each object is judged separately. Useful tools commonly inherit the names of their owners. Thus a pruning knife may be called Mara for a century, passing through many hands while retaining the worn notch made by the first Mara’s thumb. Objects deemed finished are placed in a reclamation court and left without plaques. Residents visit to watch fine roots rise around them.

Children grow up learning that neglect is more dangerous than breakage. They are taught to mend, but first they are taught to notice. Their earliest lessons are rounds: the cistern lip, the back stair, the wall cavity, the seldom-opened cupboard. They strike each place with felted sticks and listen. A clear tone means a hollow remains. A dull answer means wood has begun to fill it.

Their games mimic civic labor. They map shifting cracks in the road, race pollen carts, or hide marked tokens in courtyards for others to rescue before the moss covers them. One common game involves standing perfectly still while the other children circle and assign increasingly elaborate names. The still child must remain solemn as long as possible while being proclaimed a bridge, a boiler, a municipal spoon, or an emergency western annex.

True stillness, however, unsettles them. A quiet room is acceptable only if someone has declared its purpose. A person who sits motionless for too long may be touched on the shoulder and asked their name—not because it is forgotten, but to renew the claim. The ill are kept surrounded by gentle activity. Their chairs are rocked, their blankets lifted and resettled, their names spoken at intervals through the night.

They eat mostly what the town can spare without compromising its structure: mushrooms cultivated in warm motor housings, pale tubers from dedicated beds, pressed seed cakes, fermented sap, bitter greens, and translucent sheets of starch dried on copper roofs. Food is smoked lightly because open flame is dangerous. The furnaces are guarded and fed with charcoal brought from outside the basin or made from carefully designated prunings. Nothing structural is burned, even in winter.

Meat is almost unknown. The rare marsh bird is never hunted. Its visits are regarded neither as sacred nor lucky, but as an interruption so unusual that it acquires civic importance. A bird walking down a lane gives that lane a temporary second history. Clerks record where it stepped; children imitate its gait; engineers inspect whatever roof or gutter attracted its attention. Long after the bird departs, residents may say that a certain workshop stands “three turns beyond the bird’s fifth print.”

Most people work in maintenance, though they distinguish hundreds of kinds. There are grafters, hollow-keepers, root planers, cistern listeners, belt braiders, pollination drivers, plaque enamellers, moisture readers, pressure stewards, fungal scrapers, and those who crawl through warm conduits to clear invasive buds from machinery. The most respected workers are the negotiators who can alter a living structure without provoking excessive healing. They know where to score bark, where to apply salt or heat, where to press a brace until new grain accepts it.

The workers of the pulp house are broad and slow-moving, their hearing shaped by the interval between hammer blows. They can detect trouble from a change in the silence after impact. Relay attendants are more restless. They spend their shifts among the black trunks, following clicks through the hall and touching insulated leaves to locate weak currents. In wet weather their hair sometimes rises faintly, and amber sparks move under their fingernails.

At dusk, nearly everyone joins the occupation rounds. This is partly duty and partly social life. They travel in small groups, sounding walls, opening disused gates, rolling carts over roads that need weight, and speaking the names fixed to plaques. Courtships develop on these walks. So do quarrels. Lovers volunteer for remote circuits where the lanes are narrow and the chimes cannot be heard from the central square.

Marriage is registered as shared access. Each partner teaches their home the other’s pressure, temperature, and scent. This can take weeks. Newly joined couples often bear bruises from doors that have not yet accepted the arrangement. Separation is harder. A house accustomed to two keepers may continue opening for the absent one, or close resentfully against the one who remains. There are specialists who help buildings forget.

The inhabitants speak quietly, but not from timidity. Loud voices carry unpredictably through hollow trunks and may trigger shutters, valves, or old mechanisms in adjoining districts. Their language contains many words for kinds of opening and closure: a seam that yields from recognition, a passage pinched by drought, a room filling from neglect, a door that refuses in error, a door that refuses correctly. There are equally precise terms for rain depending on what it wakes.

They have little interest in speed. A hurried person misses changes and bruises the town. Meetings begin when all necessary routes have admitted their travelers. Deliveries are promised by moisture rather than hour: after the second damp morning, before the gutters withdraw, during the next strong turning of the western wheels. Mechanical clocks exist, but their housings swell, their hands catch, and no one entirely trusts them.

Their faces often seem grave to outsiders. This is because they are listening past conversation—to a belt’s murmur, a wall’s contraction, water rising under the floor. Yet they are not joyless. Their humor is dry and patient, full of stories about pompous officials outwitted by cupboards, maps rendered obsolete before their ink dried, and citizens forced to petition for the return of alleys they themselves forgot to walk.

Music is made mostly with struck wood, water-filled copper vessels, stretched cord, and the low breath of hollow stems. Songs are repetitive, designed to accompany rounds and labor. Some are no more than lists of names given melody. During long rain, when every mechanism is active and the town’s own sounds grow dense, people gather in the pulp house and perform rests rather than notes, fitting deliberate silences between the padded hammers.

A few inhabitants leave for the plain or for larger settlements beyond it. Those who return are easy to recognize. They walk too quickly at first. They fail to touch thresholds. They leave chairs in one position for days. The town receives them cautiously, tightening familiar passages or budding over old handholds. Reintegration may require months of daily circuits.

Those who never return remain in the registries as “elsewhere,” a legal condition between presence and abandonment. Their names are read once a year so the homes that remember them do not seal their rooms entirely. Some such rooms survive for generations: narrow, pale chambers maintained for bodies that will never again supply their expected warmth.

No ruler governs the basin in any simple sense. Authority belongs to the survey, the works crews, the water readers, and the councils that assign or withdraw names. Decisions are practical, local, and slow. Before a new structure is begun, residents must prove that they can keep its interior empty. Expansion is rare. The town does not fear growth; it fears creating more absence than it can maintain.

On damp evenings the inhabitants pause when the first amber bulbs brighten. They stand in lanes, workshops, galleries, and root-walled rooms, feeling the minute adjustment pass beneath their feet as water enters the buried channels. Some lay a palm against the nearest trunk. Others simply shift their weight so the street can register them.

The town inhales.

Its people answer by continuing to move.
