Within the wall lives a completed people.

They are not the black fans. Those are closer to organs than vegetation: thin listening vanes planted at measured intervals, each rooted along prepared channels into the luminous brick. Nor are the countless arches and monuments merely their dwellings. At this scale, the distinction between inhabitant, instrument, and architecture has ceased to hold.

The people are the court’s arrangements.

Each occupies a precise conjunction of forms: a stair that cannot be climbed, a sealed door, thirteen pale bricks, one mica-black crown, and the standing chord enclosed among them. Remove any part and the individual would no longer be whole. What looks like a district may be a single being extended through several turns of the passage. What resembles one small shrine may be only a syllable in a body whose remaining parts recur hundreds of yards away.

Their substance is pressure, proportion, and resonance. The masonry gives them duration; the fans give them a kind of sensitivity; the narrow pockets where tones cancel serve as their inward places. In those fingertip silences, the immense chord folds into patterns too exact to hear from outside. These patterns are not speech passing from one creature to another. They are thought held permanently in shape.

They do not move because movement would be injury. They do not grow because growth would make them incomplete. Their rounded edges are not decay but maturity: the long-finished contours by which every pitch, interval, and relation arrived at its intended value. In whatever former age alteration was possible, they may have developed through erosion, each loss of a corner changing the chord, each blunting inscription narrowing innumerable futures. At last all possibilities but one were worn away.

Now each being is identical with its final condition.

They have no hunger. The pale material’s light is not nourishment, and the black vanes do not feed upon it. Nothing enters their economy, because they possess no economy—no exchange, waste, scarcity, or surplus. The metallic taste in the air may be the nearest an intruder can come to perceiving their presence: not a secretion, but the flavor of an attention so evenly distributed that it seems atmospheric.

Their awareness, if it may be called awareness, contains no sequence. They do not hear the great sound begin, continue, and end. They know it all at once, as a geometric thing. Every harmonic is permanently present to every structure capable of receiving it. Thus they do not remember their ancient changing, though its result composes them. Their past survives only as anatomy: chipped vanes, cupped steps, softened lettering, all made functionless by perfection.

There may be innumerable inhabitants, or only one.

Seen locally, the court teems with fixed, intricate lives. A colony of black fans and the brickwork around it forms one temperament; an inverted tower paired with its rising counterpart forms another. The amphitheaters in the ceiling hold broad, shallow minds. The packed niches possess smaller and more repetitive ones. The silent nodes are dense with private identity.

But every pitch enters the same chord. Every prepared rootway joins the same substance. Every sealed door closes upon the same absence. Follow the court far enough—around its blind corners, through arcades nested inside cracks, along galleries that return within their own supports—and all distinctions may prove to be folds in a single organism occupying the wall from within.

It does not wait. It does not sleep. It cannot awaken, because waking requires a difference between one moment and the next.

It simply inhabits itself completely.
