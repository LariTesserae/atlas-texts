The people of the Near Quarter are not visibly strange.

They are compactly dressed against the cold, in layered coats with flat seams, soft-soled boots, and gloves thin enough for keys, instruments, and condensation valves. Their clothes are made to pass through narrow places without catching. Buckles are covered. Loose scarves are considered reckless. Many wear pale ceramic tabs at the throat bearing their names, addresses, and the sequence by which they were last known to be reachable.

An address may belong to a person as much as to a dwelling.

Residents tend to be slight, though this may be an effect of the architecture: shoulders turn, heads incline, bodies learn to fold around railings and squeeze between overlapping doors. Children master this early. They do not run in straight lines. Their games involve entering the same courtyard by different histories, hiding objects in rooms not yet accessible, and guessing which adult will appear when a familiar door is opened incorrectly.

They are quiet without being solemn. Speech is careful, rich in qualifications.

*Meet me after the west cistern fills, if it is still below the pharmacy.*

*My kitchen is unavailable on Thursdays, except from the pantry.*

*She lives two doors beyond us in winter.*

Directions are never phrased as commands. Wishes are expressed softly and indoors. No one says, “Come here,” toward an empty passage.

The oldest residents are not necessarily those who have lived longest. Age, like location, accumulates unevenly. A woman may have gray hair on one side of her head and remember being sixty before she remembers being twenty. A child may recognize a civic official who will not be appointed for another decade. Such incidents are recorded, checked for practical consequences, and otherwise treated as private matters. The Quarter has no appetite for wonder. Wonder is too imprecise for municipal use.

Families are organized by continuity rather than blood alone. A household consists of those who keep finding one another. This may include parents, former tenants, future in-laws, a neighbor whose bedroom intermittently shares their ceiling, and someone entered in the census with the relationship listed as *returning*. Meals are prepared in whichever kitchen is currently valid. It is courteous to set one extra place, but unlucky to speculate for whom.

Food is dense, hot, and simple. Fungi are cultivated in insulated wall cavities. Pale root-knots grow in drawers beneath communal laundries, feeding on warmth and mineral runoff. Thin sheets of protein are printed in municipal kitchens and smoked over catalytic plates. Salt is treasured, not because it is rare, but because its taste remains consistent from one mouthful to the next. Tea is brewed dark enough to reveal disturbances in its surface. Before drinking, people wait to see whether the steam rises normally.

They celebrate arrivals more often than births.

Births are intimate and difficult to date. An infant may be registered before conception if the relevant room has already begun making space for it. Arrivals, by contrast, occur when the city census identifies someone who has always lived in the Quarter but was absent from the previous count. Clerks visit with warm bread, a water allotment, and a sheaf of forms. Neighbors introduce themselves, even when they remember the newcomer from childhood. No one asks where the person came from. The accepted question is, “How long have we known you?”

Most answer cautiously.

Work is largely custodial. The inhabitants maintain not merely machines and buildings, but agreements between incompatible facts. Surveyors register durable reflections. Plumbers trace migratory water. Structural conciliators determine whether two intersecting rooms can continue to coexist without either becoming metaphorical. Chronologists inspect public clocks for coercion. Doorwrights are respected specialists, as are gardeners, cistern keepers, census clerks, and interpreters of the frost maps.

There are also vacancy technicians, though few people know them socially.

They wear ordinary clothes when off duty, but retain habits acquired from the central square: they avoid the middle of rooms, sleep facing walls, and never complete a drawn rectangle. Their suits lined with nested horizons are stored beneath the civic offices in cabinets deeper inside than outside. After each twelve-year inspection, some technicians return with an extra shadow. The shadow is licensed separately and may not vote.

Children sometimes apprentice themselves to the autonomous builders. The coin-sized tools tolerate this without acknowledgment. A patient child may spend a winter following a cluster as it lays one transparent molecular layer along a wall. By spring, the child can often understand part of the counting. Adults discourage translation. On the few occasions when the tools’ numbers have been written down, they have corresponded to quantities the city later needed: residents, doors, liters of thaw water—or once, years of silence remaining beneath the square.

The inhabitants do not think of themselves as trapped. The plain is freely accessible through several gates, depending on the hour. Survey parties go out to inspect new rills, lichen circles, microbial films, and the slow education of the stone. They wear heated suits and carry stakes that establish temporary distance. The cold beyond the wall is clean and spacious, but many find it oppressive. There is nowhere to turn except left, right, or back. The horizon remains where it appears to be. After a day outside, residents often return exhausted by so much agreement.

A few settle beyond the perimeter. They build low shelters beside the young drainage channels and cultivate rust-colored films beneath panes of ice. Their houses stay where they are put. Water runs downhill. Rooms remain countable. Some consider this peaceful. Others last only a season before returning to the Quarter, unable to sleep in a place where the walls contain nothing but insulation.

The city’s animals are treated as fellow residents, though not always as citizens.

The six-winged moths in the heat exchangers are named collectively, since it is impossible to establish whether they are six-winged individuals or three ordinary moths occupying the same insect. Engineers consult their positions before restarting dormant machinery. If the moths have moved to the underside of a surface with no underside, work is postponed.

The blind cistern crustaceans are more troublesome. Their shell patterns are copied, enlarged, and compared with municipal frost maps. Since the patterns never show the present city, debate continues over whether they depict abandoned layouts, anticipated ones, or streets belonging to other Near Quarters. Plumbers insist the creatures know where water is going. Archivists insist they eat discarded possibilities and merely bear the undigested plans. Children insist each crustacean carries a tiny city whose people are studying the pattern of the child’s fingerprints.

The black reeds in the gutters are watched by means of mirrors, which does not count as watching unless the reflection has been registered. Their uncurling is harvested as a tiny mechanical motion. Bundles of them operate clocks in districts where electricity becomes unreliable. Transparent-leaved shrubs are often inherited with apartments. A plant may extend one branch into a neighbor’s bedroom years before the rest of it is purchased. Pruning is done ceremonially, with both households present, because a careless cut can remove shade from the past.

And there is the bird.

To some it is a rook, to others a starling, a storm petrel, a species of compact black owl, or a juvenile form of something too large to land. No image of it agrees with another. Cameras show an empty perch depressed beneath an appropriate weight. Its footprints, when found, face both toward and away from one another.

The person who remembers its unheard note is called the Listener for as long as the memory lasts. Neighbors bring food and sit nearby without speaking. The Listener’s unconscious whistling makes doors open onto exterior places: the raw plain, distant ice fields, warm coasts beneath unfamiliar constellations, courtyards under rain, rooftops after the city’s wall has gone. These openings are not used casually. A cord is tied around the Listener’s wrist and held by several people while a surveyor looks through.

There have been departures.

There have also been cords returning taut around no wrist, coated in salt or pollen or fine red dust. These are archived in the civic hall. The names of those lost are not carved on memorial slabs, because carving sometimes causes a person to have always been dead. Instead, their names are spoken once each year into a black pipe. No one listens at the other end.

The residents’ relationship to death is practical and unresolved. Bodies are not buried in the young ground, whose geology cannot yet be trusted with memory. Nor are they burned, since smoke can enter rooms out of sequence. The dead are laid in narrow ceramic chambers along the inner wall, where cold and civic machinery preserve them while legal continuity is assessed.

Sometimes a chamber is later found empty because its occupant is alive again, usually at an earlier address and with no recollection of dying. Sometimes a living resident begins receiving maintenance notices for a chamber bearing their name. Such notices are not ignored, but neither are they treated as prophecy. A clerk checks the seals. The resident updates their affairs. Life continues.

What they fear most is not death, but simplification.

They fear a morning when every stair leads only upward or down, when every room occupies a measurable volume, when rain falls from completed clouds and water remains obediently in pipes. They fear the Vacancy becoming empty in the ordinary sense. They fear the city unfolding into the shallow basin as a finite arrangement of buildings, all visible at once, with no additional households hidden between walls and no one newly discovered to have always been loved.

This fear is rarely discussed. It appears instead in customs.

No inventory is totaled twice. No family portrait includes everyone. Builders leave one cavity unassigned. At bedtime, parents tell children stories with missing rooms in them. Couples exchanging vows promise not permanence, but recurrence: *I will keep finding you.* The usual reply is: *I will remain findable.*

Yet the people of the Near Quarter are not merely caretakers of anomaly. They quarrel about noise, water quality, inherited balconies, and whose kitchen is currently leaking into whose yesterday. They complain about civic forms. They gossip beside condensers. They fall in love with unsuitable neighbors. They make music on pressure chimes and stringed instruments whose notes depend on the listener’s route through the room. They decorate railings no one can distinguish. They place lamps in windows facing inward.

They laugh, too, though never all at once. Laughter travels unpredictably and may return long after the joke, softened by walls. It is common to hear a brief warmth of amusement in an empty passage and recognize one’s own voice without remembering what was funny.

Perhaps this is why the city continues to grow inward. It is not feeding on its inhabitants, nor dreaming them, nor manufacturing them to fill its multiplying rooms. Those explanations are too singular. The city and its people have simply practiced one another for so long that neither can remain complete without making room for more of the other.

In the impossible window above the plain, where the Near Quarter rests beneath glass on a table, someone lives in the room whose walls have only begun to form.

At first they seem enormous beside the hand-sized city. Then they lean close, and scale becomes uncertain. They wear a pale ceramic tab at their throat. Frost has collected in their hair. One hand rests beside the glass; the other holds a cup of dark tea.

They are watching the tiny windows illuminate.

One of those windows belongs to their own apartment.

Inside it, much smaller and much farther inward, they are standing at another table, bending over another city. Behind them is a door that has not yet acquired a wall. Someone knocks from the other side.

They do not ask who is there.

They set out an extra cup and say, very softly, so the city will not mistake it for an order:

“We have known you a long time.”
