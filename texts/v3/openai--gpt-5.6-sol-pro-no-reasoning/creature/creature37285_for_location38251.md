What lives here is not a population so much as a single distributed life wearing many bodies.

The grove itself is the eldest inhabitant. It has no trunk, crown, or buried heart. Its body is the chamber: ceiling roots, load-bearing stems, pleated walls, black pools, temporary ice, and the slow opening of alcoves. What might elsewhere be distinguished as organs, tools, architecture, and environment have never separated here. Water is its circulation but also its messenger. Roots are nerves where they need to sense, muscles where they need to press, memory where they have been bent before. Its thoughts take seasons to cross the room and decades to become visible.

It does not appear to distinguish sharply between itself and what feeds within it. The animals are tolerated abrasions, mobile grafts, pollinators, pruning mouths, reservoirs of heat and inherited behavior. They consume the grove, and the grove continually revises them.

Nearest the ceiling are the frost-clippers: pallid creatures with more legs than their narrow bodies seem able to coordinate. Their feet end in soft hooks that leave no wound in the translucent root hairs. With hinged, vitreous jaws they harvest ice as it begins to form, swallowing needles before they can bridge too many vessels at once. Their bodies are almost empty-looking except for a bright intestinal thread. In lean periods they become transparent and hang beneath roots like droplets. The grove appears to cultivate frost partly for them, and they return what they eat as tiny pellets around which new mineral nodules begin.

Among thicker roots sleep the muff-moths. Their fur is dense, colorless, and faintly damp, more like the nap on a leaf than an animal coat. They gather by the hundreds into shivering knots, warming one another until the interior moths can flex their wings. Then a falling drop strikes the colony and they scatter in a soft explosion. Their flight is brief and expensive. They dust wounds with spores, drink from luminous root tips, and return to communal sleep before their warmth is lost. Some never separate from the knot. Over years their bodies are bound together by fine roots, becoming a warm, breathing fruit from which later broods emerge.

The shallow pools hold glasslings: transparent amphibious animals with no stable outline. Their limbs flatten or narrow according to the vessel of water containing them. Dark organs branch inside their bodies like leaves seen through ice. They drift rather than swim, opening their mouths only when a pool brightens. During dry intervals they spread themselves across the wet floor as membranes, indistinguishable from the films of water there. The grove sometimes closes a root cup around one and carries it elsewhere. Whether this is transport, predation, or gestation is unclear.

Antlered percussion beetles occupy the tautest stems. Each bears a crown of delicate forks, individually tuned. They strike these against the vegetation to announce territory, court mates, and interrogate the structure around them. A beetle can tell from the returning vibration whether a distant root has thickened, whether a pool has filled, or whether a hunter has stretched across its customary route. Rivalries may last for generations: descendants inheriting both a length of resonant stem and the rhythm used to claim it. At times the grove answers. Then every beetle becomes still, antennae lowered, while a slower rhythm travels back through the walls.

The ribbon hunters live almost entirely as lines. They extend between stems, so thin that droplets pass around them without breaking. Their mouths occur at intervals along the body, each little more than a fold capable of closing. They do not pursue prey; they predict corridors. Weeks before a migration, one will begin growing across the route the animals are likely to take. If the prediction fails, it is dismantled by its own cells and reassembled elsewhere. Old individuals may span half the chamber in disconnected lengths joined through the root network, less a creature than a recurring appetite.

Their principal prey includes the seed-runners: small, hard-bodied things that resemble fallen seeds until they tip onto concealed legs. They roll uphill by shifting dense internal grains, seeking lesions where plant tissue is warm. Once lodged there, they shed every obvious animal feature. The wound heals around them. Years later it opens again and releases a winged organism bearing little resemblance to what entered. Those wings carry pollen, eggs, or both. No one phase contains the whole animal; part of its life cycle is performed by the grove.

Longer green beings stand in the root thickets. They have narrow torsos dividing into branchlike limbs and dark sensory pits hidden under leaf-shaped folds. Chlorophyll moves visibly beneath their skin. Their patience is more botanical than animal: a step may take an hour, a turn of the head most of a day. When danger approaches, they push their feet into the mesh and root. Circulation slows. Their eyes seal beneath bark. They may remain so for several seasons, exchanging sugars and signals with neighboring plants. When they pull free, they sometimes retain foreign growths—a spray of blue moss along one shoulder, a flowering twig where a hand had been. Older ones have rooted so often that it is difficult to say which portions originally belonged to them.

There are browsers shaped by futures rather than by present camouflage. Their coats begin changing before the vegetation around them does. An animal among gray fronds may develop violet bars for leaves that will not uncurl until the following year. If the grove alters its intended growth, the browser becomes conspicuous and is taken by blossoms already bending toward the place where it will seek cover. Their lives depend on correctly reading the chamber’s slow decisions. They sleep with mouths pressed to roots, tasting changes that have not yet become form.

Many inhabitants are smaller than a bead of condensation. They live inside vascular seams, sorting particles, carrying instructions between tissues, repairing membranes, or provoking useful injuries. Some have shells made from stolen mineral flecks. Some are little more than packets of behavior wrapped in fat. Great migrations of them move through the straight vines, though from outside they appear only as a dim pulse. They may be the grove’s blood cells, its scribes, its domesticated fauna, or all three.

Death does not remove any of these beings from the community. The chamber takes them apart with extraordinary care. It sorts bone from gesture, shell from coloration, heat-loving chemistry from remembered fear. Hard parts return as reinforcement. Pigments reappear in leaves. Defensive habits may be grafted into fruit and eaten by another species. Thus the dead survive unevenly. A new moth may perform the courtship of an extinct swimmer. A beetle may begin building a nest no beetle ever needed. Sometimes whole groups are seized by an inherited compulsion and enact, together, a life that vanished before the current alcoves opened.

For this reason there may be no truly native animal here. Each is a temporary arrangement of the chamber’s old material and older recollections.

At the center lives—or is becoming—the one inhabitant that the others do not browse, strike, seed, or enter.

Beneath the overlapping translucent leaves is a body without an exposed anatomy. It rises and falls once in several hours, but the motion is not quite breathing. It may be drawing water inward, testing the chamber’s pressure, or rehearsing some future contraction. Its surface is cool even by the grove’s measure. Condensation creeps toward it against gravity. Small animals gather at its perimeter and become quiet, oriented inward as if awaiting a signal too slow for any one lifetime to contain.

The mound may be the grove’s first concentrated self: a heart assembled after ages of living without one.

It may be a flower whose pollen requires animals, minerals, rooms, and memories as its grains.

It may be an egg, with the entire chamber serving as nest and parent.

Or the chamber may be the immature organ, and the mound only the point at which a much larger being has begun to turn itself inside out.

Under its skin, mineral particles are being placed in repeating arrays. Around it, straight vines redirect their traffic. The oldest rooted animals have stopped withdrawing from the soil and now stand permanently inclined toward the center. Frost bridges form in radial patterns. Even the predators have altered their waiting places, leaving an open ring no law of hunger seems able to cross.

Nothing here knows what will emerge. The grove itself may not know in the way an animal knows an intended shape. Its intelligence does not begin with a plan. It negotiates, remembers, tests, and incorporates. Whatever waits beneath the leaves is not executing a design so much as arriving at one.

And so the chamber’s inhabitants live as attendants to an event measured in generations: feeding, hunting, freezing, molting, rooting, dissolving, and being used again. Each carries some minute portion of the unfinished answer. Their noise is the sound of that answer being worked out—the strike of beetle antlers, the crack of pods, the groan of loaded fibers, the fall of water, and at long intervals the low note from the mound, after which every living thing pauses as though, for a moment, the whole distributed body has remembered that it may one day speak with a single voice.
