The people who live in the basin are not themselves changeless. This distinguishes them from almost everything they make.

They are born soft, grow tall or broad, scar, stoop, lose teeth, and die. Their hair whitens in a city whose oldest walls retain the color of yesterday’s mortar. A child may age into a grandparent beside the same muddy hoofprint, renewed each dawn in precisely the same place. Thus the inhabitants do not measure time by weather, erosion, or architecture. They measure it by bodies: the deepening of a voice, the loosening of a tooth, the number of children who can no longer pass beneath a table without ducking.

Their calendars are genealogies. A house is not said to be three hundred years old, but to have stood since the mother of a named potter’s mother. Public records list births, marriages, apprenticeships, debts, arrivals, and deaths beside impressions of fixed landmarks. Since the landmarks offer no visible proof of age, memory must be continuously attached to them. Every district employs reciters who know who laid each wall, who misjudged each channel, and whose cart remains trapped in which alley. To forget a maker would be to let the work slip outside human time.

The people tend to be compact and strong from carrying jars, turning mills, hauling sledges, restraining livestock, and forcing carts through constricted streets. Their feet are broad and hardened by warm stone. Many have hearing damaged by the basin’s ceaseless roar. Older inhabitants often speak little, relying on signs, touches, writing boards, and expressions read at close range. Even those who hear well watch mouths carefully. Conversation is intimate by necessity: forehead near temple, lips beside ear, one hand resting on the listener’s shoulder so meaning can be felt as well as heard.

Their language has an extensive silent half. A flat palm means a fixed price. Two crooked fingers indicate a wall deliberately left open. A fist circled at the wrist warns that something is near completion and must not be touched. Builders, drovers, potters, and canal keepers each have their own gestures. Children learn these before they learn formal speech. From a rooftop, an entire household can argue without making a sound distinguishable from the city.

They dress for warmth, wetness, and labor rather than for cold. Garments are short, wrapped, and easily dried: woven cloth from the uplands, softened hide, felted animal hair, leather aprons, and broad shoulder cloths waxed with imported oil. Sandals have thick soles but few nails, since a dropped fastening may become an eternal obstruction if it settles in the wrong place. Hair is braided or cropped close against damp and insects. Market workers wear caps with narrow brims to keep condensation from their eyes. Canal workers rub their legs with fat and bitter mineral powders against mosquitoes.

Ornament is small and transferable. Permanently attaching a precious thing to a building is thought reckless. People wear rings, stamped clay beads, shell disks, bronze wire, polished teeth, and plaques naming their household or district. Jewelry is often deliberately flawed: a bead left unpierced on one side, a broken sequence in a necklace, a clasp whose decorative border stops just short of joining itself. Completion belongs to tombs, contracts, and curses. Living people prefer an opening.

Their houses reflect this caution. A household may occupy rooms raised by dozens of generations, but one corner remains active: uncapped, unplastered, or roofed with removable hide. This is the growing corner, where births are marked, debts negotiated, repairs planned, and new rooms attached. To seal it would imply that the family has no future. Even prosperous houses preserve some visible incompletion—a stair ending below the roof, a mosaic with one blank tile, a doorway whose right jamb remains raw.

Few inhabitants live alone. The city favors large, interdependent households because every necessity must be brought in, guarded, rationed, processed, and carried through crowded lanes. Families include blood relatives, adoptees, apprentices, widowed in-laws, hired drovers, elderly accountants, and migrants from the green uplands. Their courtyards contain animals as well as people. A valued milk goat may sleep beside the children; pigeons nest over grain rooms; dogs are trained to distinguish household rats from intruding ones, with inconsistent success.

The animals are not merely livestock. They are the city’s substitute for vegetation: its moving shade, its source of fiber, dung, milk, leather, fat, bone, horn, companionship, and smell. Children know breeds and bloodlines as readily as kinship. Cattle are led to warm drainage courts to soak aching joints. Goats are fitted with padded hoof-wraps when taken through sleeping quarters. Pigeons carry short messages across districts where street travel is slow. Ducks patrol cistern margins for larvae. Dogs guide the hard of hearing by tugging cords tied around the wrist.

Nothing is wasted willingly. Dung feeds kilns and cooking fires. Bones become needles, counters, toggles, and handles. Blood goes into food, plaster, dye, or glue. Fat seals lamps and cloth. Hair becomes stuffing and cordage. Even flies are trapped in sticky trays and fed to caged birds. The city imports nearly all plant matter, so every husk and stalk is accounted for. Grain sweepings belong by custom to pigs; fine flour dust to communal gruel; fruit pits to oil pressers or bead makers; spoiled fodder to the dung heaps.

Food is rich in grain and animal products but poor in freshness. Flatbread, porridge, curds, hard cheese, blood sausage, dried fish, eggs, rendered fat, onions already yellowing from the journey, and fruits preserved in syrup form the common diet. Green leaves are expensive and eaten quickly, often at the gate before they wilt. A fresh herb sprig is a luxury more poignant than gold. Lovers give each other living cuttings not because they can be kept, but because they cannot. The gift’s value lies in watching it fail together.

Some uplanders find this morbid. Basin-born people consider it honest.

The most respected trades concern beginnings and endings. Surveyors decide where a structure may safely start. Foundation readers test the crust with weighted rods and listen to the sound below. Clay modelers construct miniature districts so drainage, traffic, shadow, and animal movement can be argued over before stone is laid. Capstone witnesses determine when a work has crossed into permanence. Their judgment carries legal force. If a building hardens with an error, blame may survive for generations.

For that reason, builders are patient to the point of ritual. Before the final course is set, neighbors walk through the new rooms carrying baskets, stretchers, water jars, cages, and mock funeral biers to ensure every necessary object can pass. Children are sent crawling into drains. Goats are driven up stairways. Doors are tested by the widest person in the household. Water is released in measured cups and watched for days. Only then may a capstone approach its place.

Sometimes it never does.

Across the city stand buildings intentionally suspended at the last instant: walls awaiting one brick, domes with a covered opening at the crown, bridges whose final paving slab lies beside them. Such places remain workable, alterable, and vulnerable. They require constant guardianship. Rain must be kept from unfinished mortar; thieves may pry at soft joints; feuding families may sabotage a rival’s future by completing something badly. Night watchmen therefore guard incompletion more fiercely than treasure.

The city’s criminals understand the basin’s law well. The gravest offenses involve malicious finishing: setting an unwanted threshold, sealing a drain, driving a final peg into a cart axle, capping a wall around disputed land. A murderer kills one life; a malicious finisher may impose an error upon every life to come. Punishments often require the offender and their descendants to maintain the detour, pump, stair, or warning lamps made necessary by the act.

Conversely, deliberate breaking has little power once equilibrium has taken hold. Tools glance from fixed walls. Fire blackens but does not consume timber that the basin has fully accepted. Such structures can sometimes be removed only by cutting away the still-unfinished matter around them and hauling the whole object elsewhere. Houses have been transported room by room only in the sense that streets were excavated beneath them and sledges inserted before their final attachment. More commonly, inconvenient permanence is absorbed. A blocked door becomes a shelf. A misplaced wall becomes the spine of a market. A stranded wagon becomes a shrine, stall, sleeping platform, or landmark.

This has shaped the inhabitants’ temperament. They are skilled at accommodation and suspicious of purity. They do not expect a clean plan to survive contact with the accumulated decisions of the dead. They make routes through, under, and around. Their laws are full of easements, shared corners, inherited apertures, rights to condensation, obligations to maintain access for animals, and agreements permitting a neighbor’s roof-drip to fall through one’s kitchen.

They are not passive. Argument flourishes precisely because decisions matter so much. Councils can debate a single paving slab for years. Models are revised, smashed, and remade. Experts produce competing water-table measurements. Families hire children to count carts at an intersection or place bowls beneath a proposed gutter line. A district may hold a festival merely to test whether a planned square can contain all its bodies without crushing the exits.

Their religion, where it can be separated from civic practice, concerns acceptance. Some say the basin is a listening thing that decides when a work has become itself. Others say completion closes a circuit between warm water below and gray sky above. Priests disagree on whether the equilibrium is divine, natural, or the lingering command of forgotten makers. All traditions agree that intention alone is insufficient. A thing is not finished because its maker declares it so. It is finished when the place refuses further change.

Temples are therefore among the city’s strangest structures. Many have stood ceremonially unfinished for centuries. Their sanctuaries contain an empty socket, an unset lintel, a final tile wrapped in oiled cloth. The holy object is often the missing piece. Worshippers circle it, touch it, argue over it, and swear never to install it except at the end of the world. In other temples, completion is the central mystery: a sealed chamber, flawless and inaccessible, believed to contain a god who can no longer act because perfection has immobilized it.

Funerals provide the one occasion when completion is openly sought. The dead are washed in warm spring water, wrapped, named aloud or by gesture, and placed in low fired-clay tombs beyond the inhabited courts. A final brick is set in the presence of witnesses. Its placement means that obligations have been counted, inheritances opened, debts transferred, and the dead released from alteration. Yet even tombs often include a narrow speaking tube. No one calls this an inconsistency. The dead may be finished, but the relationship with them is not.

Children fear burial less than accidental permanence. Their stories tell of boys who stand too long in wet plaster and become unliftable, or girls whose braids are caught beneath a capstone and must remain there while the rest of them grows old beside the wall. Adults insist such tales are exaggerations, though every district has objects it avoids explaining.

The basin does not seem to fix living flesh. Nor does it readily accept objects still being used, moved, argued over, or maintained. What matters is not simple stillness but a kind of settlement: physical, social, and perhaps conceptual. This uncertainty governs daily behavior. Owners move precious tools regularly. Doors are opened even when no one needs to pass. Carts are rolled a handspan each morning. Looms are left with one thread uncut. A cup intended as an heirloom is turned upside down and back again at every new moon.

No one wants to discover the exact boundary by crossing it.

The city’s animals participate unknowingly in this labor against completion. Goats jostle scaffolds. Dogs shift mats. Rats keep granaries in a state of continual revision. Pigeons dislodge tiles and repopulate abandoned rooms. Termites are hated in imported wood yet valued as proof that it remains alterable. In old districts, families sometimes introduce beetles into unused furniture so that the basin will not mistake it for finished.

Migrants from the uplands arrive expecting sterility and find excess instead: excess heat, wetness, sound, bodies, rules, memory, and appetite. Some cannot bear the absence of growing things. They keep seeds in pouches and test them obsessively in pots of imported soil. Nothing sprouts. Others adapt and become traders, animal doctors, timber assessors, or interpreters between the city and the green country beyond the vapor.

Those who leave the basin often sleep badly. Wind in leaves troubles them because its pitch changes. Rivers alarm them by moving their banks. They mistrust wooden houses that swell, rot, and creak. The night silence of a field can feel like suffocation. Many carry little whistles tuned to the vent of their home district and blow them beside the bed.

The basin-born regard erosion with fascination bordering on horror. To see a rounded stone is to see matter forgetting its first shape. To watch rain enlarge a rut appears almost miraculous. Travelers return with pebbles, flakes of bark, handfuls of soil, and stories of ruins collapsing without permission. Such objects are displayed in cabinets but rarely trusted. Soil in particular is unsettling: dark, loose, full of dead things becoming nourishment for living things. The city’s pale ground permits no such cycle.

Yet the inhabitants are not joyless. They hold loud feasts no louder than ordinary commerce, dance by stamping patterns that can be felt through the paving, race ducks along the nearly motionless channels, paint each other with bright clays, and bet on which rooftop will receive the first thousand pigeons after rain begins. Weddings end with the couple deliberately leaving a shared object incomplete—a blanket with an unwoven border, a bowl with no glaze beneath its foot, a song whose final line will be composed only if they separate or one of them dies.

At the appointed rain, awnings unfold and cistern mouths open. Market counters are covered. Children run barefoot into the lanes before their elders can catch them. Pigeons rise like loosened ash, then settle again onto the same cornices. The inhabitants barely look upward. Their attention is on one another: a hand signal through the downpour, a jar passed from grip to grip, a goat hauled beneath shelter, a mason shielding an uncapped wall.

Everything fixed remains as it was.

Everything living hurries among it, carrying food, noise, error, memory, and the dangerous possibility of something new.
