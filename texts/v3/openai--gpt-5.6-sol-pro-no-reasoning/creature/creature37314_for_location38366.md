The chamber is inhabited by a complete ecology arrested at the threshold of action.

Its plants are dry-bodied things adapted to heat, mineral vapor, and a world without liquid: hollow bell-trees with violet interiors and hanging canopies; crystal fans veined with living tissue; furred red cushions; shell-branched shrubs; unsupported ribbons of pale growth. They feed on sulfur, carbon, iron, and warmth. Powder moves through them where sap would run. Their flowers remain joined in pollination, their seed cases eternally open, their kernels forever ready to fall.

Among them live ceramic-plated grazers, vapor-feeding bridge-worms, heavy mothlike creatures, glass-backed insects, burrowers, and predators. None are dead, and none can complete a movement. Organs work, muscles strain, gases pass through lungs and spiracles, and food traverses granular guts—but wings never finish beating, feet never leave the ground, and jaws never complete a bite. One hunter holds a grazer perpetually between feeding and wounding.

Their interrupted actions fill the chamber with an immense, continuous sound. Calls do not fade. Impacts do not decay. Wingbeats rasp without rhythm because no second beat follows the first; resonant trees sustain brasslike tones; hidden bodies press an endless knock into the stone. High in the wall, something holds a single flawless shriek.

At least one toolmaking being lives there: a small, broad-shouldered figure in a cape sewn from wings. It stands behind a hut of bone, stone, and insect shell, poised to strike a crystal outcrop with a hammerstone. It breathes. It intends. Its arm never falls.

The hut suggests there may once have been—or may somehow still be—others. It contains two sleeping hollows, tools, cord, and marks resembling a tally. Nearby stand fitted walls, fired bowls, sharpened blades, a bone tripod, and stored red seeds. These are the traces of planning and repeated labor in a place where repetition should be impossible.

Most troubling is the polished green oval hidden inside the hut. Around its middle, someone carved a sequence the chamber cannot enact: the hut being built, completed, ruined, and buried, followed by the stone roof breaking open beneath a sky full of stars. Whoever made it knew change—whether by memory, prophecy, or an inward time inaccessible to the chamber.

Thus what lives there is not frozen life, but life denied consequence. It can metabolize without aging, hunger without starving, attack without killing, bloom without reproducing, and perhaps think without reaching a second thought. Every organism inhabits an everlasting verge.

In the black-bead basin, green shoots have just broken from the salt.

They are the chamber’s newest inhabitants, and they will remain new forever.
