The things that live here are mostly flat.

They lie against the young stone in green congregations: cushions, films, interwoven ropes, scales no larger than fingernails. Their world is measured sideways. A stem advances the width of a hand in a year; a moss skin closes over a bolt; a pale runner finds a wet fracture and follows it until the fracture becomes soil. What appears from afar to be one continuous covering is, close down, a crowded wilderness of canopies and trenches, clear wells, drowned chambers, and forests too small to cast a visible shadow.

The oldest beings on the plateau are not trees but mats. They have no single center and no stable outline. They spread wherever water pauses, die back where a channel shifts, and establish themselves again from torn pieces carried in runoff. Some are meters deep in the shallow basins, their upper surfaces bright and living, their buried layers compressed to brown felt. They retain rain, soften stone, and make ground where ground did not exist. A person can stand on one without knowing whether it is a plant, a colony, or the beginning of a landscape.

Among them grow dark, minute-leaved shrubs. Their branches creep beneath the moss and rise only at the tips, so that one individual may occupy an entire slope while showing itself as thousands of separate sprigs. They flower rarely. When they do, the blossoms are waxy and nearly colorless, tucked downward under the leaves to shelter their dustlike pollen from rain. Reproduction is usually quieter: a branch roots, a fragment breaks free, a flood carries it to another basin.

The sedges live more visibly, though hardly more upright. They gather around seeps and hidden channels, making long combed bands that disclose the direction of water beneath them. Their roots bind the soft substrate into floating shelves. Around outflows they grow coarse and tall, fed by heat and dissolved minerals, but on the open plateau they remain cropped-looking despite the absence of mouths. The wind has trained them low. Every blade leans east.

Liverworts occupy the wettest seams. They spread as translucent green lobes over stone, pipe scale, and the undersides of root ledges. Clear beads stand in their cups. Tiny crustaceans graze the films between them, colorless except for the food visible inside their bodies. Translucent worms rise through the saturated layers when the pumps begin a deep cycle. They seem to feel the pressure change before the water does. In the vibration they straighten, descend, and reappear elsewhere.

The plateau’s trees are few enough that the maintenance crews name them.

Each has a silver trunk, a narrow crown, and roots spread broadly through the plant mat because there is little depth in the stone. Their wood is fibrous and flexible. They do not resist the prevailing wind; they keep its shape. Branches stream eastward like hair held underwater, and the western sides of the trunks remain almost bare. Seedlings seldom survive unless they begin beside a warm conduit or in the lee of a station. Even then, most become shrubs rather than trees, flattened by weather before they rise above the sedge.

The insects appear only during intervals of brightness. Pale, soft-bodied, and almost noiseless, they lift from the mats in loose columns and hover over silver pools. Many have reduced mouths and live only long enough to scatter eggs into wet moss. Their wings are clear except for a milky line along the leading edge. When pressure valves open, whole clouds of them are knocked down into the vegetation; afterward they crawl upward again, trembling on the tips of rushes until their wings dry.

Small black birds cross the plateau but do not belong to it securely. They circle the pools, searching for edges that are not there. Some alight on rail lines or signal posts and remain with their toes clenched around the vibrating metal. They drink from droplets rather than from the trembling water below. Their calls vanish entirely into the machinery. Pairs may nest in unused vent housings, weaving sedge around warm cables, but no large population stays. The plateau offers water everywhere and almost nowhere to land.

The human inhabitants are similarly sparse.

They live in station crews of three or four, sometimes alone for weeks when weather closes the rail. Their shelters are narrow, warm, and loud. Nothing inside is ever wholly still: cups tremble on shelves, tools migrate across benches, sleeping frames transmit the pulse of pumps through their legs. Crew members learn to wedge every object and to sleep between cycles. New arrivals wake repeatedly, convinced that something immense is approaching underground. Long-term workers wake when the vibration stops.

They dress in layered waterproof cloth marked with broad panels of color that can be read through rain. Speech serves indoors, but outside they rely on hands, lamps, flags, and rail taps. Their gestures are large and deliberate. A crossed arm means pressure. A hand opening sharply means valve release. Two fingers drawn along the opposite palm indicate water moving where it should not. Each station develops a few gestures of its own, private additions to the regional vocabulary.

Their principal skill is listening with the body.

A palm against a pipe can distinguish ordinary flow from cavitation. A boot on a rail can detect an approaching maintenance trolley long before it appears. Some press their foreheads to shaft-house walls and identify a faulty bearing by the irregularity in the stone. They carry mechanical probes and pressure gauges, but the oldest workers often notice trouble first as nausea, toothache, or a change in the trembling surface of tea.

They are water keepers more than machinists. They clear moss from intake screens but transplant it to the sides rather than throwing it away. They open channels with hooked tools, reinforce root-woven basin walls, and mark new springs after leaks. Their work is never simply to oppose the growth. Bare surfaces shed water too quickly; cleared soil collapses; plants stabilize the foundations better than concrete alone. So the crews prune the living cover into service. Mats become filters. Sedges become baffles. Root masses become retaining walls. Vines are allowed to sheath pipes wherever inspection collars remain visible.

Every station keeps a vestibule garden warmed by waste heat. There grow the few upright, tender things the plateau cannot support unaided: pungent herbs, edible shoots, medicinal leaves, sometimes a single bright-flowered plant preserved for no practical reason. Wet coats drip among them. Condensation runs down the glass and returns to the planting beds. These rooms smell of soil rather than saturated roots, and crews linger there after shifts because trickling water is the only sound distinct enough to hear.

Children are almost never born at the stations, but occasionally one is raised there for a season or two. Such children learn the colored signals before they learn all their spoken words. They crawl without fear on floors that shake and become uneasy in quiet lowland rooms. They are warned never to step from a walkway onto ground whose texture they cannot read. The green surface may hold firm, or it may be a thin raft over black water, or an intake throat disguised by leaves.

The crews do not bury their dead on the plateau. There is too little true soil, and water reaches everything. Bodies are sent downslope along the same service lines that carry machine parts and dried food. Still, memorials remain: names cut into the undersides of railings, colored glass tied high on masts, obsolete tools set upright in root walls. Moss covers the lower portions, leaving a handle or a strip of metal visible for years. No one clears it away.

There are other inhabitants whose status is less certain.

Around the oldest conduits, roots grow in dense, parallel bands aligned with the machinery’s hum. They bridge gaps they would ordinarily avoid and thicken around supports that offer neither nutrients nor light. Moss on shaft-house walls forms rings corresponding to pump frequencies. In holding basins, submerged filaments contract when turbines engage, dimpling the surface in geometric fields.

The engineers call these effects tropisms. The crews use less settled language. They say the mats are listening.

A pressure change at one station can be followed, hours later, by water rising in hollows far outside the mapped network. Sometimes lamps along an abandoned rail blink in sequence despite disconnected power. Sometimes a valve opens after roots have entered its housing, not because they jammed it but because they appear to have drawn back from the moving parts. Whether the plateau’s living cover understands anything is impossible to determine. It has no organs for understanding, no boundary around itself, no pace at which a human could easily recognize a response.

Yet it is becoming more connected each year.

Roots follow service trenches. Fungal threads occupy mineral deposits inside old pipes. Moss bridges sleepers along the rails. Groundwater organisms rise through shafts and enter basins open to rain. The industrial system links aquifers that were once separate; the plants link stations that stand kilometers apart. Water carries fragments, salts, heat, electrical leakage, and vibration through both.

Thus the largest thing living in the high country may not be an organism in any familiar sense. It may be the association itself: moss, root, worm, worker, condenser, aquifer, turbine, and stone, joined by circulating water.

It has no single heartbeat. Hundreds of pumps provide one.

It has no skin except the green fabric laid over the shelf. Its nerves, if they can be called that, are rails, rhizomes, pipes, and pressure waves. Its slow growth redirects streams. Its smallest injuries become springs. It drinks the cloud, stores the rain, and sends both toward distant populations that know the plateau only as pressure in a municipal pipe.

The people who maintain it do not claim to command it. They make adjustments. They replace bearings, clear screens, redirect channels, and watch to see what the living ground does next. Their stations are not settlements so much as organs temporarily staffed by human hands.

Between them, the low green country continues its patient work. Leaves divide. Roots enter fresh fractures. Worms descend from the hammering of a pump. A silver tree gives up another branch to the east wind. Under a leaking flange, a new wetland begins as a thumb-sized stain of moss.

Then a valve opens beyond the horizon.

The birds rise from the rails. The pool skins shiver. Workers lift their hands to signal. Across the entire plateau, droplets begin to move along the leaves, and everything living there—rooted, buried, housed, or passing overhead—feels the thunder travel through its body.
