The Close is inhabited not by one people so much as by a single vertical ecology, its citizens distinguished from its vermin, livestock, crops, and predators mainly by the rules they make among them.

The people themselves are slight, hard-muscled, and exceptionally sure of hand and foot. Children learn to climb before they can walk on level ground. Adults move without looking down, testing pegs and ropes by touch, turning sideways through passages that would stop a lowlander at the shoulders. Their toes spread broad against stone; their palms are thickened by grit, fiber, and ladder rungs. Most bear old white scars on elbows, shins, and knuckles. A person without scars is either very young or newly arrived.

They dress close to the body. Loose hems, necklaces, and flowing hair are dangerous in a city of thorns, pulleys, horns, and crowded ladders. Hair is cropped, braided flat, or plastered with resinous dust. Garments are wraps of softened hide and tough plant cloth, tied rather than pinned. Aprons have many narrow pockets for wedges, cord, seeds, chalk, and bits of food. Precious tools are secured directly to belts. Nearly everyone carries a small hooked knife, though its blade may be chipped black glass rather than metal.

Dust marks rank and occupation more clearly than clothing. Gardeners are stained green at the wrists. Stoneworkers are pale with chalk. Dung-keepers carry a sweet, fungal smell. Swift-climbers wear dark smears of guano and feather oil. Butchers are rubbed with red absorbent powder so that no blood reaches bare rock. Those who tend the condensation cloths rise with wax shining on their fingers.

They belong first to stair-clans: households stacked along a shared route, bound less by ancestry than by mutual dependence. A stair-clan maintains its ladders, catches what falls through its levels, apportions shade, witnesses the use of rare tools, and answers collectively when a bridge or roof fails. Marriage often means moving several body-lengths up or down rather than crossing the city. Kinship is spoken spatially. Elders are “those above the beam”; dependents are “those under our floor.” An estranged relative is said to have “pulled up the ladder.”

Authority rests with people who remember loads, shadows, and obligations. The most respected elders can tell which roofs may bear a gathering, whose garden was shaded by a bridge three generations ago, and how much dung a household owes the terraces below it. Disputes are judged at narrow landings before witnesses arranged vertically. A speaker must address those above and beneath in turn. The worst accusation is not theft but blockage: hoarding water, light, air, passage, or decay so that nothing can continue downward.

Their speech is sharp enough to carry through noise. It has many clipped warnings for falling objects, animals coming up, animals coming down, loose stone, taut rope, sudden wings, and the dangerous silence before a structure gives way. Names are often two-part: a personal name joined to a stair, bridge, plant bed, or memorable repair. Voices matter greatly. People recognize neighbors they may seldom see by calls passing through shafts and floors.

They sleep lightly in shallow wall chambers, often in nets or hide slings that cannot roll toward an edge. Infants are carried tightly against the chest until they learn the danger of reaching into open space. The old are given places near busy routes, where they can watch children, sort seeds, mend cord, and serve as living records. Bodies are compact in death as in life. Flesh is returned to root beds, scavengers, or insect chambers under strict supervision; cleaned bones become pegs, handles, measures, whistles, and parts of the house. To be built into one’s stair is considered a durable form of belonging.

Among them live the horn-faced lizards, tolerated everywhere because they eat mites and eggs. Each is small enough to bask on a wrist, with blunt brows, granular ochre scales, and toes that cling to nearly sheer stone. They steal crumbs, shed skins into bedding, and make soft clicking sounds before shifts in heat. Children distinguish individuals by broken horns and tail patterns, though adults insist that no lizard belongs to anyone.

The cliff mice are less welcome and almost impossible to exclude. They are long-bodied, dust-colored creatures with gripping feet and tails that coil around cord. They raid seed jars by descending from above and escape along roof braces too thin for cats. Families hang polished bone rings around storage ropes; the mice learn to leap them. A clever mouse can become notorious across several levels.

The cats are lean, broad-pawed, and nearly silent. Their coats resemble sandstone: dun, smoke-grey, or mottled with dark seams. They are not wholly tame. A household may leave scraps for one and name it, yet no one claims ownership when it takes a chick, a swift, or a neighbor’s mouse trap. In noon heat they disappear against the walls except for their open eyes. At night they patrol beams above sleeping rooms, placing each paw with greater care than many people.

The goats are narrow as ladders and high at the shoulder, with long pasterns, split hooves, and horns swept close to the skull so they do not catch. They browse vertically, bracing hind feet on one ledge and forefeet on another. Their horns carry clan cuts, breeding marks, and records of debt. Their milk is scant and resinous; their blood is taken more often than their flesh, a little at a time and always over a root basket. An old goat that knows every stair may be more valuable than a strong young one.

Bristle-backs occupy the lower kitchens and refuse ways. Squat, wedge-snouted, and armored with stiff hair, they consume rind, gristle, beetle larvae, fungus waste, and anything insufficiently guarded. They are useful, foul-tempered, and strong enough to overturn a sleeping platform. Their piglets are carried in baskets when stairways become crowded, squealing with such force that traders pause their calls.

Bright carrion kites haunt the railings. They are small, fearless birds with rust-red breasts, white faces, and quick black feet. They follow knives, childbirth, slaughter, and injury. Because they descend moments before a fainting person falls, some believe they foresee death; more practical observers say they merely notice blood sooner than people do. Their shed feathers ornament signal darts and children’s toys, but killing one near a butcher’s landing is unlucky.

The pale cave swifts belong to the depth. Their feathers are cream or translucent grey, their eyes black and enormous, their wings long enough to brush both sides of a passage. They sleep in colonies like folded leaves, then erupt by the thousands when warm air surges upward. Their arrival extinguishes lamps, shakes awnings, and fills mouths with dust and feathers. People eat their eggs and sometimes the birds themselves, but approach the largest colonies carefully. A frightened nesting wall can bury a terrace in guano or send a climber into darkness.

Moths rise in soft-bodied multitudes during the warm season. Some are no larger than fingernails; others spread across a person’s face. Their wings carry muted patterns of ash, rust, cream, and green. They pollinate the night-opening vines, feed the swifts, choke lamps, spoil fermenting mash, and collect in edible drifts beneath screens. Children roast them on clay plates. Their emergence is so central to the Close that years of scarcity are remembered as “thin-wing years.”

Below visibility, beetles and mites govern much of the city. Black compost beetles grind dung and fiber into soil. White-bellied borers soften limestone and old mortar. Red mites swell on goat blood. Tiny silver insects inhabit books of knotted cord and eat the memories stored there. Whole professions exist to encourage one species and thwart another.

The plants, too, are treated as inhabitants with appetites and claims. Vapor ropes hang in braided curtains, their grey skins dull by day and cool before dawn. Paddle shrubs store bitter fluid in fleshy leaves. Bladder-fruit trees twist from baskets and masonry seams, producing red sacs that contain pulp, seed, and a few precious mouthfuls of moisture. Thorn fans spread toward the light and must be negotiated around rather than simply pruned. Deep fungi bloom in pallid shelves from dung walls, old hide, and the remains of baskets. Gardeners know individual plants by the direction of their growth and the taste of their stored liquid.

None of these lives independently. The people drink the leaves; the leaves drink their breath. Goats crop vines and feed root beds. Swifts eat moths and fertilize terraces. Beetles make soil from what the city discards. Cats guard grain by hunting mice, then surrender their own dead to the fungi. Even a fallen antelope is not merely meat but an abrupt flood of water, fat, horn, tendon, hide, bone, and conflict descending through every level.

What lives in the Close is therefore the Close itself: a many-bodied creature fastened inside stone, feeding upward and downward, molting roofs, growing bridges, sealing dead chambers, and extending green fingers toward a stripe of sky. Its people are its hands and memory. Its goats are its wandering teeth. Its birds are its pulse. Its insects are its digestion. Every living thing carries a little moisture through the dry dark, and nothing is permitted to carry it away forever.
