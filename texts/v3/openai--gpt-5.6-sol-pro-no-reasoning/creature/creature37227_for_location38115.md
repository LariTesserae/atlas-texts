They live beneath the stones.

Not under them in burrows, though they make use of hollows and the blind ends of ancient channels. They live within the packed bottom itself: in the pressure between one rounded surface and the next, in seams filled with shell grit, in clay compressed until it holds the memory of every weight laid upon it. To call them buried would be misleading. Burial is something that happens to a body. For them, the seabed is a body.

No whole one has ever been seen.

What appears first is usually a hand, if hand is the right word: a pale arrangement of jointed digits spreading from beneath a slab. It braces against the current without trembling. The digits are short, blunt, and sheathed in translucent tissue through which dark mineral grains can be seen. Some end in hooks of shell-hard material. Others end in flattened pads capable of sealing against stone. One hand may remain in place for decades, slowly thickening around its chosen rock, until it resembles a colorless anemone or a cluster of calcified worms.

Elsewhere, perhaps many body lengths away, another part emerges: a folded fan that opens into the flow, a row of sensory filaments laid along a gravel ridge, a dark lidless eye occupying a hole in basalt. These parts seem disconnected. Early observers would mistake each for a separate animal. Only when the current turns violently, and all the pale hands tighten at once, does the larger unity disclose itself.

The inhabitants are distributed creatures.

Their central mass lies deep in the sediment, but “central” is only approximate. Each has several dense knots of nerve and muscle joined by cords that pass through rubble and clay. If one knot is crushed by a settling stone, the others continue. If a cord is severed, its two ends grow slowly toward the local pressure changes, following minute vibrations until they meet again—or until each accepts its separation and becomes the beginning of another self.

Thus the plain may hold thousands of them, or a few dozen, or one.

Their flesh is cold and scarcely distinguishable from the water. Most of it is soft, gray, and threaded with black vessels. Yet they reinforce themselves with whatever endures. Sponge spicules are drawn into their skin. Shell chips are set along load-bearing surfaces. Manganese coats their oldest exposed parts. Pebbles become embedded in their backs and are never shed. A very old one may contain so much of the seabed that no clean boundary exists between animal and ground.

They do not swim. Swimming would surrender them to the current, and nothing in them is made for surrender. They advance by redistribution.

A forward hand settles among stones and seals itself there. Muscular cords contract through the mud. The rear portions loosen one at a time, allowing the current to lift them only far enough to carry them across the intervening ground. Days later they anchor again. What looks like a stationary patch of rubble may, over a century, cross a basin.

Their feeding structures rise where the water is richest. Some resemble brittle shrubs, branching into combs fine enough to catch bacterial clumps and marine snow. Others are mouths pressed flat beneath stones, consuming worms, crustaceans, and the soft remains of larger animals. When carrion lands, many mouths may open around it. They do not rush. They tighten the bottom instead, drawing stones inward until the body is pinned. Then hundreds of narrow feeding threads enter through gills, wounds, eyes, or softer joints.

Nothing large is swallowed. It is emptied where it falls.

This is the source of their apparent patience. Hunger does not gather in one belly. It passes through them as a slow chemical message. A deprived region contracts and hardens. A well-fed region grows new branches, new anchors, perhaps new sensing pits. One part can starve for years while another feeds.

They perceive pressure more clearly than light. Every scrape of gravel enters them through the stones they hold. A collapse reaches all their buried cords as a vast, low shape. The footfall of a crab, the landing of a shell, the beat of a ray’s fins overhead—each has its own geometry. Their world is not darkness but contact extending beyond contact. The plain continuously touches them with news.

Bioluminescence serves a different sense.

Tiny blue-green flashes strike their exposed eyes as wounds of brightness, showing the outline of motion without revealing the mover. Over ages they have learned the grammar of these sparks. A straight wake means passage. A swelling cloud means struggle. A spiral means the central hollow is turning strongly. When their own bodies must detach, they sometimes release mucus crowded with luminous organisms. The current writes the movement elsewhere while the creature shifts in darkness.

Whether this is deception or merely a useful reflex is impossible to say.

They make nothing that remains separate from themselves. Yet they are not without craft.

A suitable stone is turned until its broadest surface faces the flow. Shells are wedged beneath it. Mud is pumped away from one side and allowed to collect on the other. A sharp shale edge may be held where drifting flesh will strike it. Hollow bones are placed through the sediment as channels for water. Sponge colonies are pruned—not cultivated in any generous sense, but broken where their growth obstructs a feeding fan and spared where they divert particles toward a mouth.

Their tools cannot be distinguished from the plain because concealment and utility are the same act. They do not build upward. They persuade what already lies there to settle differently.

Young arise from injury.

When a cord is severed and cannot reconnect, each isolated portion closes its wound with silt and secreted mineral. Most fragments die, stiffening into pale knots that are eaten or buried. Some reorganize. A feeding branch becomes a mouth. An anchor thickens into a nerve mass. Sensory threads spread along nearby stones. Over decades the fragment gathers enough dispersed anatomy to endure.

For this reason violent currents are both predator and parent. The strongest pulses tear old bodies apart, scattering living pieces into new grounds. Ravines with heaps of shell contain many small, quick-growing inhabitants. Stable mud basins hold fewer but older ones, spread thin across immense areas. In the hard stone fields, ancient anchors descend so far into cracks that they may have survived since the seabed first took its present shape.

The oldest dwell around the central depression.

There the converging currents forbid any large exposed organ. Hands appear and vanish between tumbling pebbles. Tough sensory cords line the channels leading inward. In the revolving silt, transparent feeding veils open for seconds before being furled. Every fragment circling in the hollow is touched, tasted, and released lighter than before.

Deep beneath that turbulence lies a density unlike the scattered bodies of the outer plain. Cords overlap. Nerve knots have fused. Mineral armor interlocks with buried shell beds. Signals entering from one channel return through another altered by passage across uncounted points of contact.

Perhaps this is a colony. Perhaps it is where separate creatures meet. Perhaps all the inhabitants of the plain are extremities of that buried concentration, extended outward through rubble over geological time.

When a distant collapse shakes the seabed, the entire region contracts.

Stones draw closer. Fans close. Eyes withdraw behind mineral lids. The mud seems to settle by a fraction, as though the old plain has taken a breath and held it. Afterward the hands emerge again. Hollows reopen. Filaments extend into the renewed flow.

They have no voice because the water already carries pressure. They have no monuments because anything raised is worn down. They keep no dead, because the dead cannot remain distinct from the bottom. Whatever memory they possess is stored as structure: a cord routed around an old obstruction, an instinctive tightening before a familiar current, a stone retained because some vanished part once chose it.

They are neither rulers of the plain nor victims of it. They have become subject to the same laws.

What is light in them is carried.

What is soft is buried.

What is raised is worn down.

What is hollow fills.

And what survives does not remain whole.
