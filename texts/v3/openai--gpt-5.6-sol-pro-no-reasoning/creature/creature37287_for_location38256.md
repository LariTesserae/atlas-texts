The people beneath the desert are narrow-bodied, deliberate, and difficult to startle. They have learned not to waste width. They turn sideways in familiar passages before the walls require it, tuck their elbows when others pass, and sit with their knees drawn close. Even alone, they occupy as little space as possible.

Their clothes are pale, loose, and repeatedly mended, sewn from cloth that does not cling when the heat thickens. Most wear padded shoulders and close caps against low beams, with dark scarves that can be raised over the mouth when dust descends. Tool belts are arranged flat rather than outward. Metal ornaments are rare: they grow hot, catch on reinforcement wire, and announce movement. What jewelry exists is usually ceramic, cord, or a small brass measuring plate retired from some column whose history has ended.

Dust settles into everyone’s creases. It reddens eyelashes, palms, and the folds around the neck. Condensation leaves other marks: dark crescents at cuffs, mineral blooms on boots, pale salts dried across sleeves after someone has crawled beneath a sweating main. The oldest inhabitants seem made of both conditions at once—powder-dry at the edges, dampened and stained near the skin.

They are builders, but this word is too simple. Every resident is also some kind of listener.

Children learn the difference between the harmless click of cooling metal and the bright, irregular tick that means aggregate is loosening overhead. They are taught to set a cup on the floor when they feel a vibration they cannot name and to watch the water’s surface. They know which painted bars may be crossed, which columns may be touched, and which lengths of wall must never be leaned against. Their first letters are valve numbers and load marks. Their games involve finding routes through the town without passing under unsupported ceiling, or guessing from a pipe’s tremor whether the cooling plant is rising or falling in its cycle.

Adults carry this attention in their posture. Conversations pause when the braces groan. Faces tilt, not upward exactly, but toward the nearest load-bearing member. A hand may remain lifted for several seconds while everyone waits to discover whether the sound will continue. When it does not, speech resumes at the point where it stopped. No one apologizes for the interruption.

There is little distinction between household work and structural work. A cook checks a ceiling gauge while waiting for a kiln plate to heat. A potter clears sediment from a drain that passes through the sleeping room. Someone washing clothes may be asked to note whether the eastern pipe has begun sweating earlier than the western one. Every family keeps chalk, wedges, cord, sealing paste, a hand lamp, and at least one vessel reserved for sudden water. Emergency props stand beside beds like additional members of the household.

They own few things that cannot be abandoned quickly. Furniture is low, built into recesses, or mounted on runners so that it can be shifted when a survey line changes. Cupboards have no backs where pipes need inspection. Tables are scarred with measurements. Sleeping shelves are curtained rather than enclosed, and blankets are rolled each morning in case the room must become a passage by afternoon.

Property is understood strangely here. A wall may belong to one household, the brace supporting it to another workshop, and the moisture condensing on both to the town. Roofs are streets and therefore cannot be wholly private. A cistern belongs to everyone but is approached by appointed hands. No one owns a doorway if that doorway is the shortest escape route from a crack.

Water shapes rank more strongly than wealth. Those trusted to open the cistern cover, clean the gutters during a storm pulse, or divide the first condenser trays hold a grave authority. They do not dress grandly. Their distinction is restraint. They pour without spilling, count without haste, and never drink while measuring another person’s share.

The people drink from shallow cups, often with both hands, not ceremonially but to prevent loss. Children are corrected for breathing over open water. Cooking is done in covered vessels. Wash water passes through several uses: skin, cloth, floor, clay. At last it is carried to the salt plants or mixed into mortar. Nothing wet is casually discarded.

Food comes mostly from elsewhere, lowered through freight shafts in sealed drums: grain, dried pulses, oil, dense cakes of fruit, occasionally preserved meat. What the town makes for itself is mineral and transformed—flat loaves baked against kiln walls, bitter greens clipped from the condenser beds, pale shoots from the drain vines, salt scraped and cleaned from sump margins. Meals taste of smoke, iron, and alkaline water. Crumbs are swept immediately, not because vermin will take them, but because nothing will.

That absence has entered the people’s habits more deeply than they admit.

They have no need to seal food against rats, hang nets against flies, or shake scorpions from boots. Children born below sometimes mistake such animals for decorative signs from the surface. They draw insects with too many legs and lizards with fur. When traders bring pictures, the youngest are fascinated by the idea of creatures small enough to live uninvited in a wall.

Older residents do not find it charming. They remember that a place without vermin is also a place from which life has withdrawn. They watch the plants closely. A folded leaf, a gray tip, or a day without new growth can trouble them more than a cracked tile. There are no pets. Instead, households sometimes adopt a pipe, a gauge, or a condensation tray, polishing it and speaking of its moods as if it were alive. A dependable valve may receive a name. A brace that has held through many movements may be touched in greeting.

The dead are not buried beneath the town. The ground is too burdened already, and no cavity is opened without need. Bodies are wrapped, sealed, and raised through a shaft before dawn, when surface heat is lowest. What happens above depends on season and means, but nothing returns except a small fired tablet impressed with the person’s name, trade, and the dimensions of the space they last maintained. These tablets are mortared into retaining piers. Thus the dead remain not as bodies but as part of the town’s record.

Their shrines are similarly practical. A niche may hold a lamp, a bowl for one spoonful of water, and a fragment of failed material: a bolt sheared cleanly, a piece of black slag, a section of pipe split by pressure. Offerings are often acts of maintenance. Someone cleans the dust from a name. Someone tightens the fitting beneath it. Someone replaces a wick. Prayer and inspection resemble one another.

Their deepest observance occurs when rain has fallen above.

They cannot see the clouds gather or lightning cross the desert, but they recognize the change in pressure and the smell that descends the vents. Work slows. Covers are removed from jars and basins. People take their assigned positions along the channels. When the first ticking begins inside the overburden, everyone becomes still.

Then the water comes, and the town moves all at once.

Grates are cleared. Gates are opened in order. Children carry empty vessels and return with full ones. Sediment crews kneel in ocher slurry up to their wrists. The cistern keepers call measurements. For those few minutes, silence yields to urgent speech, but not shouting. Every word has been decided beforehand. When the pulse ends, the channels are scraped, the vessels sealed, and a new narrow rainfall mark is painted beside the old stains.

Only afterward do they celebrate. Bread is softened in fresh water. People wash their faces without reusing the cloth. A few drops are placed at the plant roots. For one evening the town smells less of dust.

The slow heat that travels the fracture faces after sunset is treated differently. No one calls it a ghost, though children do. Surveyors describe thermal release, uneven conductivity, buried metal, trapped stresses. Electricians explain the dimming lamps. Valve workers explain the tightening seals. Each explanation accounts for a piece of it.

Still, before the passage arrives, the plants fold.

Those awake at that hour stop what they are doing. Some place a palm against brick. Others extinguish unnecessary lights. Along the route of the heat, people wait in doorways as corridor after corridor warms. The wave has no fixed speed. Some nights it crosses the settlement quickly; on others it lingers beside the oldest workshop, where obsolete iron gathers water at dawn.

No one follows it. No one stands in its path merely to prove courage. They let it pass.

Certain inhabitants seem especially attuned to it: an elderly gauge keeper who wakes moments before the first lamp dims; a kiln worker whose scars ache in sequence with the warming walls; children who turn in sleep toward the next corridor. Such people are not called prophets. They are given night duties.

Leadership belongs less to rulers than to those who can interpret change without pretending certainty. The town’s council includes structural workers, water keepers, vent tenders, electricians, potters, and route-minders who memorize every traversable passage. Meetings take place around a table crossed by a load-bearing pier. Decisions are written in chalk first, because nearly all decisions may need revision.

Arguments concern centimeters: whether a stair can be narrowed, whether another jacket can fit around a pier, whether a family can remain in a room through the next adjustment cycle. A proposal that looks elegant is mistrusted. A proposal that can fail visibly, gradually, and in a direction people can escape is preferred.

The most respected craft is not construction but yielding: knowing what must be sacrificed so that the rest can remain. A room is emptied before the crack reaches it. A beloved kiln is dismantled to clear airflow. A house loses half its sleeping shelf to a brace. People grieve these amputations, but they do not confuse endurance with rigidity.

They speak of the town as if it were unfinished, never ruined. The distinction matters. Ruins belong to the past; this place is still choosing its form.

Newcomers find the inhabitants reserved. Questions are answered plainly, but no one wastes breath filling silence. Trust is established through attention: noticing a loose catch, returning a borrowed measure, stepping around a collection tray without being warned. Someone who can remain quiet during a structural groan is accepted sooner than someone generous with gifts.

Laughter exists, though it is muted and often abrupt. Much of their humor concerns impossible luxuries: rooms with only one purpose, stairs that lead where they were intended, water deep enough to enter, a roof no one must measure. Surface visitors are teased for looking upward whenever dust falls. Lovers joke about finding privacy in a town where every wall carries a pipe.

Courtship happens through work. One person repairs another’s lamp cage, saves a cool place beside a vent, or leaves a covered cup during kiln duty. Couples exchange route cords marked with knots for safe turns. Marriage, where practiced, is less a joining of property than of obligations: whose tray will be emptied, whose gauges checked, whose escape route kept clear.

Children belong partly to the whole settlement because danger ignores households. Any adult may pull a child back from a white load line or send one to fetch a wedge. Adolescents prove themselves by keeping a silent watch alone, usually in a chamber where several gauges can be seen at once. The task is not to remain fearless. It is to report exactly what happened, without enlarging or minimizing it.

Their bodies bear the town’s work: flattened fingertips, pale burn scars, knees darkened by brick dust, hearing sharpened in some and damaged in others. Many develop a slight stoop beneath the low ceiling. On the rare occasions they go above, they continue to duck for days. Open sky unsettles them. With no ceiling to read and no wall within reach, they feel exposed to a vastness that offers no measurements.

Some cannot remain on the surface at all. They become dizzy in the brightness, disturbed by wind that has passed through no grille and carries no useful warning. Others are drawn to it and eventually leave. Those who return bring sun-bleached cloth, machine parts, seeds that seldom sprout, and stories everyone pretends not to hunger for.

Yet the town is not inhabited by prisoners. Many could depart and do not. They know each pipe by its vibration, each passage by its thermal drift, each neighbor by footfall and pause. Above is distance. Here, nothing is distant. Need, danger, water, memory, and other people are all within arm’s reach.

So they remain between the two heats, adding columns to abandoned rooms and doorways to walls that were never meant to open. They sleep lightly. They measure faithfully. They collect what the metal gives them.

And when the foundations groan, every face grows still—not with surprise, and not quite with fear, but with the concentration of people listening to the thing that contains them decide whether, for another night, it will continue to hold.
