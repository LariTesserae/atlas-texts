What lives there is not one creature, though at times the distinction seems merely grammatical.

The plateau is inhabited by a commonwealth of organs that seldom acknowledge the boundaries of bodies. Some stand under the clouds in silver groves; some swim through root tissue; some graze the upper skin on delicate articulated legs. Others exist only as pressures moving belowground, as reflexes shared among distant marshes, or as temporary anatomies assembled when the landscape discovers a need.

The most pervasive life is the mat itself.

It is composed of innumerable small plants—mosses, bladdered creepers, dark-haired condensers, translucent succulents—yet beneath their apparent variety their tissues interlock. Root enters root. Wounds are grafted before they can rot. Water, heat, memory, and half-formed organs pass between species. The mat does not think as an animal thinks, but it notices weight. It remembers injury. It can withdraw warmth from a patch of ground, stiffen a plain against an approaching storm, or soften itself beneath something it wishes to swallow.

Its age is distributed. No single part is ancient, but the pattern by which one part replaces another may be older than the present shape of the plateau. Death rarely removes anything from this system. The dead are opened, wetted, and taken back into circulation. A fallen stem may become a valve. An animal’s cartilage may reappear as the teeth of an underground mill. Old eyes, dissolved into mineral broth, become light-sensitive red flowers.

Thus the ground is less a colony than an agreement that has lasted too long to be broken.

Above it rise the bellows-groves, among the nearest things here to sedentary giants. Their pleated trunks inhale weather. Mist passes through their bark; rain collects in the funnels of their crowns; spores and small animals are drawn into warm chambers and expelled elsewhere. They pump with the solemn rhythm of immense sleeping hearts, though no heart is central to them.

A grove may contain hundreds of columns and still be one individual. Another may appear identical but be no individual at all: merely a hydraulic arrangement formed for a season by unrelated plants. When two groves meet underground, they exchange pressures before they exchange tissue. They may cooperate for centuries, quarrel through drought, or slowly reverse one another’s circulation until one flowers and the other becomes a pond.

Their reproductive bodies are not seeds in the ordinary sense. They manufacture components.

In buried nurseries, pods swell around useful shapes: a hinge, a lens, a gas bladder, a toothed ring, a length of insulated nerve. These parts are released into the watercourses and carried away. Some graft onto damaged roots. Some join with other parts and emerge as animals. Some remain dormant in pools until a particular thunder sequence wakes them. The creatures they produce are therefore not descendants so much as provisional solutions.

Among the most visible are the spindle herds.

They move over the wet plains on legs so fine that each hoof spreads its pressure through a fan of hairs. A mature grazer may possess twelve or sixteen joints per limb. It can lengthen itself to cross a flooded hollow or collapse into a knot small enough to hide beneath a leaf. Its body hangs high between the legs: a dark, seedlike capsule containing little more than a rasping mouth, balance sacs, and a spool of gut.

They feed on the faintly luminous films that form over vegetation at dusk. A herd leaves broad paths of darkness behind it, not because the plants are eaten, but because their light has been shaved away. By morning the glow returns.

Spindle grazers have no eyes. They sense approaching danger through the tension of the surface beneath them. Alarm crosses a herd faster than sound: one animal loosens its joints, the tremor travels through the water skin, and hundreds fold simultaneously. Their legs lie parallel to the prevailing vegetation, their bodies flatten, and what was a moving congregation becomes a patch of wind-pressed grass.

Many do not carry all their organs with them. During migration, their digestive chambers trail beneath the turf, connected to their mouths by root-thin tubes. A herd crossing the plateau above may share a wandering stomach below. When the animals separate, the stomach divides by constriction, assigning each traveler a warm portion of itself.

The bladder-winged creatures occupy the low sky.

They resemble loose, transparent kites, though their internal scaffolds continually branch and dissolve. Their wings are fermentation chambers inhabited by bright microbial gardens. By feeding or starving these gardens, they alter temperature and pressure, rising into colder air or settling until their trailing mouths comb the moss.

They drink clouds. Water condenses within their wings until their bodies darken and sag. Then they travel toward thirsty districts and release the burden through narrow valves. Rain follows them in silver threads. Plants below open before the first drops arrive, alerted by shadows that the fliers have not yet cast.

Some bladder-wings live for decades. Others are aerial organs launched by the plateau during drought. When their task is complete, they descend, split along their seams, and root where they fall. Their transparent membranes cloud, thicken, and become the first skin of a new pool.

Beneath the surface, life is more populous and less certain of its outlines.

Blind burrowers chew through living tissue without killing it. Their bodies secrete compounds that persuade roots to part and seal behind them. Filter worms anchor themselves in pressure channels and spread their feathery mouths into the current, catching spores, mineral grains, scraps of sensation, and unattached organs. Colonies of hot mites cluster around rotary growths, maintaining them by eating warped tissue before it can obstruct their movement.

Pale swimmers pass between water and plant flesh without recognizing a boundary. They enter a root as easily as a fish enters reeds. Within the root they become thin and branching; upon reaching a chamber, they thicken again into soft amphibious forms. They may surface through a flower, breathe once, and dive back through the stem.

Many are assembled only when required.

A hunting mouth can bud at the edge of a tarn while its stomach matures kilometers away in a heated cavern. Between them grows a temporary digestive cord threaded through existing roots. Eyes may be borrowed from flowers. Legs arrive in pods and fasten themselves beneath the feeding body. If prey is scarce, the arrangement disbands: the mouth becomes a valve, the stomach joins a bellows-grove, and the legs walk elsewhere carrying eggs.

Predators are even less committed to bodies.

One kind travels as a shallow lens of coordinated air. Beneath it, moss bends without wind, and the horizon appears displaced by a finger’s width. It is made from thousands of nearly invisible animals holding a shared membrane between them. Alone, each is harmless and scarcely larger than mist. Together they form a moving refractive skin that traps warmth and dazzles grazing herds into stillness. Once over its prey, the skin contracts. What follows looks like sudden frost.

Another predator lives high above the clouds and is known chiefly by its discarded teeth. These fall in showers of clear pellets, striking pools with the sound of hail. Each tooth remains alive for several hours. It wriggles toward vibration, bites whatever it reaches, then dissolves into a cold gel that roots readily in wounds.

The great migratory sirens are rarer. No one sees an entire one. Their passage is inferred from a rising mechanical howl, from the simultaneous leaning of distant groves, and from the displacement of direction around them. They may be long organisms distributed across weather fronts, their parts separated by leagues of cloud. They do not migrate north or south. Rather, the local meaning of north gathers around their leading organs and travels with them.

There are also inhabitants made from repetition rather than flesh.

Ancient plants return during atmospheric disturbances as dim, incomplete ecologies. They are not ghosts, because nothing corresponding to them may ever have lived. They are possibilities retained by the plateau: forests anticipated and abandoned, forms suggested by fossils that pressure later invented. Vast flowers drift above the tarns without stems. Tree-ferns appear as vertical patterns of dripping. Trunkless canopies move over the moor, shading it from a sun that is not visible.

These revenant floras last minutes or days. Present vegetation enters them with pale feeding threads and dismantles their patterns. When the apparitions fade, unfamiliar sugars remain in the roots, and new animals are sometimes assembled from memories the plateau has consumed.

The oldest inhabitants may be the absent ones.

A vanished hill persists as weight. Roots bend around it. Creatures entering its former mass crouch and grow pale, behaving as though buried. Streams inhabit courses from which the land has fallen away, flowing unsupported through the air. Waterfalls continue to descend beside slopes erased millions of years ago.

These absences are occupied by specialized life. Flat, eyeless things graze pressure from the sites of former mountains. Silver larvae live inside suspended streams and never touch the ground. In old waterfall curtains, colonial animals arrange themselves like ladders, feeding on particles that fall from elevations no longer connected to any source.

Even the industrial noises have their fauna.

Hammering comes from colonies that communicate by striking mineral plates grown into the roots. Rotary drones are the voices of circular tissues grinding stone. Brake shrieks issue when long tension-vines contract against wet cartilage. Whistles are pressure parasites calling through the trunks of bellows-groves. What sounds like distant machinery is millions of living acts synchronized by water.

And then there is the thing mistaken for a train.

It has no locomotive, no carriages, and perhaps no enduring body. It begins when the plateau’s distributed processes fall into a single cadence. Pumps align. Milling mouths lock their rotations together. Suspended rivers straighten. Herds collapse, bladder-wings empty themselves, and the entire root mat opens along a line extending beyond the horizon.

That line is its passage.

Rain rises in advance of it, drawn upward through trembling vegetation. Amber signals race beneath the turf. White vapor erupts from the groves in evenly spaced columns. The hammering grows louder—not approaching through distance, but recruiting each district into sequence. The “wheels” are pressure fronts striking old geological joints. The horn is the combined exhalation of organs over many leagues.

For a time, everything on the plateau becomes part of one migratory anatomy.

Pools serve as eyes. Groves become lungs. Mills become teeth. Streams tighten into nerves. Animals caught in the sequence are not crushed; they are assigned functions. A herd may become sensory hairs along its flank. A cloud of mites may carry heat from one end of the passage to the other. Travelers lying beside shallow water may feel their own heartbeat taken up, repeated miles away, and returned altered.

Then the plateau passes through itself.

The line closes. Water falls downward again. Groves resume their private breathing. Animals unfold, sometimes with unfamiliar organs. The apparent train departs without having gone anywhere, leaving no track except two parallel changes in the vegetation: one flowering early, the other remembering winter.

Nothing rules this place. Nothing comprehends it whole. Yet all that lives there participates in a life larger than ownership—a life that uses landscape as anatomy, weather as behavior, and deep time as instinct.

The plateau has forgotten height.

Its inhabitants have forgotten where one body ends.
