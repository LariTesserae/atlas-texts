The drowned station is inhabited less by any single creature than by a compact society assembled around pressure, current, and corroding iron.

Mussels hold the thresholds. They mass inside the fractured intake throats, layered over one another until the masonry seems to have grown dark, jointed lips. Their shells narrow the passages and accelerate the water, bringing more suspended food across their gills. At each turn of the tide they open in thousands, quietly sieving the harbor through themselves. Tube worms occupy slower water. On the dead pump they have built a chalk-white reef so thick that the fixed wheel no longer resembles machinery; its spokes have become sheltered lanes for small fish.

Pale shrimp belong to the moving parts. They gather in bearing recesses, under plates, and in the lee of wheel housings, nearly invisible until a shudder passes through the mechanism. Then they lift all at once in translucent clouds, catch the weak overhead light, and settle again after the vibration fades. Gobies live lower down, among rust flakes, bolt heads, glass pebbles, and broken insulators. They rest on broad heads and splayed fins, darting only when crabs approach or the reversing flow exposes something edible.

The crabs are the station’s patient workers. They patrol flanges and mortar ribs, probe old grease with their claws, and disappear into gauge boxes whose faces and needles are long gone. Some wear the chamber on their shells: bacterial fur, pale mineral crust, a small mussel attached where the legs cannot reach. They feed in the housings of obsolete instruments and shelter behind valve wheels, as if still attending the equipment.

Juvenile fish occupy the upper galleries in tight, disciplined schools. They turn around handrails, pass through stair grates, and divide cleanly at columns before joining again. In the dim water they appear colorless until their flanks align with a shaft of daylight or one of the surviving red lamps. Then the whole shoal flashes once—dull silver by day, bruised crimson by night—and vanishes back into the architecture.

Conger eels keep to the cable conduits. Their heads emerge from round ceramic-lined openings, while the greater length of each body remains folded in passages made for wires. They wait through the loudest phases of the tide with their jaws slightly open, tasting the driven water. When the pumps slow, they leave the walls and move across the engine well, long and unhurried, following routes through fallen catwalks and pipework that no other large animal can use.

Seals are visitors rather than residents. At high tide one may compress itself through the bent western grille and enter with a train of bubbles rolling from its fur. For several minutes it becomes the station’s dominant fact. Shoals burst apart. Shrimp vanish into seams. Gobies flatten against iron, and the eels retreat backward into their conduits. The seal hunts between columns and flywheels, maneuvering through the confined room with soft precision, then leaves before the current becomes dangerous. In its absence the station reassembles its life by degrees.

Much of what lives here is harder to see. Brown algae persist on the upper masonry where occasional daylight reaches. Pallid kelp ropes trail from the western grille, permanently combed toward whichever side is downstream. Bacterial films cover bare iron and old lubricants, spreading in white colonies through protected corners. Larvae drift in with the flood, settle or are eaten, and become part of the chamber’s population or its sediment. The floor itself is alive in this diffuse way: shell fragments, organic silt, rust, microbes, and powdered masonry mingled until habitation and decay are the same layer.

All of them live by the old station’s involuntary labor. The backward-driven pumps stir oxygen into the basin and keep food moving through it. Their slight warmth attracts fish in winter. Their knocks flush shrimp from cover and signal changes in flow. Their stopped surfaces provide shelter; their moving surfaces prevent anything delicate from settling. The animals, in turn, alter the machines. Mussels constrict the intakes. Worm colonies immobilize spokes. Crabs clear soft accumulations from housings. Shell and bacterial growth change how water crosses every flange and grille.

No inhabitant understands the station as a ruin. To the mussel, it is current. To the goby, a field of ledges. To the eel, a system of burrows. To the schooling fish, it is open water divided by reliable obstacles. Even the slow machinery is not foreign to them. Its cycles are as environmental as tide or winter.

Between the red service lamps, this population feeds, shelters, scatters, and returns. It inhabits not nature reclaimed from industry, but an environment in which the distinction has worn away. The station is their reef, their cave, their warm spring, their tidal channel—and, through its rust, shell, waste, and living film, increasingly their substance as well.
