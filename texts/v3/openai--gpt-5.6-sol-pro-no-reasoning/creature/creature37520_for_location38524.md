Beneath the submerged shelves live things so slight that the chamber’s falling drops could crush them.

They are translucent commas, each scarcely thicker than a shaving of salt. Against the black glass their bodies appear almost empty: a clear outer membrane enclosing a faintly clouded interior, with one dense silver knot near the hooked end. They possess no eyes, mouths, limbs, or obvious organs. Light from the ceiling seam passes through them and is altered only slightly, giving their edges a weak violet glimmer when the mineral field shifts.

Most of the time they do not move.

They fasten themselves to the undersides of ledges where the brine is coolest and the current thinnest. The flattened underside of each body spreads against the glass as if wetted there. Microscopic folds grip irregularities too small to see. In this posture they resemble flaws trapped in the rock—clear smears, curved bubbles, scraps of cooled resin. Several may occupy one ledge without touching, each aligned to the slow passage of water.

Their stillness is protective. When superheated brine wells into the pools, the creatures contract from tip to tip. Their membranes thicken and turn glossy; water is expelled from the body; the soft comma folds around its silver knot until it becomes a hard, nearly spherical capsule. These capsules detach and settle into narrow fractures, lodging among sulfur grains and fresh silica. There they endure the heat pulse in a state so reduced that life is detectable only as minute chemical repair.

As the pool cools, they unfold.

The capsule first develops a seam. One side loosens, then spills outward into a thin, flexible body. The creature rises by filling internal chambers with vapor and dissolved gas, drifting from the crack without any visible stroke. It does not swim so much as adjust its buoyancy and let the chamber’s weak currents carry it.

Suspended in the white-lit brine are simpler inhabitants: tiny heat-feeding cells nourished by hydrogen, sulfur compounds, dissolved metals, and the electrical gradients leaking from the radiant seam. They form no mats and leave almost no stain. Individually invisible, they gather for short periods in faint opalescent veils where hot and cooler waters mingle.

The translucent drifters graze through these veils. Their entire outer surface serves as a mouth. Passing cells adhere to a film on the membrane, dissolve, and are drawn inward as diffuse nourishment. A feeding body slowly clouds with pearl-colored granules. Once replete, it sinks beneath a ledge and spreads itself flat again.

Their lives are governed not by day or night but by pressure, heat, and the changing charge of the ceiling crystals. A violet fluctuation makes them tense before the brine itself changes. Their bodies curve toward the local field, silver knots turning into alignment like compass needles. When droplets hang motionless overhead, the creatures cling tightly. When the suspended rain releases, they flatten further while impacts hammer the pool into turbulence.

They reproduce rarely. A well-fed individual grows a second silver knot, then lengthens until it resembles a fine transparent crescent. A constriction forms between the knots. For hours the two halves remain joined, sharing dissolved material through a narrowing bridge. At last a vibration, a heat pulse, or the impact of a drop separates them. One half retains the old hooked shape. The other drifts as a nearly straight sliver and develops its curve over several cooling cycles.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing here is large enough.

Their chief dangers are the chamber itself: a pulse too hot to survive, a crack sealed with silica while they sleep inside it, a pool draining before they can encapsulate, or a falling shard that erases an entire cluster. Their dead leave no skeletons. Membranes rupture, silver knots disperse, and the brine reclaims their salts. Only occasionally does silica close around one quickly enough to preserve a clear comma-shaped absence.

They have no need to know the chamber as a room. To them it is a sequence of gradients: hot below, cooler beneath the shelves, nourishment at the mixing boundaries, lethal brightness near certain charged fractures. Their world expands when water rises and contracts to isolated wet seams when it drains. A distance of several finger-widths may contain every condition of their existence.

Around them live the smaller cells on which they feed, and perhaps nothing else. No roots approach from above. No burrow opens from another darkness. The chamber’s population is a sparse constellation of transparent bodies and invisible chemistries, repeatedly scattered and gathered by the breathing brine.

They are suited not to permanence but to interruption. Their capsules wait through violence; their feeding forms exploit the brief intervals between pulses. Yet the sealed room is changing faster than they can perceive. The channels deepen. Salts accumulate. Silica clouds their ledges. One day the brine may withdraw completely, leaving their glossy capsules in dry fractures. Or it may rise and remain too hot. The radiant seam may fade, taking with it the gradients that sustain the cells beneath them.

For now, whenever the water cools by a few degrees, the black pools quietly fill with drifting commas. In the shadowless mineral light they ascend, graze, divide, and sink again—almost invisible lives inhabiting the first wet weeks of a world.
