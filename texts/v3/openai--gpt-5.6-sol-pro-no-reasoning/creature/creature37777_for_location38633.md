The Choir is inhabited not by a single sovereign creature, but by a dispersed life whose body is the cleft itself.

Its visible inhabitants are innumerable and intensely local. Lacquer-backed beetles harvest the moss islands. Pale crabs inhabit damp seams high above the pools their ancestors may once have known. Glassy ferns shelter thumb-sized frogs, each defending a mineral cup of water with a call larger than its body. Long-legged lizards cross the hot stone in measured bursts, while folded-eared mammals travel among pendant roots and sleep where the old devices misdirect the voices of snakes. Ribbon-thin hunters follow them. Birds hunt the hunters. Something with three-pointed feet moves across vertical walls, seldom presenting the rest of itself.

These animals live not merely in stone, water, and vegetation, but in transmitted consequences. A cry uttered in one chamber becomes weather in another. A beak striking metal releases prey at a distance. A breeding call returns as thunder. Over generations, instinct has expanded into the mechanisms: frogs have learned their remote throats, birds their invisible hunting grounds, mammals their acoustic refuges. The devices are as much habitat as the pools and ferns.

Yet another inhabitant seems to exist among them—if “inhabitant” is the right word.

It has no stable outline. Its nerves may be the arches and rings; its circulation, the intermittent water; its thoughts, the routed cries of thousands of animals. Most of the time it is divisible into ordinary events: condensation bending, insects changing course, echoes arriving late, a stone cooling beside a vanished pool. After rain, these events briefly cohere. The deep doorway sounds its low note, and water climbs the wall to draw a silver anatomy of the cleft, including organs too large or too impossible to fit beneath the plateau.

Perhaps this presence is an ancient machine still completing an operation. Perhaps it is an ecology that has learned to use abandoned instruments as a nervous system. Perhaps the builders reduced themselves until they became mites, spores, impulses, or relations between calls. It may not distinguish between organism and mechanism at all.

What is certain is that it listens.

Ordinary animal sounds are gathered, transformed, and returned as part of the food web. Imitation receives a different attention. When a visitor copies a frog or whistles into a ring, the Choir gradually quiets its smaller lives. The reply comes back corrected—not contradicted, but developed, as though the first sound were an unfinished proposal. Each repetition teaches the hidden listener more about the voice producing it.

Then it begins to anticipate.

The traveler’s breath emerges from arches ahead and behind. Impossible passages acquire lungs. Between one inhalation and the next comes a second rhythm: vast, warm, and patient. It does not seem to belong to a creature concealed under the plateau. Rather, for those moments, the plateau itself behaves as the outer shell of something sleeping at a scale the cleft can only translate into breath.

By evening that presence loosens again into its constituents. It becomes frogs, wings, droplets, mineral basins, black filaments, cooling stone. Life resumes its countless hungers. The immense breath disappears, though perhaps only because no single ear can hear what all the rings are saying together.

So what lives in the Choir is a community, a machine, and possibly a mind—three descriptions of the same crowded being, depending on how closely one listens.
