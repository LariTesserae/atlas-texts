The city is inhabited less by citizens than by functions wearing animal shapes.

Beetles live in its electrical boxes. Their lacquered bodies bridge contacts and break them; whole neighborhoods brighten when they crowd together and dim when they disperse. They carry the Mill’s numbered pods into cracks behind the walls, where the pods ripen into batteries, larvae, or small locked rooms. Furred mites travel the handrails in grazing herds, keeping passages open by cropping moss, fresh bark, and the letters from signs. They favor vowels, so directions gradually become clusters of hard consonants.

Pale lizards cling inside chimneys and feed on soot before it can become entirely mineral. Eyeless birds occupy the ventilation ducts. At the city’s weak dawn they pass from chamber to chamber, bearing odors in their feathers: rain to the nurseries, hot copper to the factories, blood to the roots that require warning. Their migrations give each room its temporary purpose.

Where the intermittent river can be heard, silver fish swim through the wet air. They move cautiously, as though the atmosphere were deep water, turning around furniture and slipping beneath bridges. Direct light erases them. Leaf-built foxes hunt them by sensing the pressure of their passage. These foxes have aluminum bones, foliage for flesh, and cable-thin nerves. At rest they coil around transformers and dream in pulses of blue current. After feeding, their leaves sometimes acquire the prey’s silver sheen.

A six-footed grazer tends the perimeter. Broad, heavy, and clay-colored, it eats every root that ventures onto the vacant plain. It is gardener, jailer, and perhaps boundary marker. No one directs it. The roots continually test its attention, withdrawing when bitten and returning elsewhere with altered strategies.

Predators are seldom seen clearly. They may be animals, rooms, or brief errors in perspective. A patch of foliage tightens; a shadow folds inward; something disappears. The survivor then bears a new stripe, voice, scent, or habit. Here predation does not merely transfer flesh. It transfers description.

There may be larger beings in the inaccessible spaces below. Their engines throb under the roots. Furnace-red eyes open behind grilles. Pistons breathe in chambers no corridor reaches. Whether these are buried animals, autonomous machines, or organs belonging to the city cannot be determined. The distinction may not exist here.

No human population appears to maintain the streets and factories, though humanlike expectations linger everywhere: handles at hand height, stairs proportioned to familiar legs, windows placed for watchers, platforms that arrive for passengers who rarely come. Perhaps their builders are gone. Perhaps the city anticipates them. Perhaps the architecture itself is the remnant of a human-shaped organism.

The most powerful inhabitant is the collective formed by all the smaller ones. Beetles regulate current; mites prune routes; birds distribute information as scent; predators prevent doors and prey from multiplying beyond control; the boundary grazer keeps the hollow distinct from the unfinished world. Each creature performs a civic task without appearing to know it. Together they behave as a municipality, a nervous system, and a single colonial body.

At its center turns the Mill, which may be that body’s reproductive organ. Small animals enter carrying seeds, shells, stones, and warm metal. Larger animals emerge, often unrelated to those that went in. The Mill does not seem to manufacture individuals so much as revise them. Its slow wheel sorts matter from possibility. Its fast shadow rehearses outcomes. What comes out may be an animal promoted into a new ecological office—or several former creatures consolidated into one.

Even the city’s rules seem alive. Hunger stretches distances. Fear breeds exits. Repetition produces parallel streets. Solitude persuades tools and masonry to become botanical. Names act like temporary cages, and anything named too accurately molts out of its definition. These rules inhabit the hollow as surely as the foxes and beetles do. They feed on behavior and reproduce through habit.

The city itself may be the only true resident.

Its trunks are skeleton and scaffolding. Sap and electricity share a circulation. Factories act as glands; roads as reflexes; rooms as expandable organs. It feels empty because its life is not concentrated into visible persons. Consciousness, if present, is dispersed through roots deciding, insects counting, bells turning soundlessly, and water remembering what it has touched.

Beyond the rim, its shadow produces dark shoots on the raw plain. Each shoot unfolds a leaf and constructs an empty room. The animals gather to watch as they might watch an egg crack open. The engines slow. The entire hollow attends.

What lives here is therefore not a stable catalogue of species, but a city in the act of inventing inhabitants—including itself. Every creature is provisional, every machine partly embryonic, every building a behavior that has hardened for a while into form. The distant room may become a daughter settlement, a displaced organ, a future version of the hollow, or simply the next thought of the same enormous being.

By morning, whatever occupies it will likely believe it has always lived there.
