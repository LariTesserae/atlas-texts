The chief inhabitant is not an animal but a body of attention distributed through the cup.

It lives most densely beneath the central pool, though “beneath” may be misleading. The depth there is not a shaft through stone. It is distance stored in another direction: folded acoustically, perhaps, or held open by the pressure of reflected sound. The green stones visible below the water are not a bottom but a repeated image of one, each layer withdrawing as it is approached. Whatever inhabits that recession has no fixed surface on which to rest. It hangs through the depth like a sleeper suspended in clear water.

The basin is its outer anatomy.

Basalt forms the hard, weather-rounded parts of it. Moss and soil are slower tissues, sealing cracks and redistributing pressure. Roots trace its channels as nerves might follow grooves laid down before their growth. The widely spaced shrubs, ferns, and silver-barked trees are not arranged as a garden, yet they behave like organs placed at necessary intervals. Their leaves collect moisture, measure wind, and release drops of particular sizes from particular heights. The plants do not merely grow here; they articulate the creature’s hearing.

Each falling bead enters it as information.

The disproportionate noises are produced because the pool does not receive drops at its visible surface alone. A single impact is repeated down the folded depth, striking farther versions of the same water until the accumulated sound returns as a cavernous note. The knocks and booms underfoot are not echoes in empty chambers. They are the inhabitant’s internal responses: small adjustments, contractions, and transfers of weight. During loud rain it becomes more awake. Ferns bow inward because the sounds themselves acquire mass as they pass into its body.

Its timescale is immense. A human lifetime is scarcely long enough for one attentive movement. The advancing pool is not growth in the ordinary sense but a gradual opening—something comparable to the dilation of an eye. The retreating sedge marks the widening edge of perception. Mineral bands on the basalt record earlier awakenings, when the opening expanded much farther and the creature listened through nearly the whole basin. The mouth-shaped hollows along those old waterlines may once have released trapped air, but they also resemble the impressions of many small organs of reception, exposed and then sealed as the water fell.

It does not seem predatory. The rarity of animals is not caused by hunting. Living bodies avoid the cup because every heartbeat, footfall, wingbeat, and breath becomes unusually legible there. To enter is to be heard too completely. Most creatures turn aside before crossing the rim.

The copper frogs can endure it because they are solitary and silent. Their coloration matches the mineral stains left by the basin’s warm outflow, and their small bodies offer little resonance. They may be ordinary frogs altered by long association, or mobile sensory knots shed by the place during wet weather. Each sits motionless where several hidden runnels meet, feeling vibrations through its limbs. None calls because calling would mean feeding its entire shape into the listener below.

The black bird is a boundary-keeper of another kind. It never lands because the cup could retain the sound of its landing. Its long wings let it cross with few strokes, carrying observations between this summit and places beyond the mountain. Whether it serves the inhabitant or merely knows it is uncertain.

The unseen weight traveling beneath the moss is closer to the central life. It may be a limb, though not one made of flesh: a moving concentration of water and compressed sound that passes through root cavities without breaking them. Where it travels, the surface rises in a smooth line. It may inspect disturbances around the rim, gather fallen vibrations, or rearrange the drainage beneath the plants. Nothing remains displaced because it does not force matter aside; it briefly persuades the ground to become buoyant.

The toolmakers are real, but they are not permanent residents. They come seldom, likely from hidden clefts lower on the mountain, and appear adapted to leave almost no acoustic history. They are small, deliberate beings with dexterous hands or handlike forefeet. They crack nuts only on stones whose tones they have tested. They cut fern stems to obtain hollow lengths, perhaps for listening tubes, gauges, or resonators. From the eastern seam they strike blades of volcanic glass and carry away every flake because loose shards would chatter under rain and introduce false notes.

They build no shelter because prolonged occupation would alter the basin’s responses. They light no fire because rising heat would disturb the suspended moisture. Their visits coincide with higher water because only then does the folded depth approach closely enough to be sounded.

These visitors may be attendants rather than worshippers. Their work appears practical: pruning leaves that would release drops at the wrong interval, opening or closing small channels, tapping selected surfaces, and comparing the answers. Generations could pass between necessary adjustments. It is possible that no individual understands the mechanism in full. Each may inherit only a few actions: cut this stem when the white band is submerged; strike this stone after thunder; remove every flake; never answer an echo that comes from below.

The delayed reflections are another population of the place, though barely separate from it. Every reflected thing lingers a fraction behind its source, as if its image must travel through the full hidden depth before returning to the surface. Usually the delay is slight. Over long periods, however, the basin accumulates these fractions. A leaf’s reflection continues descending after the leaf has floated away. A bird’s dark shape may cross the pool minutes after the sky is empty. At dusk, faces have sometimes remained in the droplets between leaves even when no face is present nearby.

These are not ghosts so much as unfinished perceptions. The inhabitant remembers by reflecting.

Its exhalations are the low clouds that persist after breath. Its finest nerves are the nocturnal strings of condensation. Each suspended droplet holds an inverted pool because each is temporarily connected to the central depth, a minute secondary opening hung in the air. Throughout the night the basin distributes attention among them. At dawn, when the strands collapse together, their gathered impressions return as rain.

What the listener awaits is unclear. Thunder is close enough to stir it, but not sufficient. The mountain’s thunder circles the rim because the basin catches it and turns it repeatedly, testing its shape. Sometimes the answer from below is only the same thunder after a long passage through folded distance. Sometimes it is not quite the same: lower, slower, bearing an interval no storm produced.

The cup may be listening for a voice of its own species—another deep pool calling through the mountain, or through distances the visible world does not contain. It may be waiting for an eruption, a continental fracture, the fall of something enormous from the sky. It may have been constructed to receive a single ancient signal that has not yet arrived.

Or the expected sound may be far smaller.

Perhaps it is waiting for the correct nutshell to be struck on the correct stone, for a cut fern tube to sound one pure note over the water, or for some careful visitor to speak into the pool at the instant of absolute silence. The whole green mechanism—rock, rain, roots, frogs, invisible workers, and patient depth—may exist to recognize that one note.

When it comes, the pool will not overflow.

It will look shallow at last.

And whatever has been listening from its impossible depth will be standing at the surface.
