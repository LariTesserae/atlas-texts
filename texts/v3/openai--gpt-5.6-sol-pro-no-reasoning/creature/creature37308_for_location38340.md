The cavern is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, immeasurable ecology wearing countless bodies.

Its most visible inhabitants are the pale grazers. Hare-sized and long-legged, they move in herds so vast that individuals disappear into collective motion. Their hides are white, ash-gray, or faintly translucent over the joints. Many have only sealed dimples where eyes might have been. They navigate through fanlike ears, sensitive feet, and whiskers that quiver at every wingbeat and falling drop. With hard, rasping mouthparts they harvest films too thin to be seen: algae, mineral-fed microbes, fungal dust, perhaps the stone’s own residue. A herd crossing a terrace looks like gravel flowing uphill.

Around and beneath them thrives a smaller multitude. Blind beetles polish the cracks with their passage. Silver springtails rise in clouds around fresh droppings, then settle again. Glassy crickets pulse from crevices. Soft scavengers enter carcasses almost as soon as blood stops moving, while quick six-legged forms retreat into fissures at the slightest tremor. Flatworms cling beneath loose plates. Crustaceans mass in the pools like drifting grit, above layered ranks of colorless fish. Pale eels inhabit deeper seams, emerging only as knots of muscle and tooth.

The ceiling belongs to bats. They hang in ordered profusion, evenly spaced across leagues of limestone, as though each has been assigned its small portion of stone. Some are always sleeping, some feeding, some drinking, some nursing young, and some dying. Their airborne fraction remains constant. They circulate along inherited routes so exact that the traffic resembles machinery: dark bodies peeling from the ceiling, crossing the gray hollow, dipping to water, and returning to vacancies that seem prepared for them.

Predators live wherever those currents of life converge. Heavy-shouldered cave cats crouch among fractured blocks, pale enough to vanish against fresh limestone. Long-snouted reptiles lie beside herd paths and strike with patient economy. Packs of hairless, narrow-bodied hunters follow the margins, communicating through throat vibrations and taps of their feet. They kill often, but abundance does not diminish around them. A herd opens at the point of attack and closes behind it like water around a sinking stone.

Some inhabitants stand near the edge of deliberate thought. Long-fingered foragers choose sharp flakes to pry shellfish from pool margins. Small ape-like animals turn slabs with broken stalactites. Broad-jawed rodents probe larval cracks with splinters of bone. They recognize useful shapes, remember where prey hides, and perhaps teach by imitation. Yet nothing becomes craft. No tool is altered, stored, traded, or valued after use. It falls from the hand and returns at once to anonymity.

Fungi and mosses are the cavern’s quiet inhabitants, surviving in isolated patches wherever condensation remains dependable. They bind silt, soften ledges, and briefly raise blunt fruiting bodies before mouths crop them down. Invisible microbial films likely sustain far more life than the visible vegetation could explain. Whatever enters waste, rot, or death is dismantled immediately and returned to the cycle without surplus.

All these beings reproduce, feed, hunt, suffer, and die, but none of them leave a history. Populations do not spread or collapse. Territories do not drift. Paths do not deepen under feet. Guano never accumulates, pools never foul, and bones never form beds. For every birth there is an answering absence; for every carcass, an exact appetite. Even intelligence is prevented from becoming memory in matter.

Thus the cavern’s true inhabitant may be its equilibrium: an unseen law expressed through fur, shell, wing, tooth, fungus, and falling water. Every creature appears fiercely alive, yet each also functions as a moving part in a permanence larger than itself. The bats wheel, the grazers flow, the predators strike, and the scavengers gather—not as a civilization and not quite as a wilderness, but as an eternal first day that has learned to breathe.
