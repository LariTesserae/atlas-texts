The beings of the plateau are lithic, metallic, and patient. They do not inhabit the land in the ordinary sense. Together they are its circulation, its erosion, its weather, and perhaps the machinery by which it keeps its impossible shape.

Most belong to no category that cleanly separates animal from engine. They have no common flesh. Their tissues are ceramic matrices, braided conductors, porous alloys, pressure-grown crystal, and sacs of reactive salt. Some possess blood: dense eutectic fluids traveling through coppery vessels, molten only while the body is active. Others transmit charge without circulation, each limb and plate serving as a temporary pole in a moving electrochemical system. Their wounds do not bleed for long. They oxidize, vitrify, or seal beneath beads of self-made slag.

The tallest are the fault-striders.

They stand on narrow, many-jointed legs, their pale hides crazed like fired pottery. A mature strider may reach high enough that its upper body appears displaced by the curvature of the horizon. Yet it is lightly built, with most of its mass concentrated in its feet. Each foot is a branching organ of mineral needles driven into opposing strata. By spanning a fault, the animal closes a circuit through itself and feeds on the difference between buried chemistries.

Fault-striders spend years choosing where to place a single step. They test the ground with descending filaments, withdraw, pivot by fractions, and test again. Once established, they may remain motionless for decades while internal coils charge. Their apparent stillness conceals a slow manufacture: worn joints are dissolved, recast, and fitted into place; cracked leg segments are rotated inward and melted down; eggs grow in pelvic kilns as polished metal spheres.

When a strider finally lifts its foot, the release of pressure makes the rock beneath it fracture. Needle swarms descend at once.

These are among the smallest and quickest lives on the plateau: black, bright-bodied things shaped like splinters, with fins of conductive foil folded along their sides. They sense the brief electrical emissions of breaking crystals. A swarm can darken a new crack before the sound of its opening has finished traveling. Each needle inserts itself into the fracture and drinks the fading current through its pointed body. The feeding lasts seconds. Reproduction follows immediately. A fed needle divides along a seam and stamps out two imperfect versions of itself, one inheriting the parent’s forward parts and the other its rear.

Most such offspring fail. Those with workable asymmetries join the swarm. Thus the air around a fracture fills with tiny variations: mismatched fins, doubled hooks, useless gearlets, accidental resonators. Repeated forms survive more easily. The plateau recognizes them.

Lower to the ground move the shield caravans.

They are broad, many-limbed grazers whose overlapping backs resemble trains of armored roofs. From between their plates rise dense fields of conductive filaments. The filaments are neither fur nor antennae, but both functions emerge from them. They gather charge, detect vibrations passing through the stone, shed heat into the thin air, and entangle with neighboring animals during evening circuits.

Their mouths occupy the undersides of their bodies. They do not bite so much as mill. Stone enters through rows of ceramic rollers; useful salts adhere to charged membranes; the residue leaves as fused tiles. A caravan’s droppings can pave a migration route kilometers wide. Over centuries these pavements become smoother, and therefore easier to follow. Eventually the road shortens in ways that distance alone cannot explain.

The shield grazers trust old routes absolutely. Their nervous systems are distributed through the plates, and memory persists in replaceable parts rather than in any central organ. An individual may lose every original segment over the course of its life and continue along the same migration, carrying a recollection no surviving tissue experienced. Very old animals sometimes follow tracks that have not yet been made. They pause before future obstacles, lower their milling mouths, and begin wearing those obstacles away.

Above them are the floaters, enormous translucent bladders stained amber by suspended iron compounds. Their bodies hold gases produced in thermal organs, lifting them into the thin upper air. Long feeding veils trail beneath them, combing mineral dust from windless heights. At dusk they descend until the veils touch the plain.

Within each floater, dust passes through concentric chambers where grains are sorted, heated, and fused. Waste is exhaled as hollow glass beads. These fall in soft, ceaseless curtains, gathering into dunes that ring when crossed. The largest floaters contain internal weather: metallic clouds, electrostatic storms, and slow droplets of molten salt that never reach the bottom of the bladder before vaporizing again.

They communicate through pressure pulses. Their lowest calls are felt more readily than heard. A breeding congregation can make teeth ache hundreds of kilometers away. Sometimes a call arrives before the floater that produced it. The herds below respond anyway, turning filamented backs toward an empty part of the sky. Generations later, the caller drifts over that place and releases the note already remembered there.

Not every inhabitant moves.

In sheltered basins lie the hexagonal colonies: thin plates fitted edge to edge across the mineral floor. They possess no metabolism recognizable as hunger. Instead, the boundary of each plate imposes its lattice on adjacent stone. Compatible crystals realign and become part of the colony. Incompatible ones halt it.

A colony may spend ten thousand years advancing across the width of a footprint, then reach a band of resistant mineral and stop completely. It does not die. Its pattern remains under tension, awaiting an earthquake, a thermal crack, or the pressure of a passing herd. If the barrier breaks, growth resumes at once, as though no interval had passed.

Animals sometimes carry fragments of these colonies embedded in their feet. The fragments copy themselves into joints, shells, and eggs. Usually they are harmful, locking moving parts into sixfold symmetry. Occasionally they create a useful structure: a stronger bearing, a more regular tooth, a chamber capable of surviving greater heat. Whole lineages may begin as infections of geometry.

There are also hammerbacks, creatures with heavy reciprocating organs mounted along their spines. They feed by shattering oxidized crust and swallowing the exposed surfaces beneath. Their hammers began, the fossils suggest, as simple weighted nodules that struck the ground during walking. Now each is driven by a combustion sac and timed by an organic escapement. A traveling group produces the measured pounding of a distant foundry.

Wheel-bodied rollers follow them. So do magnet-jawed scavengers that pull shed teeth and broken bearings from the dust. Some scavengers insert these parts directly into themselves. Others carry them until matching components are found, then assemble offspring around the collected mechanism.

Largest of all is the migratory refinery, though calling it a single creature is misleading.

It is a society without minds and a body without a singular boundary. Thousands of specialized organisms lock together around a shared furnace tract several kilometers long. The front ranks are scrapers: wedge-headed castes that peel vanadium-rich layers from the plateau. Behind them come crushers with rotating stone-filled stomachs. Separators roast the powder. Smelter castes live close to the central heat and survive only while physically joined to the colony. Transporters pass useful metals backward from mouth to mouth, while slag-bearers crawl outward and die, becoming buttresses along the refinery’s temporary flanks.

The furnace itself may be the oldest living thing on the plateau. No single organism owns it. Generations are born into its walls, labor there, and are incorporated into its lining when they fail. It remembers through dimensions: the diameter of a duct, the rhythm of a valve, the ratio between gear teeth. Any caste that does not fit these inherited measurements is ground down and reused.

At the rear, reproductive molds stamp out bright juveniles. At first they are nearly identical—small six-legged creatures with polished shells and a single exposed gear train. Variations in tooth count and ratio determine what they become. Some return to the refinery and replace worn castes. Some detach and migrate alone. A few develop new arrangements, but novelty carries a physical burden here. The farther a form departs from established patterns, the less reliably space accommodates it.

Near the central escarpment, these lives become difficult to count.

A needle swarm climbing the knee-high ledge may emerge as towering tripods. Whether each needle enlarges, whether the swarm becomes one body, or whether both descriptions are locally true cannot be determined. Mountain grazers descend and vanish into cracks, continuing below as mites that feed on microscopic thermal gradients. One animal may occupy two valleys, its front feet in one and its hind feet in another that does not adjoin it. Some herds have audible members but no visible bodies. Others cast shadows centuries after extinction.

The creatures do not appear distressed by this. Their senses evolved within a geography where repetition matters more than continuity. They navigate by resonance, inherited route, and expected form. A place is near if it has been reached often. A body part belongs if it performs the rhythm long associated with the whole. Scale is not an intrinsic property but a relation negotiated at the escarpment.

Even identity yields to habit.

If enough shield grazers pass in the same formation, later generations are born with joints suited to that formation. If a call is repeated long enough, nearby organisms develop resonant chambers with which to hear it. If scavengers imitate the beat of hammerbacks, pistons gradually appear in their descendants—not as inventions, but as anatomical confirmations of a pattern the world has come to expect.

At evening, distinctions between species weaken further.

The shield caravans climb the ridges. Fault-striders plant their feet across conductive seams. Floaters lower their veils. Needle swarms pack themselves into cracks like metallic dust. The refinery opens its dorsal chimneys, exposing incandescent chambers to the darkening air.

Then charge begins to move.

Blue arcs leap between filaments, legs, veils, and glassy spines. Herds separated by impossible distances become adjacent for the duration of a discharge. The floaters pulse like dim lanterns. The hexagonal colonies answer through the ground with minute expansions. Even fossils may conduct: buried shells taking current along extinct pathways and passing it toward living feet.

For those few minutes, the plateau’s inhabitants cease to resemble independent animals. They become contacts in one distributed organism, each lineage an organ dispersed across the high plain. The refinery is its furnace. The floaters are its lungs. The fault-striders are rooted nerves. The caravans are blood that moves without liquid. The future tracks are memory, impressed not behind the body but ahead of it.

Whether this greater being is truly alive is no easier to decide than whether its components are truly machines.

When the charge fades, they separate again. The floaters rise. The herds loosen. The refinery advances the smallest perceptible distance. Glass cools over fresh wounds, replacement teeth harden in molds, and countless feet settle into prints that have waited centuries to receive them.

They continue through the hot darkness, maintaining the plateau by feeding on it and maintaining themselves by becoming more nearly what they have always been.
