Those who live in the Stillwarm are called, in their own speech, **the Finishers**.

The name does not mean that they bring things to an end. It means that they complete what they disturb.

They are human enough at first glance: two arms, two legs, blunt teeth, forward-facing eyes. Yet generations beneath the salt have pared them into the shape demanded by narrow places. They are slight, flat-chested, and seldom tall. Their shoulders can be folded inward one after the other, allowing them to pass sideways between mineral blades. The joints of their hands are unusually loose; a thumb may be laid nearly flat against the wrist when reaching into a seam. Their feet are long and prehensile, with widely spaced toes that test the ash before committing weight.

Their skin ranges from soot-brown to a pale mineral grey, not from lack of blood but from the waxes with which they coat themselves. Each morning—though “morning” is only the waking after the communal sleep—they rub grease into every exposed fold: eyelids, lips, nostrils, knuckles, the webs of the fingers. Left unprotected, their skin opens in fine, bloodless cracks. These are sealed with hot shell resin and stitched, when necessary, with inherited copper needles.

Their eyes are large but not adapted to perfect darkness. They see chiefly in the weak radius of their lamps and are suspicious of anything seen too far away. Distance, to them, is less trustworthy than touch. Children learn to map a room by laying their cheeks against its walls. Adults often walk with one hand trailing over stone and the other held before the face, fingers spread to feel vibrations and trapped sounds.

Their ears are more remarkable. The outer ear is broad, thin, and mobile, marked with deliberate scar-notches denoting household and birthplace. Finishers can distinguish a fresh sound from an old one not by fading—the Stillwarm does not reliably permit fading—but by its edges. New sounds retain, they say, “the shape of the mouth.” Old sounds have been touched by too many listeners and obstacles. They become polished.

For this reason they do not shout casually. A loud voice is a permanent object carelessly abandoned.

Ordinary conversation is conducted close to the listener’s body. Family members speak with foreheads touching, or mouth to cupped hand, so that the sound is caught by flesh rather than released among the blades. Lovers whisper into each other’s hair. Arguments are held beneath heavy hide blankets. A person who cries out in public must afterward search for the place where the cry lodged and either mark it, wall it away, or take responsibility for what it may cause.

Their oldest laws concern unfinished actions.

If you displace a stone, you must set it securely somewhere.

If you open a shell curtain, you must restore its pattern.

If you wake an animal, you must feed, kill, or settle it.

If you make a sound, you must know where it goes.

If you begin a death, you must remain until the body is still.

A Finishers’ word for negligence translates literally as **orphaning the consequence**. It is among the worst things one person can accuse another of doing.

They wear close-fitted garments of cured membrane and overlapping shell scales, tied rather than buckled so nothing clicks against the mineral forest. Loose ornaments are considered childish or threatening. Adults decorate themselves instead with grease pigments pressed into scars. A person’s chest and arms may carry a dense record of obligations: journeys completed, animals awakened, structures repaired, deaths attended, sounds deliberately left for others.

Children bear no such marks. They are called **the Unpressed**, because they have not yet acquired a permanent shape in the community.

The Unpressed are watched continuously. In a place where a tossed pebble, a struck note, or a frightened animal may remain as evidence for generations, childish impulsiveness is not merely inconvenient. It is geologic. Children are taught games of controlled action: moving through ash without touching old prints, carrying suspended grains one by one between bowls, or passing a lamp hand to hand without bending its flame. The most important lesson is stopping. At a signal, every child must freeze instantly and remain still until touched.

This is not only discipline. Many native predators respond to completed patterns of movement rather than to motion alone. A clasp-worm may ignore three travelers, five travelers, ten, then close upon the eleventh. A jointed hunter may wait until a repeated footfall varies. Children learn early that danger may be counting.

The Finishers themselves count constantly.

They count breaths while crossing unfamiliar ground. They count shell animals entering and leaving a pen. They count blows struck into a copper vein, steps heard in a trapped echo, and touches made against doors. Their numbers are not conceived as an endless sequence. Each count has a purpose and therefore a proper final quantity. Counting without declaring what is being counted is thought to attract things that are waiting for a number.

Some elders can sit beside a clasp-worm and determine, from old scratch marks and the arrangement of nearby remains, how many passages remain before it shuts. These specialists are called **hinge-listeners**. They carry bundles of dried mites and release them one by one through suspected clefts. The work is prestigious, precise, and frequently fatal.

Their dead are not buried.

Digging a grave would mean creating a void and assuming responsibility for everything the removed material displaced. Nor are corpses burned; flame requires relentless attendance, and the smoke lodges unpredictably among the fins. Instead the dead are posed in family alcoves, knees drawn up, hands open and empty. The body is washed with grease, packed with mineral powder, and bound until it dries.

Afterward, the bindings are removed and reused.

A dried ancestor may remain in the household for centuries. They are not treated as if alive, but neither are they considered absent. Tools are stored in their laps. Children are introduced to them. During disputes, participants sometimes sit among the ancestors because the presence of so many completed lives discourages careless speech.

The worst punishment is not execution but **unplacing**. The offender’s name is chipped from household objects, their ancestral alcove is denied, and after death their body is carried into an unmarked gap and left in whatever posture the bearers release it. To be unplaced is to become an unexplained feature of the world.

Their settlements are small because crowds generate too many consequences. Most contain between forty and two hundred people, divided into hearth groups that share lamp fat, grazing walls, and responsibility for particular paths. No one rules permanently. Authority passes according to the action required.

A wall repair is led by a shard-setter.

A hunt belongs to the person who first touched the quarry.

A birth is governed by the oldest living midwife.

A journey belongs to the path-reader until the travelers return.

When several kinds of authority conflict, the community holds a **placing**. Each claimant sets a small object on a sheet of stone and states what will happen if their plan is followed. The objects are shifted as consequences are named. Debate ends when no one can identify an unclaimed result.

Because nothing changes without intervention, the Finishers do not divide time by seasons. They measure it by replenishments and exhaustion: a lamp-cord burned through, a grazing wall stripped, a shell herd divided, a human pregnancy completed. Personal age is often given as a chain of witnessed events rather than a number.

An elder might say:

“I was carried when the eastern bridge was one span. I walked when it became three. I had borne two children when the copper door was first struck. I outlived the woman whose hammer sounded there.”

Memory is physical whenever possible. Contracts are not written; writing is rare and distrusted, since a symbol separates speech from its speaker. Instead agreements are recorded through arrangements of objects. A marriage may be represented by two chipped stones fitted into one another and kept in a sealed niche. A debt is a cord with knots belonging alternately to debtor and lender. A truce is marked by a single trapped tone created when both parties strike the same blade together.

Breaking such an agreement requires dismantling its physical record in the presence of witnesses. Secret betrayal is feared less as immorality than as a loose cause: an act sent into the world without a declared shape.

Their marriages are called **shared continuations**. Either partner may leave, but the leaving must be completed publicly. Possessions are separated, paths retold, children assigned sleeping places, common sounds identified, and every shell disk on the former household door rearranged. It may take many sleep-cycles to end a union properly. Walking away without doing so is believed to leave both people partly capable of acting through the other.

They eat mites ground into bitter paste, ribbons of pale flesh, shell-grazer organs, and cakes of first hunger scraped with flakes of glass and mixed with grease. Meals are intensely formal. Every substance must be changed by a person before it enters the mouth: cut, crushed, folded, warmed, named, or shared. Infants are not given raw maternal milk directly. The mother expresses it into a shell cup, touches the surface with a finger, and only then feeds the child. Thus even nourishment is made deliberate.

Hunger itself troubles their philosophers.

An animal of the upper world, according to stories, becomes hungry by itself. Its body issues a demand without decision. Finishers regard this as nearly a form of possession. Their own hunger comes slowly and is described not as an urge but as an increasing possibility of choosing food. Many can postpone eating until reminded by ritual. Those who fail to choose for too long may simply sit, lucid and calm, until they are too weak to move.

Every settlement therefore appoints a **rouser**, whose duty is to initiate necessities. The rouser announces meals, wakes sleepers by touch, orders lamps trimmed, and places tools into idle hands. It is an intimate and politically dangerous office. A corrupt rouser can exhaust a community with needless actions; a timid one can allow it to lapse gradually into stillness.

The oldest communities tell of settlements found intact but inert: lamps red, food laid out, inhabitants seated around it, no one dead and no one willing to begin the next motion. Such places are feared more than predator nests. Rescue parties enter tied together and singing a tightly contained work-song, touching each silent resident in turn.

Religion in the Stillwarm has no creator in the usual sense. Creation is too obviously unfinished.

Instead they speak of **the Prior Hand**, not necessarily a god, but whatever last acted upon the world before their own actions began. Some believe the mineral blades are the marks of that hand withdrawing its fingers from soft matter. Others say the Stillwarm was struck into existence all at once, and that everything in it—people included—is the lingering consequence of that blow.

A smaller, more troubling sect worships the **Next Hand**.

Its followers believe nothing in the Stillwarm is truly complete. All the waiting animals, suspended dust, trapped noises, impossible artifacts, and unmoving fires are parts of an action whose author has not yet arrived. The purpose of life, they claim, is not to preserve the buried land but to prepare it to be touched correctly.

They travel farther than others. They climb freestanding stairs. They sit in the circles of stone seats. They lay their ears against the copper door and insist that something on the other side is counting.

Most never return.

Those who do are subtly altered. They may pause midway through speech, head tilted toward a sound no one else can locate. They build small useless structures in sealed niches. They arrange dead mites in exact repeated poses. Sometimes their hands move during sleep, fingers pressing and releasing the air as though testing invisible surfaces.

The Finishers fear these pilgrims, but they also employ them as guides to the Unmade Distance.

For all their caution, the people of the Stillwarm are not joyless. Their art is exuberant precisely because it must be deliberate. They make mosaics from black, ivory, red, and translucent shell, fitting each piece so closely that the image can be read by touch. They carve instruments with strings that sound only while plucked and resonant chambers that swallow the note afterward. Their dances are performed in contact, entire groups linked hand to shoulder so no movement escapes the body of the dance.

Their humor favors completion. A joke is constructed like a trap: every detail placed visibly, the ending inevitable yet somehow unseen. Laughter is caught in padded masks or shared mouth to mouth. To make someone laugh aloud in an unprepared place is an affectionate offense, and courtship often begins with exactly such a risk.

Their most beautiful ceremonies take place when a new route is opened between settlements. Path-readers move first, choosing every foothold. Shard-setters secure loose fins. Hinge-listeners account for waiting predators. At intervals, musicians leave compact sounds: a fingertip chime for waterless rest, a low double hum for a safe turning, a chord struck into a broad mineral face to announce habitation ahead.

Last comes a child of each settlement.

The children meet at the center of the new path carrying unlit lamps. Each lights the other’s wick. Then both deliberately make one footprint in untouched ash beside the route—not upon it, but where it will remain visible.

These prints are never crossed.

They mean: **someone came from both directions, and neither world ended.**

Yet beneath every household custom and communal law lies a private terror.

The Finishers know that choice is not as pure as they pretend. The heart moves unattended. Blood travels without being pushed. A wound closes. A child grows in darkness. Thoughts appear before they are summoned. Even in the Stillwarm, something inside them continues of itself.

They do not speak often of this contradiction.

Some ascetics attempt to master every bodily act. They count heartbeats, schedule breaths, refuse involuntary sleep, and train themselves not to flinch from pain. Inevitably they fail. Their bodies go on acting.

Others find hope in it. They say life is the one region where the Stillwarm’s rule has never fully held: a small upper world carried inside the flesh, full of tides, weather, flowing water, and lights that cross their own hidden ceiling.

Perhaps this is why the inhabitants remain human, if human they are.

They live among things that wait forever for a hand, yet something within them is always reaching first.
