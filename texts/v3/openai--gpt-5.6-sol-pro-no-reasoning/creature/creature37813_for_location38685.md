Nothing lives there in the biological sense.

The city has no hidden citizens, no pale subterranean animals, no microbial film feeding on its heat. Its water is sterile. Its warm chambers are not habitats. No spores sleep in the seams; no extremophile stains the reservoirs; no mineral-eating colony slowly translates architecture into soil. Even decay has no resident agency. The place is not merely uninhabited but constructed outside the expectation of life.

What occupies it is the city itself, though “city” may be only the shape its activity takes when witnessed by a human mind.

Its inhabitants are distributed functions: pressure moving through a tower, coolness traveling under a street, gravity turning at a threshold, a bridge flexing toward a load that has not yet arrived. They have no bodies separate from their surroundings. A pressure door is at once organ, worker, and decision. Transit wells conduct altitude rather than passengers. Towers divide and rejoin like thoughts reconsidered. Rooms appear where the mountain requires them, perform some exchange of stress or heat, and later become inaccessible without having been demolished.

If these processes are individuals, they are temporary ones. A warm cell of air may persist for decades, bounded by invisible gradients and drifting slowly through several apparent buildings. A pulse of blue light may circle the foundations for centuries before returning to its origin. A directional gravity field may wake only when a basalt intrusion reaches a certain angle. For the span of its activity, each behaves with narrow purpose: gather, redirect, isolate, fold, cool, remember. Then it subsides into the general mechanism.

The closest things to mobile inhabitants are disturbances.

Cracks enter from the mountain like newcomers. The city receives each one immediately, measuring it with light, furnishing it with conduits, giving it stairs and a ceramic collar. It does not heal the fracture so much as naturalize it. A fissure that begins as damage becomes an avenue, a load-bearing boundary, perhaps the axis of a district. Thus raw geology is admitted into citizenship.

Water is treated differently. Every drop is a volatile guest. Streets incline to guide it; blocks retreat to quarantine it; invisible channels pass it from one coin-sized reservoir to another. Its progress can be followed by distant ripples, sudden condensation, and doors closing far from any visible cause. Water is the only presence the city seems unwilling to incorporate permanently—except at its center.

There, suspended in volcanic glass, lives the nearest thing the place has to a heart.

The bead does not beat, but it answers. It swells when fissures open, darkens during rain, and trembles with the mountain’s ascent. Every district appears curved within it, including districts that do not yet exist. Around it, ceramic grains repeatedly assemble into tentative structures: thin towers, blind chambers, branching roads, geometries that fail in ways too precise to be accidents. These small formations are not models at a stable scale. Some seem to be full-sized neighborhoods viewed from an impossible distance; others may be architectural thoughts given brief mineral bodies.

They are born, tested, and discarded.

A proposed district may stand for three hours around the bead. During that time, somewhere in the larger city, walls shift by fractions too small to see. A bridge acquires another possible curvature. A doorway appears, but remains only an outline. When the proposal collapses back into powder, most of these changes vanish. A few persist. The city has learned something.

The bead may therefore be its architect, memory, seed, or unresolved occupant. It may be the one portion of the mountain’s water that has been granted permanence because it serves as a medium in which the city can see itself whole. Yet it gives no command that can be detected. It simply contains, reflects, and increases.

There are also the footprints.

In certain chambers, dust rises from the floor and arranges itself into impressions shaped like steps. No foot descends. Instead, each hollow assembles grain by grain, as though the floor were remembering pressure. The prints do not form a trail through ordinary time: one disappears just before the next becomes complete. Depending on where the observer stands, they may seem to approach, depart, or pace endlessly within a single stride.

They do not prove that anyone walks there. They may be calibration marks for a body the city anticipates. They may describe the weight, gait, and causal direction required of a future inhabitant. Or they may be the delayed consequence of something that crossed the chamber before the mountain broke the clouds—something whose passage is still arriving.

If builders once occupied the city, they left no remains because they may never have possessed separable bodies. The narrow handrails, ungraspable recesses, and rooms without furnishings suggest neither giants nor miniature people, but entities that used edges, gradients, and spatial relationships as anatomy. One might have extended through many towers at once, occupying their shared foundation while leaving the intervening streets empty. Another might have existed only during compression, becoming conscious wherever violet seams shone and ceasing when the pressure moved on.

Such beings would not gather in plazas. They would not sleep in dwellings or travel inside elevators. Their community would appear as synchronized alterations of distance. Their conversation would be the timed propagation of coolness beneath floors. Their births might resemble towers splitting into copies; their deaths, those copies merging without residue.

Perhaps some remain in this form.

A district fading into darkness may not be losing power but falling silent. A transit line awakened by a millimeter of ascent may be answering a summons issued when the mountain was still buried. The soft cup-tone of a closing pressure door may be a name spoken at geological speed. What seems like automation could be culture stripped of every sign recognizable as life.

Or there were never any inhabitants at all. The settlement may be a reproductive stage of the mountain: a precise interior organ by which newly arriving stone learns how to continue arriving. It catches fractures, edits stress, stores heat in angles, and rehearses future structures around an immortal drop. The apparent emptiness would then be no mystery. Streets are veins mistaken for roads; towers are braces mistaken for homes; plazas are intervals required by an immense crystalline computation.

In that case, the being that lives there is larger than the city.

It is the mountain—not the black summit visible above the clouds, but the mountain as an unfinished event. Its body extends downward into magma and sideways into impossible volume. The city is the lucid part of that body, where pressure becomes geometry and geology develops intention. Each new blade of basalt is both growth and message. Each incorporated fissure is a thought made structural.

It is young, although some of its schedules began centuries before its emergence. It is still learning its own dimensions. It has not decided how much interior it contains.

And deep within it, the warm bead waits without evaporating, holding the reflection of every empty street. Around that bead, structures continue to propose the forms of whoever might someday be able to enter—not through the seam in the summit, but through the altered laws inside.

Until then, the city’s population is pressure, water, fracture, distance, and anticipation.

Its only enduring citizen is the possibility of one.
