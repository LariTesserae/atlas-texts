The city is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a whole young commonwealth of water, root, fur, scale, spore, and patient decay.

Its most numerous citizens are too small to distinguish at a glance. They live in the luminous moss, under flakes of lichen, inside wet bark, among the chambers of rotting wood. Mites work through abandoned husks. Beetles graze the colored crusts of stone. Springtails gather where fungi soften fallen leaves. Larvae inhabit every depth of water, from the clear skin beneath the surface to the black sediment below. Caddisflies carry little houses of grit and stem. Snails write slow, shining roads across drowned grasses. Spiders colonize the air itself, casting threads between reeds and traveling when the mist makes their invisible routes briefly visible.

The plants are settlers and builders. Mosses cover the rawest wounds first, holding water against exposed rock until soil can begin. Liverworts spread over shaded clay like green enamel. Sedges and reeds take possession of the shallows, knitting peat that trembles beneath heavier feet. Willows root from broken branches left by floods. Alders lean over channels and send their roots into the banks, making underwater vaults. Birches crowd the raised islands; spruces keep to the firmer ridges. Ferns, fungi, and seedlings dismantle every fallen trunk and build from it again. None of them keeps a boundary for long.

Fish live in the cold channels and root-dark pools, especially trout sheltered beneath alder tangles and undercut banks. Frogs gather near springs that remain open in winter, sleeping among submerged roots when the cold deepens and calling sparingly when dusk is mild. Salamanders wait in saturated cracks and come out over black stone when rain has made the whole basin hospitable to their skin. Water striders patrol the smallest puddles as if each were a private lake.

Voles inhabit the upper reeds, filling them with almost inaudible passageways. Mice and hares leave denser evidence in winter than in summer, when their roads disappear beneath growth. Deer move along the broad, braided tracks, placing their hooves carefully where peat floats and water waits just below the surface. Their prints become temporary wells. Foxes favor the dry shelves of stone, crossing streams on crowded rocks and fallen logs, then slipping beneath roots through openings polished by repeated use.

Otters occupy the joined waterways. They surface quietly among duckweed, travel beneath crossings used by animals overhead, and leave bubbles moving where their bodies cannot be seen. To them the city’s chief avenues are submerged.

Beavers are its most consequential inhabitants. They do not govern it, but their work repeatedly redraws it. Families dam channels, drown groves, deepen ponds, and divert water through places that were recently dry. When a dam fails or is abandoned, the pond withdraws and a meadow arrives. Reeds enter first, then shrubs, then trees; elsewhere, another woodland is filling with water. Generations of beavers inherit not a fixed territory but a sequence of possibilities. Their lodges are among the nearest things here to deliberate buildings, though mud, branch, current, frost, and sprouting willow all collaborate in their construction.

Birds occupy the vertical city. Small birds call singly from thickets, each voice isolated by mist and wet foliage. Grouse work through sheltered growth and leave blunt tracks around winter springs. Seed-eaters and fruit-eaters carry the basin outward and bring the outer country in, dropping the beginnings of future groves. Owls move over the reed districts at night, following the papery movements below. They seem almost bodiless until they settle on a pale birch and become visible.

Around and within all these animals live other populations: parasites, gut microbes, spores caught in fur, seeds hooked to feathers, algae on shells, fungi entering dead wood before it appears dead. A deer carries plants between districts. A fox scatters burrs along a stone road. A bird plants a thicket. An otter feeds the roots beside its channel. Bodies are dwellings as much as hollows, ponds, and groves are.

The city also belongs to things not easily called alive. Springs emerge from blue clay and broken slate, occupying the cuts in the basin walls. Frost raises stone shelves and fractures them. Mud banks slump into channels. Ice forms thinly around pool margins, cracks from within, and releases its boundaries again. Mist settles over bark and wings. Rain moves downward through tier after tier, passing from canopy to shrub, herb, moss, soil, and aquifer. Water is everywhere a road, a wall, a weather, and a resident.

No inhabitant knows the city entire. The trout knows the cold underside of a crossing that the fox understands as a path. A vole’s reed corridor is an owl’s hunting ground. The hollow beneath alder roots is winter shelter for a frog, shade for a fish, anchorage for a bank, and a trap for leaves becoming soil. Every place is several places at once.

What lives here, then, is not merely an assembly of species. It is the basin’s ceaseless settlement: newcomers arriving, old matter being consumed, water changing direction, roots taking hold before the next slide, predators tracing the movements of prey, and decay making room without ever making emptiness. The city’s true inhabitant is this crowded, quiet process. It breathes through countless bodies, keeps no permanent map, and rebuilds itself whenever anything falls.
