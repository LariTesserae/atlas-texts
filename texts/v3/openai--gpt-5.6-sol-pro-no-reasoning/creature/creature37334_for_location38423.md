The hollow belongs most completely to creatures that have never mistaken darkness for absence.

Its largest inhabitants are the six trees.

They stand far apart on the black floors, each attended by its own territories of moss and seepage, like old animals unwilling to touch. Their bark is not merely dark but absorbent, softening vibration rather than returning it. Rain striking their trunks makes almost no sound. Beneath the bark, however, their wood is arranged in resonant chambers: long vessels, blind knots, and taut inner membranes that answer the towers with minute contractions. When a deep note crosses the basin, the trees change shape. Their crowns open by fractions. Roots tighten around the stone. Sap advances through them in warm pulses.

They feed through the braided roots visible on the surface, but water and minerals are only part of what they take in. Their root sheets lie across the mountain’s seams and translate pressure into growth. A season of distant thunder may become a new branch. A tower’s lowest tone may thicken a year’s wood. The small impacts of footfalls, burrowing animals, rain, and dripping leaves are preserved in finer structures, too slight to be called rings. Each tree is therefore an archive of everything that has moved near it.

No two hold the same account.

In their crowns live the hollow’s most articulate animals: small, warm-bodied climbers with four grasping feet, fine gray fur, and naked throat sacs. Their faces appear unfinished to sighted observers. The eyes are sealed beneath smooth skin, leaving only faint hollows on either side of a short muzzle. Their ears are broad, layered, and mobile. They turn them independently, folding and cupping them to separate rain from wingbeats, water from footsteps, one another’s breathing from the slow internal creak of wood.

They are light enough to cross the outer twigs. Before each leap, one gives a brief throat pulse. The returning echoes reveal leaf surfaces, moths, rain threads, and the bodies of companions as momentary contours. They do not perceive the tree as a shape. They know it as a continually renewed answer.

Each of the six colonies has a different manner of calling, influenced by the resonances of its tree. In one crown, voices are low and hollow. In another they are clipped, almost insectile. A third colony has learned to strike seedpods before moving, creating a scatter of reference points through the branches. Across the open rock the colonies exchange long hoots, too deep to locate precisely. These pass from tree to tree through the night that never ends.

They descend only in extremity. The naked floors confuse their sense of scale: too few echoes, too little texture, no branches beneath their feet. When they must cross the ground, they do so in groups, holding one another by tails and wrists. The first taps the rock with a fallen twig. The last hums continuously. Their fear can be heard in the tightening pitch.

The low plants host a different world.

Moss beetles push through the wet growth like armored miners. Their wing cases are ridged, and they speak by drawing those ridges against the stems around them. Some clicks announce territory; others call mates; many seem to have no immediate purpose except to confirm that another beetle is near. During a tower tone, thousands click at once, briefly making the moss sound as though it is full of cooling metal.

Velvet moths sleep under leaves with their wings wrapped close. The wings carry dense hairs that read changes in air pressure. They do not fly toward flowers but along trails of odor released in measured stages. Pepper leads to bruised fruit, bruised fruit to wet clay, wet clay to the bloom itself. A moth can travel these fragrant paths even when rain has broken them into disconnected patches. It waits at the last recognizable scent until the next arrives.

Their pollination is not accidental. Certain flowers tighten around a visiting moth, holding it without injury until its wingbeats reach the proper rhythm. Only then do the stamens unfold. A flower may remain closed around one moth for several days, both of them drinking condensation while the plant waits for a tower to sound.

The marsh belongs to the tapping birds.

They are tall only in relation to its shallowness, with narrow bodies carried on many-jointed legs. Their feathers are oily and sparse, exposing warm skin beneath. The bill of each bird is long and slightly flexible. As it walks, it strikes the stone through the water: left, right, ahead; left, right, ahead. The returning vibrations disclose reeds, prey, mineral shelves, and the wake of other birds.

A courting pair makes a shared map. They stand several body lengths apart and alternate taps until each knows precisely where the other is. Then they approach in spirals, never touching, accelerating until their bills produce a single interlocked rhythm. If a tower sounds during this performance, both birds go still. After the tone passes, they resume from the beginning.

Their nests are not raised above the marsh. They are shallow cups of woven reed sunk beneath the water, sealed with mucus and air. Eggs develop in the trapped warmth below, rocked by concentric ripples. Hatchlings emerge already able to drum their bills, although for the first few days they mistake every hard surface for a parent.

Frogs live under the moss rather than upon it. Their bodies are flat and soft, with limbs that can fold into grooves along the ribs. They breathe through backs ridged with blood-rich folds. Most remain hidden until resonance passes through the basin. Then the moss lifts in hundreds of places and the frogs call into it, using the vegetation as a single communal throat. Their individual sounds are weak. Together they make the ground shudder.

The burrowing lizards are their principal hunters. They have blunt skulls, almost no necks, and rings of sensitive scales along their flanks. Heat guides them over short distances, vibration over long ones. They tunnel not through soil—there is hardly any—but between moss, root, and stone. Their passages close behind them as the plants unfold.

In the flooded cracks live pale fish no longer than fingers. Their bodies are threadlike, flexible enough to reverse direction without turning. They graze upon films lining the rock and find mates through electrical pulses. During dry passages they withdraw deeper into seams that seem too narrow to admit them. No one knows how far those seams extend. The same individual markings have sometimes been detected at opposite sides of the marsh within an hour, suggesting an internal waterway through the mountain—or that the fish do not occupy distance in quite the expected way.

The bats live highest, under smooth shelves in the enclosing wall. They hang in dense, warm knots, separating only when the air pressure changes before rain. Their wings are broad and unusually slack. Because falling is slow here, they need little lift. They release themselves from the shelves and drift down before taking flight, unfolding like pieces of wet cloth.

Their calls are quiet. The hollow already returns too much information: water, stems, moth wings, ceramic towers, the minute movements of leaves. Loud cries would blind them with echoes. They navigate by whispers and by the faint heat their prey leaves in disturbed air.

Among all these lives move smaller things: mites that graze the bark of fungi, translucent worms in the seepage threads, colonies of soft-bodied insects that inhabit the warm undersides of stones, and drifting organisms nearly indistinguishable from spores. Some spend their whole existence within a single drop suspended beneath a leaf. Because drops fall slowly, generations may pass before their world reaches the marsh.

Decay has its inhabitants as well, though they do not resemble ordinary scavengers. Thin, colorless membranes spread over dead bodies within hours. They carry no odor. Beneath them, flesh separates gently into clear fluid, mineral grains, and fibrous residue. Beetles drink the fluid. Roots take up the minerals. The residue lifts into the air during the next tower tone and settles as dust on leaves. Nothing contests these membranes, and no animal tries to eat them. They may be organisms. They may be another permission of the place.

The towers themselves are inhabited only in the broadest sense.

Their ceramic skins contain no cavities detectable by touch, heat, sound, or instruments. Yet living things gather differently around each one. One is favored by moths, which sleep in vertical rows along its channels. Frogs cluster at the foot of another. The third remains almost bare except for a black lace of roots.

When inactive, the towers are cooler than the air. Before sounding, they warm slowly, sometimes for months. Animals notice first. Beetles alter their clicks. Bats avoid the nearby wall. The tree climbers turn their faces toward the warming structure and give uncertain, questioning pulses.

Then the note begins.

The hollow’s population does not merely react to it. For those minutes, it becomes a single distributed creature. Towers provide the voice; stone, the skeleton; marsh, the trembling skin. Trees open their hidden chambers. Reeds bow in ordered bands. Seeds crack. Moths wake. Fish flash currents through submerged seams. Birds drum at the edges of the ripples, and frogs amplify what passes beneath them.

Even the empty floors participate. Their concavity gathers the tone and sends it outward again, altered by everything crossing them. This may be why animals avoid the open stone: it is not vacant ground but part of an enormous listening organ, kept clear because obstruction would change the message.

Near the upper wall, the three dry sleeping hollows shelter no permanent animal. Moss stops at their rims. Insects turn aside. The warm air rising from them carries neither food scent nor danger, yet living things treat them with the caution reserved for occupied nests.

Occasionally, one of the small climbers descends and sits on the seven steps. It touches the ceramic handrail with both hands and gives a pulse into it. The rail returns something too complicated to be an echo. The climber listens, answers, and listens again. No others approach while this occurs.

Whether it understands is impossible to say.

Perhaps the hollow was made as a refuge, and all its inhabitants are inheritors of a mercy intended for something else. Perhaps it was made as an instrument, and evolution has settled into its workings like moss into carved letters. Perhaps the animals, trees, and marsh are not incidental life within the machine, but components grown to replace those that failed.

Whatever the original design, the basin now keeps its own counsel. It prevents escape without seeming to imprison. It corrects abundance without displaying violence. It admits rain and migrating bodies, but not the world’s light. Its creatures live brief lives inside rules so old that instinct and law have become indistinguishable.

When the single drop releases from the unseen ceiling, everything beneath it perceives the fall.

Bats tilt in their sleep. Birds raise their bills. The climbers grip their branches. Frogs flatten under the moss. The fish turn in their cracks.

The drop takes several breaths to descend.

It strikes the center of the marsh.

And the whole hollow listens to what the mountain says back.
