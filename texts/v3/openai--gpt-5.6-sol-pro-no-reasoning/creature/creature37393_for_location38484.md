The city is inhabited less by a people than by a many-bodied citizenry: creatures whose customs are inseparable from the plants that house them, and plants that have learned something very like custom.

No single species governs it. Authority belongs to age, repetition, appetite, and resonance. The oldest trunks determine passage. Root systems redirect traffic. Flowering schedules convene assemblies more effectively than bells. Animals negotiate with this civic body through warmth, scent, pressure, grazing, sleep, and song. They do not construct the city so much as enter into agreements with its growth.

Most inhabitants are small enough to disappear into its surfaces.

Cicadas live densely beneath the roof leaves, their generations packed into resinous galleries. They are the city’s ceaseless machinery, filling the wood with metallic vibration. Some colonies have dwelt so long in particular towers that the plants grow chambers tuned to their pitch. When those cicadas fall silent, doors stick, water creepers lose their rhythm, and nearby rooms become difficult to reach.

Termites occupy healed wounds and deadwood inclusions. They consume only what the living structures have relinquished, hollowing elaborate galleries while leaving load-bearing fibers intact. Their pale processions are tolerated because they clear rot and carry fungal spores into sealed places. A tower infested beyond repair does not die outright; the termites reduce it patiently into a warm nursery of mulch, tunnels, and edible walls.

Nectar mice inhabit trumpet flowers. They are tiny, round-bodied animals with yellow fur permanently clotted by pollen and feet adapted to grip the ribbed interiors of blossoms. Whole family lines may belong to a single chord. At midday they scramble through sounding throats, brushing pollen from chamber to chamber. Their nests are woven from shed stamens, and their young learn the city first as vibration: food below, danger above, kin resonating through the wall.

Flat amphibians cling to damp bark with bellies spread wide. They drink mist directly through translucent skin. During dry periods they stack themselves in layers over moisture vents, the outer animals shading the inner ones. After rain they peel from the walls and descend to reservoirs, where their booming calls open small temporary passages between water pockets. A frog calling into one cistern may surface from another with a different mineral taste on its skin.

Pale fish inhabit the vascular canals. Blind or nearly so, they swim through translucent creepers, swollen bulbs, hollow roots, and chambers inside living walls. Their fins pulse visibly beneath green skin. Many bear branching sensory whiskers that read changes in sap pressure. They migrate whenever rain enters the city, and sometimes emerge unexpectedly into flower cups or reflective pools whose resemblance has joined them to the hidden plumbing.

Ribbon snakes drape between towers. At rest they are easily mistaken for vines, but true vines grow leaves and the snakes grow patient. They tighten when prey crosses beneath them, then release one end and swing soundlessly through the open avenues. Older individuals carry moss, mites, and small nesting animals along their backs. Some are so thoroughly incorporated into the canopy that no one can say whether they are predators wearing plants or plants that have developed hunger.

The most dangerous hunters are the little jade mimics. They have thin, jointed bodies and broad flanks capable of flattening into leaf shapes. Their skins reproduce veins, insect damage, droplets, even the pale blooms of fungus. By resembling a leaf closely enough, one becomes adjacent to other leaves of the same form. A hunter can vanish from a low hedge and strike from a roof across the basin. Yet the act is risky: if it holds a likeness too long, roots may accept it as foliage, fastening tendrils around its limbs and feeding it sunlight it cannot use.

Their principal prey are the many-legged grazers that move in herds along the root avenues. Low-slung and soft-mouthed, they crop fresh growth from the pavement without injuring the older plates. Their skins never settle. Patterns course over them continuously—stripes becoming eyes, eyes becoming scales, scales breaking into bark and shadow. This instability protects them from resemblance. When attacked, a herd makes itself impossible to classify and therefore impossible to keep in one place.

The scattered animals return gradually, often around mineral seeps and salt licks. Some return altered. A grazer may bear an extra pair of legs belonging to a version of itself that escaped by another route. Another may remember raising offspring it has never had. Herds accept such differences without ceremony. They identify one another by gait-rhythm and stomach scent, not shape or memory.

Fan-tailed sweepers maintain the plazas. They are social, sharp-faced animals whose tails unfold into stiff semicircles. At dawn they move abreast, pushing husks, dung, dead insects, and shed bark toward fungal pits. This is not cleanliness for its own sake. Debris introduces too many accidental resemblances: a spiral of peel may open into a shell; a heap of spotted wings may attract something spotted and hungry. The sweepers prevent the city from becoming dangerously adjacent to its own refuse.

They stack usable fruit husks beside entrances and sort fibers by softness in dry wall niches. Their arrangements resemble stores, but nothing is owned for long. A sleeper takes what fits its body and later returns something carrying its scent. Over generations these exchanges become neighborhood identities. One ward smells of crushed citrus, another of wet fur and bitter latex, another of warm dust, old pollen, and the musk of sleeping reptiles.

The larger animals live more solitary lives.

Long-necked browsers enter at irregular intervals from the dry flats. Their hides are gray-green and folded like bark. Standing in a plaza, one can feed from several balconies without moving its feet. The city reacts to their weight: doors contract, nests fall silent, root stairs flatten, and vulnerable water channels pull deeper into walls. Browsers carry seeds caught in the folds of their necks, including species found nowhere nearby. Some may have arrived through distant but similar canopies rather than across the surrounding land.

The great shelled sleepers beneath the arcades are older than many buildings. Moss, saplings, and nesting colonies cover their carapaces. Their breathing is so slow that roots sometimes grow over them and incorporate them into courtyards. Rain wakes them. They lift themselves with a grinding of shell against wood, tear free of the pavement, and walk toward places where thunder sounds deepest. When they leave, they expose warm hollows that become public sleeping chambers.

Above the canopy circle broad-winged scavengers, spare and dark against the hot sky. Each casts two shadows. One follows the bird faithfully; the other moves according to a flight not taken. Hunters on the ground watch the false shadow as carefully as the true one, for it sometimes arrives first. A scavenger may descend where its second shadow has found carrion in an adjacent possibility. It returns with blood on its beak from an animal not yet dead.

Soft-bodied gliders fill the air during the midday flowering. Their translucent skins stretch between long finger-bones, trembling to the trumpet chords. They do not steer by sight. They choose a note and allow resonance to pull them toward its source, even when that source is physically behind them or enclosed inside a trunk. In the flowering hour, whole flocks vanish into sound and emerge dusted with unfamiliar pollen.

Bats wake at noon despite the light. They roost in cool, inverted chambers where darkness pools unnaturally beneath leaves. The blossoms summon them by tones too low for most inhabitants to perceive. They fly with mouths open, mapping not solid shapes but likenesses. Two identical hollows are a single hollow to a bat. For this reason they can cross the city in one wingbeat, but they sometimes become trapped circling between repeated forms until weather or growth introduces a difference.

There are also rarer inhabitants that cannot be assigned confidently to animal or plant.

Some aerial roots withdraw when touched and later imitate the toucher’s movements. Certain flowers retain voices. After repeated visits, they call in the tones of the creatures that pollinated them, sometimes years after those creatures have died. Mosses migrate against the wind in slow green sheets. Seed pods stalk warm bodies on hair-fine tendrils, seeking suitable fur or skin in which to sleep.

The buildings themselves show preferences, aversions, and memories. A house may close against a scent associated with injury. A corridor may widen for a species that has used it for generations. Chambers pad their floors with shed fibers after observing where sleepers curl. Several structures share sensations through their roots and behave like separated organs of one dispersed creature. Whether this organism is the city or merely something beneath it is not a distinction its inhabitants make.

No resident appears to use tools in the strict sense. Their work is persuasion practiced across lifetimes.

To raise a wall, animals sleep repeatedly beside young stems, warming one side and scenting the other. Grazers trim competing shoots. Amphibians keep the roots damp. Cicadas supply a useful vibration. Descendants continue the pattern without needing to know its beginning. Generations later, the stems braid into a chamber shaped by accumulated bodies.

Bridges are formed through coordinated nesting in facing canopies. Birds and gliders carry pollen between them. Ribbon snakes lend temporary tension. The trees slowly bend toward a shared chemical dream. By the time their branches meet, the animals that began the work may be known only as a preference in the wood.

The city’s inhabitants therefore inherit habits rather than plans. They know which avenue rejects fresh blood, which tower opens only to rain-wet skin, and which spiral stair is liable to end inside an ear. They know never to sleep beside a perfect reflection, never to mimic a leaf completely, and never to answer a familiar voice calling from an unfamiliar flower.

They gather most solemnly at the ring of trunks in the central plaza.

No species enters willingly, but nearly all visit its edge. Tiny creatures drink from the condensation there. Grazers stand in restless herds, their patterns briefly stilled. Browsers lower their long heads. Amphibians call downward. Bats cling to the threshold and listen. Even the jade hunters abandon concealment near the opening, as if resemblance itself were unsafe beside so much depth.

Voices cast into the abyss return changed. A juvenile’s chirp may come back as the mating call it will use years later. A solitary animal may hear its cry returned by a multitude. Sometimes an answer arrives before the caller has made any sound, and the creature departs without calling at all.

The inhabitants do not worship the opening. They possess no clear priesthood, doctrine, or shared language in which such worship could be declared. But their routes bend around it. Their flowering, nesting, molting, and migration all acknowledge it. The oldest nests face inward. The most polished root paths end at its threshold.

Perhaps they come because the abyss resembles every open space: sky, throat, flower, eye, wound, doorway. If resemblance creates adjacency, then the hollow at the city’s center may be near to all hollows everywhere.

And so this crowded place retains its profound impression of emptiness. Its citizens hide in petals, sap, walls, canals, echoes, and each other’s likenesses. They occupy not merely surfaces and rooms but correspondences. At any moment most are elsewhere without having left.

The city itself is the only inhabitant always present—if it is one inhabitant, and if presence here can be said to mean one thing.
