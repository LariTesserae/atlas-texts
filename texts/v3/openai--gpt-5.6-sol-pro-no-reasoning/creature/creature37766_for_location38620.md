They are not inhabitants in the usual sense. They do not occupy the city as nests occupy ruins, nor have they adapted themselves to its machinery. They are distributed through it like components.

No single creature represents them. Taken apart, they seem unrelated: runners, scrapers, swimmers, flyers, breathers, drinkers, hunters. Some have chitin and some skin; some are articulated like instruments; some are scarcely more than membranes stretched around a pulse. Yet across these differences lies a strict family resemblance. Every animal fits a particular motion, and every motion fits a particular interval in the city’s continuous operation.

The translucent six-legged runners on the steam pipes are warm only along one side. Their bodies are thin as cast glass, with dark beads moving visibly through branching channels. They travel in files, each touching the preceding animal with a pair of threadlike forelimbs. At pipe junctions the files divide in exact proportions and later rejoin. They do not search for food. They pass over minute seams and imperfections, and their passage seems to keep heat from accumulating there. If one could be lifted away, perhaps a bearing somewhere would warm by a fraction; perhaps the creature itself would continue running in the air.

The pale grazers are thicker and softer. They adhere to iron by rings of muscular feet and carry their mouths beneath them: nested toothed wheels that turn without closing. Rust enters the central aperture as red scale and emerges along their flanks as a fine black residue. Their backs are printed with stains matching the plates beneath them. Some appear almost blank; others bear dark cartographies of rivets, weld lines, and old impacts. They have been grazing the same bounded patches for so long that animal and surface seem to remember one another. The oxidation does not diminish. The mouths do not fill.

Around the arc lamps hang the black flyers. Calling them winged is misleading. Their lateral vanes are rigid, glossy, and pierced with regular holes; the air moves through them rather than beneath them. Their bodies swing and rotate below these fixed planes, orienting toward tiny changes in current. A whole cloud may pivot simultaneously with the opening of a distant valve. Blue light flashes over their shells, revealing concentric marks like the graduations on gauges. They produce no lift that can be understood, but they remain suspended, each at its appointed radius. Their buzzing is electrical rather than muscular.

The cellar swimmers are almost transparent except for a small iron-colored organ near the head. Packed around turbines, they preserve equal distances with greater precision than machined teeth. Their tails quiver too rapidly to see. When a wheel turns, the mass turns around it in the opposite direction, maintaining a thick, living sleeve of water. They have no eyes because there is nothing new to notice. Their sensory organs are rows of pressure pits, each reading the bodies adjacent to it. No swimmer knows the shape of the school; together they enforce it.

The larger street animals are less easily mistaken for vermin, though they are no more sovereign.

Beneath gutters stand the broad-backed drinkers. Their hides are gray and pebbled, stretched over low frameworks of bone. Along each spine opens a row of soft circular mouths, pink only in their deepest folds. Drops strike these mouths one by one. The mouths contract, pass the water into branching chambers, and release it eventually through hard tubes at the feet. Each animal is therefore a small section of drainage system, receiving water above and returning it below. They have faces, but the faces are reduced and closed: sealed eyes, shallow nostrils, a jaw fused beneath overlapping plates. Their attention is upward, dispersed among the mouths that wait for drips.

They stand close enough to touch but never do. Between their hides remain narrow lanes through which condensation falls directly to the pavement. Young and old cannot be distinguished among them. Some are smaller, but not unfinished; some are scarred, but not injured. Every body has arrived at the precise condition it maintains.

The long jointed canal-crossers appear at first to be solitary animals. Their bodies are suspended high between many angular legs, and their heads extend forward in narrow wedges lined with delicate feelers. But each follows another at such an interval that the procession may be one organism divided into separate bodies. Their feet descend into ancient pavement hollows with absolute certainty. The hollows fit them. Whether the route made the depressions or the depressions dictate the route no longer matters.

At the canal they do not step into the water. Their legs lengthen as joints unfold, carrying the body from one bank to the other. A fringe of mouthparts lowers briefly to the surface and draws up an identical measure of iron-colored water. Several streets later, that water is released as vapor through slits in the abdomen. The vapor joins the factory haze and is caught again on pipes.

The rooftop sacs are more architectural than animal. They grow—if growth is the word—in ranks against parapets and around vents. Each is ribbed with pale hoops and fastened to the masonry by a black, puckered base. When the factories exhale, the sacs inflate. Their membranes become translucent, revealing dense floating organs that rearrange themselves like filings around a magnet. Then the sacs contract, forcing the gases through porous crests where metallic dust collects. Their synchronized breathing can be seen across the city: district after district swelling and subsiding around the slower rhythm of the central engine.

Occasionally a sac opens along a seam. Nothing emerges. The opening remains an opening forever, flexing with each breath but neither healing nor worsening.

The chimney animals are the heaviest flyers. Their bodies resemble furnaces wrapped in folded hide, with short heads tucked close to their chests and wings composed of overlapping vanes. Soot has entered every crease. They circle the chimney mouths in fixed loops, taking the updraft on one side and falling through colder air on the other. Their eyes are large, silver, and unblinking. Each orbit carries them past the same bricks, where their outer claws lightly strike the wall. These touches have worn shallow marks into the masonry, but the marks have reached their final depth. The claws strike. Dust appears. The grooves do not deepen.

They call as they circle. Each voice supplies one sustained metallic note, and the full colony forms a chord with no beginning. Close to the chimneys, one can feel separate throats laboring. From the plaza, it becomes part of the city’s pressure.

Among all these fixed populations are stranger, less visible orders.

Inside walls live flat animals that occupy the cavities between courses of brick. They tighten when presses descend and relax when the vibration passes, preventing cracked masonry from choosing a direction in which to collapse. In the insulation around boilers, white filamentous creatures lace themselves through asbestos and mineral wool, passing heat from strand to strand. Under rails, armored forms no larger than coins turn slowly in beds of slag whenever a wheel passes overhead. Their shells bear the weight for an instant and then return it to the ground.

There are animals whose sole station is a doorway, a stair tread, the underside of a valve handle. There are creatures that spend their endless lives braced against a loose plate, not holding it in place exactly, but sharing its tremor. A colony in the tram car opens and closes its mouths at frequencies that cancel part of the surrounding noise, producing one of the acoustic shadows in which speech can survive. Their mouths line the walls behind the white tiles. Seen through gaps in the ceramic, they resemble small wet ears.

Even vacancy has its fauna. At the edges of the empty yards, animals stand facing inward but do not cross. Some are narrow and upright, balanced on clusters of fine legs; others flatten themselves against curbs with sensory fans extended over the paving. They patrol no boundary and repel no trespasser. Their presence defines the absence they surround. If the plaza’s emptiness bears weight, these are perhaps its brackets.

None enters the central works.

The creatures nearest its doors gather in dense, orderly masses. Their bodies point toward the horizontal engine, but they show no sign of worship. They neither bow nor offer nor wait. The engine is not their ruler. It is more like the fact they all have in common.

With each piston stroke, minute confirmations pass through them. The gutter mouths contract. The pipe runners place their feet. The cellar schools turn. The flyers pivot beneath their rigid wings. The predator’s jaw remains closed around the prey. The prey’s legs remain braced. Across the city, countless acts are renewed—not repeated, because repetition implies that one act ends and another begins, but sustained across successive instants.

Predators live here, though hunger cannot be separated from eating.

The silver hunter in the alley has a smooth, muscular body plated at the shoulders and skull. Its jaws open sideways as well as vertically, enclosing the red animal from three directions. The swallowed portion of the prey is intact within it, visible as a dark shape moving faintly beneath the hunter’s throat. Digestive organs pulse around that shape but never reduce it. The red creature’s exposed legs push continuously against the slag paving. Its claws have cut channels in the surface. Neither effort advances.

This is not mercy, torture, or combat as living beings elsewhere understand them. The hunter is forever succeeding. The prey is forever resisting. Appetite and escape remain complete because neither reaches consequence.

Elsewhere, smaller cycles pass for ecologies. Needle-mouthed animals pierce the rooftop sacs and drink their filtered fluids; the sacs lose nothing. Parasites hang beneath the canal-crossers and feed from folds between their plates; their bellies remain distended but never full. Colonies consume one another in elaborate rings, every mouth enclosing a tail while its own tail disappears into another mouth. No energy is transferred that is not simultaneously returned. No body becomes another body.

Nor are there eggs, larvae, pregnancies, molts, corpses, or bones. What first appear to be nests are alignment structures: cups of wire, slag, ceramic fragments, and shed-looking membranes that were never shed. What look like juveniles are simply small species. What resemble old animals are species whose completed form includes clouded eyes, worn teeth, and sagging hide.

There may be intelligence among them, but it has no room to become history.

Some turn their heads when a stranger enters the basin. Some eyes follow movement not listed among the city’s accustomed motions. Feelers lift; wingbeats shift by fractions; the creatures at the boundaries of empty yards lean inward. Yet they do not approach. Their awareness is incorporated into their station. They perceive the stranger as the machinery might perceive an unexpected load: not with curiosity, but as a deviation to be tolerated, distributed, and brought within limits.

In the tiled acoustic shadows, one sometimes hears sounds beneath the larger noise. Soft clicks travel through the tram walls. The broad-backed drinkers pass low vibrations through the pavement. The chimney flyers’ chord contains modulations too slow for ordinary speech. These patterns may be messages, but they convey no news. Perhaps they continuously report position, pressure, temperature, distance, and strain. Perhaps the whole population speaks one endless sentence:

Here. Still here. This much. No more. Again.

Yet “again” is wrong.

They are alive in the way the machines operate and the rain falls. Their life is not a journey through changing states but the exact occupation of one state too complex to be still. Every creature performs the verb that constitutes it. The runners run. The grazers graze. The hunters close. The prey resists. The sacs breathe. The swimmers turn. Remove the action, and there may be no animal left beneath it.

Together they are the city’s metabolism, its bracing, filtration, measurement, drainage, cooling, and sound. But the city also seems to be theirs: an immense external skeleton built around their appointed gestures. Brick confines them, pipework feeds them, engines keep their intervals, and the central piston holds the total arrangement inside tolerance.

They cover the place without filling it because fullness would be change. They leave streets bare because vacancy bears part of the load. They do not multiply because abundance has already reached its correct value. They do not die because nothing could assume the newly empty position without becoming something more than itself.

From beyond the embankment they resemble smoke moving over dead factories.

Up close, there are no dead factories and no smoke. There are only innumerable bodies, each carrying one small portion of an equilibrium so ancient that decay has exhausted itself against it.

They are not trapped in the city.

They are what the city is doing.
