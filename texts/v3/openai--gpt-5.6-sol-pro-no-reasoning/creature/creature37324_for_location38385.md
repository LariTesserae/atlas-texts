The one plant is not alone, though every other life in the high country lives inside its terms.

Its most visible inhabitants are the **glasswings**, translucent grazers that travel in flocks above the silver scale-fields. At rest—on the rare occasions they rest—they resemble loose panes of smoked crystal laid against a rib or folded into a wind groove. In flight, their broad membranes are held taut by black carbon spars. They have little muscle and almost no stored water. The plateau’s updrafts carry their weight; electrical gradients guide them.

They feed by planing the microscopic films from old leaves: fungal analogues, condensed hydrocarbons, pollen grease, and the tender mineral skins the plant grows wherever grazing stimulates renewal. Their mouthparts are rows of vibrating ceramic teeth. A feeding flock makes a faint, high rasp, usually lost beneath the towers’ thunder.

Glasswings do not watch for danger with eyes alone. Lines of charged pits along their wings read the plateau’s electrical field. Because every animal in a flock receives nearly the same warning, thousands turn at once. For a heartbeat they become a sheet of white fire. Then they rotate edge-on and disappear into the violet air.

Some never land after their first ascent. They sleep by locking their wings and allowing themselves to circle within stable pressure cells. They mate in airborne swarms over warm engine districts, shedding eggs no larger than salt platelets. The eggs remain aloft until their charge decays. Those that settle on suitable young tissue adhere and hatch. Those that fall into a furnace vent are consumed as fuel.

Beneath them move the **ironmouth herds**.

These are low, six-legged browsers built for sudden changes in slope. Their bodies are broad and flattened, with overlapping pale armor and joints that can lock mechanically without effort. Each foot ends in a magnetic pad surrounded by hooked fibers. On ordinary ground they walk with a deliberate, rolling gait. When the plateau resonates, every animal drops at once, spreads its legs, and fastens itself so tightly that a moving pressure ridge can tilt it upright without dislodging it.

They graze only on designated tissues. Along ancient migration corridors the plant produces dark, knuckled growths rich in waxes and trace salts. The browsers crop these knobs, digest the softer compounds, and retain grains of iron, nickel, vanadium, and rarer catalysts in chambers around the gut. Farther on, prompted by changes in the plant’s chemistry, they excrete those metals into depleted regions.

Thus the plateau feeds them in order to move parts of itself.

Their mouths are perpetually red from iron-rich pollen. Old animals become red along the throat and forelimbs as well, their armor stained by decades of feeding. Herds follow pressure ridges in files extending beyond sight. They navigate less by landmarks than by the deep internal pulses of vascular flow. A route can vanish when an old vein closes, and a new migration may begin overnight when some distant organ starts pumping.

Calves are born soft and blue-green. For their first season they ride in depressions between an adult’s armor plates, fastening themselves beneath the overhanging rims whenever the wind rises. Their first magnetic pads develop only after they have consumed enough metal. Many are lost before then, swept into the sky or swallowed by seams that close behind them.

The plateau’s predators are called **hushes**, because they are known by absences.

A hush crouches inside a hollow spar or beneath a lifted scale, tuning the cavities of its body to the surrounding roar. It does not try to hear through the noise. It becomes part of the noise. The animal inflates branching resonators along its flanks until it matches the local chord so precisely that the nearest towers seem to speak through it.

Then it introduces an error.

To a glasswing, the altered note resembles a sheltered vent, a stable updraft, or the feeding hum of a distant flock. The grazers descend toward it. At close range the hush unfolds into a fan of narrow limbs and adhesive threads. The strike is almost invisible. The plateau’s music falters for an instant as the predator collapses its resonators, and one flier is gone.

Other hushes prey on the herds. These do not imitate towers but reproduce the subsonic reassurance carried by a stable pressure ridge. Browsers following that false signal may leave the safe migration line and enter a soft patch where the predator waits beneath a membrane. The oldest herd leaders test suspicious ground by hammering with their forefeet and listening through their locked skeletons. Their pauses move backward through the migration in waves.

Within the plant live the **vein-runners**, pale animals that may never encounter the sky. They inhabit the warm conduits where oils, salts, and carbon pellets move between organs. Their bodies are long, segmented, and blind, with ringed mouths that seal against the inner wall when a pressure surge passes. They swim not through liquid but through slow waxy suspensions, kicking from one branching junction to another.

Some graze on deposits that would otherwise obstruct circulation. Some pierce vessels and drink. Some hunt by detecting the pressure wakes of other vein-runners. They distinguish the plateau’s operations as surface animals distinguish weather: the closing of a valve is dusk, a hot transfer is summer, and the violent reversal of a major vascular trunk is a flood.

Many are useful enough to be tolerated. The plant walls off others in sacrificial chambers and floods them with crystallizing salts. Whole vein-runner ecologies can be entombed in this way. Centuries later, wind erosion may open such a chamber to the air, exposing thousands of delicate mineral casts curled inside the ivory tissue.

Where hot exhaust emerges from temporary chimneys, **jetlings** gather. These are small, hard-bodied animals shaped like seeds, each with adjustable fins and a ring of mouths around its underside. They ride the hammering gas columns, spinning rapidly as they sift edible spores, pollen fragments, and organic soot from the flow. When a chimney weakens, the colony abandons it in a glittering burst and searches for another.

Jetlings breed in the mouths of active vents. Their larvae cling to catalytic membranes, where they consume residues and are in turn culled by the plant’s chemical defenses. A vent with too many larvae begins to cough. Nearby tissues respond by changing the exhaust mixture, and surviving larvae emerge with different tolerances. Thus every engine district develops its own jetlings, fitted to its poisons.

During the great reproductive cycle, the animals vanish before any obvious change appears. Glasswing flocks climb into higher air. Ironmouth herds turn toward the margins. Hushes seal themselves in old stems. Jetlings abandon warm chimneys that still seem healthy. They have felt the diversion beginning in the plant’s deepest channels.

Not all escape.

The launch concussion kills flocks in the air and tears whole herds from the crown. For years afterward, fragments of armor and carbon wing-spars can be found fused into white scar tissue. Yet reproduction also creates abundance. Shattered leaf-towers leak edible compounds. Pressure fractures expose warm vascular chambers. Exhausted launch barrels become long sheltered valleys where temporary ecologies assemble before scavenger tissue digests the habitat around them.

Beneath the high country hang the largest inhabitants: the **under-sails**.

No one seeing only a portion of one would easily recognize it as an animal. A fold of black membrane may extend for kilometers below the living shelf, pleated around a branching frame. Its upper vanes hook into the plateau’s root-lattice without penetrating it. Its lower body hangs in darkness, opening and closing like a slow hand.

Under-sails station themselves where engine exhaust spills past the continent’s margins or escapes through apertures in the underside. The upward thrust supports them. They funnel entire jets through internal meshes, harvesting spores, drifting larvae, pollen platelets, and the fine organic debris shed by the crown. What passes through them emerges chemically altered and is often recaptured by the plant. Their relationship with the plateau is therefore neither simple parasitism nor mutualism. Each exploits the other’s waste.

They grow slowly. Their frames thicken one carbon-dark rib at a time, and the oldest carry forests of smaller organisms in the shelter of their folds. Blind vein-runners adapted to open gas channels crawl across them. Jetlings nest in their filter meshes. Pale ambush animals dangle on filaments beneath them, feeding on anything dislodged from above.

An under-sail communicates by pulling against its anchorage. Its movements send tremors through the crown: long pulses separated by silences, too slow to be heard as sound. Other under-sails answer along the rim. The question-and-response may travel for days around the continent’s underside.

The plateau reacts as well.

At times it opens more exhaust apertures beneath a calling animal. At others it closes them and forces the under-sail to detach or starve. Whether these exchanges are communication, reflex, manipulation, or merely the mechanical conversation of two metabolisms cannot be cleanly separated. In the high country, behavior and physiology often occupy the same machinery.

There are smaller lives almost everywhere: salt mites moving in the seams of white armor; threadlike scavengers following fresh scars; charged spores that behave like both seeds and animals; colonial films that migrate across leaves by reversing their electric polarity. Many spend their entire existence on a single scale. To them, the tilting of that scale is tectonic disaster, its nightly fluorescence a season, and the passage of an ironmouth’s shadow an eclipse.

None can live independently of the one plant for long. Even the airborne species feed on its exhalations. Even the predators hunt by its music. Their migrations trace its circulation, their breeding follows its furnace cycles, and their bodies are made from the substances it refines.

They do not inhabit a forest, because there are no separate trees.

They inhabit a body so large that its anatomy has horizons, its reflexes arrive as weather, and its reproductive convulsions are mistaken for epochs.
