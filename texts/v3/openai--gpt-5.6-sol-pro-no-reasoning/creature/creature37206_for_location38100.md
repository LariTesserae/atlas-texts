The inhabitants of this plain are creatures of inches, intervals, and recurrence. They do not merely live beside the water; they inhabit its changing measurements.

Long-legged grazers move loosely across the flood sheets, never forming a dense herd where the footing is uncertain. Their bodies are spare and elevated, with narrow hooves or widely spreading toes that test the mud before committing weight. They know the difference between a pale film over stone and the same brightness over bottomless clay. With lowered heads they crop waxy grasses, pull submerged shoots from shallow basins, and browse the sparse leaves of low trees. When the river expands, they retreat without haste to blunt rock rises scarcely higher than their knees. Generations have rested on these same dry backs, though no trail remains between them.

In deeper channels live broad, dark animals seen mostly in portions: nostrils, eyes, the curve of a wet back, a brief disturbance among floating seeds. They spend the heavy daylight submerged and emerge after dusk to graze the soft margins. Their routes pass between channels and persistent pools, following submerged shelves invisible from shore. Mud coats their hides, dries in plates, and is renewed the following night.

Reptiles occupy the boundary between warm stone and still water. Heavy-bodied hunters lie along low shelves, colored like algae, mud, and mineral stain. Their immobility is so complete that birds sometimes approach within a few steps before recognizing them. Smaller reptiles shelter in rock fissures, in hollow drowned wood, and beneath mats of herbs. They emerge to hunt among insect clouds or to flatten themselves against stone holding the afternoon’s heat.

After rain, the plain belongs most visibly—and most loudly—to amphibians. Small frogs and toads rise from cracks, buried chambers, and damp seams that appeared lifeless hours before. Some are clay-gray, others green as sedge blades or mottled like mineral crust. Males call from every suitable cup of water, turning separate pools into a single acoustic territory. Eggs cloud the new shallows. Tadpoles darken them, feeding rapidly before evaporation, fish, and birds narrow their time. Where pools endure, they grow slowly; where water is brief, development races the falling edge.

Fish move with the flood into places that were recently bare stone. Small, quick species follow water only a hand deep, taking larvae and newly submerged seeds. Larger fish remain in the old channels and spring-fed basins, waiting for seasonal reconnection. As the plain dries, they gather in shrinking pools, where the water becomes warm, turbid, and crowded. Some survive in the deepest refuges; others become food for birds, reptiles, and scavengers, returning their substance to the black mud.

Birds are everywhere the water is shallow enough to stand in. Fine-billed waders stitch through mudflats after worms and crustaceans. Broad-billed birds sweep and sift, leaving parallel furrows in soft sediment. Tall, lean hunters hold themselves rigid beside isolated pools, then strike with abrupt precision. White flocks gather on distant bars so densely that they resemble mineral deposits until they rise. Smaller birds nest among sedges, on bare rock beyond the flood line, or in the flattened crowns of the scattered trees. Their colonies shift with the water, but favored ledges and islands are used year after year.

Insects occupy the air in sharply bounded multitudes. Midges hover above particular pools; mosquitoes rise from sedge rings; dragonflies patrol the brighter water and return repeatedly to the same exposed stem. Beetles skate the surface or burrow into wet margins. Winged termites and ants emerge after storms, briefly filling the warm air and feeding nearly everything that can catch them. Beneath the water, larvae graze films from stone, shred drowned leaves, and hunt one another among stems. Their abundance is immense but patchy: a step may carry one from humming density into open silence.

The mud itself is inhabited. Minute crustaceans, worms, mollusks, and dormant eggs occupy its upper layers. Some seal themselves against drought; some survive as resistant embryos among grains of clay. When a basin fills, they seem to appear at once—not immigrants, but the plain’s stored life becoming active. Algae and bacterial films spread over submerged rock, tinting it green, rust-red, or nearly black. These thin coatings soften the stone grain by grain and feed the smallest grazers. Life begins here as a sheen.

The scattered trees support another, quieter population. In their roots live burrowing animals seeking firmer ground above saturation. Lizards use the warm exposed wood; frogs crowd into damp cavities; birds nest in low forks. Insects tunnel through dead limbs, while fungi enter waterlogged trunks and reduce them slowly from within. The trees themselves are broad-rooted and conservative, keeping modest crowns because neither soil nor season permits extravagance. Their roots remember old channels better than the visible surface does.

Scavengers arrive wherever heat and shallow water concentrate death. Birds pick stranded fish from contracting pools. Small carnivores patrol the margins at night, reading tracks that will be gone by morning. In the deeper channels, soft-shelled feeders work over drowned remains. Bones left on stone bleach and crust over; bones pressed into mud are dismantled by microbes, roots, and mineral water.

No inhabitant can depend on a permanent boundary. Aquatic life enters grassland during flood. Grazers walk where fish fed days before. Birds nest on ground that will later lie beneath a silver sheet. Frogs wait inside apparently dry stone, and crustaceans persist inside hardened clay. Even the largest animals survive through memory: the location of a firm crossing, a deep refuge, a stone rise that remains exposed when all lesser differences disappear.

What lives here is therefore not one community but a succession folded into the same shallow space. At high water there are fish over grass, reptiles among drowned ledges, and herds compressed onto stone. As water falls, amphibians and insects multiply in isolated basins. Then birds gather, grazers spread out, seeds germinate, and burrowing creatures retreat into the damp below. At the driest point, the plain seems sparsely occupied, but much of its population has merely become hidden, dormant, buried, or concentrated in the old water.

Its creatures share the land’s patience. They wait beneath mud, stand motionless beside pools, sleep through heat, follow floods without urgency, and return to places whose significance is measured by a few finger-widths of height. Together they make the worn surface breathe: dark backs rising, white wings lifting, frogs calling from stone cups, insects suspended over silver water, and distant herds crossing where no road can remain.
