They are a people of knots, measures, and cupped hands.

Their bodies tend toward the compact and corded, shaped less by hunger than by a world without level distances. Even the elderly retain strong fingers, thickened soles, and shoulders built by ladders. They crouch easily and stand carefully. Toddlers learn three commands before most other words: hold, tie, and wait. A child’s first confident crossing of an untethered roof is celebrated more solemnly than a birthday.

They move differently according to where they belong. Those of the upper town walk with short, economical steps, slipping sideways through passages without turning their shoulders. Cliff workers have a swaying gait even on firm stone, as though still counterbalancing a rope. They test every hold before trusting it. Their toes spread wide; their palms are crossed by old fiber scars. Jar carriers develop a slight forward bow from ascending beneath loads, while capstan crews become broad through the back and chest. Cave gardeners often smell of wet mineral, a rare and enviable scent.

Clothing is layered, patched, and close-fitted. Loose hems are dangerous, broad sleeves catch on hooks, and anything untied may be taken by the wind. Tunics are woven from goat hair, vine fiber, feathers, and salvaged threads, with reinforced knees and shoulders. Waist cords carry stoppered cups, bone tools, tally pieces, and coils of fine line. Every object has a loop. Every loop has a practiced place on the body.

Color survives in small, guarded forms: iron red, ash white, soot black, lichen gray, the bruised purple of certain figs. Brilliant dyes are reserved for edges, knots, and ownership marks. Cliff families wear patterns legible at a distance so that observers above can identify a body on the rock face. A yellow shoulder stripe may mean salt scraper; three black bars, corpse bearer; a white hood, keeper of the lower cistern caves. Widows, orphans, and those responsible for communal water wear blue-gray cords stained with powdered copper salts.

Hair is braided tightly or cut short. Cliff workers bind it beneath fitted caps. Beards are forked and tied. The wealthy are not those who display abundance but those whose garments contain old material: a strip from an ancestral climbing sash, a bronze bead, cloth known to have been rewoven for twelve generations. To wear something new and fragile without purpose would suggest either foolishness or theft.

No one owns water in the ordinary sense. Families possess vessels, condensation screens, carrying rights, and shares in particular caves, but the liquid itself enters a web of obligations as soon as it reaches the summit. Clay tallies record who hauled it, who lost sweat fetching it, whose goats supplied leather for the seal, whose rope lowered the jars, and which households are owed portions. Water keeping is therefore less a trade than a form of law.

The measurers are chosen for steadiness, poor imagination, and resistance to pleading. They work with nested cups whose volumes are checked against ancient stone hollows in the central pillar. They account for evaporation, broken seals, sickness, births, lactation, and the needs of animals and plants. They are watched while they work. So are the watchers. No calculation of water is ever made alone.

Drinking is intimate because need is intimate. People turn slightly away from one another. They sip beneath a cloth to catch moisture from the lips. Children are taught not to stare at another person’s portion. To ask how much someone received is indecent; to notice that they received too little is a duty.

Generosity takes forms suited to scarcity. A neighbor may shade another’s wall, lend labor to mend a cistern plug, give a nursing mother salt, or allow an exhausted climber to sleep near a condensation pit where the air is coolest. Lovers exchange mouth-cooling leaves, fresh cord, or polished stoppers fitted so perfectly that no damp can escape. A marriage is proposed not with jewelry but with a joined vessel: two inherited jars repaired into one household set.

Their households are dense and permeable. A room may belong to one lineage in winter and another in summer, depending on heat, births, infirmity, and which vines are fruiting. Children sleep wherever there is cool stone and responsible company. Parentage matters, but maintenance matters more. The person who taught a child to knot a safety line, the aunt who yielded part of her ration during fever, and the old neighbor who remembers the history of a wall may all possess recognized claims of kinship.

Names accumulate in the same way buildings do. An infant receives a household name, then earns others through work, mishap, or service. A person might be called Nara at home, Third-Rope at the capstan, Fig-Wall in the market, and She-Who-Heard-the-Western-Cleft after detecting a dangerous change in its whistle. Formal recitations of identity can be very long. They include teachers, rope partners, inherited obligations, and the niches where one’s dead are kept.

They distrust solitary labor. On the cliff, another pair of eyes is life. In the town, witnesses prevent disputes over scarce material. Even craft is social: one person chips glass while another catches the flakes; one scrapes condensation cloth while another holds the cup; one opens an ancestral niche while two count the bones. Their language has many distinctions between being alone by accident, alone by duty, alone because others trust you, and alone because others do not.

Privacy still exists, but it is made rather than found. A lowered mat, a particular knot on a doorway cord, a lamp placed behind a red membrane—these ask others not to enter. Conversation can become private by dropping beneath the surrounding noise. People speak closely, forehead to forehead, while mills rasp and goats stamp overhead.

Their voices are generally low. Shouting is reserved for warning, hauling cadence, market work, and ritual. Cliff speech uses clipped syllables designed to survive wind. A call identifies first the danger, then the direction, then the person addressed. Songs do the opposite: they lengthen vowels until crews can synchronize beyond sight of one another. Many songs are work records disguised as melody, naming replaced beams, repaired ropes, cave routes, debts, and deaths.

They have no separate class of priests. Sacred duties belong to specialists whose practical responsibilities have deepened into ritual ones. Water measurers bless cups because they measure them. Stone listeners press ears to walls and interpret cracks because they maintain foundations. Bone keepers recite ancestry because they sort and house the dead. Wind readers tend the sounding holes, clearing insects and grit without altering their pitch.

The oldest prohibitions are concise:

Tie what may fall.  
Shade what may dry.  
Witness what is divided.  
Return what can be used.  
Name the dead before cutting bone.  
Never seal a crack you have not listened to.  
Never drink in anger.

Anger is regarded as a kind of inner spillage. So are boastfulness, uncontrolled grief, and promises made too quickly. This does not make the people placid. Quarrels are frequent in close quarters, and grievances can outlive those who began them. But disputes are conducted through claims of balance: who carried, who mended, who consumed, who exposed others to risk. Fines are paid in labor, shade rights, compost, rope fiber, breeding access to animals, or future portions. Exile is nearly equivalent to execution and therefore rare. More often an offender is assigned to exposed or humiliating work under supervision.

The gravest crime is deliberate waste. Pouring out water, throwing a useful object over the rim, allowing fire to consume stored fiber, or hiding structural damage can strip a person of all unsupervised rights. Such an offender may be made “open-handed”: forbidden to carry a closed basket, seal a jar, keep a private store, or work beyond another’s sight until trust is restored.

Yet accidental loss is treated with unexpected tenderness. Everyone has dropped something. Everyone has watched a tool tumble soundlessly into haze. When this happens, witnesses touch the loser’s shoulders before discussing blame. If a retrieval bird returns with the object, its reward is generous. If not, a small mark is cut near the rim—not as a memorial to the object, but as acknowledgment that the town is diminished.

Their crafts favor repair over manufacture. Objects are understood as temporary arrangements of older substance. A bone awl may once have been a hinge pin, before that a cage brace, before that part of a grandmother’s leg. Such transformations do not trouble them. Matter is not dishonored by use. It is dishonored by careless ending.

Craftspeople are known less by what they produce than by how many further lives they leave in a thing. A good jar maker shapes walls thick enough to survive lowering but thin enough to yield useful shards when broken. A good weaver plans a garment’s future as bindings, filters, wick, and plaster fiber. A good butcher leaves bones unscored where tools may later be cut. Beauty lies in foresight.

The plants are members of this economy but not merely resources. Every mature tree has custodians, genealogies, and structural obligations. Some roots stabilize walls; others threaten them. Vines are trained across family boundaries and produce complicated fruit rights. A household may own a trunk while another owns the branch shading its roof and a third claims every fifth fig because its ancestor carried the compost that fed the planting shaft.

Children learn plants by touch in darkness. They identify leaves by wax, edge, temperature, and smell. They know which succulents may be punctured in famine, which die if tapped twice, which vines indicate hidden coolness, and which mosses should never be scraped from a wall because their return takes decades. Plant keepers speak of greenery as slow livestock: it must be fed, restrained, bred, divided, and sometimes slaughtered.

Goats are less respected and more loved. They are thieves, hazards, milk-givers, dung-makers, and warm companions. Their ownership is marked in ear cuts and horn bindings. Dogs recognize household routes through the town’s vertical maze and sometimes guide people through smoke or dust-darkness. Corvid handlers build relationships through patient exchange; the best birds distinguish between a fallen object and mere glitter, and some become attached to particular families for generations of breeding.

Pigeons belong partly to everyone and partly to no one. Their eggs are counted, their droppings collected, their bodies eaten, but their movement through walls and roofs makes strict ownership impractical. A flock nesting above one household may feed in another’s court and be netted by a third. Pigeon disputes have produced some of the town’s oldest laws.

The bats are never eaten. This is explained in several ways: they are the central stone’s breath, they carry night away on their wings, they remember passages inside the mountain. More practically, they consume insects and inhabit fissures too delicate to disturb. At dusk, conversations pause as they emerge. People count the first few spirals, looking for changes in number or flight. A poor rising may foretell insects, disease, or movement deep in the rock.

Knowledge passes by apprenticeship and recitation. Clay tablets exist, but clay is heavy, brittle, and costly to fire. Important records are distributed across materials. Knotted cords record ration shares. Notches on beams record replacements. Wall marks track lean and fracture. Songs preserve hauling histories. Bone slips list births and niche placements. No single archive contains the town’s memory, because no single fire, collapse, or dishonest keeper must be able to destroy it.

Those who remember across systems are called joiners. They know that a knot in one family’s water cord corresponds to a mark beneath the eastern capstan and a verse in a song about three sisters. Joiners settle inheritance and reconstruct records after damage. They are valued, watched, and rarely popular.

Time is measured by bells, shadows, plants, winds, and work cycles. Years are not remembered primarily by rulers but by events of maintenance: the Year the North Beam Split, the Second Resealing of the Bitter Cave, the Season of Black Fig Rot, the Three Months Without the Evening Whistle. Personal age may be given as “born two vine cuttings before the western wall was faced again.”

They do have leaders, though no one person rules the whole summit easily. The town is governed by councils of functions: water, rope, wall, plant, animal, fire, dead. A speaker from each meets in the central court. Authority shifts with crisis. During a drought the water council dominates; during tremors, the wall listeners; during disease, the keepers of dung, insects, smoke, and corpses. The system is slow, contentious, and resistant to grandeur.

Status reveals itself through obligation. The most important households are expected to surrender the coolest rooms during heat, fund replacement rope, maintain public shade, and feed capstan crews. Hoarding prestige without accepting burden invites contempt. A powerful person is described as “bearing many edges,” meaning many others depend upon them and many failures can be traced to their door.

Visitors arrive from below only rarely, exhausted and terrified by the final ascent. The townspeople seem brusque to them. Hospitality begins with inspection: loose objects are tied, footwear checked, drinking customs explained, hair bound back. Only then is a guest welcomed. Outsiders are often alarmed by the closeness of the dead, the bone fixtures, the communal accounting, and the absence of open sky beneath the canopy.

The summit people, in turn, find lowlanders wasteful and strangely careless. They tell incredulous stories of places where people wash whole garments in flowing water, discard cracked vessels, let fruit rot under trees, or walk for hours without touching a wall. Some doubt these accounts. Others dream obsessively of them.

Very few choose to leave. The lower waste offers wider ground but little safety, and descent does not cure a person of height. Those who emigrate are said to suffer from “missing pressure”: sleeplessness without wind in the walls, anxiety in open spaces, a need to sit near cliffs or crowd their homes with hanging cloth. Returnees complain that the ground below feels temporary because there is too much of it.

Death is handled without concealment, though grief remains private in its deepest moments. The corpse is washed with oil or rubbed with aromatic ash—never with water—and wrapped in cloth already assigned for funerary use. Corpse bearers carry it down at dawn, when the eastern shelves are cool and vultures are beginning to rise.

Families do not watch the cleaning. They listen for the birds.

Months later, the bones return in a lidded basket. Bone keepers lay them out, name each part, and decide what remains whole. Skulls, pelvises, and certain long bones generally enter the wall niches. Smaller bones may be retained with them, but in crowded generations some are released for communal use after formal permission. There is no contradiction between reverence and reuse. An ancestor may guard the town as a bone pin in a gate strap just as truly as from a niche.

Before ancestral bone is worked, the dead person’s names are spoken, along with the intended use. The craftsperson asks, “Will this hold?” Those present answer, “It held before.”

Children grow up knowing exactly where many ancestors reside. They tuck flowers, beetle shells, colored threads, and tiny food scraps into wall niches. In moments of fear, people lean their backs against these chambers. The dead are not imagined as hovering elsewhere. They are behind the shoulder, beneath the hand, inside the boundary.

Their religion, if it can be separated from maintenance, concerns continuance rather than salvation. The central pillar is not a god in human form. It is the portion of the mountain never quarried, the proof that everything built remains attached to something not built. People touch it before dangerous descents, major ration decisions, marriages, and dismantlings. Its polished surface carries the grease and salt of innumerable palms.

They believe wrong action loosens things. Lies loosen agreements; neglect loosens walls; greed loosens the town from itself. Right action binds—not permanently, because nothing is permanent, but well enough for those who come next.

Their highest praise is not brave, wealthy, holy, or wise.

It is: **They left good holds.**

At the evening bell, they emerge from the folds of the settlement carrying their vessels. Cliff workers come pale with dust, upper-town children with green stains on their fingers, measurers with tablets, old people led by those whose waist cords they once tied. The goats nose toward their troughs. Dogs wake. Pigeons settle into cavities as bats begin to pour from the central stone.

Each household receives its portion. Some drink at once. Some wet cloths for the sick. Some feed a child, soften grain, seal a plant cutting, or set aside a mouthful for someone still climbing below.

Then every cup is inverted and tapped once.

Not because it is empty.

Because nothing must remain where it cannot be counted.
