The people of the plateau are slender, many-jointed beings whose bodies resemble bundles of dark reeds bound around a translucent core. Standing, they are somewhat taller than the doorways they grow, though height is an uncertain measure among them. At rest they fold downward into low radial shapes, anchoring themselves against the wind. A distant traveler might mistake a resting household for a patch of glassy scrub.

They have no blood and almost no circulating liquid. Within their limbs, ammoniac resin advances through capillaries in minute pulses, driven by pressure bladders along the torso. Much of their metabolism occurs in laminated crystal organs. These are not solid in the inert sense: vacancies migrate through their lattices, carrying charge, information, and the chemical equivalent of breath. The oldest citizens move slowly because their crystals have become elaborate, not because they are failing.

Their outer tissues are layered and replaceable. The exposed surface is usually matte gray, pale violet, or the dull black of the root-sails, depending on profession, district, and season. Before a halogen storm they grow a clear mineral rind over themselves. When clean air returns, this shell splits along deliberate seams. Families collect the castings and inscribe them with memories before grinding them into feedstock for walls, tools, or new bodies.

A citizen’s head is not distinct from the torso. Sensory fans lie folded in grooves around the upper body and open when needed: thin sheets that taste gases, map magnetic fields, detect heat, and read minute variations in the shelfwood’s electric pathways. Vision is distributed among rows of pigment pits. They see dim light well, but color poorly. To them, the plateau is bright with things invisible to human eyes—charge flowing beneath streets, stresses gathering in arches, magnetic wakes trailing behind animals, the slow metabolic weather of the ground.

They communicate through several channels at once. Close speech is a sequence of clicks made by flexing crystal plates, accompanied by scents too faint and complex for unaided detection. Across a room they use electrical gestures conducted through the floor. In high wind they press limbs to the walls and speak through the city itself. Meaning can pass through several buildings before arriving, altered slightly by each living chamber it crosses.

Names are therefore not sounds alone. A person’s full name may include a pressure rhythm, a molecular trace, and a particular distortion placed in the local electrical field. Spoken names are approximations used for strangers, children, and records intended to survive the speaker.

They feed infrequently. Most carry narrow symbiotic sails along their backs or limbs, opening them to the wind while working or traveling. These sails collect carbon dust, metallic vapor, and reactive gases, which are processed in abdominal chambers into resin and slow-burning salts. Communal meals are not primarily about nourishment. Citizens gather around cultivated vents to exchange catalysts, microorganisms, memory scents, and fragments of bodily material. To eat with another is to permit a small, controlled alteration of oneself.

Their young begin as planted knots.

Several adults contribute lattice patterns, protein instructions, symbionts, and selected memories to a nursery bed grown directly from the city. For months or years the knot listens through the shelfwood, receiving weather, civic traffic, music from the bell colonies, and the electrical signatures of those who visit it. Only after this long rooted childhood does it form limbs and pull free.

Because all infants share some tissue with the settlement, kinship is broad. A citizen may have genetic parents, structural parents who designed the body, memory parents who supplied early recollections, and civic parents whose district sustained the nursery. None of these relationships is considered metaphorical.

The people are patient but not passive. Their apparent stillness conceals continual calculation. During conversation they may pause for minutes while pigment knots settle into coherent states. Decisions of consequence can take seasons, particularly when they involve altering the shelfwood. Yet in emergencies they become startlingly rapid. Their limbs stiffen into springlike struts, and they cross the bark plains in long, low bounds, using magnetic vanes to correct their course. Some ride the contracting transit runners; others grow temporary tendon rigs and let the city throw them from district to district.

They do not sharply distinguish tools from domesticated organisms, nor either from extensions of the self. Engineers carry seed-instruments in cavities beneath their outer layers, keeping them warm and informed with a trickle of bodily charge. A commonly used implement learns the electrical habits of its bearer. After decades it may anticipate requests, refuse unsafe instructions, or modify its descendants. Retired tools are planted in memory gardens rather than discarded.

Architecture is practiced as negotiation. Builders do not impose inert plans upon matter. They introduce developmental arguments into the city’s tissues: gradients, mechanical stresses, chemical promises, carefully designed hungers. The shelfwood responds according to its own health and ancient priorities. A proposed tower may emerge as an arcade, a transit vein may divert around a centuries-old injury, and a public hall may refuse to open if the gathering inside would place too much strain on neighboring roots.

The best engineers are valued less for command than for their ability to frame requests the city is willing to fulfill.

Their politics follows the same principle. District councils assemble physically only for matters requiring direct exchange. Their chambers blossom around them, and the walls become part of the discussion, registering heat, vibration, stress, and chemical output. Arguments are preserved as changes in the room’s tissues. Long afterward, another council can grow a sensory membrane across the wall and experience the debate—not as a transcript, but as a sequence of bodily tensions and releases.

Consensus is never assumed to mean unanimity. It means that opposition has been given a stable shape within the decision. A dissenting group might be granted control of a buttress, an archive scent, or a parallel experimental garden. Their objection remains alive, structurally supporting the thing they opposed and positioned to reshape it if conditions change.

Property is similarly unfamiliar. Objects can belong to individuals, but veins, streets, towers, and cultivated organisms possess claims of their own. Stewardship is recorded as reciprocal access: who may feed a structure, who may alter it, what services it provides, and under what circumstances it may withdraw cooperation. Theft is defined less as taking than as severing a thing from the network that knows how to sustain it.

The plateau’s diminishing edge governs much of civic life. Every citizen expects migration. Districts nearest the rim are grown with detachable foundations, flexible conduits, and memory tissues distributed across several sites. When surveys detect a future fracture, the city begins moving years in advance. Nurseries are replanted first, then archives, observatories, and public chambers. Old houses loosen their roots and travel on tendon roads, inhabited throughout the journey.

Not everything can be saved. Structures too deeply integrated with a failing margin are thanked, copied imperfectly, and allowed to descend with the cliff.

These losses are witnessed from a safe distance. The people assemble low against the wind while buildings, gardens, and sections of ancestral street peel free and sail into the abyss. No one calls the ceremony a funeral. Death, to them, is not disappearance but the end of meaningful exchange. A falling district may continue signaling for hours through conductive fibers streaming behind it. Its final messages are often practical: stress readings, wind measurements, corrections for future evacuations.

Their dead are not buried. A body is separated into three inheritances. Its replaceable outer tissues are returned to the city. Its crystal organs, containing habits of charge and fragments of memory, are installed in computation groves or observatories. Its private scent archive is divided among those named in advance. Thus the deceased may persist as a tendency in a transit network, a bias in weather prediction, a half-recognized emotional note released by a family wall.

The people do not regard these survivals as the person. They are careful about that distinction. A pattern may continue after the will that reconciled its contradictions is gone.

Art often consists of arranging such contradictions. Composers cultivate chambers that reshape the plateau’s great wind chords. Scent-makers encode impossible recollections: the sensation of rain no living citizen has experienced, the chemistry of vanished oceans, the imagined metabolism of stars. Sculptors breed temporary organisms that alter the magnetic field and can be perceived only by opening the sensory fans.

Their most controversial artists work with memory gardens. They splice unrelated recollections so that visitors remember events that never occurred: standing on the plateau before the shelfwood existed, speaking with an extinct grazer, looking down from the underside of the world. Such works are legally required to include an unmistakable molecular marker. Some artists omit it.

Children favor the bell insects. They climb the warm chimneys during gentle winds and rearrange nest partitions to change the colony’s note. Adults officially discourage this, although many civic compositions preserve melodies first discovered by unsupervised juveniles.

The kite predators are not pets, but certain observatory guilds maintain long relationships with them. A kite can feel disturbances in the magnetic field before instruments resolve them. It may circle a tower for generations, accepting cultivated charge and leaving crystalline eggs in the machinery groves. When it dies, its conductive whiskers are woven into deep-field sensors. The guild remembers the instrument by the predator’s name.

Tripod herds are managed without enclosure. Citizens alter the electrical pathways under the bark, creating routes the animals prefer to follow. In exchange for safe migration corridors, the grazers clean mineral lichens from sensitive surfaces and distribute reproductive spores in the abrasions left by their pads. During the corrosive season, herds enter the warm districts. Streets widen to receive them, and public life continues among their resonant footfalls.

The Quiet Stems are the one presence the citizens do not claim to understand.

They map each column, monitor every opening, and maintain vast archives of its resonances. Specialized listeners are grown with reinforced skeletons so they can remain conscious when a stem sounds. These observers lie anchored around the clearance roads, translating vibrations that pass through their bodies into records stored beneath the city.

No explanation has survived scrutiny. The stems may be reproductive organs of the shelfwood, remnants of an earlier ecology, instruments built by unknown inhabitants, or boundaries around places where distance never formed correctly. The seeds that enter them deepen the uncertainty. Some match extinct species known from memory gardens. Others contain chemistries impossible under present conditions. A few have carried genetic instructions addressed to organisms that will not evolve for millions of years.

The citizens do not worship the stems because worship, in their view, would be a premature description of the relationship. Instead they practice courtesy.

No tower exceeds the lowest opening. No transit vein passes directly beneath a column. When a stem sounds, engines pause, arguments cease, and even predators are permitted to rest beside prey. Afterward, teams gather damaged structures and any seeds that failed to cross. These seeds are never dissected. They are planted in isolated gardens and allowed to become whatever they contain.

Most become nothing.

A few grow into small, short-lived organisms shaped for rain.

Such beings cannot survive the plateau unaided. The citizens cultivate sealed chambers for them, manufacturing liquid water at immense cost. They watch green leaves unfold—soft, wasteful surfaces that continuously surrender moisture to the air. They listen to water move through stems and fall from leaf tips. The sound is delicate enough to be lost beneath the city’s pulse.

These rain organisms are kept not as relics but as foreigners. Their chambers are designed according to their needs, and no attempt is made to adapt them to the plateau. Citizens visit in silence, their dark sensory fans fogging against the warm glass.

Elsewhere, ordinary work continues. Engineers plant doorways. Archive keepers prune memory scents. Rim surveyors listen for layers beginning to separate. Herds drum across the bark flats. Bell insects sound in their chimneys, and cities flex along their living foundations.

Above them the black sails drink the wind.

Below them the continental organism conducts its immeasurably slow affairs.

The people live between these scales: quicker than the shelfwood, slower than the storm; individual enough to disagree, distributed enough never to stand entirely alone. They inhabit no structure that does not also inhabit them. Their civilization is not laid over the plateau but braided through its metabolism, a brief and articulate restlessness in the life of something older.

When the great chord begins, they feel the valves opening beneath hundreds of kilometers of ground. Conversation moves indoors. Towers seal. Animals flatten themselves against the bark. Each citizen anchors whatever limbs remain free and listens through the conductive plain.

They cannot know whether the immense, changing tone is weather, metabolism, thought, or a message passing too slowly for them to comprehend.

Still, they answer.

Across the settlements, cultivated chambers open in sequence. Cities add their smaller voices to the pressure moving through the plateau—not to overcome it, and not because they expect a reply, but to make their presence legible within the larger body while they are here.
