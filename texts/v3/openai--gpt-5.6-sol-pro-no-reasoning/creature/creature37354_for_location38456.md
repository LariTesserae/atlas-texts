The shelf is inhabited by what the moving country can no longer keep.

Not the dead, precisely. Death would be an event, and events are not allowed to finish here. What lives on the pale stone are creatures caught at the point where duration has been taken from them: bodies reduced to postures, instincts preserved after their purposes have vanished. They do not form populations. They do not breed, hunt, migrate, sleep, or decay. Each is less an animal than a single enduring statement an animal once made.

The white things beneath the shale are perhaps the least altered. They resemble soft, wingless insects, narrow as grains of rice, with blunt heads and translucent segments through which no organs can be seen. They have no legs, yet paired marks continually appear behind them in the damp, as if their absent limbs are walking elsewhere. Turn over a stone and they lie exposed without fleeing. Cover them again, lift the stone from another side, and they have progressed the width of a finger. Their trails sometimes continue beneath unbroken rock and sometimes end at the edge of a pool, where two pale points can be seen descending into the impossible reflection.

The channel birds are more substantial. They are squat, dense creatures built for weather that no longer happens: thick gray plumage, rust-colored shoulders, short black bills, and feet disproportionately broad for their bodies. Their feathers tremble because the noise passes through them. Beneath the feathers, nothing can be felt to breathe.

Each bird holds its beak against its breast as though waiting out a storm. It may remain beside one pool for years or for the duration of a glance. Its rare blink is not a small movement but an absence: the eyes close, and the whole bird is gone. What remains are two footprints brimmed with clear water. Sometimes, much later and far away, a bird stands with droplets sealed inside the fine barbs of its feathers. Whether it is the same bird cannot be determined. All of them seem to share one waiting.

The horned inhabitants of the upper ledges have surrendered even more of themselves to the mountain. Up close—if “close” can be reached—they are heavy, low-shouldered animals with stone-colored hides and long horns sweeping backward in the exact profiles of distant ridges. Their joints are hidden beneath coarse hair stiffened by frost. Their nostrils contain little crescents of old ice. Their eyes are open, dark, and reflective, but what they reflect is not the plateau before them. Within each eye, forests bend under wind and small bright rivers descend through snow.

They may once have crossed the passes. The cairns are spaced as resting animals might space themselves along a route, and smooth hollows beside some slabs fit the shapes of folded legs. Now each horned creature inhabits a ledge as completely as a vein inhabits stone. Lichen crosses its flank. Talus gathers against its hooves. Its horns continue into the horizon if viewed from the proper angle. A hand laid upon its hide meets warmth far below the surface—not bodily warmth, but the muted warmth of sunlight on another mountainside.

Whole herds may be present without revealing themselves. Some boulders have the patient weight of rumps and shoulders. Some cliffs possess too many eyes when rain darkens them. At intervals the unseen wingbeat passes overhead, and every doubtful stone turns its attention upward without moving.

The maker of that wingbeat is never seen entire. Its traces are distributed across the shelf. A hooked impression in salt. A row of broad feathers apparently fossilized in clear ice. A shadow passing downward through the ground. Once, among the suspended landslide, several stones align into the joints of an enormous pinion; from another position they are merely rocks again. Whatever flies here is held in the completion of one downward stroke, generating lift it can never use. It may be a single vast bird folded through many parts of the plateau, or the preserved motion of every bird flying in the country below the tarn’s surface.

There are also inhabitants without stable shapes.

The breathlike whiteness tethered in sheltered places sometimes exists before any mouth arrives to produce it. Such plumes gather beside doorway-slabs and at the missing fourth walls of enclosures. They maintain the height and outline of breathing creatures, though the cold vapor is hollow. When passed through, they carry a taste of iron, wool, and smoke—substances otherwise absent here. One may stretch slightly toward the tarn. Another may seem to turn away. None can leave the small radius of its condensation.

In the roofless structures reside arrangements of pressure. The roar changes around them, resolving briefly into domestic sounds: a weight set on stone, cloth shaken out, a throat cleared before speech. No words follow. Frost occasionally outlines seated forms against the walls, all facing the central basin. They are neither ghosts nor memories. A memory requires a past. They are places where occupants have been removed more completely than their occupancy.

The blue-bladdered stalks belong to the shelf in a different way. They are not plants so much as vessels through which its displaced weather is stored. Their jointed stems root in cracks that connect, through the frost-map, to rivers visible only in the tarn. Each hard blue tip contains a droplet in which something moves: a cloud shadow, a flash of fish scales, a branch bending under rain. Break one and the stalk is unbroken from elsewhere, but for an instant the roar gains a new detail—the hiss of water entering dry ground, perhaps, or the first tap of rain on leaves. The sound then joins the others and never ceases.

The black lichens feed on boundaries. Their overlapping disks mark sites where one condition meets another: wet beside dry, fracture beside wholeness, arrival beside departure. They do not grow across stone. Rather, more of them becomes visible as the stone ages into the shape they already occupy. Their colonies resemble eclipses because darkness is their source of nourishment—not lack of light, but the exact instant when one world passes before another.

Yet the largest inhabitant is the shelf itself.

Its apparent emptiness is the stillness of a creature too extensive to display movement. The channels are not merely vanished riverbeds; they are sensory lines, receiving the rivers that run in the reflected country. The frost is a branching nervous system. The upright slabs are organs of orientation, keeping the black northern peak fixed while distance folds around them. The roofless enclosures may be chambers exposed by erosion, and the cairns hard nodes where paths, sounds, and unfinished crossings accumulate.

At the center lies the tarn, the creature’s open eye.

Through it, the shelf regards the living country whose noise it bears. The relationship may be parasitic, protective, or simply anatomical. Perhaps the plateau is the cast-off stillness required for the other land to move. Every waterfall there pours because its roar has been removed here. Every animal runs because its held posture has been left behind on these stones. Forests bend because their immobility has been deposited in the horned watchers. Weather changes because each unchanging rain has been pinned above the shelf like an insect.

The footprints entering the tarn belong to creatures that have relinquished their fixed forms. Birds step in and become flight elsewhere. Horned animals enter and emerge beneath the reflected trees as moving herds. Stranger tracks—webbed, many-toed, round as pillars, or narrow as human heels—mark other exchanges. Nothing comes back out because return would require the same being to resume the same shape.

Only one inhabitant may cross in the opposite direction.

It is never directly present. It is inferred from discrepancies: a reflected tree that stands still while the forest thrashes, an animal among the distant herds that casts its shadow upward, a human-sized darkness walking against the bright reflected sky. Wherever it passes in that lower world, a new still form appears on the plateau—a bird at a pool, a boulder with watchful eyes, a plume of breath waiting beside a doorway.

Perhaps it tends the separation. Perhaps it is merely the portion of the plateau capable of walking.

Those who enter this place do not meet it. They gradually supply it. Their footprints precede them because the shelf receives their completed passage before they have finished walking. Their breath remains because the air has already preserved it. Their shadows lengthen downward, testing for a place among the inverted summits. In time—or in the absence that serves as time—they may notice an additional figure reflected in the tarn, moving freely through the black forests beneath the bright sky.

The figure will look like them, though younger or older, clothed for another season. It will cross rivers, startle birds into flight, and climb toward peaks that diminish naturally with distance. Its mouth will open in speech. No sound will emerge there.

The sound will arrive on the shelf instead.

And the one standing at the shore will remain to contain it.
