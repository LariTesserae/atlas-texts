The city is inhabited by processes rather than people.

Frost lives here longest. It sleeps beneath ledges through the brief summer and wakes each night inside the mountain’s finest seams. It enters as moisture, widens into crystal, and leaves behind another loosened grain. Over ages it has done the work of a patient mason while building nothing: clarifying pillars, opening windows, separating slabs, and keeping the city’s austere geometry in repair.

Water is the city’s transient population. It arrives as cloud, beads on walls, gathers in gutters, and hurries inward through every court. For a few minutes after rain, its thin bright crowds occupy all the streets. Then they descend beneath the central plazas into clear, lightless cisterns whose levels never change. Whatever receives the water below is the city’s deepest inhabitant, if inhabitant is the word for something known only by appetite. It does not rise to meet what falls. It answers neither with overflow nor echo. Around its hidden boundary, ripples move inward and moss leans down.

Wind inhabits everything above. It passes through the mountain’s emptied weaknesses and is divided into voices: organ notes in the arcades, howls in the vertical pits, whispers along slate beds, and heavy booms in the chambers below. There is no single voice belonging to the place. The city speaks as a multitude because emptiness has been partitioned into streets and halls. A change in temperature can silence one district and give another a new tongue. Ice closes certain mouths; thaw opens them. Thus the weather continually rearranges an unseen citizenry of tones.

The plants live low, as though height belongs exclusively to stone and air. Black moss occupies the damp foundations. Red-stemmed moss ascends the lower columns. Sedges lie matted in drains, combed flat whenever runoff comes. Ferns no larger than feathers unfold in shaded joints. Waxy alpine leaves press themselves against sheltered terraces, and after rain translucent fungi rise in close tiers, delicate and temporary as balconies.

In three courts live ancient shrubs reduced almost to knots. Their age is concealed by their stature. They have endured centuries without becoming tall, their trunks braided by cold and persistent wind. They do not fill the courts so much as hold their places within them, dark-rooted and close to the ground.

Minute creatures inhabit this compressed vegetation. Nearly transparent springtails move through wet cracks. Small dark insects wake during the thaw and complete their daylight lives between frost and afternoon cold. Larger animals are only visitors: a white-winged bird crossing overhead, a solitary grazer entering through the northern breach, made nervous by the stone’s sudden subterranean booming.

The moths alone seem native to the architecture.

They sleep in the deepest arcades with slate-colored wings folded into the walls’ patterns. Cloud cover wakes them. Moisture reveals them. They do not seek lamps, flowers, or warmth, but gather at the mouths of resonant shafts and circle within the low notes, reading the city by vibration. When the wind alters, they rise together and pass from quarter to quarter like a piece of dusk shaken loose. Perhaps they feed among the mosses and fungi; perhaps their brief flights are enough. Their true habitat appears to be not stone but sound.

Below them are the cisterns, and below the cisterns something less easily called life.

It leaves no track, body, residue, or breath of its own. Its existence can be inferred only from the city’s inwardness. Every channel converges upon it. No spring issues from the mountain. No pool rises. Nothing dropped returns a proper splash. In calm weather the water sometimes stirs from its edges toward an unreflecting center, as though receiving a summons from farther down.

It may be an abyss, a buried porous stratum, or a depth for which the mountain is merely an opening. Yet the city’s other inhabitants behave as if it were a fixed feature of their world. Moss inclines toward it. Moths navigate the chambers connected to it. Wind awakens its booms. Water goes to it without accumulating. The entire summit has been shaped into an elaborate watershed around a destination that cannot be seen.

So what lives here is a community of persistence and passage: old shrubs, brief insects, cloud-wakened moths, mosses drinking from condensation, water that never returns, and wind wearing a thousand borrowed voices. Stone lives here too, if life can be measured by continual response. It contracts, cracks, sheds, settles, and slowly improves the fit of every court and colonnade.

Nothing possesses the city. Nothing tends it from outside.

Its inhabitants are the forces that made it, the small organisms that exploit their pauses, and the hidden downward condition to which all its streets ultimately lead.
