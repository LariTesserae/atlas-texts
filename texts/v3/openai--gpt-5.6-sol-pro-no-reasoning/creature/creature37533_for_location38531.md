The people of the basin are compact, deliberate folk, shaped by low ceilings, wet footing, and the knowledge that anything neglected will soon become part of something else.

They move with one hand free. A palm rests on bark-wrapped rails; fingers test a cable’s vibration before crossing a machinery court; toes seek the worn notches in roots that serve as stairs. Children learn early which green surfaces are masonry under moss, which are water under duckweed, and which are neither path nor floor but a roof viewed from above. Nobody runs except at the sound of a broken-bell alarm. Even then, they run carefully.

Their bodies tend toward strength without speed: broad hands, braced legs, shoulders accustomed to loads carried close. Skin is perpetually filmed with humidity. Hair is cropped, braided tightly, or bound beneath oiled cloth to keep it clear of belts and dripping branches. Clothes are layered but light—woven bast, coarse linen, waxed aprons, sleeveless work coats—with wooden toggles or ceramic clasps in place of metal fastenings that would rust. Soles are ridged and flexible enough to grip roots. Most people carry a pruning hook, a wedge, and a length of cord, as ordinary elsewhere as keys.

They are loud at arm’s length and nearly mute at a distance. Conversation happens face to face, with hands supplementing voices: two fingers circling for a wheel problem, a flat palm lowered for falling pressure, knuckles tapped against a pipe to request attention on the other side. Families and workshops develop their own cable codes. A child in an upper room may call someone home by pulling a line that trembles against a wooden sounding board three terraces below.

Their hearing is peculiar. They do not hear the machinery less than a visitor does; they hear more within it. A pump operator can distinguish a loose mounting from a flooded cylinder through several walls and the roots between them. Mill workers know when a belt has taken up too much moisture by the interval of its slap. Sleep comes easily under the ordinary concussion, but a missed beat wakes whole households. Silence is the town’s alarm.

There are gardeners everywhere, though few call themselves gardeners. The distinction between tending a house, tending a machine, and tending a plant is too slight to support separate professions. A roof keeper prunes its rafters. A mason grafts young support roots across a failing corner. A pipe fitter clears liverwort from an overflow mouth but presses it into the joint below, where its swelling will slow a leak. Millwrights read the posture of nearby trunks for shifts in foundations. Nursery workers raise not only food and medicines but future lintels, braces, filter beds, ladder rails, and walls.

Nothing is planted without an anticipated duty. Figs hold terraces. Laurels drink beside the cisterns. Reeds clarify wastewater. Fibrous creepers are trained along scaffold frames, harvested in strips, and allowed to regrow. Moss is cultivated as packing and insulation. Broad-leaved plants are positioned to catch condensation from cold pipes. Even flowers are practical: their opening marks moisture, heat, or the turning of a season. Beauty is abundant, but seldom separate from use.

Because no animals live in the basin, the people perform many small labors that other places surrender to them. They pollinate blossoms with soft brushes, featherless vanes, or puffs of air from hand bellows. They shake flowering frames over cloths, carry pollen between terraces in stoppered tubes, and keep careful graft books. Fallen fruit must be gathered promptly, not because anything will eat it, but because nothing will. Without beetles, worms, or flies, decay is slow in some ways and dangerous in others. Rotting matter is chopped, heated, fermented in sealed vats, or fed to managed beds of fungi. Composters are technicians, custodians of transformations the absent creatures might once have performed.

The absence has entered custom. Doors bear no latches against intruding beasts. Grain is stored against damp, never teeth. Children know animals from upland carvings, inherited diagrams, and words embedded in old expressions. They still say a machine “bucks,” a hook has a “goose neck,” a lifting frame has “beetle legs.” Such comparisons have become abstract. A painted bird may be copied for centuries until it resembles a leaf with an eye.

No one whistles like a bird. They do not imitate creatures that are not there. Their music grows from the town itself: struck pipes, water-filled bowls, tensioned wires, clappers mounted against turning shafts. Songs are built to fit between machine beats. Work choruses use the pumps as a measure, voices entering on the rebound of the piston so words remain intelligible. In the deepest chambers, people sing through their feet as much as their ears, standing on resonant boards laid across the foundations.

Their houses are less private than sheltered. Rooms fit among load-bearing roots and borrowed factory walls. A family may sleep in a chamber whose outer surface is the settling tank of the floor above. Curtains of woven fiber divide spaces that cannot safely be bricked off. Cupboards are hung rather than set on wet floors. Beds occupy platforms in the driest vertical pockets, often reached by ladders. Cooking is done over enclosed burners with flues carefully threaded through foliage; open flame is treated with greater unease than deep water.

Households are rarely defined by a single doorway. A dwelling may have three entrances on different levels, each opening into a different terrace or workshop. Neighbors cross one another’s galleries because routes are dictated by roots, channels, and structural load rather than property lines. Ownership consequently attaches more readily to obligations than to surfaces. One household has the right to harvest a vine but must maintain the trellis beneath it. Another occupies a room but owes inspection of the wall’s weep holes. A workshop may own a press while the branch carrying its upper bearing belongs to the nursery ring.

Their records reflect this. Maps show flows and burdens rather than plots: where water must pass, how much weight a root arch may carry, which family clears a channel, which mill receives power during which hours. Every structure has several ages—the brick’s laying, the first planting, the grafting of a brace, the diversion of a spring—and all may matter legally. Disputes are settled by tracing consequences downhill. The favored question is not “Whose is it?” but “Who receives what follows?”

Authority belongs to those who allocate passage. Gate keepers, reservoir readers, root wardens, pump stewards, and the surveyors of settling beds hold more influence than merchants. Their councils meet beside visible water so no decision about its division is made abstractly. Schedules are marked on glazed boards with movable ceramic tabs. In dry periods, a gate may be opened by the width of a finger; in storms, entire courts are surrendered to overflow. Refusing water its path is among the town’s gravest offenses, close to sabotaging a pump or severing a load-bearing root.

Death, too, is understood as a problem of return. The dead cannot be buried in the saturated basin, and burning them wastes fuel and burdens the air. Bodies are carried up the rim stair to dry upland ground, while clothing, tools, and cultivated cuttings remain below. Memorials are living supports: a graft in a communal arch, a tree assigned to a cistern wall, a shade vine trained over a household gallery. Names persist in practical speech. Someone may say, “This stair rests on Mara,” meaning the fig planted for her, or “Open Iven at dusk,” meaning the sluice whose reconstruction was his life’s work.

Children are given small channels to manage. At first these are hand-wide runs between nursery beds, with gates made from shards of tile. They learn that blocking one outlet floods another, that clear water can still carry force, that a plant bent too sharply may live but cease to bear weight. Later they receive pollination rounds, cable messages, or watch duty at overflow mouths during rain. Adulthood is recognized not by age alone but by being trusted with something that continues overnight.

The oldest inhabitants know the town almost entirely through change. They remember when a staircase was level, when a certain house still had four built walls, when a pipe ran outside the laurel instead of through it. Their expertise is not resistance to alteration but judgment about which alterations can be accepted. They sit near junctions where people must slow down and advise on the temperament of foundations. Their sayings are patient and hydraulic: make three paths before closing one; a dry wall is hiding water; what leans today may be bearing tomorrow.

Visitors often mistake the town’s people for subdued. They are not. Their humor is constant, practical, and sharpened by proximity. They joke about being outgrown by their own homes, about apprentices who prune structural limbs, about council decisions moving more slowly than header tanks. They decorate tool handles, carve faces into overflow spouts, and train vines into elaborate knots above factory doors. Festival processions do not travel far—there is nowhere for a crowd to gather—but unfold vertically, lamps descending terrace by terrace while drums answer from the pumping halls below.

They seldom look toward the sky. Weather reaches them first through water temperature, pipe tone, leaf angle, and changes in the smell of stone. They know rain before it falls into the basin because the springs cloud, roots tighten against masonry, and the old channels begin to murmur. Then shutters are opened rather than closed. Screens are cleared. Lamps are raised. People take their assigned places along the terraces.

When the rain arrives, the whole population seems to become part of the drainage system. Figures stand ankle-deep in lanes, lifting grilles with hooked poles. Gate crews signal by lamps. Nursery workers bind branches away from spillways. Children carry wedges and coils of bark rope. Below them the pumps deepen their beat, and every floor trembles.

No one commands the water to stop. They accompany it downward.
