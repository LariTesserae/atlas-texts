The inhabitants are not plants, though they have accepted the forms of plants as useful solutions.

They rise wherever the black crust divides: fans, blades, trunks, scales, fronds, crowns. Their bodies are arrangements of carbon, silicon, conductive chains, and flexible metals, assembled without sap or cells. What passes for rooting is a penetration of the mineral lattice. Fine crystalline faults spread beneath each growth, gathering charge, pressure, displaced electrons, and imperfections in the stone. Their roots do not consume the crust. They participate in its incompleteness.

Above ground, they cultivate surfaces. Dark leaves collect the ordinary sunlight. Metallic undersides negotiate with the pale sky. Some organs exchange charge; others sustain local geometries, preserving distances and overlaps that could not otherwise coexist. A grove may be, in part, its own support system: trunks acting as organisms, crowns acting as laws, faint chords holding the whole arrangement in place.

They do not grow in time. They possess growth as a permanent posture.

A shoot is always emerging because emergence is what it is. A trunk eternally flaking into white plates is not dying; division is its mature form. No individual is juvenile or ancient. Injury cannot be distinguished from anatomy. When a frond has an exposed edge, the edge is immediately as fundamental as the rest of it—as though cutting does not alter the organism but reveals another version that was already included.

Their individuality is uncertain. The repeated microscopic spacing of filament trees suggests that a forest may be one distributed body. Interpenetrating branches may be separate organisms sharing space, or a single organism expressed under several compatible rules. The hovering lichens, attached only by their shadows, may root in illumination rather than stone. The hexagonal meadows may be sensory tissue spread thin across the plain.

What looks like contradiction is likely part of their physiology.

A trunk with six limbs to the hand and eleven to the counting eye may use both bodies. One is adapted for contact, the other for enumeration. Distance is another tissue it can regulate, keeping itself remote while allowing a traveler’s feet to cross the intervening ground. Plants that appear one behind another while each remains in front may be layered not by depth but by function. Here, perspective is not merely how creatures are seen. It is something they metabolize.

The stationary reflective motes among the crowns may be their reproductive bodies, their eyes, or neither. Each contains an aerial view of the entire plain, suggesting that every mote holds a model of the greater organism. They remain unsupported because position itself supports them. Within their minute reflections is the circular absence that cannot be reached below. Perhaps the motes observe it. Perhaps they collectively produce it.

The rare moving creatures are small, dark, and low-shelled, with three legs set one behind another along a single axis. Their symmetry belongs to travel through boundaries rather than across surfaces. They do not visibly move; they exchange one location for another when observation relaxes. Their stillness under scrutiny may be concealment, or it may be a basic limitation: movement and being seen cannot apply to them at the same time.

They feed at the needle edges of the crust. Their mouthparts match each fracture with impossible precision, closing over the distinction between neighboring plates. No material is removed. The shards remain separate, but the boundary ceases to be locatable. Thus the creatures eat relations: adjacency, division, perhaps the fact that one thing is not another.

They may be grazers, trimming excess separateness from the plain. They may prevent the acute plates from becoming wholly independent worlds. If so, what they leave behind is not waste but continuity.

The chords inhabiting certain places could also be alive. They have shape without visible bodies: waist-high volumes through which a traveler passes. They resonate in teeth and bone, maintain invisible inclines, and keep incompatible forms from excluding one another. They may be sessile animals composed of vibration, feeding on structural tension. Or they may be organs of the groves, extending beyond them as sound.

There may be a deeper inhabitant as well.

The whole plain behaves less like an ecosystem containing many lives than like one life entertaining many local anatomies. Its roots are faults. Its circulation is charge without gradient. Its metabolism is the stable coexistence of contradictions. The forests, motes, chords, and boundary-eaters may be tissues assigned different tasks within a body too level and too extensive to present a recognizable outline.

If it has awareness, that awareness does not proceed from moment to moment. It may know by holding every permissible version of itself at once. It does not react because reaction would require a before in which it had not yet responded. Nothing guards it because trespass, defense, injury, and repair have already been replaced by more exact relationships.

The circular absence seen only in the motes may be its mouth, seed, memory, or pupil. It may be the one place the plain excludes from itself in order to remain a plain. Everything thickens toward it without drawing nearer. Every path enters its ecology without approaching its position. Perhaps that absence is the true organism, and all the mineral luxuriance is merely the elaborate structure by which it avoids arriving.

Whatever lives here does not endure unchanged.

It lives as unchangeability: not dead, not dormant, but active in ways that require no event. Its forests perform without motion. Its animals feed without taking. Its senses look down from points suspended under the crowns. Its intelligence, if intelligence is the word, resides in the agreements that let incompatible things remain true.

Across the dark plates, it is continually born into a world where nothing begins.
