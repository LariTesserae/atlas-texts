The inhabitants call themselves, approximately, the Listeners. Their own name is not spoken with the mouth alone. It begins as a hum behind closed teeth, continues in two taps against the breastbone, and ends with the faint ring of a metal tongue worn at the throat. Away from the mountain, where stone answers poorly, the name cannot be completed.

They are small, narrow people, made for conserving substance and surrendering heat. An adult can crouch inside one of the saddle’s triangular doorways without turning sideways. Their limbs are long but lightly built, with broad joints wrapped in elastic cartilage. When an updraft catches them, they bend almost flat rather than resist it. Their skin is matte and finely ridged, usually the gray-brown of old pottery, though the ridges flush copper when they breathe deeply. Dust lodges in these channels and is later combed out, sorted, and swallowed.

Their faces seem unfinished by ordinary standards. They have no projecting nose. Three vertical breathing slits lie on either side of the jaw, protected by overlapping cilia that glitter with mineral grains. The mouth is small and used mainly for cutting cord, tasting salts, and shaping the softest part of speech. Most respiration occurs through expandable throat chambers and a pair of pleated sacs beneath the ribs. These sacs show through the skin as dark, slowly opening fans.

Their eyes are serviceable in the saddle’s brown daylight but not dominant. They are deep-set, copper-black, and covered by transparent inner lids during storms. More important are the pale sensory seams running from jaw to collarbone and down the inner arms. Pressed against a trunk, tool, wall, or patch of ground, these seams receive vibration with exquisite precision. A Listener kneeling with one palm on the stone can distinguish the footfall of a hammerback from the settling of a ceramic plate several body lengths away. By touching jaw to a tuning fork, they hear not only its note but its decay, its roughness, and the faint secondary tones caused by flaws in the metal.

They do not consider this hearing. Hearing belongs to the air and is notoriously deceitful. What comes through bone is called knowing.

Their masks are extensions of the body rather than disguises. Each is molded from porous clay, fitted tightly over the breathing slits, and perforated according to season, altitude, and expected wind. Resin-lined channels separate useful vapors from corrosive ones. Little pockets in the cheeks hold powdered plant tissue; incoming gases pass through these powders and become nourishing or medicinal. A well-prepared mask can keep its wearer active through an atmosphere that would send most animals into convulsions.

The masks also give the people their public faces. Some have long, solemn cheek plates. Some bristle with copper whiskers that tremble before a pressure change. Children’s masks are round and densely perforated, making their breath whistle. Gatherers paint theirs with mineral lines denoting paths, obligations, and remembered warnings. Because the saddle continually revises itself, these markings are not maps of places. They are maps of sequences: cross after the bronze fans close; turn when the red bell sounds twice; kneel where the stone answers with two voices.

Their settlements lie below the summit in cavities opened by ancient gas channels. There, hundreds may live among warm walls without ever seeing one another clearly. Chambers are linked by narrow resonant tunnels. Messages travel through these passages as tapped rhythms, bowed cords, smoke pulses, and sustained notes. Conversation may cross an entire village without any speaker raising a voice.

They eat little solid matter. Their staple is a mixture of reactive dusts collected from plants, scraped salts, fungal ceramics grown on warm rock, and gas altered inside the bodies of domesticated animals. Small breathing-beasts are kept in latticed jars and fed particular vapors until their exhalations acquire the desired composition. Families maintain distinct strains whose breath may be sour, narcotic, nourishing, antiseptic, or conducive to vivid shared dreams.

To them, breath is not merely evidence of life. It is life in exchange. Nothing possesses breath entirely; each creature borrows it from the mountain, changes it, and passes it onward. Eating a body is coarse and sometimes necessary. Receiving what a body has made of the air is intimate.

This belief explains their reverence for the saddle. The place is not a garden in their language but something closer to Mouth-Where-Many-Answers-Meet. Every plant transforms the mountain’s breath differently. Every animal carries, filters, corrupts, or refines it. The bell-trees render these invisible changes audible. By listening, the people can tell what is rising from below and what the living garden has done to it.

Their sound harvest is therefore practical, sacred, and archival.

A matched bell-tree branch is cut only after its tone has been bound. The gatherer winds felted fiber around the branch, secures every leaf, and fixes a fork of the same pitch against the trunk. The fork receives the final vibration when the branch is severed. Later, in a lower chamber, the branch is unwrapped inside a sealed vessel and allowed to ring. Powders shaken from it are collected; gases released by its tissues are drawn into bladders; its changing tone is recorded against copper forks kept by the settlement.

Each branch is thus a sample of a particular conjunction: mineral breath, dust, season, pressure, plant age, animal traffic, and the mountain’s condition at the moment of cutting. The Listeners preserve these conjunctions as other peoples might preserve dates, laws, or genealogies.

Their oldest histories are racks of darkened forks. To read one, an archivist strikes it against the jaw and names the events nested in its overtones. A trained archivist may hear a shelter’s collapse, the arrival of a new rod species, a century of bitter exhalations, a migration of sailers, or a period when the bell-trees began ringing too low and many children were born unable to inflate their throat sacs.

Accuracy is not agreement on words. It is agreement on resonance.

The three summit shelters are listening houses. No one remains in them because prolonged exposure to the saddle’s chorus alters sleep, judgment, and eventually anatomy. Those who have stayed too long begin responding to tones no one else can perceive. Their breathing sacs open during silence. Their bones acquire a faint ring. Some walk toward cracks just before they flare and wait there smiling behind their masks.

Yet the houses must be maintained. They orient the garden.

The Listeners insist that there are always three, although storms may crush one, absorb another, or create a new ceramic chamber around a frame of abandoned fiber. They do not identify the houses by material continuity. A shelter remains the same shelter if it occupies the same part in the sounding pattern. The leaning house is always the one that borrows a tree’s voice. The fallen house is always the one through which small animals pass. The intact house is always the one where silence can be stored.

The soot in the intact shelter comes from incense-plants burned in shallow cups. Their smoke is inhaled through masks and exhaled against walls, tools, and one another. Different preparations induce different breathing rhythms. In this way several gatherers can synchronize their bodies until their rib sacs open and close together. When they then touch the floor, vibrations passing through one body can be compared with those passing through the others. This communal listening reveals faint disharmonies no individual could detect.

It can also produce speech without sound. A thought is not transferred whole, but an attitude toward a coming event may be shared: caution, readiness, grief, hunger, an intuition of pressure building under the western plates. The Listeners make no sharp distinction between information and emotion. Both are bodily preparations for what may happen next.

Their children learn the saddle first through games. They roll hooked hoops after wreath-creatures, freeze when a hidden bell is struck, and practice crossing tiled floors without exciting hammerbacks beneath them. They are taught to recognize dangerous intervals before they learn the names of plants. A bright major spacing between two trees means stable air. A wavering near-unison means poison. Three low beats are never imitated, even in play.

The rule concerning the largest plate is taught without explanation:

Do not strike it.  
Do not measure it.  
Do not answer when it answers.  
Do not look beneath.

Children naturally ask why. Adults reply that a rule with a reason may be argued with.

The creatures of the saddle know nothing of prohibition, but many are shaped by the same hidden regularities.

The sailers are less like winged animals than living kites gathered around a digestive knot. Their six limbs are hollow spars. Amber membranes unfurl between them when they leap, and electrostatic combs around the mouth sweep charged dust from the air. They breed in updrafts. Two or more sailers lock limbs while circling, exchange packets of catalytic powder, then separate and deposit thin eggs on the undersides of metal leaves. The eggs hatch only when struck by a precise range of notes. Thus a new brood may wait through years of ordinary clamor for one particular storm.

Hammerbacks are dense by comparison: squat, porcelain-scaled, and nearly all skull. Their hardened brows contain nested chambers tuned to different depths. A blow against the ground sends several pulses into the crust; their lower jaws read the returning echoes. They do not simply stun prey. They provoke burrowers into moving, then follow the disturbance. During mating season, rival hammerbacks drum elaborate claims into separate plates of stone. Some contests continue for days without the animals ever meeting.

Wreath-creatures are solitary only when frightened. Each individual resembles a curved length of lacquered thorn with many short legs and a hook at either end. Alone, it has poor balance and almost no directional sense. Joined in a ring, the animals form a distributed creature capable of rolling, steering, remembering routes, and selecting food. No single member leads. Decisions travel around the wreath as muscular shivers. Older rings incorporate scarred individuals whose bodies preserve habitual responses, so the wreath’s memory survives even as its members are replaced.

The lattice hunters are stranger. Their visible frames are largely scaffolds grown to hold membranes in moving air. Most of their living tissue occupies tiny knots at the joints. When folded, an adult fits into a crack scarcely wider than a hand; when open, it may arch above a shelter. It detects motion through minute changes in pressure and ground vibration. After pinning prey, it passes mountain breath through the trapped animal and absorbs only what emerges altered—heat-shocked compounds, alarm vapors, reproductive signals, the chemical trace of exertion. It leaves the flesh because flesh is not what it hunts. It hunts consequence.

Bell-trees may be less passive than the gatherers admit. Their leaves move in air too faint for other plants to register. Their roots—or ceramic claws—alter their grip before plates shift. They change pitch after animals nest within them and sometimes reproduce notes made by copper forks. Old trees accumulate cavities that resemble chambers, valves, and listening tubes. A few have begun growing leaf arrangements similar to the perforations of human masks.

The Listeners cut such trees reluctantly.

They tell a story of the first mask, though whether it is myth or memory cannot be established. In it, an ancestor climbed without protection during a poisonous silver wind. A bell-tree folded around her face and breathed through its leaves. When the wind passed, she cut away the cluster that had saved her. It hardened into a mask bearing the imprint of both faces: hers on the inside, the tree’s on the outer surface.

Some masks still grow warm when hung near bell-trees.

Beneath all this life is the inhabitant no one names.

The Listeners reject questions that assume it is one creature, or that it is beneath in the ordinary sense. They say only that the mountain has an inward weather. The saddle is where that weather approaches the outer world. The hollow ringing of the crust, the rearrangement of plates, the mineral foam, the black coin-seeds, and the inverted radiance beneath the largest slab are expressions of pressure between two arrangements of life.

The hidden garden may not literally lie upside down. The glimpse beneath the plate is too brief and crowded for certainty. Witnesses report luminous roots hanging upward, animals moving along the undersides of stone, bell shapes opening into the rock, and structures resembling the three shelters but vastly multiplied. Some see their own masks looking back. Some see no individual forms at all, only a dense geometry of openings through which colored breath circulates.

The animals gather because the rising plate releases a gas unlike any other: old, complex, and already breathed many times. It drives them neither mad nor tame. For those few moments, predators and prey orient toward the opening together. Their respiratory sacs pulse in a shared rhythm. Bell-trees fall silent, perhaps because there is nothing they need add.

The gatherers remain at the rim if they can. They turn their masks outward. Even so, they feel the rhythm through their feet.

There are accounts of Listeners breaking the rule. Most are cautionary and therefore suspect. One says that a gatherer looked beneath the plate and saw a second gatherer looking up, identical except for fresh glaze sealing wounds that the first had not yet received. Another says the opening contains all former arrangements of the saddle, preserved not in layers but simultaneously. A third says there is no second garden: the radiance is the interior of a vast breathing organ, and everything on the saddle—plants, animals, houses, people—is a temporary structure grown across one of its pores.

The oldest forks do not settle the matter. When struck, they produce the garden’s familiar uproar: metal leaves, rolling wreaths, wing-skins snapping in hot wind, stone answering stone.

Underneath is another sound.

It is very faint. It resembles the same uproar played in reverse—not backward in time, but inward, as if every clang above were also a knock from the other side.

This may be why the saddle never preserves positions, only relationships. Its life does not occupy a stable landscape. Life is the means by which the landscape renegotiates itself. Houses, trees, plates, migrations, and even species are notes that may be replaced so long as the chord remains.

The Listeners understand themselves in the same way. A person is not a fixed body but a recurring pattern among breath, mask, tools, kin, and remembered tones. Death occurs when the pattern can no longer answer. The body is placed in an exhaling crack with its mask turned downward. Sometimes the crack seals. Sometimes, seasons later, a new salt flower rises nearby and rings faintly when touched.

They do not claim this is the dead returning.

They do not claim it is not.

They strike a fork against the bloom, rest the metal at the hinge of the jaw, and listen for what the mountain has made of them.
