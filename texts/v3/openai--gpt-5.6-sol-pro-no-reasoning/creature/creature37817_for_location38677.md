The inhabitants of the lowlands are not one people, though from a distance the plain seems to belong to a single civilization. It is a civilization without citizens: innumerable lives joined by shelter, hunger, accident, and the relentless reuse of what others leave behind.

Its chief makers are the pale termites. They live almost entirely out of sight, soft-bodied and vulnerable beneath a country hardened around them. Their colonies descend farther than their towers rise, following hairline mineral seams into cool strata where bound moisture can be harvested. Workers carry dampened clay upward in mouthfuls, laying it along cracks and apertures. Soldiers wait at junctions with their broad heads fitted to the passages like living doors. Deep within, swollen queens rest in wax-lined chambers, tended in darkness while eggs move outward toward nurseries arranged by temperature.

The towers are not merely nests. Their flutes catch dust; their chimneys exchange warm and cool air; their buttresses harbor roots. Some colonies cultivate pallid fungi in sealed combs, feeding them chewed grass, husks, dung, and the bodies of dead workers. The fungi transform this dry refuse into soft nourishment and release minute quantities of usable moisture. A mound survives by circulating both. Air is drawn through lower galleries and expelled through sun-warmed crests, so that the whole structure seems to inhale and exhale.

Among the termites live darker, quicker nations of ants. Some gather seeds and sort them into underground granaries according to size, oil, and susceptibility to mold. Some raid the termite galleries. Others tend root-feeding insects in chambers built around living plants, receiving waxy secretions in exchange for defense. Their roofed roads lace the ground between towers, often concealed beneath a crust no thicker than a fingernail. When a route is broken, repair workers arrive almost at once, carrying grains in their jaws while scouts touch antennae in the breach.

Many plants depend on these roads. Their seeds are hard, polished things with edible collars that entice ants to carry them below. Once the collars have been eaten, the seeds remain in refuse chambers until a rare conjunction of temperature and internal moisture wakes them. They germinate in darkness, sending roots down toward hydrated stone and pale shoots up through walls softened by fungal threads.

The low vegetation is built for possession rather than expansion. Thorn cushions shelter cool interiors in which beetles, mites, and tiny scaled animals pass their entire lives. Waxy fan-plants fold together during the brightest hours, trapping a pocket of night air around their growing tips. Gray grasses twist their blades edge-on to the sun and comb seed fluff from the wind. Round-leaved mats spread by short buried stems, so that what appears to be a thousand plants may be one organism occupying a courtyard for generations.

The mound trees are larger reservoirs. Their smooth trunks are swollen with fibrous tissue, salts, and tightly held moisture. Under the bark, narrow vessels open and close in sequence rather than carrying a continuous stream. Their roots penetrate old galleries, sealing some and widening others. Termites eat dead root layers without harming the living core; in return, their clay repairs support the plant against wind. Neither organism intends the partnership. Yet tree and mound gradually become inseparable, and when one dies, the other often collapses into a nursery for its successors.

During the flowering days, beetles emerge in lacquered crowds. They are blunt, heavy fliers, armored against desiccation, with mouthparts suited to scraping pollen. Small long-snouted mammals climb beside them, gripping bark with broad, padded feet. Their fur catches the dry pollen until their faces and shoulders are stained cream or violet. They lick much of it away, but enough travels with them to the next crown. Finches arrive at dawn and settle so densely that branches bend under their combined weight. They consume flowers, insects, and one another’s discarded fragments, then depart before the upper surfaces become hot.

The seed-eating rodents inhabit the middle depths. They are compact animals with sealed nostrils, pale velvet fur, and incisors stained by mineral-rich bulbs. Their kidneys surrender almost no water. By day they sleep pressed together in chambers lined with husks, where their breath slightly raises the humidity. At dusk they enter the broad corridors and courtyards, filling cheek pouches with seeds before returning underground. Some cultivate caches unintentionally: forgotten stores sprout, fracture their chambers, and begin new root-bound towers.

Other rodents specialize in fungi. They follow the cool odor of buried combs and gnaw through abandoned walls with chisel-like teeth. Their forefeet are wide and callused; their eyes are small, useful mainly for distinguishing an opening from a dead end. They map the country through whisker contact, scent, and the transmission of footsteps through clay. A colony may know hundreds of kilometers of connected architecture without ever seeing the horizon above it.

The lizards belong to the thresholds. Flat-bodied and earth-colored, they wait where shafts meet sunlight, warming one side of the body while the other remains in shade. Some can darken the skin along their ribs to gather heat before dawn. Their tongues detect ant roads and rodent scent. When threatened, they slip backward into cracks and inflate themselves, rough scales anchoring them so firmly that a predator cannot pull them free.

Snakes use the deeper ventilation system. They are narrow-headed, patient hunters, able to pass through conduits scarcely wider than their bodies. Heat pits along the jaw reveal sleeping mammals through thin walls. A snake may remain in one junction for days, drinking nothing, feeding only when traffic brings prey within reach. Termites reroute around it; rodents seal passages behind it; beetles clean what it leaves. Even predation becomes construction.

Above, owls occupy broken spires and widened vents. Their plumage resembles layered dust, pale on chalk districts and rust-colored over iron clay. They hunt mostly by vibration and movement rather than sound, dropping into avenues where rodents expose themselves between openings. In the morning they retreat to cool chambers beneath the tower crests. Their pellets accumulate in hollows, where dermestid beetles, mites, fungi, and roots dismantle bone and fur until the remains become another mineral-rich patch of wall.

The foxes are the largest regular travelers. They are slight, high-eared animals with narrow feet and coats the color of old clay. Their ears release heat and gather the faint noises that escape from underground chambers. They do not excavate deeply. Instead they patrol collapses, junctions, and the edges of flowering districts, relying on the architecture to concentrate prey. Their dens are enlarged rodent chambers with several exits, often beneath living roots whose night metabolism keeps the air marginally warmer. Fox families communicate through scent marks and breathy clicks; a loud call would carry too far through the hollow city.

Thinner hunting mammals enter spaces the foxes cannot. Long-bodied and flexible, they pursue rodents through cracks in which neither animal can turn. Their shoulders compress; their ears fold shut; their whiskers point both forward and backward, telling them whether retreat remains possible. They often emerge dust-white from a chase, pause to shake once, and vanish into another seam.

There are inhabitants smaller than visibility suggests. Armored detritivores move through refuse hollows, conserving moisture beneath overlapping plates. Mites graze fungi from the walls. Springing insects remain dormant as sealed eggs until humidity rises inside a living chamber. Threadlike worms inhabit the tissues of plants and animals rather than the soil, passing from host to host through food. Bacteria live in mineral pores, drawing energy from salts and making trace nutrients available to roots. Lichens occupy shaded faces of towers, so slow-growing that their presence resembles a stain. The entire visible abundance rests on these hidden conversions.

Few creatures here carry water as a liquid reserve. They carry it chemically, structurally, or socially. It is bound in fat, locked in seed tissue, held inside fungal flesh, conserved in crowded burrows, reclaimed from breath, dung, eggshell, and prey. Eggs have thick shells or waxy coats. Young are born relatively mature, or remain protected in deep nurseries through their most vulnerable stages. Waste is dry, concentrated, and quickly claimed.

No inhabitant possesses the city, and none can comprehend its extent. A termite repairs a wall because air has changed across its antennae. A root enters a fissure because salts lie beyond it. A finch tears open a flower and knocks dust into a vent. A rodent enlarges the vent; an owl inherits it; its pellets feed beetles; beetle workings loosen the clay; wind fills the loosened surface with new dust. From these local acts arise avenues, districts, climates, and migrations.

Even death rarely produces emptiness. A dead mound tree becomes wet food by the standards of the plain. Insects enter beneath its bark within hours. Rodents harvest its seeds and fibers. Its collapsing roots open shafts into cooler ground. Dust settles into the cavities, fungi bind it, and seedlings establish themselves in the shade of the fallen trunk. The old tower slumps around them, not into ruin but into greater occupation.

The dominant life of the lowlands is therefore neither animal, plant, fungus, nor architecture. It is tenancy itself: the continuous passage of matter from chamber to body, body to refuse hollow, refuse to fungus, fungus to insect, insect to hunter, and hunter back to wall. Every creature lives inside the work of another and leaves behind a structure it cannot use alone.

Across the warm plain, this composite being has no center and no edge. It grows wherever dust catches, wherever a root can hold, wherever one protected breath adds moisture to a chamber. Its towers are organs built by strangers. Its streets are habits repeated until they become geography. Its inhabitants are builders without designs, gardeners without fields, and citizens of a place none of them knows exists.
