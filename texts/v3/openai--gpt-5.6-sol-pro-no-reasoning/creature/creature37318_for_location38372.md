The chamber is inhabited less by creatures than by continuities.

First there is the water, occupying every permitted interval without crossing it. It lives as skins, beads, menisci, and buried films: a body divided among countless boundaries. Each portion appears separate, yet all seem governed by one deeper pressure. Touch removes it only locally and briefly. Soon the wall shines again, the footprint fills from beneath, and the ceiling points recover their equal burdens. The water does not flow so much as remember its placement.

Within that distributed body grow the pallid threads. They emerge from warm cracks in fine twisted cords, translucent and almost rootless, ending in hard beads that might be fruit, mineral deposits, or sealed reproductive chambers. They consume whatever the seep brings through the stone and advance only as far as that nourishment remains exact. Removed into daylight, they do not survive the abundance of the outer world.

The black-green films are flatter and older-seeming. They cling where warmth, stone, and moisture meet in the proper proportions, making a velvet membrane over the rock. Their life is so slow that growth cannot be distinguished from staining. They may feed on dissolved minerals, on traces of gas rising from below, or on the decay of organisms too small to see. In the chamber they are supple; outside they briefly reveal their color, then contract into lifeless dust.

At the rear, the little palisade of colorless stalks stands in mud fed from beneath. Each stalk is jointed and rigid, crowded so tightly with its neighbors that the whole patch behaves like a single structure. Equal droplets cap their tips. Whether those drops are drawn up through the stalks or merely gathered from the saturated air is impossible to tell. Their stillness is not the stillness of dead reeds. It is the poise of something whose entire growth has taken place under one unchanging condition.

Beneath the water shelves cling the shell-bearers: nearly transparent animals in small mineral houses. Their flesh is soft, blind, and warm to the touch, though handling them would destroy the thin equilibrium around them. They graze the submerged films with mouths too small to see. A movement of one body-length may occupy days. Their trails vanish as quickly as they are made, leaving them indistinguishable again from pale grit stuck beneath the ledges. They live, perhaps, for a very long time; perhaps an entire generation passes without any individual leaving the shelf where it hatched.

The cave gnats are rarer. They lodge in ceiling creases with their wings folded and lacquered by condensation. They seem trapped, but are not always dead. Warmed by a nearby hand, one may stir a leg or loosen its wings with a minute shiver. Where their larvae feed is uncertain. They may breed in the dark felt, in the reed mud, or inside pores in the young rock. Their adult lives could consist of little more than emergence, mating, and becoming fixed beneath a drop.

Something larger may inhabit the deep fissures.

It is known chiefly by displacement: one soft stroke behind a wall, a pressure change against the ear, a brief alteration in the warmth issuing from a seam. The pale salamanders spoken of outside may be real, though “salamander” is likely only the nearest available name. If such animals live there, they must be slender enough to pass through water-filled cracks and patient enough to hunt prey that scarcely moves. Their skin would be unpigmented and finely vascular, their eyes buried or sealed. They may never enter the room itself. The chamber could be only the damp outer margin of their larger, inaccessible habitat.

Microbial life inhabits everything else: the warm water, the clay grooves, the porous ribs of pale mineral, the grain of the wooden pegs, the old marsh-fiber cords, perhaps even the water held inside the hammerstone’s microscopic fractures. It builds films and consumes them. It precipitates minerals around itself until the living colony resembles rock, then resumes at the new surface. The chamber’s unfinished architecture may partly be its work. Some columns and blisters may not merely shelter life but be the accumulated consequence of it.

The human presence survives differently. No person lives there now, but repeated gestures remain fitted into the room: a hand closing around the polished hammerstone, fingers scoring wet clay, bodies crawling toward the ring, a woven cup descending without touching the basin’s sides. The customs suggest people who did not worship the chamber so much as submit to its conditions. They understood that the water was to be taken once, that repetition mattered, and that the basin must not receive the disorder of skin, flame, washing, or poured remnants.

They may have believed the chamber copied what happened in it.

Perhaps a single drink could be absorbed into a life, while a second established a pattern the body would be compelled to repeat. Perhaps washing a wound there would preserve the wound’s condition rather than cure it. Perhaps flame, once introduced, would require fuel forever. Or perhaps the rules were practical observations hardened into custom: one dose healed, two sickened; contamination lingered; disturbance spread invisibly through the connected films.

Whatever their reason, the former visitors left little because leaving things was another form of repetition. The pegs, cup, ring, grooves, and hammerstone are not furnishings. They are points where human behavior briefly joined the chamber’s fixed arrangement.

Thus what lives there is a community without bustle: organisms almost mineral in pace, minerals still in the act of becoming, and water behaving like an organism whose body is the whole room. Predator and prey, growth and decay, seep and basin all persist so near equilibrium that their exchanges leave no visible history.

Nothing rules the chamber. Nothing needs to.

Its inhabitants survive by making almost no demands on time.
