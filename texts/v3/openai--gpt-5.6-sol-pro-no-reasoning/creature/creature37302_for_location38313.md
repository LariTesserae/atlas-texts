They call themselves, in most cities, simply the people. The word is not opposed to beast, spirit, stranger, or god. It means something closer to *those who must keep their shape*.

From a distance they look slight and dark against the pale breadth of the plateau. They travel in files because a group walking abreast may arrive at different times, each member following a subtly different width of country. Their clothes are layered wraps of grass cloth, felted bark, and thin sheets peeled from the inner surfaces of river plants. The outer layers are usually gray, peat-green, or undyed white. Color is kept underneath, close to the body, where it is less likely to be forgotten by the cloth.

They are recognizably human, though the plateau has altered their habits so deeply that posture itself has become a kind of local craft. They walk with the knees loose and the feet placed flat, testing not merely whether the ground will bear weight but whether it is agreeing, for the moment, about downward. Their hands are broad from stonework and root pulling. Their faces weather early. Cold reddens the eyelids and darkens the hollows beside the nose; fine pale lines spread across the skin like the lichen maps on exposed rock.

Many wear their hair long, braided with interval knots. The knots record home, kinship, journeys, unresolved obligations, and occasionally age, although years are not considered equally reliable everywhere. Children receive their first knot when they can give the same account of a dream on three consecutive mornings. The dead have their braids untied.

What happens to a human body after death is one of the plateau’s few obscenities. Plants soften into soil. Wood blackens. Cloth frays. Human flesh does none of these consistently. Left alone, a corpse may remain unchanged while its clothing rots away, or lose depth until it resembles a painted figure, or become warm and begin casting the shadow of someone still alive. Burial is therefore not abandonment but prolonged work.

The dead are washed in water from three sources and wrapped with packets of their household soil. Their name is spoken in diminishing forms, each repetition shorter than the last, until only the first sound remains. Then they are placed in open stone chambers beyond the city walls. For months or years, relatives visit to describe recent events, anchoring the dead to a completed life. Only when the body begins to obey ordinary decay is the chamber sealed. Some never do. These are called the Remaining, and their chambers become small civic burdens tended by families whose blood relationship may have ended centuries before.

No one agrees where the people came from.

The oldest songs begin after their arrival. They mention wet feet, a doorway without a wall, and a great noise that had no source on the plateau. In one city the first ancestors are said to have climbed from a soil seam into the cold. In another, they descended a stair and discovered that its top had vanished. Vel Astan teaches that humanity fell upward with the rain. Oru maintains a list of forty-seven original households, but the list gains and loses a household whenever the central plaza changes width.

Whatever their origin, they know themselves to be anomalous. Their bodies contain hard white structures for which the world provides no precedent. Teeth are called mouth-stones. The bones under their flesh are known only through injury and death, and are regarded with a mixture of shame and wonder: a hidden architecture, jointed and exact, of the sort the plateau ordinarily punishes.

No bone is made into a tool. To use it would be to ask the world to notice the resemblance between a skeleton and a machine.

Children are watched closely, though seldom confined. They tend to perceive the plateau’s discontinuities before they understand why adults fear them. A child will greet a delayed reflection, step over a line that has not yet appeared, or insist that a room has another corner concealed behind the existing ones. Such remarks are neither dismissed nor encouraged. An adult marks the place, asks the child to repeat what was seen, and returns with two witnesses.

Play consists largely of practicing invariance. Children clap patterns while moving around stones, exchange objects and reconstruct their histories, or hide while calling out their own names so that voice and body remain associated. A common game uses loops of root cord laid on the ground. Each player must enter and leave a loop without changing the number of people inside it. In some districts this is difficult enough to occupy an entire afternoon.

Twins are treated cautiously. They are not unlucky, but places can mistake one for the other. Their clothing is made deliberately dissimilar, and they are rarely permitted to sleep under the same roof before adolescence. Twins who remain distinguishable into adulthood are believed to have exceptional integrity and often become gatekeepers, judges, or witnesses to contracts.

Names are flexible protections rather than permanent labels. A person has a hearth-name, a road-name, and a name used only in solitude. The hearth-name connects them to soil, household, and city. The road-name describes observable features and may change after any long journey: Carries-Red-Cloth, Left-Hand-Covered, Walks-After-Rain. The solitary name is never spoken to another person. Its purpose is to give the self somewhere to retreat if every public description becomes false.

Identity is established collectively. Each household keeps a low room called the remembering place. Its walls hold spaced marks, knotted cords, worn tools, patches of fabric, handprints, and shallow dishes of local earth. None is trusted alone. Together they form a cloud of evidence dense enough to resist casual revision.

Portraits are avoided. A likeness can outlast its subject too aggressively. Instead, families preserve sequences of gestures: the way a mother folds a sleeping mat, the particular hesitation before a brother laughs, the movement by which a grandparent tests the wind. To remember someone is to perform them briefly. Mourning gatherings can therefore seem, to an outsider, full of imitation and sudden laughter.

Households are seldom based only on parentage. They include those who share a roof, a reservoir allotment, a migrating grove, a kiln, or an obligation inherited from an impossible event. A person who returns from travel with uncertain memories may be adopted provisionally by several houses until one set of relationships becomes stable. Belonging is considered something repeatedly done, not something conclusively possessed.

Their hunger is modest but constant. Without animals, their food lacks many textures humans elsewhere might expect. Meals are soft, fibrous, mineral, smoky, and sour. River leaves are pounded into dark paste. Tubers are baked for days beneath peat embers. Lichen is washed in successive waters to remove bitterness, then pressed into thin cakes. Subterranean fungi are cut along their layers and roasted on hot stone; some have flesh dense enough to carve, though no one compares it to meat.

Salt is precious where it can be found. Certain cities mine blue-gray deposits from rooms inside boulders, but the chambers are unreliable and sometimes contain the tools of miners who have not yet entered. Salt-cutters work in silence, take less than they can carry, and never turn around inside the stone.

People eat in circles with an intentional gap. A fully closed ring is avoided for the same reason walls are left incomplete: enclosure invites substitution. Before a meal, the eldest present touches the floor and names the city. Afterward, the youngest counts the bowls. If there is an extra bowl, it is filled and left where it stands. If one is missing, no one speaks the name of the absent diner until dawn.

Their settlements are governed by keepers rather than rulers. Authority belongs to those who maintain continuities: reservoir keepers, boundary walkers, shadow readers, soil stewards, stair witnesses, and counters of doors. A council may debate planting, construction, trade, or punishment, but if the keeper responsible for a local discontinuity says conditions are wrong, work stops.

This does not make their societies gentle. Continuity can become an instrument of exclusion. A household may refuse to corroborate one of its members. A gatekeeper may decide that a traveler’s three origins agree only in wording, not substance. A city may declare that a troublesome district was never fully inside its walls. Exile is rarely pronounced directly. Instead, a person’s place in communal recitations is shortened, then omitted. Their storage niche is reassigned. Their handprint is allowed to weather away.

The harshest punishment is not expulsion but singularity: the community ceases to confirm the condemned person’s memories. Few survive it intact.

There are wanderers nonetheless. Some are traders, surveyors, messengers, grove-followers, or professional witnesses. Others have become too uncertain for settled life. These latter travel with poles hung with small objects, each representing an event they still believe occurred. They may stop strangers and ask for recognition of a city, a family, or a weather pattern. Payment is offered for honest confirmation, never for comfort.

Travelers sleep within a ring of home soil scattered loosely enough not to constitute an enclosure. Their bedrolls are narrow. Their packs contain food, cord, spare footcloth, a covered mirror, three differently shaped cups, and a return object: something plain and unmistakable that has never left home before. On waking, the traveler uncovers the mirror only after touching the return object and naming the direction of the nearest city.

Mirrors are made from hammered copper or polished black stone. Their purpose is diagnostic, not decorative. A healthy reflection follows without eagerness. One that anticipates motion is covered and taken to a specialist. A reflection that lags is less dangerous; it may simply have become entangled with Mere’s delayed shadows or passed too near an old stair.

Those who tend reflections are among the closest things the plateau has to physicians. Illness is divided into bodily sickness and disagreement.

Bodily sickness includes fever, swelling, cough, numbness, wasting, and the white ache that enters joints during long wet cold. Treatment uses heated stones, bitter plant infusions, fungal compresses, stretching, cautery, and rest beside particular kinds of soil. Surgery is rare but not unknown. Surgeons work with obsidian flakes and ceramic clamps, and assistants continuously describe the patient aloud so that an opened body does not become mistaken for a room.

Disagreement occurs when some part of a person ceases to share the same conditions as the rest. A hand may remain cold in firelight. A sleeper’s breathing may be heard from an empty adjoining room. Someone may arrive before their own footprints. These afflictions are treated socially first. Friends repeat shared memories, imitate familiar gestures, and place objects of known history around the patient. If that fails, the person is carried to neutral ground outside the city, where fewer structures reinforce the error.

Their religions, if they can be called religions, contain few personalities. The plateau provides no animals from which to imagine composite gods, no cries in the night to populate with spirits, no swarming life to suggest invisible multitudes. What they revere are conditions: Continuance, Opening, Return, the Unfinished Edge, the Kindly Interval.

They do not worship the discontinuities. Worship would imply intention, and intention is regarded as a dangerous courtesy to extend toward them. Instead they practice etiquette. They leave doors ajar. They vary repeated phrases. They avoid thanking rain until it has ended. They never say that a bridge has always been safe.

The nearest thing to a sacred symbol is an incomplete line.

It appears on lintels, clothing hems, grave chambers, water jars, and the foreheads of infants during naming. It means that the thing marked is coherent but not closed; itself, but not trapped inside a perfect definition. The eastern city has elevated this principle into civic beauty. Its finest buildings are sweeping stone spirals with gaps positioned so that wind crosses every chamber. From above—if above could be trusted—the city might resemble a hand that has chosen not to clench.

Music is made sparingly. Sound travels too far, returns too late, and occasionally acquires another speaker. Their instruments are therefore quiet: flat drums tapped with fingertips, reedless pipes that breathe rather than whistle, strings stretched across shallow wooden frames, and stones rubbed in bowls of water. Songs are antiphonal, with long silences between phrases. The silence is considered part of the melody and must be accurately kept.

Large choirs are uncommon. Once, according to a Mere account, three hundred singers sustained the same note until the plateau answered from below. The response continued for nineteen years.

Most singing is practical. There are songs for setting walls, songs whose rhythm spaces written numbers safely, songs for crossing fog trenches, songs that hold the stages of fungal cultivation, and songs that preserve routes. A route-song changes mode at each landmark. If the expected landmark does not appear, the traveler does not alter the song. They stop and wait for the country to rejoin it.

Art favors sequence over image. Cloths are woven with patterns that cannot be seen from a single angle. Stone carvings record the progress of a hand rather than the outline of an object. Public walls carry rows of shallow hollows, polished by generations repeating the same gestures. In Oru, dancers perform across the widening evening plaza. Their choreography contains pauses during which the dancers are too far apart to see one another, yet must continue from memory and arrive together at the final figure.

The people possess imagination, but they distrust invention detached from witness. Stories are introduced with careful gradations: *I saw; my mother saw; this was sung before the western reservoir; this is told only because it may become true.*

Their strangest stories concern animals.

The shapes that life refused differ from dreamer to dreamer, yet certain forms recur. A small quick thing that runs on four jointed limbs and watches from the edge of firelight. A soft-bodied presence that lives in houses and chooses particular humans. A vast grass-eater carrying branches from its head. Bright weightless beings that enter the sky by beating paired leaves.

People wake from these dreams devastated by a longing for something they cannot categorize. They make no carvings of the forms, but children sometimes model them secretly from river clay. The results are usually destroyed before firing. Not because they are forbidden—there is almost no law concerning them—but because the sight of a body shaped for running, flying, or swimming produces a horror deeper than ugliness. It suggests that flesh might belong here naturally.

A few become seekers of refused life. They walk the remote mosslands looking for tracks no one has made, listen beside soil fissures, or lower baskets of food into the warm daylight below. Nothing takes the offerings. When the baskets are raised, the food may be fresh, ancient, or replaced with unfamiliar seeds.

The seekers insist that absence is not emptiness. They say it is pressure: the outline left by an enormous exclusion.

This belief troubles many people because human beings may be part of the same exclusion. The plateau sustains them, but never confirms them. It grows no food shaped for their mouths. It offers no other warm body, no precedent for blood, bone, birth, or decay. Humanity survives by making small zones of mutual evidence in a land that neither attacks nor acknowledges it.

Birth is therefore celebrated with relief rather than wonder. During labor, doors are opened and all unnecessary objects removed from the room. Too many things present can confuse the count. The laboring parent is addressed continuously by hearth-name. When the child emerges, it is placed first on bare soil, then lifted by three people in turn. No one asks whether it is healthy until it has cried, breathed, or otherwise demonstrated succession.

Infants sometimes look toward empty corners and move their mouths as if imitating speech. This is treated as ordinary. The world is large, adults say, and a new person has not yet learned which parts of it are absent.

Old age is respected because it is difficult to accumulate. Elders are not valued chiefly as repositories of facts—facts can shift—but as examples of sustained personhood. An old woman who has used the same morning gesture for eighty years is a civic landmark. An old man whose road-name has survived seven journeys may be asked to stand beside a disputed boundary. Their presence does not prove what is true. It encourages truth to remain where it is.

Above all, the inhabitants are maintainers.

They trim migrating roots and replant the severed lengths. They scrape frost from the insides of jars. They rebuild stairs whose lower steps now emerge in distant basins. They place poles to read tomorrow’s shadows. They walk the margins of squares at dusk and note how many paces the night has added. They teach children the names of plants, stones, clouds, tools, kin, and directions—not because names master the world, but because naming is one of the quiet labors by which a place can be persuaded to continue.

Each morning, watchers climb the highest built structures. Nothing natural rises far enough for the task. They face outward and compare the horizon to cords knotted the day before.

The horizon is always farther.

They do not know whether the plateau is expanding, thinning, aging, remembering, or approaching some final flatness in which every distant place will become visible at once. They record the change without dense numbers. One knot for what can still be walked. One for what can only be seen. One for cities appearing above other cities. One for lights below the soil. One for the pale moving roads.

Then the watchers descend. Kilns are opened. Water is measured. Meals are divided around their customary gap. Travelers answer the gate questions. Masons return to walls they will not complete.

Across the moss country, the solitary stones hold the colorless sky. No wing crosses it. No hidden creature stirs in the grass.

Only the people move—carefully, provisionally, together—keeping one another real.
