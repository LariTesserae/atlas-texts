They are a people of pauses.

A stranger notices first how often they stand motionless: at pressure doors while the seals decide whether they are sound, beside road cuts with one hand against the stone, on platforms long after a train has become visible. They do not spend movement casually. In thin air, every exertion declares itself; in dust, every hinge remembers use. Children are taught not to fidget, not as a discipline of obedience but as the beginning of competence. Before crossing a room, one considers what else might be carried. Before opening a housing, one listens through it.

They are tall only by the standards of older records. The plateau has made them narrow through the chest and broad through the lower ribs, where supplemental breathing membranes and implanted gas-exchange tissues sit beneath the skin. Their blood is dark, nearly plum-colored when drawn, dense with metallic carriers suited to an atmosphere that offers little. Most have long hands, toughened feet, and a slight forward inclination acquired from walking into the persistent ground-hugging dust. Exposed skin is rubbed with conductive powder so charge will leave the body gradually rather than leap from a fingertip into a valve or another person.

Their clothing is layered, close-fitting, and nearly soundless: pressure cloth, ceramic scales over joints, veils for grit. Bright color is uncommon because dyes are chemically expensive, but seams and fasteners carry district colors—oxide red, sulfur yellow, refinery black, the cloudy amber of salvaged coolant. The richest ornament is not brilliance but stability. An heirloom bearing still turning after six generations is worn at the throat. A perfectly sealed watch case may be carried though the watch inside no longer keeps any recognized time.

They do not speak loudly. Raised voices waste breath and flatten meaning in air that already blurs sound. Conversation happens at close range, with pauses long enough that outsiders mistake deliberation for refusal. Much is conveyed by hand signs laid against tables, walls, sleeves, or the other person’s palm. In factories and pressure tunnels, people communicate by tapped rhythms that travel through material better than words travel through air.

Their names are spoken, tapped, and recorded as vibration profiles. A person may have one name for ordinary rooms and another for bedrock: a short sequence of intervals by which family can recognize them during evacuation. The dead are remembered this way. Memorial floors contain buried drivers that reproduce their identifying cadence once each year. Whole neighborhoods wake before dawn to feel the departed pass softly through the foundations.

Death does not return anyone to soil. There is no soil in that sense, and decay is unreliable. Bodies are separated into useful chemistries with extraordinary care. Metals and salts go back to the civic stores; complex organics are sealed into dark vitreous tiles. These are set into interior walls where temperature and pressure are steady. A house may therefore contain its ancestry physically, not in portraits but in faintly translucent panels that hold bubbles, mineral threads, and occasional shadows like folded limbs.

Wastefulness is their nearest equivalent to obscenity. This does not make them austere. They delight in intricacy, repair, and transformations so gradual that the pleasure lies in attention. Their gardens are trays of crystallizing salts warmed from beneath, coaxed over years into branching mineral forms. Their perfumers compose scents from hot stone, ozone, machine oil, sulfur, and the rare sweet volatiles secreted by cliff fauna. Their cooks work with cultivated films, pressure-grown tissues, fungal analogues imported long ago, and animals whose bodies are only provisionally animal. Meals are dense, dry, and small. Sauces are gels or warm oils; steam has no place in their imagination of comfort.

To them, water is not merely absent but conceptually grotesque: a restless mineral solvent that falls out of the sky, fills hollows, enters porous things, and carries whole landscapes downhill. Ancient descriptions of oceans are taught beside accounts of magma planets and corrosive atmospheres. Children ask why coastal cities were built where the ground could move in waves. They are fascinated by the idea that people once crossed enormous surfaces with nothing beneath them but liquid.

Their own abyss does not trouble them in the same way. The Undersky is not a place one might fall into; it is where place ceases. They call a person reckless not “groundless” but “low-minded”—someone who mistakes depth for destination. Religious and philosophical traditions differ over whether the haze conceals a lower world, an unfinished one, or no world at all. None encourages descent. Even heretics travel laterally.

Their maps resemble genealogies. Habitable heights are nodes joined by routes, sight lines, pressure relays, and remembered crossings. Distance is measured less in kilometers than in bearing cycles, seal changes, lubricant allotments, and the number of safe rests between compressors. A neighboring summit may be geographically close yet socially remote if the intervening air is turbulent. A mesa three hundred kilometers away can be considered adjacent if the freight rail has run without interruption for generations.

They govern through maintenance.

Public office is held by custodians of systems: air, pressure, load, heat, charge, motion, and fauna. Laws are written as operating tolerances. A district is not declared unsafe; its permissible occupancy declines as seals age. A bridge organism is not owned, because ownership implies permission to exhaust a thing. Instead, families and guilds hold obligations toward portions of its shell. Taxes are assessed in inspection hours, replacement parts, stored gases, and guaranteed stillness.

Political arguments can last decades because decisions are expected to outlive those who make them. This slowness is not always wisdom. Harm can be preserved as efficiently as anything else. A dangerous factory may remain active because its closure sequence requires thirty years. A family denied access to a pressure tunnel may stay isolated for generations while review proceeds. The city’s patience has grandeur, but also cruelty: it knows how to wait until the injured are dead.

Surveyors possess unusual authority. They walk with sensor staffs, resonant hammers, and narrow cases of pigments. They decide whether a line has moved, whether a wall has begun to carry weight differently, whether something categorized as terrain has crossed the threshold into life. They repaint roads not where they were intended to be, but where the ground now permits them. The best surveyors are said to have “long hearing.” Kneeling on bare stone, they can distinguish a compressor fault from an approaching herd, a thermal crack from the redistribution of a sleeping bridge-creature far below.

Children often follow them.

Plateau children are grave in public and ferocious in private. They race maintenance sleds through abandoned ducts, collect cast-off mite shells, and dare one another to sleep atop warm seams where the bedrock pulses irregularly. They build small machines designed to perform one motion only once: unfold a flag after seven months, crawl the length of a room over a year, strike a bell on the builder’s next birthday. Adults pretend to disapprove, then help improve the bearings.

They are warned never to harm an animal whose function is unknown.

This is less compassion than historical terror. Too many structures depend on creatures first mistaken for obstruction, mineral deposit, leakage, corrosion, or trash. A pale film inside a pressure regulator may be feeding on contamination—or producing the sealant that keeps the regulator closed. A colony beneath a road may undermine it—or distribute loads better than any engineered foundation. Municipal biology is therefore a practice of negotiated ignorance.

“Load-bearing fauna” has become a phrase applied to people as well: those on whom a household, district, or institution has settled so gradually that no one can say when support became dependence. It is praise with an edge. Such people are protected, consulted, and rarely permitted to leave.

Animals inhabit the citizens’ stories without becoming symbols in quite the old way. The sailback does not mean freedom; it means sensitivity without flight. The vaulting herds represent commitment, because after stepping from the cliff they cannot reverse direction for hours. Conduit mites signify theft, symbiosis, or municipal accounting, depending on who tells the tale. Lovers compare one another to pressure thieves when they become difficult to breathe around. A dependable companion is called a warm stratum.

Some citizens tend creatures deliberately. Roofkeepers maintain heated stone shelves where sailbacks gather during charged weather. Retired electrical workers cultivate ceramic mites in isolated cabinets, breeding colonies whose branching tracks resemble calligraphy. Cliff monasteries—or observatories, for the distinction is unclear—watch the armored strata-feeders across lifetimes. Each generation adds one line to enormous diagrams of the feeding scars. No individual sees a circle completed.

There are people who live inside the dormant factories.

Officially they are salvage custodians, gasket wardens, power auditors. In practice many are solitaries, fugitives, mystics, or those who prefer the company of residual systems. They sleep in former control rooms under red colonies of conduit feeders. They learn which dead machines warm before the ring alignment and which relays click in response to footsteps. Some insist the factories are not awakening but dreaming procedures they no longer have the parts to perform.

The city tolerates them because they notice changes.

One may arrive at a district office after thirty years of silence to report that a sealed retort has begun counting. Another brings a strip of amber insulation cut from a wall, within which tiny forms have arranged themselves like writing. Their testimony is entered into civic record without endorsement or dismissal. On the plateau, an impossibility may simply be an event with an interval longer than the observer’s career.

Those nearest the edges are different from inland citizens. Cliff dwellers use tethered garments and carry buoyancy bladders modeled after the herds. They sleep in chambers carved deep enough that the wall behind them remains warm. Their sense of orientation includes empty space; they can work for hours with the Undersky opening beside one shoulder. They speak of lower peaks as if they were acquaintances seen through illness.

A few claim to have watched lights moving beneath the haze.

Such reports are not prohibited. They are assigned coordinates, atmospheric conditions, observer health, and confidence grades. No expedition is sent down.

The Still Migration has its own keepers. They occupy low stations at the edge of the white plain and never approach the forms except along fixed surveyed paths. Their work is mostly waiting: recording thermal variations, shadows, compass deviations, and the direction in which each dark figure seems to be looking. They reject the city’s habit of naming individuals among the forms, arguing that names create expectations and expectations distort measurement. Nevertheless, privately, every keeper has one form they watch more closely than the others.

The warm ones are not always the ones that move.

There are rumors that some are citizens who walked onto the salt and became dark at a distance. There are older records of forms standing where current maps show empty plain. There is at least one authenticated image in which a figure appears to face east. No corresponding figure has ever been found.

The nineteen-year alignment organizes lives more strongly than birthdays. People speak of having seen three stripings, or being born between the sixth and seventh shadow. Contracts mature by alignments. Apprenticeships end at one. Long marriages may be renewed at another. Those unlikely to survive to the next prepare roof garments and observation masks for children not yet born.

As the season approaches, work changes tempo. Exterior repairs are completed. Loose objects are fastened. Buildings reduce their pulse as if listening. People who have quarreled sometimes reconcile—not from sentiment, they say, but because the contraction should be felt without unresolved vibration in the household.

Then the animals come.

No citizen can fully explain why this abundance inspires reverence rather than alarm. Roofs disappear under translucent sails. Trenches whiten with ribbons. Great buoyant bodies hover among the sky mills, so close that their shadows pass over windows. For several weeks the city ceases to look like an occupation imposed on dead stone. It looks briefly submerged in a life that had been present all along, dispersed below the threshold of notice.

People stand among it quietly.

When the plateau contracts, they bend their knees. This is practical—the pulse can throw an unprepared person down—but it is also the nearest thing the city has to universal prayer. Palms touch roofs, floors, bridge shells, exposed bedrock. For that instant everyone feels the same event through a different structure.

Far west, the Migration advances.

Afterward, disputes begin over whether the step was longer than last time, whether all forms moved equally, whether the plateau’s contraction preceded them or answered them. Surveyors depart with pigments and instruments. Children search for new tracks. Factories restart one pressure stage at a time.

And beneath the city, the great load-bearing bodies adjust themselves by increments too small to witness.

The citizens build for them anyway. They set flexible joints in towers, slack in cables, margins in laws. They leave empty ground around everything important. Their finest structures do not resist change; they admit it slowly, plate by plate, seal by seal, generation by generation.

They have no word exactly equivalent to permanence.

Their closest word means: **still here when measured again.**
