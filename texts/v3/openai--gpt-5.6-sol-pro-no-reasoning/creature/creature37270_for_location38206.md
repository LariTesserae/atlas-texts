The people of the drowned saddle are long-limbed, broad-handed, and difficult to distinguish from their gardens when they are still.

Their skin has the muted translucence of a root held against light. Beneath it, blood moves darkly—violet in the extremities, almost black around the ribs—and fine branching vessels show at the throat, wrists, and temples. Color varies by lineage and diet: kelp-brown, deep green, chalk-gray, the blue-white of submerged bone. Children are clearer-skinned than adults. Age brings opacity. Mineral traces accumulate in old scars until they shine like pale seams in stone.

They have narrow waists and deep, flexible chests. Folds along the ribs and beneath the jaw draw breath from the water, opening and closing with a motion like leaves stirring. They also possess small lungs. These are of little use in ordinary life, but allow them to hold pockets of air for delicate work, regulate buoyancy, and survive briefly in fouled or stagnant water. Their voices begin in those lungs and are shaped in resonant chambers of the throat. Close speech is soft and percussive; a sentence can be felt through a shared wall or a hand laid against another person’s sternum.

Their eyes are large but not enormous, protected by clear inner lids. In daylight the pupils contract to thin crescents. At night they open until the iris nearly disappears, catching the glow of animal lamps. Most cannot see sharply across great distances. They understand far water through movement, pressure, sound, and the tremor of lines. A citizen might fail to recognize a face across a plaza yet identify a neighbor several streets away by the rhythm of their pruning knife.

Their ears are no more than sealed hollows at the sides of the skull, but the jaw and collarbones carry vibration inward. Knotted cords, taut stems, pillars, and one another’s bodies all become extensions of hearing. During formal conversation, people often rest fingertips together or hold the same length of vine. Intimacy is not merely closeness but shared resonance.

Hands are central to their appearance and culture. The fingers are long, with a slight webbing that can fold back against the knuckles. The nails are dark, thick, and ridged, useful for scraping stems clean before grafting. The feet are similarly broad and prehensile. They do not swim everywhere. Within the city they move by grasping, pushing, drifting, and catching themselves—a calm succession of contacts that makes an experienced adult appear to be falling with perfect control.

Clothing is sparse but elaborate in structure. Long membranes wrapped tightly around the torso preserve warmth without snagging. Over these they wear lattices of cord, shell plaques, tool loops, seed cases, and little ballast weights that can be shifted to change posture. Sleeves and skirts are uncommon unless split into narrow streamers. Nothing broad is allowed to trail where it might catch a current or brush a delicate graft.

Rank is not shown by abundance. It is shown by maintenance. A respected cultivator may wear one immaculate living clasp, coaxed for years into a spiral around the shoulder. A line keeper knots the history of repairs into the cords at their waist. Those who tend the warm seeps stain their hands rust-red with mineral deposits. Children wear bright bladders until they learn to control their buoyancy; the youngest bob above family groups on short tethers like fruit.

They are born live, usually one at a time, in warm interior chambers. Infants cannot yet manage the cold or the currents. For their first years they are carried in ventral slings against a parent’s ferment-warmed body, passed freely among relatives and neighbors. Parenthood belongs first to those who keep a child alive, and only second to those whose flesh produced it. Households are grafts like buildings: blood kin, apprentices, widowed elders, migrants, and companions joined gradually until separating them would injure everyone.

No household is entirely self-sufficient. One tends a patch of vessel gourds; another breeds wall-binding moss; another knows the temper of grazing animals. Food, labor, and warmth circulate through obligations remembered over generations. Debts are spoken of as currents. A favor should not be forced directly back toward its source. It is expected to travel onward and return altered.

Their food is mostly cultivated tissue: tender vine tips, dense root curds, seed pearls, fermented saps, and sheets of algae folded around shellfish meat. Herd grazers provide eggs, milk-like secretions, and occasionally flesh. Hunting is practiced, but killing a large free animal without communal cause is regarded as both wasteful and presumptuous. Meals are taken from covered bowls or bitten directly from tethered bundles. Flavor is subtle in cold water, so cooks prize texture, mineral salinity, fermentation, and the sudden heat of certain defensive plants.

Fire is known to them as a surface phenomenon, half natural fact and half nightmare. Their equivalent is fermentation: controlled rot, warmth coaxed from dissolution. Every mature house has a keeper of the core, someone skilled at feeding bacterial mats without letting them sour, poison the water, or consume their chamber walls. The role is humble in title and powerful in practice. In the coldest months, a careless core keeper can empty a house.

They organize themselves around tending rather than territory. The city has councils, but few permanent rulers. Garden keepers, current readers, animal wardens, graft surgeons, line drummers, seep stewards, and memory cutters each hold authority within a particular problem. A person who commands during a wall breach may have no special voice in deciding a marriage, a migration path, or the pruning of a public roof.

Council chambers are open beneath and above. Speakers arrange themselves around a central cord, changing height to indicate the kind of statement they are making: near the roots for testimony, level with others for proposal, above the circle for warning. Anyone claiming certainty must take hold of the cord with both hands, surrendering gesture and making their tension perceptible to all.

Their law is concerned less with possession than with interruption. Crimes include choking a public current, concealing disease in a garden, cutting a message line, overharvesting a regenerative bed, fastening an unsafe tether above another household, or frightening animals into destructive routes. Punishment aims to restore flow. Offenders may be required to open blocked channels, tend what they damaged, surrender access to scarce warmth, or work at the city’s outer gardens where the blue drop is always visible.

Exile is rare and terrible. To be sent beyond the marker ropes is not simply to lose shelter. It is to lose the maintained world: the lines by which warnings travel, the familiar stems that carry voices, the animals that know where not to bite, the inherited map of warm crevices and stable holds. Their oldest curse is not “May you die,” but “May nothing answer your touch.”

Most citizens are cultivators in some degree. Even a singer or historian knows how to make a clean graft, recognize rot, and prune without provoking defensive sap. Children learn species before numbers. Their earliest games involve directing schools of tiny fish through loops or passing rhythmic messages down branching cords. Adolescents spend a season in the wandering gardens beyond the last houses, learning the distinction between growth that merely lives and growth that can coexist.

Master builders are patient to a degree outsiders might mistake for passivity. They design for descendants they will never meet. Their plans are living diagrams—small model gardens trained in baskets, each shoot corresponding to a future wall, chamber, or support. If the model sickens, the proposed building is abandoned. If it thrives too aggressively, the design is revised. A structure is considered successful not when it is complete, but when it can be neglected for a generation without becoming dangerous.

The most honored specialists are graft surgeons. They work on buildings, crops, animals, and people, seeing no absolute boundary among them. With obsidian blades and fish-spine needles they close torn gills, join vascular cords, transplant luminous tissue, and remove parasitic growth from the Anchor. Their bodies are patterned with deliberate scars from training. Each scar records a cut learned first upon oneself.

The people do not write on loose surfaces. Records must survive water, grazing mouths, and slow decay. Brief messages are tied into cord or bitten as notch patterns into shell. Longer histories are trained into plants: branching sequences, scar bands, mineral deposits, and recurring clusters of colored nodules. Reading such a record requires touch, sight, and memory. No inscription stands alone. A historian must know which changes were intentional and which came from disease, current, or age.

The Anchor’s inner walls hold their deepest chronology, but it is not treated as a simple account of the past. Each generation adds marks while the plant thickens beneath them, distorting intervals and drawing some events apart while pressing others together. History is therefore understood as something alive enough to alter its own proportions. Dates matter less than relationships: which famine preceded which migration, which collapsed garden led to which treaty, which family first heard a change in the mountain’s groaning.

They believe the Anchor listens, though not necessarily as a person listens. Its columnar trunk receives every district’s vibrations. Festival drumming enters its tissues and can be felt far above in the floating crown. During crisis, the city falls quiet and waits with hands against its inner walls. Minute shifts in tension—the opening of a vascular passage, the drag of a root, the knock of one buried column against another—are interpreted by specialists called deep hearers.

Some say these messages are the plant’s counsel. Others say the Anchor merely reveals what the mountain and currents are doing. The distinction is not considered urgent. A thing need not think like a person to be worthy of attention.

Religion consists largely of correct participation. There are few prayers asking the world to change its course. Ceremonies instead align bodies with processes already underway: migrations, pruning seasons, spawning, sediment fall, the waking of luminous vines. Worshippers gather along tension lines and repeat old pulse patterns until their drumming becomes difficult to separate from the creaking city.

Silence has ritual force. After a death, a household stops making intentional sound for one full turning of daylight. They still work, eat, and tend the core, but communicate only by touch. In that quiet the ordinary voices of the place become immense: rasping grazers, distant stone, sap moving through walls, the pulse of neighbors through shared roots. The dead person is said to be “widening” during this interval, ceasing to answer from one body and beginning to answer from many smaller things.

When the body is taken to the outer gardens, mourners do not follow it all the way. The carriers continue alone beyond the last cultivated arch. There they secure the seed cloth lightly enough that scavengers can enter and roots can find it. Months or years later, relatives may visit the garden, but they do not try to identify which shoot contains which part of the dead. To demand such certainty would be to misunderstand the gift.

They fear enclosure more than darkness. Sealed rooms, blind pockets, clogged channels, and structures that do not flex all carry the suggestion of suffocation. Even private chambers have multiple apertures. Doors contract but never close completely. Children who hide inside root hollows are taught to keep one foot or hand in moving water.

The open ocean inspires a different fear: not confinement, but unanswerable vastness. Those who work at the edge sometimes experience a condition called loosening. They become unable to tolerate walls or tether lines. They drift farther each day, listen obsessively to distant animal songs, and speak of the city as a knot pulled too tight. Some recover through warmth and constant touch. Some leave. Occasionally, years later, one returns changed—scarred, carrying an unknown seed, accompanied by an animal no one has seen, or unable to use the city’s rhythms correctly anymore.

Such returners are not rejected, but they are approached cautiously. The ocean beyond the mountain contains other summits, trenches, drifting forests, and perhaps other cities. Stories arrive in fragments: settlements built inside the rib cages of dead leviathans; people who travel beneath living bells; forests that never touch stone; warm black plains where water wells upward from cracks and metal grows in brittle towers. Whether all these accounts are true matters less than the fact that the city is not presumed to be the center of anything.

The inhabitants’ greatest collective labor is keeping the saddle habitable. They trim canopies so daylight reaches the lower beds. They guide sediment from threatened terraces. They redirect herds, mend migration gaps, watch for strangling species, and loosen structures before growth turns them rigid. Much of their work resembles undoing: cutting what was once carefully planted, opening what ancestors enclosed, allowing a cherished chamber to collapse because it now obstructs a current.

This is not regarded as failure. Permanence would mean death.

Their festivals are therefore often acts of relinquishment. An old bridge may be untied and allowed to rise into the canopy, trailing decades of knots. A district may cut open a ceremonial wall so a new animal migration can pass. Families harvest the final fruit from a dying communal vine, then gather to sever its roots. The released body climbs slowly into the blue until it vanishes, accompanied by drumming that grows softer not because the players stop, but because the plant carries the sound away.

Their humor is tactile, patient, and sometimes cruel in the affectionate manner of people who live amid constant risk. They tie harmless false warnings into one another’s tool cords. They imitate the solemn pulses of council speakers on empty gourds. Youngsters hide buoyant bladders beneath an elder’s sleeping sling, causing them to wake pressed indignantly against the ceiling canopy. There are comic songs composed entirely of badly executed grafting noises.

At rest, they gather in loose clusters. One person anchors a foot in a wall loop; others hook arms or grip belts, the whole group slowly revolving in the current. Work passes hand to hand. Stories pass through touches and taps. Small fish shelter among their limbs. No one remains perfectly still.

Seen from beyond the marker ropes, they are scarcely visible at first. A face appears behind a membrane window and is gone. A gardener unfolds from the side of a tower. Children detach from a floating crown and descend in bright spirals, tugging their safety lines. A procession crosses an open court at several heights, carrying shell tools and a bundle of living cuttings. Pale hands move over dark leaves. Throats pulse. Weighted cords swing beneath narrow bodies.

Then a district drum sounds.

The nearest citizens pause and answer against walls, rails, tools, and one another’s shoulders. The message enters the vegetation. It passes into root, tower, bridge, and street. Other chambers take it up until the whole saddle seems to speak—not with one voice, but with hundreds joined by the living structure between them.

That structure is what truly lives there: people, plants, animals, stone, cultivated warmth, inherited vibration, and the cold water moving through all of them. The inhabitants do not imagine themselves as creatures enclosed by a city. They are one of the city’s mobile tissues, brief and necessary, tending the knot for as long as their hands can hold it.
