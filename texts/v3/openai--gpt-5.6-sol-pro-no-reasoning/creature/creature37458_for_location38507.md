The Rookery is inhabited by beginnings.

They wear the shapes of animals because animals are the most intricate things the chamber knows how to almost make. It can produce the soot-dark curve of a swift, the lid of a cave finch drawn sideways over a pearl eye, the minute architecture of a moth’s scales. It can assemble claws that clasp, throats that tighten, lungs full of thin air. What it cannot produce is the next condition. No hatchling becomes a bird; no bird becomes old. There are no eggs unless the black revolving specks inside the droplets are eggs, and they cannot decide whether they have been laid.

The birds are therefore less a flock than a single crowded proposal.

Each carries one syllable of it. A whistle belongs to a narrow white bird wedged behind the oxygen gauge. A concussive coo comes from something pigeon-shaped inside a pipe too thin to contain it. Metallic shrieks issue from the swifts on the cable trays, though their delicate mouths seem incapable of such weight. None sings to another. None listens. Taken together, however, they make the voice of the room: one sustained announcement that has no meaning until every creature is included.

Their bodies are warm in the way the machinery is warm—not by possessing heat, but by enacting it. A bird lifted from a pipe feels alive against the palm. Its breast flutters. Its heart delivers a rapid, terrified beat. Yet it has no weight that can be transferred, no warmth that remains in the hand, and no position other than the one from which it was taken. The fingers close around it; the fingers open; the bird is still clinging to the pipe, and also, for a moment, seems to remain in the hand. Its claws leave small white dents in a glove. The dents vanish before they can be counted.

The salamanders are gentler versions of the same impossibility. They embrace the valve stems with blind, almost affectionate patience, their colorless toes spread against brass. Fluid moves visibly beneath their skin. One can trace it from the throat to the tail and back again, though no pulse completes the journey. Their mouths hold the beginnings of breaths. Their eyes have not yet acquired pigment, or have long since surrendered it; the distinction cannot be made here.

The silver mites on the condenser fins may be animals, frost, or fasteners. Each has jointed legs and a tool-like head. Under a lens they seem busy: scraping, sorting, passing bright particles from claw to claw. But the same particle belongs to every claw in the sequence. The labor has no result. Their colonies form exact geometric fields interrupted only by the black-green films in the screw heads, which they neither consume nor cross.

The eels below the grating are the nearest thing the Rookery has to a foundation. Their bodies are braided through the reservoir so densely that the water may merely fill the narrow shapes between them. They gleam when the amber lamps flicker. Mouth opens into tail, tail passes beneath belly, belly vanishes under another mouth. Whether there are ten eels or ten million depends on where one begins counting, and no beginning remains available long enough.

Above them hang the six-footed mammals. Their thin bodies show shadows of ribs and folded organs. They resemble bats remembered by a machine that had only been told about hands. Every foot grips a separate wire. Their open mouths contribute a tone too low to hear directly; it is felt instead in the fillings of teeth, in old fractures, in the water behind the eyes. They may be responsible for the sensation of height. Or perhaps they are suffering it too, perpetually clinging above a fall that cannot occur.

The moths are the chamber’s darkness made anatomical. Frost rims their wings but does not conceal the patterns there: concentric diagrams, rows of tiny gauges, mountains shown in cross-section, black dots joined by hair-fine routes. No two wings appear identical, yet any moth inspected twice bears the same design as the first. Beneath the upper layers are more moths, then more, pressed flat without injury. The wall behind them has never been seen. It may not exist. Their overlapping bodies may be what the room calls a wall.

There are also creatures without convenient names.

A transparent thing like a knotted finger lives in the mouth of each copper face and drinks each tear at the moment it falls. Flat white animals shuffle forever along the undersides of shelves without advancing. In the fog drift tiny shells, each containing a soft fan that opens continuously but never reaches full extension. Hair-thin worms span the gaps between gear teeth and remain uncrushed as the teeth engage. Behind certain gauges, something with feathers on the inside taps patiently against the glass.

The smallest inhabitants are indistinguishable from processes. The articulated gnats in the air may be carrying droplets, or may be hinges on which the droplets turn. The black specks inside the droplets revolve with the concentrated regularity of engines. Under stronger magnification each speck is said to contain a chamber, and in that chamber a bird sits on a wire above cold water. No instrument has magnified far enough to find an end. Instruments, too, develop fauna in their lenses.

All of these lives crowd the Rookery, but none can be said to live there in the ordinary sense. Living usually means taking something from a place and giving something back: oxygen for carbon, hunger for flesh, youth for age, solitude for offspring. The inhabitants exchange nothing. They consume neither one another nor the room. They leave no shed skin, no droppings, no corpses. Their innumerable bodies impose no burden on the grating or shelves. They are not a population. They are occupancy itself—the room’s refusal to admit a vacancy.

And yet there is fear among them.

It is not expressed by flight, because flight would require departure. It does not widen an eye or quicken a heart, because those gestures are already fixed. The fear exists as a pressure distributed through the whole assembly. It is in the swifts’ fixed cries, the bats’ sixfold grip, the eels knotting the water against any possible gap. It is the fear that somewhere, however minutely, a place might become empty—and that through that emptiness time might enter.

Perhaps this is why they pack every opened cabinet and every lifted cup. They are not rushing in. They have always occupied the exposed space, though the space did not exist until exposed. They seal each new absence before it can deepen into an interval. Even the beetles in the bolt holes brace themselves head to tail like plugs.

The figure in the observation niche may be their keeper, their product, or merely another form of them.

Seen through the first frosted window, it appears human only because it is seated as a human sits: spine bent slightly, feet planted on the grating, hands perhaps resting in its lap. In the smaller Rookery beyond, its shoulders are narrower. Beyond that, it seems feathered. Farther still, it is no more than a dark pin fixed before an amber lamp. Every figure faces away, toward its own descending series of rooms.

There may also be a figure in the niche from which the visitor looks. The riveted stool is cold, but pressure marks sometimes appear on its surface: two hands, the edge of a seated body, the parallel impressions of heels. Looking quickly at the frosted glass may show not one reflection but two—one standing, one already seated. The seated one does not turn.

It is likely the speaker.

Not because its mouth can be seen moving, but because the sentence in the tube has the character of something learned by repetition rather than understood. *It is nearly finished.* The words are built correctly. They arrive in the listener’s own voice. They promise an ending with perfect confidence and no knowledge of what an ending is.

Perhaps the entire multitude is attempting to manufacture the speaker. Perhaps the mechanisms refine motion into sound, sound into animals, animals into crowding, crowding into thought. Each duplicate room could be a finer stage of the same process. At some unreachable depth, the seated figure may at last be complete enough to rise.

But rising would leave the stool empty.

So the creatures hold their places. The mites pass their particle without passing it. The salamanders keep their first breath. The birds sustain the one note allotted to each of them. Every claw, scale, cilium, and translucent lid contributes to the prevention of the next instant.

They are not waiting for the Rookery to finish.

They are what keeps it nearly finished forever.
