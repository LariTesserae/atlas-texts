A colony lives there, though not in any form the surface would readily count as life.

Its body is distributed through the chamber’s differences: the narrow interval between hot air and cooler water, the mineral collars on the pipes, the damp fractures beneath the slab. No single patch is essential. The translucent films in the shaded recesses are part of it, as are the colorless roots of the jointed stems, the amber stain spreading from the spring, and certain pale crystals mistaken for ordinary efflorescence. What appear to be separate growths are slow organs of one system, joined beneath the crust by threads finer than cracks.

It has no animal motion. Nothing swims out from it or closes around food. It lives by gradients—heat leaning away from metal, salts separating in shallow water, tiny electrical leaks from buried cables, pressure passing through concrete. Where two unlike conditions touch, it thickens. Where the chamber becomes uniform, it retreats.

The spring is its oldest and densest part. Around the black fins, a clear membrane coats the submerged glass so thinly that it can be detected only by the way amber water refuses to wet certain curves. Silver bubbles trapped inside the fins are linked by hairline channels. The colony enters these cavities and occupies them without breaking the stone, making of the arc a buried comb of chambers. It does not cross the dry inner ring because the ring is not empty: there the greater part of it lies below the visible floor.

The sparse stems are not plants in the ordinary sense. They are temporary instruments raised toward daylight. Each grows alone because each samples a different rectangle of sun. Their green tips manufacture little; most of what sustains them comes from below. When a strip of light shifts with the season, the corresponding stem ceases adding joints. Months later another emerges beneath the new path. Taken together, their spacing records the movement of illumination through the grating more faithfully than any clock.

The wall films serve another purpose. They receive vibration. The engine’s continuous note passes through their wet layers, arranging suspended particles into minute ridges. Pipe hammering produces branching scars; the steam scream lays down finer parallel lines. Speech is rarer and therefore more disruptive. The western wall lowers a voice because the colony absorbs its upper frequencies, carrying them through damp fissures toward the spring. A spoken word is not understood, but it is retained as structure: a microscopic change in mineral grain, preserved until the next growth covers it.

This is why the machinery has no echo. Its roar is already inside the organism.

The red indicator belongs to it now. Filaments have entered the cable sheath and gathered around a minute current too weak to operate any useful device. The glow is steady because the colony steadies it, bleeding fluctuations into the wet ground. The enamel needle remains above zero not because the pedestal measures pressure, but because mineral growth behind the dial has braced the mechanism there. If it ever reaches zero, it will mean the support has dissolved—or been deliberately withdrawn.

Loose objects migrate inward because the colony alters the crust beneath them. It hydrates mineral grains on one side and dries them on the other, tilting a washer, shard, or forgotten tool by fractions too small to see. Yet nothing enters the spring’s innermost ring. Objects collect along its edge in an incomplete orbit: iron filings, flakes of paint, wire ends, a ceramic fuse, the corroded teeth of a key. These are not offerings or defenses. They are materials awaiting separation. Iron goes toward the amber channels. Copper disappears into green-black seams. Glass remains almost untouched.

The bridge does not rust because it is useful as a conductor. The colony maintains a film over its buried supports, diverting corrosion elsewhere. The bridge carries vibration from the sealed wall to the wet floor, allowing the distributed body to register stresses in both foundations. Long before a crack appears, it feels the change as a shift in resonance. The chamber’s gradual closure is not merely happening around it. The colony is measuring the approach.

Perhaps it is also assisting.

Its activity is minute, but it has time. Salts are deposited in one fracture and removed from another. Water is encouraged along hairline paths into the concrete, then held there through heat. Mineral terraces collar bolts; rust opens seams; the foundations settle. No individual action amounts to damage. Over years, however, the room is being reshaped toward enclosure. The useless bridge, the blind pedestal, and the inward-leaning walls are slowly becoming ribs around a sealed wet cavity.

The chamber seems uninhabited because the thing living there has no appetite that produces pursuit, no muscle that produces visible motion, and no need to distinguish machinery from geology. To it, the buried engine is a hot, regular stratum. Concrete is a soluble cliff. Steel is a vein of reactive ore. Daylight is a moving chemical boundary. The industrial works and the emerging spring are not opposing worlds; they are simply the two halves of its environment.

Whether it began below or arrived with the foundations is impossible to tell. It may be an ancient deep biosphere exposed by excavation, newly awakened where heat, water, and manufactured metals met. It may be a consortium born inside the plant—microbes, crystals, and electrical residues becoming mutually dependent until the distinction between colony and deposit ceased to matter. It may have existed nowhere before this room.

It does not wait in the way an animal waits. Still, its whole form points toward the same future: the grating closed, the steam buried, the foundations touching overhead, the water risen above the bridge. In darkness and confinement, the chamber would cease to be a crack beneath the works and become a vessel—hot at its empty center, cool at its flooded edges, armored in mineral, resonant with the engine.

Then the colony may withdraw entirely beneath the surface.

Or the room itself may be what it is growing into.
