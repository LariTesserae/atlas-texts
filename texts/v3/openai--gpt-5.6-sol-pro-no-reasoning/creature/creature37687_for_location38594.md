They call themselves citizens only when law requires a noun.

Among themselves, they are gradients, recurrences, maintained differences in the city’s water: a warmth that remembers its shape, a braid of ions carrying an old argument, a pressure rhythm passed from pipe to pipe without losing the pauses that make it individual. Each is less a body than a refusal to equalize.

No two persist in quite the same way.

Some travel openly as coherent currents, visible only by the dust and glittering salts they gather. They wait at intersections, yield to emergency flows, and coil patiently outside crowded valves. Their moods reveal themselves in temperature: irritation as a fine heat against the skin of nearby walls, grief as sudden cold, concentration as a barely perceptible increase in density. When they speak, they do so by pressure pulses too intricate to hear as language. The pipes translate them into chords, knocks, illuminated text, or patterns trembling across public membranes.

Others need containers. They inhabit pressure suits with shoulders, hands, and featureless heads—not because those shapes resemble anything living in the city, but because they are inherited tools from some earlier civic imagination. Brine circulates through the limbs. Mineral grains collect at the joints like dark blood. Knots of pale light gather behind the faceplate when the occupant attends closely. Such citizens operate benches, ride trains, paint warnings around vents, and carry cups whose contents are distinguished from themselves by custom and paperwork.

A suit can be empty and still finish a task from habit. This is considered embarrassing rather than supernatural.

The oldest citizens are districts.

They reside in arrangements too large to move: a transit loop and all the water passing through it; a residential stack whose rooms warm and cool in a remembered sequence; a municipal pump with a particular asymmetry in its beat. Their thoughts may require hours to complete. They answer questions by rerouting traffic, opening long-sealed doors, or changing the pitch of an entire neighborhood. Many younger currents consider them slow. The districts consider the young dangerously brief.

There are also citizens of very small extent: a charge held between two ceramic plates, a whirlpool no wider than a coin, a metallic taste that migrates against the flow. These must be carefully housed. A careless valve opening can scatter them. A cup overturned into public water may amount to injury, liberation, or the birth of several disputing descendants.

Continuity matters more than substance. Every resident is replaced constantly by the flood, yet remains legally themself so long as enough structure survives to recognize its own history. Identity is witnessed by the city. Doors remember whom they admitted. Pipes preserve the cadence of each passing current. Apartments compare returning warmth to stored warmth. When disputes arise, the water itself testifies.

Their dwellings are not shelters from the sea but instruments for becoming distinct within it. A room may hold a resident by temperature, another by salinity, another by a loop of pressure that never quite repeats. Decorations are functional perturbations: ridged tiles that shape thought-currents, suspended metal threads that preserve dreams, colored salts released on anniversaries. Furniture provides eddies in which memory can collect. Privacy is a glaze of ice, grown thinly around a chosen volume. Behind it, a citizen may divide, recombine, rehearse conversations, or become temporarily unreadable.

They work because the city’s complexity must be continually inhabited. Some tune the pressure cages around fresh rubble. Some escort obsolete memories into brine canals. Some persuade folded rooms to remain larger inside than out. Some cultivate the ceremonial algae in straight black bands, collecting every dead filament before its chemistry can become public. At the construction fronts, specialists enter stone as hair-thin penetrations of reactive water and return carrying maps of possible hollows.

Their children are not made in one way.

Two currents may mingle under contract, exchanging selected memories until a third stable pattern condenses between them. A household may cultivate a new citizen in a heated vessel, feeding it stories as dissolved metal. Occasionally a pump develops a persistent hesitation that survives repair, begins answering signals, and petitions for recognition. Entire lineages descend from infrastructure faults.

The young are diffuse and must be taught boundaries. They flow into strangers, forget which memories are theirs, or follow pipes beyond the districts from which they can return. They are warned about filters, stagnant pockets, open construction stone, and the seduction of perfect mixing. Their earliest game is to hide in municipal circulation while their guardians identify them by pressure signature.

Their greatest fear is not pain but irretrievability.

To be cut apart is survivable if every portion can be found. To lose memory is survivable if other citizens retain enough of it to rebuild a self. But diffusion into the whole city—past the point where law, affection, or machinery can distinguish one pattern from ordinary water—is death. Funerals are acts of careful dilution. The deceased is released slowly into civic systems while chosen memories are precipitated as crystals and placed in walls. Thus the dead become literally structural, though the living debate whether this is honor or merely reuse.

No citizen remembers flesh firsthand.

Still, the city is full of gestures adapted to absent bodies. Cups are raised. Chairs face one another. Doorways preserve unnecessary headroom. Pressure suits have fingers, though most controls respond more efficiently to current. Public notices use symbols for eyes, mouths, lungs, and footsteps. The green plant in the Empty Reservoir is described in official documents as a witness, though it has never testified.

They gather around it because it contains separations they cannot reproduce.

Inside the dry chamber, water is absent without being lost. Soil remains soil rather than municipal solution. The plant keeps fluids enclosed in private vessels, carries memory no pipe can read, and lives behind a skin that neither freezes nor dissolves. It is one thing, stubbornly bounded. Its green leaves convert light by means the citizens understand chemically and cannot understand civically.

Then a crescent vanishes from a leaf.

The missing piece terrifies them because it implies an appetite.

Not corrosion: corrosion leaves residue. Not fracture: fracture leaves edges consistent with force. Not extraction by tool: every instrument is recorded. The mark is rounded, intimate, almost delicate. Something has taken plant matter into itself. Something may be living by consumption, preserving its continuity not through pressure law or municipal memory, but by making another living thing become part of it.

Some citizens believe this unseen eater is the city’s first animal.

Some believe it is the ancestor of all animals, enclosed in a fold too small for instruments to enter.

Some believe the bite is made by the structure outside the seam—the immeasurable black slabs, feeding through the only dry place.

A quieter faction argues that the missing leaf is not consumed at all. It has gone elsewhere: into the hidden volume the city gains whenever the plates separate. According to them, the plant is not being eaten. It is excavating.

Whatever their beliefs, when the crescent appears, every citizen comes as close as their continuity allows. Suits kneel against the transparent wall. Currents layer themselves through the galleries. Trains stop with their passengers suspended inside. District-minds interrupt thoughts begun hours earlier. Even the microscopic citizens are borne inward in sealed capillaries.

For that interval, the metropolis becomes a single attention.

The leaf trembles in impossible stillness. Its wound darkens. Beneath the city, or outside it, or deep within dimensions the water cannot compute, something makes room.

The slabs part by less than an atom.

The citizens feel the newborn interval open through every wall and pipe. They unlock themselves from one another. They loosen streets, soften foundations, unfold reservoirs, and pour into the brightness. Each expansion risks diffusion; each is also the possibility of more selves, more rooms, more memory, more city.

They rush forward not because they know what waits in the new space.

They rush because space is the one substance none of them can manufacture—and because somewhere beyond the cut edge of the leaf, something else may already live there.
