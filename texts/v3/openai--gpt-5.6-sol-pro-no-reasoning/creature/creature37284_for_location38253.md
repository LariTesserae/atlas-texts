What lives inside the drowned tree is not quite a community. It is one life practicing many forms.

The most visible inhabitants are the root-fish: thin, pointed creatures pressed flat against the heartwood until they are indistinguishable from leaves. Fine veins branch through their translucent bodies, carrying greenish fluid in pulses so slow they resemble changes in the light. Anchored to the walls by filamentary tails, they have neither mouths nor open eyes. They feed as plants do—or remember feeding that way.

At rare intervals, one loosens itself.

Its stem withdraws with a small shiver, and two eyes clear beneath the skin. For several minutes it swims, awkward and urgent, through the crowded chamber. Its fins unfold from what had seemed to be leaf margins. It searches the walls for an unoccupied crevice, though such a place scarcely exists. When it finds one, it drives its tail into softened wood. The eyes cloud, close, and slowly become buds. Roots grow inward from its spine. Within hours it is a leaf again, and within years it may flower into several smaller fish.

White larvae sleep inside the tree’s knots. They lie curled together in circles, each knot holding as many larvae as the tree had years at the moment that portion of wood drowned. They wake one at a time, perhaps once in a decade. A waking larva moves laterally through the grain, eating a single growth ring from beginning to end. The wood remains, but the year vanishes from it. Events belonging to that year become difficult to recall: a season of rain, a lightning wound, the nesting of a bird among branches now gone. When the larva has consumed the full circumference, it returns to its knot and sleeps with a darker line visible through its body.

The lone blue crustacean patrols the perimeter.

It is no longer than a finger joint, armored in plates the color of old glacial ice. Its eyes are sealed beneath a mineral film. Its left forelimb is absent, ending in a smooth socket that suggests not injury but omission. It travels always counterclockwise, feeling its way with delicate antennae and scraping invisible residue from roots and ledges.

What it eats are abandoned directions: the lingering impulse of things that began toward the entrance and forgot where it was, the upwardness shed by sediment settling onto the ceiling, the lost orientation of root-fish between anchorages. After feeding, the crustacean leaves behind tiny geometric pellets. Moss grows around these, and in time each becomes the first step of a five-step stair.

Whether there is one crustacean or many cannot be determined. Two may sometimes be heard clicking from opposite ends of the chamber, but only one can ever be seen. Every cast-off shell is also missing its left forelimb.

The fungal fans are alive in a more deliberate way. Their pale tiers meet across narrow passages and part only when touched. They do not feed on rot. They digest distance. Space enters their soft margins and emerges shortened, pleated, or turned around. This allows a passage along the outer bark to arrive beneath the floor, and permits the chamber to exceed the trunk that contains it. Old fans darken at their centers with swallowed remoteness. If damaged, they release a sensation of elsewhere: wind over reeds, pressure under ice, the warmth of an animal’s mouth.

The moss tends everything.

It covers new wood as soon as it rises from the hollow. It withdraws from imagined fire. It gathers around injuries but not scars, and avoids the seven root arches while no one is counting them. Its colorless leaves overlap like scales, each holding a minute quantity of water older than the lake. Where the moss is thickest, it produces the suspended droplets containing scraps of open sky.

Yet the moss may be only an organ of the chamber, as the arches may be ribs and the fungal doors valves.

The deeper inhabitant is the tree itself—or something the tree is becoming.

It does not think quickly enough to possess intentions recognizable from one visit. Its smallest gesture takes a season. Its breath takes longer than many lives. It senses touch as a redistribution of pressure, flame as an idea of warmth, injury as an absence to be patiently corrected. The repeated arches, stairs, basins, and hand-sized shelters are its thoughts in wood: structures formed, revised, erased, and attempted again.

It may be teaching itself how to house something.

During each inhalation, the walls ease apart and the chamber becomes briefly less crowded. Root-fish detach. Fungal doors open. The crustacean pauses with one foreleg raised. In those moments a shape can sometimes be discerned at the center—not a body, but a body’s allowance, a narrow vacancy extending through roots and leaves. It has the proportions of no known animal. It appears jointed in some places and branching in others. The chamber clears room for it instinctively, as flesh clears room for a growing bone.

During exhalation the vacancy disappears, and anyone who has remained throughout receives a ring.

In a fish or larva, the ring forms in the obvious tissues. In other visitors it may appear around a tooth root, within a fingernail, in the lens of an eye, or encircling an old scar. The ring records no ordinary year. It contains the chamber’s cycle: the rise of a future tree through the cold hollow, the migration of the root-fish, the crustacean’s uncompleted circuit, and rain striking leaves before the valley flooded. Those who carry such a ring sometimes dream in wood. They wake knowing where every tree nearby stands, including trees buried underground and trees that have not yet germinated.

There may also be an inhabitant beneath the leaf-covered hollow.

It cannot be seen directly. Below the stone-dense water, among the inverted drowned trunks, something passes from tree to tree without crossing the intervals between them. Its movement appears as a sequential darkening of roots, like thought traveling through a submerged brain. The black shoots that rise into the chamber may be its feelers, its offspring, or simply the marks left by its attention.

Each shoot brings a slightly different forest with it. Some remember trees rooted at both ends. Some carry bark from species not yet evolved. Some bleed clear sap into the water, and the root-fish gather around them without detaching.

At the center of all this is the seed-sized pocket: the chamber’s smallest and oldest resident.

Within it waits another drowned tree, and within that tree another chamber, complete with moss, larvae, fungal doors, root-fish, and a blue crustacean circling counterclockwise. The repetition is not exact. In one inner chamber the arches may truly number eight. In another the crustacean retains its left forelimb and lacks the right. Deeper still, perhaps the shelters are occupied.

The drowned tree is constructing its inhabitants inward before it releases them outward.

For now, whatever it means to house remains unfinished. The little roofs cover emptiness. The basins hold dark water. Five-step stairs end at unbroken bark. Yet the chamber rehearses them with immense patience, correcting every disturbance and sharpening each form anew.

Something will eventually fit them.

Or something already does, but lives so slowly that its arrival and its birth are the same event.
