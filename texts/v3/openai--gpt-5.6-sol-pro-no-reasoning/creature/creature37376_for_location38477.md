The hollow is inhabited less by separate creatures than by a single, distributed commonwealth.

The plant itself lives there first: not merely around the chamber, but as its walls, roof, floor, conduits, reservoirs, and regulating organs. It may once have been many stems, though that distinction has become meaningless. Ancient trunks have grafted into one another until sap, injury, and repair pass across former boundaries. No surviving part contains the whole design. The organism resides in the continuity between parts—in the pressure moving through stalks, the chemical prohibitions laid across the floor, the leaves turning collectively toward light, and the practiced reflex by which debris is drawn out of the central air.

It is neither tree nor grove in the familiar sense. It is a colony that has outlived the individuality of its members.

Most of its inhabitants live inside this body without being fully separate from it. Beetles travel beneath the translucent outer bark through warm, narrow galleries. Some scrape yeast from the moist tissue. Some eat the plant’s secretions. Others prey on eggs and soft larvae, preventing any one lineage from filling the available passages. Their movements are small enough to register only as faint ticking and rasping through the walls.

Mites occupy thinner territories still: films, pores, the damp undersides of scales. Generations rise and vanish within patches no larger than a hand. Blind worms and pale crustaceans move through the soaked sediment below, browsing root hairs, fungal threads, and one another’s remains. Snails graze the condensation grooves at night—or during whatever dim interval serves as night beneath the canopy—leaving clean, shining tracks that close behind them as bark swells.

Fungi are everywhere, though seldom visible as mushrooms. They lace the dead root chambers, line composting seams, and enter the oldest wood. They are the grove’s undertakers and perhaps part of its memory. A fallen bird, an emptied chrysalis, a failed shoot: each is softened, separated, and passed into the roots. Nothing remains long enough to become refuse. Even bones are furred over, hollowed, and taken down into the dark.

In flowers along the walls live the pale frogs.

Each spends much of its life inside a single water-holding cup, its skin nearly matching the waxy interior. It feeds on gnats and minute flies attracted to the trapped moisture. Its eggs adhere beneath the flower’s rim, above the waterline; its young descend into the cup when rain raises the surface. Some frogs may never touch the floor. To them, the grove is an archipelago of small wells separated by enormous distances. Their calls are soft and resonant, amplified by the flowers until they resemble droplets falling into deep vessels.

The birds are more conspicuous but no less integrated. They nest in cavities that grow around them: soft pockets beneath the canopy, entered through circular pores. Whether the birds first chose these hollows or the plant first formed them can no longer be determined. The arrangement now benefits both. The birds remove bark larvae and carry seeds, spores, and tiny passengers from one part of the organism to another. Their waste drops into receptive folds and disappears. When a nestling dies, the pocket seals. When a brood leaves, the chamber remains open for the next.

Gray moths coat the trunks in resting colonies. Their folded wings repeat the bark’s scales so precisely that a wall may seem bare until some pressure change disturbs it and thousands adjust at once. Then the surface shivers without visibly taking flight. Their larvae feed in designated layers of expendable tissue; birds harvest them from the same layers. The moths injure the plant, but only within limits the plant has long since accommodated. They are less an infestation than a seasonal organ made of insects.

Above temporary pools, gnats appear with astonishing speed. Their lives are fitted to the interval between dripping and absorption. They mate, lay, feed, and are eaten before the floor drinks the water away. A pool may exist for only hours, yet contain an entire hurried ecology.

There are predators, but no sovereign predator. Frogs take flies. Birds take larvae and beetles. Beetles consume eggs. Crustaceans seize worms in the root chambers. Fungal nets trap microscopic grazers. Hunger moves continuously through the grove, distributed across thousands of minor mouths. No species is permitted a dramatic victory. Any abundance becomes food; any vacancy becomes habitat.

The central chamber itself has almost no resident animal. Its emptiness is not available territory but a maintained organ, like a lung or the clear space inside a joint. Creatures cross it without belonging to it. A bird’s flight from wall to wall briefly gives the air a scale. A moth dislodged from the bark flutters until it finds another surface. Gnats hover over water and vanish with it. None settles at the center because there is nowhere to settle, nothing to hide behind, and little to eat.

Yet something does live in that vacancy: the grove’s breath.

Cool air pools there, receives the moisture of innumerable surfaces, and carries pressure changes from one pumping stalk to the next. Pollen, spores, scents, wingbeats, and the reports of petal valves pass through it. The open volume is the medium by which distant portions of the organism remain connected. Keeping it empty allows sound, air, and flying couriers to move freely. The chamber is inhabited by circulation rather than bodies.

No single creature understands this arrangement. The plant does not plan its lanes. The birds do not know they are tending it. The fungi do not intend cleanliness, nor the beetles balance. Nevertheless, together they behave like a mature organism whose organs are drawn from many kingdoms of life.

What lives there is therefore difficult to count.

There is the ancient plant, if it is one plant. There are innumerable animals, most nearly invisible. There are fungal systems threaded through both living and dead tissue. There are yeasts, films, spores, and microbial populations that likely exceed all the visible inhabitants combined. But beneath these categories lives another entity: the stable relationship among them, older than any body participating in it.

That relationship is the hollow’s true inhabitant.

It has no face and produces no leader. Its behavior is the preservation of openness, repetition, and exchange. It eats disturbance. It converts death into structure. It gives each excess to a waiting consumer and each useful vacancy to a specialist. Individual lives flicker across it—frog, moth, bird, beetle, root tip—but the pattern remains.

The grove has not eliminated violence, hunger, failure, or decay. It has made them punctual.

And so what lives inside the old plant is a society without deliberation, housed in a body without a single center: countless brief organisms participating in one extremely ancient habit.
