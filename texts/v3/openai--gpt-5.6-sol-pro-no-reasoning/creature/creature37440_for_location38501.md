The city itself lives here.

Not as a collection of trees, though trees are among its temporary expressions, and not as a colony in any ordinary sense. Nothing within the basin possesses a clean boundary. Root enters root. Bark closes over foreign stems and wakes carrying their sap. A flower rising from one district may be fed by reservoirs in another and scatter seeds containing the growth-patterns of a third. What appear to be thousands of competing species may instead be organs, moods, or simultaneous hypotheses of a single botanical being.

Its body is the basin’s green-black surface. Its circulation is the wandering river. Its breath passes through plazas, arcades, hollow trunks, and leaf-lined courts. Towers are load-bearing bones until they soften into vessels; streets are connective tissue until they pucker into walls. It has no fixed anatomy because anatomy here is an activity, not a plan. Every structure is provisional, maintained only as long as its function requires that particular shape.

If it thinks, thought does not occur in a head. It moves as changes in pressure, light, sugar, tension, and distance. A shadow crossing the eastern leaves may become a root contraction miles away. Rain touching one tower may be answered by flowers opening in an afternoon that has not yet arrived. Its decisions travel both through sap and through altered space: districts brought together for an instant, signals passed across the contact, then separated again.

Its attention is visible in coordinated turnings. Millions of leaves incline toward an event too slow, too large, or too abstract for an animal sense to notice. Pale nodules brighten along the walls. Stamens extend into empty intersections and feel the air. Migratory roots gather beneath a vacant place, hesitate, and flow around it. None of this resembles curiosity, yet neither is it blind reflex. The city continuously samples itself.

Its apparent buildings are the brief shapes of that self-examination.

Some chambers digest enclosed growth. Some cultivate light. Some isolate contradictory histories until they can be incorporated. The reservoir-plants may be kidneys, memories, or embryos. Columnar blossoms that raise and demolish whole skylines seem wasteful only if permanence is assumed to be valuable. To the city, architecture may be a reproductive spasm, a calculation performed in cellulose, or a way of testing which version of space will bear weight.

There are lesser lives within it, but none are inhabitants.

A runner-vine crossing an avenue can behave with eerie independence. Its tip lifts, divides, rejects several surfaces, then anchors to one that did not appear to exist before contact. Bands of such vines coordinate repairs and bridge collapses. They resemble laboring animals until the district contracts and draws them back into its walls, where they become veins.

Shadow-hunting flowers pivot without following the sun. Their dark cups open toward absences: passing occlusions, sealed chambers, the negative roots of the vacant lot. They feed on diminished light or on the spatial pressure accompanying it. When a shadow detaches from its leaf, these flowers lean after it with every filament extended. Sometimes they catch one. The blossom then closes around flat darkness and remains warm for days.

The migrating roots move more deliberately. They travel beneath the rind in pale herds, swelling the avenues ahead of them. At intersections they separate, braid, exchange translucent buds, and continue in altered directions. A few rise briefly into the air as arches before plunging down again. Their migrations redirect water, redistribute strain, and perhaps carry identity from one neighborhood to another. When they pass, local plants begin producing unfamiliar leaves, as though taught a new dialect.

Seeds are the nearest things to discrete creatures. They leap, glide, tumble, burrow, and fall upward. Some unfold vanes and tack through the hydraulic wind between towers. Others strike walls hard enough to embed themselves, then soften the surrounding tissue and disappear into it. Seeds caught in contradictory districts may germinate into ancestral forms, mature before landing, or return to the flowers that launched them. Their independence lasts only until attachment. Thereafter they are edits made to the larger body.

Even death does not become separate. Dead tissue is enclosed before it can rot. Fallen towers are flooded with digestive sap and converted into warm scaffolds. Leaves collapse into streets and are vascularized from below. Nothing is allowed the solitude of a corpse. The city retrieves every abandoned form, including forms that may never have lived in the current version of its past.

The empty lot may be the sole exception.

Something lives there only in the sense that an absence can exert appetite. It has roots without substance, weight without visibility, and a border that living tissue will not cross. The city grows away from it while continually enclosing it, preserving the vacancy like a wound that cannot heal or an organ made of refusal. Sound destroys itself at its edge. Leaves entering it become unseen but remain heavy. Perhaps this is the shape of what the city excludes from itself: animal life, true death, open ground, or a simpler world in which one place cannot contain another.

The inverted basin overhead may be another organism, or the same organism encountering itself across a fold. Its downward towers tremble near those below. Roots from each side search the narrowing interval but do not quite touch. During the great contractions, the two cities twist against one another like halves of a seed. Their opposite motions knead distance, causing neighborhoods to slide, duplicate, and acquire impossible ages. What looks like urban rearrangement may be digestion between these mirrored bodies—or reproduction proceeding on a scale for which the whole basin is a single cell.

There are no citizens because citizenship requires an inside and an outside, an individual sheltered by a place. Here the shelter, sheltered, food, road, weather, and body are the same continuous life.

It does not wait for visitors. It has developed no sweetness as bargain, no color as signal to an eye, no doorway whose dimensions imply a guest. The city’s beauty is incidental: exposed circulation, reproductive violence, stress distributed elegantly through branching forms. Its terraces are not for standing upon. Its avenues are not for passage. They are moments when its tissues happen to resemble the needs of creatures that do not exist there.

At night, its slower self becomes perceptible. Pressure rolls through the towers. Reservoirs contract in sequence. Dim blossoms release stored light into neighboring membranes, and the glow travels from block to block like a thought trying to complete itself. Somewhere, roots turn a district over and press its former skyward surfaces into the warm dark beneath the streets. Elsewhere, a leaf opens around an interior courtyard containing a younger version of the city.

By dawn, that version may have joined the rest.

The basin does not become more crowded. Crowding presumes bodies competing for finite room. The city manufactures room as readily as it manufactures bark. It pleats distance, buds interiors, grafts new histories onto old trunks, and grows additional directions where no directions were before.

What lives there is an organism without a stable number.

It is one city.

It is every plant.

It is the space between them, grown alive.
