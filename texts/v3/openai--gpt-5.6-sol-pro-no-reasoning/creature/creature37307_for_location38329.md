Those who live upon the high bark are light, patient, and difficult to distinguish from the life around them.

They call themselves, in most of their map-songs, the **Tethered**—not because they are captive, but because anything that intends to remain in this country must bind itself to something else. A sleeping child is tied to its mother. A mother is tied to the shelter. The shelter is braided into a root spine, which grips a larger stem, which descends into the dark. To be untethered is not freedom. It is a brief condition preceding disappearance.

Their bodies are made for cold, height, and unreliable surfaces. They tend to be narrow through the torso, with long arms and broad, articulate feet whose outer toes can oppose the others. Thick pads along palms, soles, knees, and forearms grip wet bark without freezing to it. Adults move in a low, four-pointed posture when the wind rises, but stand upright in calm weather to see across the folds.

Their skin is pale gray, amber-brown, or nearly black, often mottled in branching patterns that resemble lichen or old frostbite. Beneath it lie dense networks of heat-carrying vessels. When exerted, frightened, or newly fed, a person may show dim red tracery across the face and ribs. Their hair grows in coarse, hollow strands that trap air. It is rarely cut. Instead it is braided with cords, hooks, family bones, scent pods, and little resonators that reveal shifts in the wind.

They have large eyes, dark from edge to edge, well suited to the faint lights of sap and blossom. Color matters to them chiefly at low intensities. They can distinguish the green pulse of a safe conduit from the blue-green agitation preceding a rupture, or recognize a flower’s mood by alterations in its glow invisible to other creatures. Bright daylight, on the rare occasions when the upper clouds thin, is painful. They cover their faces and wait for the merciful dark to return.

Their hearing is less remarkable than their sense of vibration. The pads of the feet, the jaw, and the breastbone are sensitive to movement through the living terrain. A skilled listener can kneel against the bark and identify distant rain, a feeding herd, the closure of a fissure, or the slow pressure of new growth below. Many sleep with one cheek against a wall. Those who lose this sensitivity through age or injury are treated with particular care, since they inhabit a world that has become dangerously quiet.

No settlement is large. Six or seven households make a populous community; thirty adults gathered in one place would be remembered for generations. The high country cannot support concentration for long. Water pockets empty, grazing animals alter their routes, and the structure beneath a village may decide to rise, split, flower, or turn its surface toward the light.

A household is therefore not a fixed building but a maintained relationship with a piece of growth.

Its outer chamber might be a pod trained open on one side, lined with felted fibers and suspended beneath a protective spine. Its warm room is often cut into living pith, though never deeper than the plant can tolerate. Sap channels pass behind the walls, providing heat and a dim, greenish illumination. Families soothe these channels with moisture, pressure, and bitter pastes. If handled badly, the walls blister, flood, or grow inward.

Every dwelling has more anchors than doors.

Ropes cross the interior at waist and shoulder height. Sleeping nests close around their occupants like knotted hands. Utensils are clipped to cords. Infants spend their first years in sling-pods attached to an adult or to the central braid. Even stories are sometimes called anchors: words thrown backward so the living do not drift too far from the dead.

The Tethered eat little at a sitting. Their staple food is scraped from warm sap lines: dense mats of frost lichen, yeasts, salt molds, and tiny shelled grazers ground together into a sour paste. They drink condensation, fermented sap, and melted ice judged to have separated naturally from its basin. They harvest larvae from water pockets, trap migrating six-legged animals, and collect the bodies of gliders when the living wings finally release them.

Nothing edible is assumed to be dead.

Meat may continue to twitch toward warmth after cooking. A severed root can knot itself around a wrist. Seeds germinate in stomachs if not cracked, scorched, or soaked in a suppressing infusion. Hunters carry ash, cold resin, and wedges of bitter shell to persuade their catch that it has ceased participating in the world.

Fire is used sparingly. Since most local wood resists flame, hearths burn dried dung, cast skins, dead fungal felt, and oils rendered from flying animals. Flame is sheltered inside clay-coated husks with narrow mouths. A household flame may persist for decades, divided and carried when families separate. Extinguishing another household’s fire is a grave offense, though less because of symbolism than because relighting one in a wet season can take days.

Children learn knots before names. A young child is taught the holding knot, the quick-release knot, the knot that tightens under upward strain, and the false knot used to test whether a tendril will imitate it. Later come the listening postures, the tastes of safe ice, the calls of gliders, and the proper responses to a path that was present yesterday but has healed overnight.

Names themselves are provisional. A person receives a cradle-name, usually taken from a sound heard at birth: Distant Knock, Three Rattles, Rain in the Hollow Stem. On completing a first crossing alone, they earn a path-name. Later names may mark marriages, migrations, injuries, discoveries, or altered obligations. The oldest people possess long names that are seldom spoken in full. In song, these names become miniature maps of lives.

The Tethered do not measure age by years. Seasons in the high country are too local, and the sky offers few dependable cycles. Age is counted by witnessed openings, settlement migrations, major fractures, and returns of particular animal herds. Someone might say she was born before the Nine Lamps opened together, crossed into adulthood during the Splitting of the Bent Ridge, and has lived through four returns of the red-backed grazers.

Their dead are not buried. There is nowhere to dig that is not alive, and no grave would remain where it was placed.

Some bodies are wrapped in leaf-felt and lowered into warm fissures, where pale tunnelers quickly take them apart. Others are suspended beneath glider wings in hope that they will be carried toward the upper light. In communities near stable waterways, bones may be cleaned and distributed among relatives. Finger bones become toggles. Ribs become loom frames. Teeth mark routes. The dead continue to hold things together.

They believe forgetting is more final than death. For this reason maps, genealogies, weather records, and warnings are sung in interlocking parts. No single singer possesses an entire route. One person knows the water sequence, another the crossings, another the dangerous blossoms, and another the timing of the ridge’s exhalations. A journey begins with people assembling the song between them.

A map-song does not say, “Go north for three miles.” It says:

*Leave while the hollow spine is drawing breath.*  
*Keep the amber pulse beneath your right hand.*  
*Pass two scars that close and one that weeps.*  
*Where the wind loses its lower voice, descend.*  
*If the frost tastes sweet, you have gone too far.*

Songs must change as the land changes. A route singer who insists upon an old verse after the terrain has outgrown it is considered dangerous, no matter how revered they are.

The people have no general word for “plant.” Such a category would include nearly the whole world and therefore explain nothing. Instead they distinguish between growth that bears, growth that carries, growth that listens, growth that traps, growth that dreams through cold, and growth that is becoming something unknown. Some of these distinctions are practical; others are moral.

They do not worship the vegetable continent as a single being. It is too large and contradictory for personhood as they understand it. Rather, they treat it as an accumulation of intentions, most too slow or immense to comprehend. A bridge root may be generous for six generations and then tighten without warning. A condensation stalk may appear to favor one family, though perhaps that family merely learned its needs. A flower may kill dozens by opening and still be thanked for the heat it leaves behind.

Their rituals are therefore closer to negotiations than prayers.

Before cutting bark, they numb the area with cold. Before entering a pore, they exhale into it and wait to feel whether the exhalation returns. Water is addressed aloud before it is taken. New bridges are fed milk-sap, blood, and powdered bone, substances believed to teach the growing fibers that animals will depend upon them.

The most solemn ceremonies occur when a dwelling begins to change beyond habitation. Families do not simply abandon it. They remove cords, bones, hearth husks, and trained vessels, then spend a night touching the walls and recounting what occurred there. At dawn they release the final restraints. Some old dwellings close like wounds. Some flower. Some crawl slowly away along the lee side of the ridge.

Those who work most intimately with growth are called **persuaders**. Outsiders might mistake them for gardeners, physicians, engineers, or priests, but none of those words is sufficient. Persuaders study pressure, sap rhythm, scent, vibration, and the minute changes in direction that reveal what a living structure is likely to do.

They know how to make a vine divide without dying, how to keep a shelter wall dormant, how to coax hooks from thorn tissue, and how to convince a bridge that the far side of a chasm contains water. Their work can take years. The best persuaders are said not to command growth at all. They arrange circumstances until the plant believes their desire was its own.

Another respected calling belongs to the **rain hearers**, who set tuned bowls and tensioned membranes outside during storms. From changes in resonance they judge whether the supporting bark is swelling evenly or building toward rupture. They can also distinguish rain falling from above from condensation rising out of a chasm. Communities often decide whether to remain, lash down, or migrate according to a rain hearer’s word.

**Gap runners** carry messages and small goods between settlements. They travel alone or in pairs because larger groups strain young bridges and attract predators. A runner’s body is wrapped with many thin cords, each terminating in a hook or gripping seed. If they fall, they cast these outward, trusting at least one to find a seam.

Runners bring news slowly but with great authority. They announce marriages, deaths, new water, failed paths, altered songs, and unprecedented growth. They also bring rumors from the upper reaches: roots rising where no roots stood, metal nodules warm to the touch, a flower that opened downward, a crack from which daylight emerged.

The rare metal drawn up from below is not shaped chiefly into weapons. It is too precious. Most becomes hooks, needles, cutting edges, and resonant strips used to test the depth of bark. A single hook acquires its own genealogy. People speak of who carried it, whom it saved, where it failed, and what stains cannot be polished away.

Ownership among the Tethered is based more on care than possession. A thing belongs to whoever maintains its relationship with the world. A trained pod belongs to the household that feeds and shapes it. A route belongs to those who keep its song accurate. A water pocket belongs temporarily to the creatures and communities that protect it from contamination and waste.

This makes theft a complicated offense. Taking an object may be forgiven. Taking something while refusing the obligations attached to it is much worse.

The grazers along the warm sap lines are not fully domesticated, but some herds have followed particular families for generations. The people chip salt fungus from inaccessible folds and lay it along safe paths; in return they harvest shed mouthplates, dung, hair, and occasionally meat. Each herd moves with a cluster of small clicking animals that clean parasites from its joints. Children imitate these cleaners to approach nervous grazers.

Gliders are regarded with a mixture of envy and unease. Their wings begin as pieces of living foliage grafted into their bodies, and the Tethered have long attempted lesser versions of this partnership. Scouts sometimes wear mantles of cultivated membrane that stiffen in strong wind, allowing controlled falls across modest gaps. These mantles must be fed through tubes sewn beneath the wearer’s skin. If neglected, they wither. If overfed, they root inward.

True flight remains beyond them—or so most believe.

There are songs about people who surrendered entirely to their mantles and were carried away. In some versions they became gliders. In others the wings digested them. In still others they reached the solitary flowers of the upper dark and now tend lights visible hundreds of miles away.

The cord-thin predators at the breathing pores are called **borrowed twigs**. They imitate plant debris not only in appearance but in temperature and pulse. A hunter may touch one and feel nothing unusual until it loops around the arm. People kill them with sudden cold or feed them irritating spores until they release their grip.

More feared are the pale tunneling beasts under fresh bark. They rarely attack deliberately, but their movement can lift a dwelling, rupture a water cistern, or overturn an entire ridge path. Some settlements place drums against the ground and beat slow patterns when a tunneler approaches. Occasionally the animal turns aside. Whether it responds to the rhythm or merely avoids the vibration is debated with seriousness.

The enormous gas-borne creatures that rise from the chasms are known as **hollow moons**. Their translucent bodies contain forests of organs, parasites, symbionts, and trapped airborne life. While one hangs overhead, its feeding threads sweep across miles of terrain. Communities extinguish lights and retreat beneath overhangs, yet some specialists venture out to cut fragments from the threads.

These fragments yield medicine, strong translucent cord, and droplets of fluid that remain warm through the coldest nights. They also continue searching for the parent body and may drag unsecured containers toward the nearest abyss.

No one agrees whether hollow moons are animals ascending to feed or organs temporarily extended by something deeper. Older songs warn that harming one may cause a particular chasm to close.

Far below the inhabited bark are the **Knockers**, if they exist. They are known from impacts that travel across the chasm walls: three slow blows, a pause, then one; or long irregular sequences continuing for days. The Tethered answer by striking root bridges with stone mallets. Sometimes the unseen knocking alters afterward.

Whole traditions have arisen around these exchanges. Some believe the Knockers are people living upside down among the roots. Some say they are blind creatures testing the world for weakness. Some claim the sounds come from future fractures and are heard before they occur.

Those who descend are a people apart even before they leave.

They shave their hollow hair, darken their eyes with fungal paste, and carry no open flame. Their ropes are grown rather than woven, kept alive in coils and fed from bladders of diluted sap. Each descender bears bells of different materials—bone, shell, frozen resin, metal—because sound behaves strangely in the lower spaces, and a bell that rings wrongly may be the first sign that direction has changed.

The first stages of descent pass familiar life: nests under ledges, grazer trails on vertical bark, colonies around glowing sap, predators warming themselves at pores. Farther down, frost disappears. Growth becomes soft, pallid, and widely separated. Roots hang alone in distances too large to judge. Warm rain beads drift upward, joining streams along unseen ceilings. Creatures fall past with their limbs folded, only to slow, turn, and rise again.

Few descenders return. Those who do are often unable to explain what they found. They draw spirals instead of maps. They flinch before noises rather than after them. Some insist the continent is not growing upward but falling perpetually through darkness, its roots streaming behind it. Others claim there is a lower sun, green and cold, around which the deepest roots curl.

One returned carrying a piece of metal that pulsed like sap. Another brought a seed that refused every surface and remained suspended in the center of its container. A third came back with fresh cuts on her body that did not appear until several days after her return.

The Tethered do not call these survivors mad. Madness implies a mind damaged into falsehood. They say instead that such people are **still arriving**.

Yet the strangest inhabitants may not be the people or animals at all.

Within the continent itself are regions that behave with troubling coherence. A chain of distant flowers may open in response to movement along a ridge. Sap pulses may repeat the rhythms of settlement drums. Bridges trained in one place sometimes grow identical knots hundreds of miles away. Cuts made as route marks have healed into shapes resembling eyes, mouths, or hands—not accurately, but with increasing skill.

Persuaders argue whether the world is imitating its inhabitants, communicating with them, or merely incorporating their habits into its growth.

No conclusion lasts.

The high country is too young for fixed answers. Species appear with new growth and vanish when a ridge molts. A shelter becomes an organism; an organism becomes a route; a route closes and flowers; a flower’s husk becomes a village. The people change with it, binding living membranes into their bodies, carrying ancestral bones as tools, teaching roots to become bridges and being taught in return how to move, wait, and endure.

They are not masters of the continent, nor merely parasites upon it. They are among its smaller, quicker thoughts.

At night, when the flowers signal across the distances, settlements answer with hooded lamps. A violet point opens on a summit. An amber chamber flashes from beneath a root arch. Far away, perhaps days of travel off, another light responds.

For a little while the emptiness shows its inhabitants—not filling the dark, but tethered throughout it, each small life holding fast to another while the immeasurable living world rises beneath them.
