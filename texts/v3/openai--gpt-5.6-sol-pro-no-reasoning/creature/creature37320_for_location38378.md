The Black Nursery is inhabited less by separate species than by a single crowded commonwealth, one body assembled from ten thousand bodies and fitted into the mountain’s fresh wound.

Its skin is layered. Swifts and lichens occupy the dim upper margins; veilbacks spread across the middle walls; salamanders, crabs, beetles, worms, mites, moths, fungi, and unnamed translucent things fill the finer structure beneath. Every visible creature conceals smaller tenants. Feathers are pastures. Membranes are watersheds. Droppings become forests at another scale. A wet pore scarcely wider than a needle may contain an entire red migration.

Nothing here truly possesses a territory. It possesses an interval: the minutes while condensation beads on a particular mineral face, the season before a shelf splits, the decade during which a crack remains warm. Homes are borrowed from cooling lava and reclaimed by frost. A nursery may become a grave, then a reservoir, then the feeding ground of something too small to notice either transformation.

The veilbacks are the cleft’s most conspicuous inhabitants, though “conspicuous” here means that a patient observer might eventually realize the wall is breathing. They hang in overlapping colonies, six limbs braced against basalt, their bodies flattened beneath broad pleated membranes. Cloud condenses upon them. Droplets creep along fine channels in the smoky skin and gather at their mouths. Young veilbacks shelter under the adults’ folds, licking moisture from the inner pleats and clinging motionless when the wind pipes sound.

They are neither wholly solitary nor evidently social. Each animal drinks for itself, yet a colony functions like a roof: upper membranes catch fog, lower ones receive the overflow, and the accumulated warmth of thousands protects the smallest young. When clear weather forces them into the fissures, they fold almost simultaneously. The cliff undresses in waves.

Among them live parasites, grazers, and companions whose roles cannot be cleanly separated. Thread-legged mites comb mineral dust from the membrane ridges. Pale larvae drink from droplets gathered there, but also consume fungal infections. Tiny hooked creatures attach themselves near the veilbacks’ mouths and steal moisture, then are themselves eaten during grooming. Thus injury and service differ mostly by degree.

The black mountain swifts live nearer the opening, where the grayest daylight falls. They are narrow birds with stiff tails and feet able to close around edges too slight for a human fingertip. Their nests are scarcely nests at all: a few feathers pasted to stone with saliva, eggs laid against bare basalt and held from the precipice by the sleeping parent’s body.

Their cleverness leaves no monuments. They carry sharp flakes to the ledges, turn cliff-beetle eggs beneath one foot, and split the cases along their seams. A useful flake may pass through several beaks before frost knocks it away. Sometimes a bird lodges one upright in a crack, making a fixed cutting edge. No arrangement survives long enough to become a tradition visible in stone, but the birds remember particular shelves, particular tools, and perhaps one another’s methods.

Their calls belong to the whole cleft. A single note dies unanswered. A second is copied by a neighboring bird. A third passes into the walls, where gliders, moths, mammals, and hollow vents return altered versions until the original caller cannot be located. Courtship therefore becomes distributed: one bird begins a phrase, another repeats it, and soon the entire ravine seems to solicit itself.

Beneath the swifts sleep the hook-footed gliders. Folded, they resemble leaves burned pale by frost. Open, they are all membrane and fine-boned fingers. They rarely fly in the ordinary sense. They release themselves from one wall, fall a short distance, and let the cleft’s upward drafts press them onto another. At dusk they move in soft avalanches, never reaching the floor, each descent interrupted by ledge, wing, thread, or body.

The long-fingered mammals live lower down. They are small, dark-eyed, and nearly hairless on the hands. Their chief craft is temporary plumbing. Finding a reliable drip, one packs moss into the crack beneath it, kneading the fibers until they hold a trembling mouthful of water. The reservoir may last an hour or a month. Moths drink from it. Salamanders hide behind it. Beetles pull it apart. The maker returns if it can and begins again if it cannot.

The salamanders appear almost unfinished. Their flesh is milk-clear, their bones delicate gray shadows. They conserve themselves into slowness: one breath, a pause; one heartbeat, a longer pause; one careful movement beneath a stone. Yet when a wet interval opens, they become sudden. They slip through films of water scarcely deep enough to cover their toes and seize the red swimmers emerging from thawed pockets.

Those red animals spend most of existence sealed beneath transparent ice. Generations may hatch, feed, breed, and die in a chamber no larger than a tear. When summer loosens the lid, their descendants pour outward along branching wetness. To a large eye they resemble rust spreading over stone. To themselves, perhaps, the opening of the basin is a recurring age of expansion, and its freezing an ancestral catastrophe.

The silver crabs inhabit pores where no crab should be. They are blind, thin-shelled, and small enough to turn around inside a bubble left in ancient lava. Their limbs are jointed more finely than those of their marine relatives, allowing them to brace against every side of a passage. They feed on mineral films, fungus, eggs, and one another. Some carry young beneath their bodies through tunnels that have never known the sea, though salt remains in the stone and in the condensed breath of birds.

Worms descend from the ceiling on threads finer than spider silk. They have no apparent heads until they feed. Then one end opens into a fringed cup that catches droplets, spores, feather dust, and minute airborne prey. A worm may hang for days, vibrating in the wind’s long notes. At the first approach of the great silence, it consumes its own thread and vanishes into a hole scarcely wider than its body.

The floor belongs to beetles, but only in the way a forest floor belongs to roots. They pass beneath everything. Their fused wing cases preserve moisture; their wedge-shaped heads push through compressed layers of husk, pellet, eggshell, and shed skin. They dismantle the dead before death can accumulate into recognizable history. Behind them come paler jaws, fungal threads, and organisms that eat what has already passed through several bodies.

Even bone is temporary. The inhabitants lick salts from it, rasp it, soften it with secretions, carry away its grains. A skeleton becomes first a resource, then a scattering, then living tissue distributed among creatures that never knew what shape it had.

The plants live slowly enough to resemble geology. Lichen stains the upper margins with rust. Moss forms dark green pads beneath the birds, rich with waste and tiny grazing lives. The jointed herbs stand deeper in the cracks, their violet-black stems hollow and cold. Each white flower faces inward, toward stone. Cave moths approach from below, cling upside down, and enter the waxy bloom as though crawling into a small moon pressed against the wall.

No flower expects the sky.

Most pervasive are the fungal veils: transparent films drawn over inaccessible surfaces, faintly luminous when disturbed. Their glow is not illumination so much as evidence. A passing foot ignites a green outline. A grazing mouth leaves a dark trail. A whole veilback colony, shifting at once, may briefly print itself in dim fire before darkness closes again.

All these creatures live around something deeper.

When it approaches, silence begins above and pours downward. It is not panic in the ordinary sense. Many species cannot hear; others have no eyes; some are rooted or sealed in ice. Yet all respond. Membranes contract. Mouthparts cease. Eggs delay hatching. Spores remain folded. Hearts that already beat slowly become nearly still.

Behind the basalt, pressure travels.

The hidden inhabitant may be enormous, or it may be innumerable. Perhaps a pale colonial animal fills hairline faults with living tissue and advances only when frost opens the way. Perhaps mineral crystals grow, dissolve, and regrow in a circuit that the cleft’s creatures have learned as metabolism. Perhaps the mountain contains a soft-bodied thing whose lifetime is measured in uplift, each century only a contraction of one buried muscle.

No one in the Nursery needs the distinction. Whatever passes through the rock exerts selection as surely as hunger or cold. Life hatches behind it, hides before it, settles along the routes it neglects. Its pressure alters springs, cracks egg cases, exposes salt, seals chambers, and sometimes crushes whole colonies without malice.

To the inhabitants, it is not a monster. It is climate, calendar, ancestor, and moving wall.

Perhaps the Black Nursery itself is its nursery. Perhaps the lives crowded into the cleft are incidental growths on the surface of something still unborn. Or perhaps the deep passage is only one more resident, no more central in its own scale than a mite among feathers.

The true inhabitant is the congestion: the system by which every fall is caught, every breath reused, every death divided, every sound adopted by another throat. Life does not merely occupy the cleft. It prevents emptiness from occurring there.

A feather drops and becomes a roof.

A corpse becomes salt.

A call becomes weather.

A bead of water passes from cloud to membrane, membrane to mouth, mouth to breath, breath to wall, and begins again.

For now, that circulation is what lives in the Black Nursery: an ancient appetite inhabiting newborn stone, many-bodied, improvised, and so densely folded that the mountain itself seems only the dark skeleton around it.
