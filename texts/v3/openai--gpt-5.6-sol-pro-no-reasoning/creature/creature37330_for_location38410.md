What lives there is chiefly a skin.

It has no single body. It lies thinly over brick, iron, concrete, clinker, enamel, and mineral scale: moss in the mortar lines, liverwort pressed flat against the walls, algae varnishing the submerged floor. Ferns lift from bolt holes as if the old fastenings had germinated. Their roots do not descend into earth; they enter rust pockets, packed dust, and the damp interiors of obsolete housings. The vine on the pipe is perhaps the nearest thing to an individual inhabitant. It has measured the available height, reached the lamp, and ceased.

This life is neither wild nor cultivated. It does not conquer the room. It accepts a precise allotment: the wet edges, the sweating joints, the enriched trickles, the dim radius below each cage lamp. The center is not for it. The center belongs to clear ankle-deep water and the uninterrupted passage toward the grate.

Most of what lives here is microbial and unseen. Green films work beneath the lamps. Pale colonies inhabit the mineral rind around the drain. Bacteria occupy the warm interfaces where water meets oxidized metal, feeding on traces brought from distant machinery. Fungal threads penetrate old sealant and clinker without visibly weakening either. Together they form a quiet metabolism spread across manufactured surfaces, converting leakage and dust into a little soft tissue, then surrendering that tissue leaf by leaf to the drain.

The larger residents are temporary and nearly accidental. A gnat sometimes suspends itself over the basin, held steady in air that seems too dense to cross. Under the drain lip, a colorless isopod grazes where the water thins. It resembles a shaving of fingernail until it moves. Neither appears numerous enough to constitute a colony. They are less a population than recurring details.

And then there is the frog.

If it exists, it is small and pale—not white, but washed of the colors expected in open weather. Its skin matches mineral deposit, wet concrete, the underside of a fern stem. It sits where the basin meets the wall, forefeet resting on the shallow slope, throat moving beneath the mechanical roar. No one hears it call. Perhaps it never does. Its eyes retain a muted gold, the room’s one unnecessary color.

There is no evident food enough for it, no mate, no clutch of eggs, no route through which it reliably comes or goes. Workers find no tracks except occasional marks in the algae, and those are erased by dripping water. Some believe several frogs reach the chamber over the years. Others believe it is always the same one, surviving impossibly long in the stable warmth. A few say the reports are only what happens when mineral lumps are glimpsed through trembling reflections.

The room offers no answer. On most inspections the corner is empty.

The deepest inhabitant may therefore be the arrangement itself: a durable association of pump heat, condensation, drainage, corrosion, mineral accretion, lamplight, and modest growth. Each part maintains the conditions of the others without intention. The machinery supplies warmth and water. The drain preserves the open basin. Maintenance preserves the machinery. The plants take only what appears on the surfaces. Small creatures, when they come, take only what the plants and films can spare.

Nothing here flourishes by expansion. To live in this chamber is to remain within measure.

Its inhabitants have adapted not to change but to constancy. They know no flood, drought, frost, noon, night, or season—only perpetual warm damp, fixed light, and the pressure of sound. They do not wait for the pumps to stop. The roar is the climate inside which their lives are shaped. Silence, if it ever arrived, would be their first catastrophe.
