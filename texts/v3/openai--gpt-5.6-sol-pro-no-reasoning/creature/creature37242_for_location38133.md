The inhabitants of the notch are not numerous, and not all of them are alive in the same sense.

Most visible are the basalt lizards. They are narrow as cracks and nearly indistinguishable from wet stone, with limbs so fine that they seem drawn onto the rock rather than resting upon it. Sunlight does not attract them. They gather instead in the secondary shadows of the seven posts—the pale, inward-pointing shadows that persist beneath clouds. There they lie with their throats pulsing slowly, absorbing something other than heat. When frightened, they slip into fissures smaller than their skulls. The skins they leave behind are seamless, warm, and complete, less like cast-off coverings than replicas from which the animal has somehow been removed.

At dawn, black insects appear on the warm pools. Their feet touch the surface in six points, but the impressions spread into precise hexagons, briefly tessellating the water around them. A whole colony can cover a spring in diagrams before the day grows hot. They never cross the central circle. When rain approaches, they fold their legs beneath themselves and sink through the surface without making ripples. Whether they shelter in the mud or pass somewhere else is unknown.

The long-legged marsh bird is the notch’s only regular visitor from the world beyond it. Gray-bodied and orange-throated, it arrives alone and leaves alone. It has learned to hunt the things beneath the mud, though perhaps “hunt” is too simple a word. Standing beside a spring, it waits until the submerged life imitates the pressure and arrangement of its feet. Four pale points rise under the silt. The bird then strikes between them.

What it sometimes extracts is translucent and jointed, with too many small articulations and no visible organs. Out of the mud it flexes into angles rather than curves. In air it soon slackens, leaking clear water from every joint, and the bird swallows it quickly. At other times the bird strikes, finds nothing, and flees at once—as if the imitation below has been made by something too large, or by something that has learned the bird imperfectly.

There are plants, though they behave less like a community than like separate experiments. The reeds are built for divided climates: waxed against boiling vapor, silver-ribbed against fierce light, rooted in cool channels threaded through hot mud. Red ferns crowd the breathing cracks and close when touched by ordinary rain, opening only to droplets that have condensed directly from the cliff. Orchids grow on unsupported stones above the central water. Their roots hang upward as often as downward, and their flowers turn not toward the sun but toward whichever spring will surge next.

The glass-rooted shrubs on the ledges are more watchful. Their transparent roots remain exposed, delicately gripping the black rock. During thunder they tighten. During the basin’s absolute silences, they release the cliff altogether and stand balanced on their own root tips until the marsh becomes shallow again. Their berries, when present, contain warm mineral grains instead of seeds.

Algae inhabit the cold vents in brilliant green mats. They never spread beyond boundaries invisible to the eye, though fragments placed elsewhere remain alive for days, straining back toward their original patch. Eventually each loose fragment narrows into a green thread and disappears into the mud, as if returning by a route beneath the surface.

Yet these are only the shallow inhabitants.

Below the marsh lives—or occurs—a greater presence. It is glimpsed only when depth changes without displacement, when the black mud falls away and the pools become clear abysses. The slow lights far below may be attached to it, may surround it, or may be apertures through it. No observer has seen enough at once to decide. The suspended stones seem to belong to its scale: not debris hanging in water, but markers, organs, eggs, or instruments arranged around an immensity.

It does not rise. Rising may not be a movement available to it. Instead, the marsh occasionally becomes deep enough to contain it.

Its passage is suggested by broad interruptions among the submerged lights and by pressures felt through bare rock. Sometimes it imitates the marsh bird’s nonexistent cry. Sometimes it imitates footsteps, though late, and continues making them after the traveler has stopped. The translucent creatures in the mud may be parasites shed from it, exploratory limbs, or merely local animals altered by proximity.

The seven posts appear to attend this presence. Their inward shadows rest on the central pool like seven narrow senses. When the deep note passes through the basin, the posts answer in sequence, and something below seems to alter course. The roofless shelter may record these adjustments in condensation: branching movements, nested enclosures, small blocks of notation. Its hidden drain carries the finished diagrams away before they can be understood.

The white arch serves another aspect of the same ecology. Nothing visible passes through it, but time does not cross cleanly. Those who walk beneath acquire a slight separation from their own sounds. Near the central pool, this separation grows large enough that a person may hear where they were a moment ago. Perhaps the builder of the arch was narrow and tall. Perhaps that is merely the shape required for a passage through which sequence, rather than matter, is meant to travel.

The makers of the structures may still inhabit the notch, but not as bodies. They may persist as the discipline imposed upon it: the borders moss cannot cross, the channels that reject silt, the nightly removal of fallen leaves, the small corrections that prevent the landscape from aging. Their unfinished garden remains under cultivation. Heat is portioned into rooms. Water is caught above water. New rock is brought up clean and sharp. Decay is allowed only where it serves some design.

If there is a gardener, it is distributed among these processes. It lives in the spring that reverses, in the post that can be dented but not cut, in the unseen drain, in the rule that keeps vines from the arch. It may be slowly assembling the notch—or maintaining it at the one moment before assembly, when every space is open and every choice still possible.

Travelers do not become inhabitants merely by staying. The place seems reluctant to keep ordinary creatures for long. But it can retain other portions of them.

A dream may remain on the shelter wall, branching in condensation after its dreamer has departed. A voice may be taken by water that reflects clouds absent from the sky. Footsteps may continue inward after the feet themselves have climbed beyond the rim. Perhaps, over years, these remnants gather somewhere beneath the central circle: unfinished crossings, delayed sounds, diagrams of sleep, all descending among the lights.

Thus the notch is inhabited by lizards without exits, insects that write on water, plants confined to invisible plots, a mute bird whose call comes from below, and an immense life that can be approached only when shallowness ceases to mean anything.

And it is inhabited, faintly, by everyone who has left it.

Not their ghosts. Only the parts of them that have not yet finished passing through.
