They are difficult to separate from where they live.

What passes for an inhabitant may hold itself together for an hour, a season, or only for the duration of a crossing. It may have organs and limbs. It may instead consist of a pressure moving through root mats, a darkening that travels from pool to pool, or a pattern of mineral grains repeatedly assembled beneath different water. The basin permits bodies, but does not insist upon them.

The smallest creatures are almost conventional.

Under the surface films graze broad, transparent animals no thicker than leaves. Their many legs do not push against mud or water. Each touches down on a separate tension line in the film, so that the creature proceeds by redistributing strain around itself. A chain of black chambers runs through its center. These chambers pulse out of sequence—frontward, backward, then from both ends toward the middle—drawing dissolved metals from the water and compacting them into temporary nerves. When threatened, the animal does not flee. Its organs separate, slipping away along different conductive branches, while the empty body becomes indistinguishable from the surface. Hours later the dark chain may re-form in another pool.

The airborne things are similarly provisional. Their amber husks are not discarded skins but anchors: hard, dry memories of where the animal has been. From each husk extends a filament too fine to see except when condensation gathers on it. During heavy weather, a pale knot of living tissue travels these lines, borrowing droplets as mass. It grows large in transit and small on arrival. What resembles flight is therefore a circulation through a web whose stations remain fixed beneath leaves.

A single animal may have hundreds of husks and no permanent center.

The groves host slower lives. Between the interlocking scales of the jointed stems dwell colonies of white, ciliated threads. They feed on the spiraling runoff and exchange signals through the plant’s conductive rind. Their coordinated contractions produce the waves of bending that pass from grove to grove. Yet the colony is not confined to any particular stand. Rain carries portions of it into the marsh, where they lie dormant among crystals until another sequence of pulses calls them upward.

When such a wave passes through a larger animal—or a person—it briefly recruits the fluids of that body into its own signaling system. The tilt of the horizon is not an illusion. For an instant the colony has used the inner ear as one more wet chamber in which to turn.

Larger creatures are less securely material.

The long volume that crosses between reed beds has no stable exterior. Close to it, one might see curtains of rain ending against a surface that cannot otherwise be found. Mud depresses before it but rebounds before the weight has passed. Rushes disappear within its breadth and emerge wet with mineral solutions absent from the surrounding marsh. The mineral chord descends wherever the creature overlaps the basin, suggesting enormous mass, though the water beneath it bears almost none.

It may be composed of omission: a region from which local events are excluded.

Its feeding is visible afterward. Pools near its route lose their familiar reflections and open instead upon pale weather elsewhere. For several hours they offer depth into another tract of basin, and along that borrowed distance dark shapes gather at the water’s edge. By the time the original sky returns, certain dissolved salts are gone, along with small portions of the basin’s remembered drainage. The creature consumes not flesh but established routes—old falls of rain, habitual currents, reliable correspondences between a place and its reflection.

Where it has fed repeatedly, the land becomes strangely immediate. Water falls only into the pool directly beneath it. Sounds come from their present sources. Distances remain fixed. Such patches are barren.

Other large inhabitants may never enter the same sequence of time as an observer. Their presence appears as coordinated aftermath: a row of reeds already bowed, a run of warm water through cold stone, several carcasses decayed to different stages although they died together. Some are known only by paired impressions in sediment, each impression centuries apart yet belonging to the same step. Whatever made them walks very slowly across chronology and quite rapidly across land.

The plants are not passive scenery around these lives. Root rafts constitute much of the basin’s memory. Sugars carry not merely nourishment but timing; salts encode the order in which weather occurred. A mat can retain the ionic shape of a storm long after its water has drained, then transmit that shape outward until clouds gather along the remembered contours. The broad floating leaves serve as sensory surfaces, comparing present rain against stored rain. When discrepancies become too great, they rotate, wrinkle, or sink, altering the conductive films above them.

The curled white fronds beneath ledges are their short-term organs. Each encloses one drop and reads it while it grows: temperature, mineral burden, electric charge, and some subtler quality belonging to where the water expects to fall. When the frond opens, its luminous underside sends the result into nearby moss. Thousands opening in succession amount to a thought passing along the stone.

No single plant thinks it. No single plant survives it.

The basin’s deepest inhabitant is therefore not the largest moving form, nor the hidden colonies, nor the transparent grazers. It is the distributed continuity that uses all of them.

Its nervous system is laid down nightly and dissolved each morning. Conductive sediments form its quick pathways; roots preserve slower tendencies; stone hollows serve as long memory. Suspended droplets reveal parts of its anatomy in the air, while reflections connect organs separated by ordinary distance. Its impulses can be heard as clicks, bellows, and tones conducted through teeth. Its acts resemble erosion because erosion is the scale and language in which it acts.

It has no permanent center. In one interval, its greatest activity may lie beneath a shallow pan of black water. In another, the same functional center occurs kilometers away—or in the reflected basin rather than the visible one. It migrates by changing which history the land continues.

Nor is it clear that it possesses intention. It regulates salinity, redirects water, suppresses tall growth, and discourages large fauna. It repairs breaks in its mineral webs and closes outcomes that produce instability. But these may be no more deliberate than a body clotting around a wound. If it knows anything, it knows in preferences: this channel rather than that one, this rain repeated, this distance maintained, this creature kept rare.

During the dim convergence, these lives cease to be distinct.

The transparent grazers align over submerged branches. Amber anchors tighten their invisible lines. Groves bend to pass pulses through the fluids inside every nearby organism. Root rafts release stored charge. The vast crossing creatures appear in multiple pools, each perhaps an organ, parasite, predator, or alternative body of the whole. For several minutes the basin gathers its scattered processes into one coherent metabolism.

The remote view appearing in every dark reflection is not necessarily another place. It may be the basin’s image of itself: not how it looks, but how it is connected. The moving forms in that farther expanse may be its internal traffic, magnified into animals by the inadequacy of reflection. Or they may be inhabitants of neighboring possibilities, drawn close whenever the wet network becomes complete.

When coherence loosens, individuality returns. Legs detach from films. Groves straighten. White fronds curl around new droplets. The long omissions continue their crossings. Each creature resumes the appearance of a separate life.

But separation here is a temporary arrangement, like a pool held between rains.

Everything that lives in the basin borrows a boundary from water, stone, weather, or time. Everything eventually gives it back.
