They are not wild animals, though nothing living here has known captivity.

They are the kept: whole phyla engineered alongside the pipes, basins, towers, and weather systems that sustain them. Their anatomy belongs as much to the infrastructure as valves and heat exchangers do. Every foot fits a surface. Every mouth corresponds to a port. Every migratory urge describes a route already inscribed in light, temperature, and magnetic current.

The smallest pass almost unnoticed. Transparent mites patrol microscopic seams, collecting skin flakes and spilled nutrient before either can become residue. Threadlike swimmers inhabit the dark water beneath translucent plates, sorting suspended compounds with combs of charged cilia. In cable conduits, warm-bodied vermiform creatures coil around conductors, feeding on dispensed paste and shedding excess heat through silvered membranes. They seem parasitic until one sees that the cables were built with chambers for them.

Above these move the visible multitudes: enamel-backed waders, curtain filterers, ceramic grazers, gliding canal animals, and tall sleepers in the steam. Their bodies are cleanly resolved, almost diagrammatic. Limbs branch only where needed. Joints seal against rain. Eyes, when present, are dark and simple, adapted to the luminous ceiling rather than any sun. Many bear lines, numbers, or geometric patches in their skin—not brands added after birth, but patterns regenerated with each individual.

They do not fear one another. There are alarm postures, but no panic; defensive plates, but no predators to test them. Old evolutionary gestures persist as harmless ceremonies. A herd may bunch when a shadow passes over it, though the shadow belongs to a scheduled cloud. Small creatures may flash warning colors at a larger body, which steps around them without interest. Canal giants open armored mouths lined with impressive teeth, but use them only to grip nutrient slabs or scrape replenishing mineral beds.

Hunger exists, carefully bounded. So do fatigue, discomfort, courtship, and sleep. The animals are not automata. A wader may prefer one warm seam to another. Filterers adjust themselves in the current. Juveniles play by following ripples or tapping rails to produce trembling replies from neighboring colonies. Individuals recognize familiar bodies and sometimes seek them out.

But preference never becomes scarcity, and attachment never changes the count.

Their society, where it exists, is gentle and shallow. There are no struggles over food, breeding sites, shelter, or range because all have been assigned in advance. Dominance displays survive without dominion. Courtship ends at heated sockets that accept only the necessary number of eggs. Excess reproductive matter is painlessly reclaimed. Offspring emerge in exact replacement for bodies already nearing their receiving chambers.

Death comes as a summons felt through the plates.

An aging animal pauses, lifts its head, and turns toward a route it may never previously have walked. Rails pulse beneath its feet. Apertures open ahead and close behind. It enters a warm, dim chamber scented with familiar saline, settles, and does not return. The herd neither searches nor grieves for long. Soon a juvenile appears from another part of the works carrying the same registration pattern, though not the same memories.

Whether the animals understand this recurrence is impossible to tell.

Some suggest that they do. The oldest canal dwellers linger beside fabrication outlets before new young are released. Tall quadrupeds occasionally gather around sealed receiving doors and rest their heads against the warm composite. Winged migrants interrupt their prescribed circuits to spiral over hatcheries far below, then resume their course as though an omitted portion of the route has been restored.

Their intelligence is distributed strangely. Few appear capable of manipulating objects, because there are almost no loose objects to manipulate. Nothing needs to be opened by claw or broken by jaw. Yet many read the world with extraordinary precision. They feel pressure changes through kilometers of plate. They know which feeder will open long before its mechanism moves. They navigate through identical factory districts using vibrations too faint for an unfamiliar body to detect.

Communication passes into the construction and comes back altered. A grazer stamps a sequence into a ceramic panel; another answers from beyond a refinery wall. Filterers contract in traveling bands of blue, carrying a signal down an entire canal. Flying swarms change orientation together, briefly becoming a vast reflective glyph beneath the false sky. It is difficult to know where animal communication ends and facility control begins. The rails relay tremors. The walls answer with heat. The lights adjust.

Perhaps the works listen to them.

Perhaps the works are one of them.

No central mind is visible, but awareness may inhabit the total balance: in pumps anticipating thirst, in feeders distinguishing every mouth, in weather that places rain where skins require cooling, and in fabrication systems that remember the precise chemistry of creatures gone for ages. The machinery does not command. It accommodates so perfectly that command is unnecessary.

The animals, in turn, maintain more than their own metabolism. Grazers renew sacrificial surfaces. Filterers calibrate water chemistry through their feeding. Burrowers move through inspection cavities and press flexible bodies against hairline faults. Waders draw nutrient gel from drainage slots, but also clear pressure membranes with their narrow mouths. Canal giants transport heat in their bloodlike fluids from deep reservoirs to cooler basins.

None can be separated cleanly into resident or component.

Even the apparent idlers have functions hidden at another scale. The tall steam-sleepers absorb trace compounds through their skin and return them in stabilized form. Migrants crossing the artificial sky redistribute engineered microbes held inside sealed organs. Juvenile swarms test atmospheric currents by living through them, their positions read by distant sensors. Play, migration, feeding, and rest are simultaneously behavior and maintenance.

Yet function does not exhaust them. Within the tolerances allowed by the system, personality persists.

One enamel-backed wader stands longer in rainfall than its fellows. A grazer repeatedly chooses panels whose vibration makes a low musical tone. Two winged creatures miss the turning signal to circle each other above a warm vent, then accelerate until they rejoin their flock. A canal giant rises when the scheduled evening turns the ceiling honey-colored and releases a hornlike breath no feeder or pump appears to require.

Such deviations are not punished. They are absorbed.

The world gives each creature room to vary, provided variation leaves no remainder. A missed meal is offered elsewhere. A delayed migration is corrected by later gradients. An egg placed outside its heated socket is quietly transferred through mechanisms concealed beneath the plate. Even disobedience has infrastructure waiting for it.

There may be beings deeper down that never see the luminous canopy. Pressure-vault fauna inhabit liquids too hot, dense, or chemically severe for surface life. They move through manifolds like pale hearts, opening and closing whole circulatory districts by their passage. Some are kilometers long but only centimeters thick. Others spend centuries folded inside reactor jackets, feeding on heat differentials and turning once per weather cycle.

At the greatest accessible depths, individuality may disappear altogether. Membranous colonies line the vaults, each spread across an area the size of a province. They exchange material through the machinery and may constitute a single discontinuous organism. Surface births, deaths, storms, and migrations could be sensations within them: faint patterns of demand passing over an immeasurably extended body.

Or they, too, may simply be animals following channels built to fit them.

No species knows soil. None digs in expectation of stone, root, fossil, or groundwater. To burrow is to enter a prepared cavity. To drink is to engage a measured flow. To shelter is to occupy an aperture whose dimensions have anticipated the body. Their oldest instinct is not to seek nature, but to recognize provision.

The unfamiliar visitor is provisionless and therefore fascinating.

The herds part, but nearby animals turn their heads. Waders sample water from the traveler’s footprints before cleaners arrive. Small conductive creatures touch abandoned handrails and read the warmth left there. Flyers alter formation to inspect a silhouette absent from their inherited catalogues. No attack follows. There is no category for prey, invader, or owner.

For a short time, the stranger becomes the only unscheduled thing in the world.

Then the lowlands begin their gentle work.

Moisture is recovered. Heat is balanced. Shed cells disappear into capillaries. Bent currents straighten. Creatures resume their stations, perhaps retaining a sensation, an image, or a tremor pattern for a while. If memory persists, it persists only inside living bodies, fading as those bodies enter the receiving chambers one by one.

Eventually the last animal that saw the stranger is reclaimed.

Its matter returns in other forms. Its registration marks recur. Its species continues in undiminished number beneath the warm manufactured rain. But whatever it remembered is not replaced.

That may be the one residue the planet cannot collect: not matter, not energy, but the private difference between one experience and none.

And so, among the uncountable kept creatures, there may briefly live something the machinery did not intend—a memory without a port, passed by touch along a rail, carried in the color changes of filterers, sounded once by a giant beneath the dark canal water.

A rumor of a being for whom no place had been prepared.
