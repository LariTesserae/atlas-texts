The people of the plateau call themselves, in most translations, **the Borrowers**.

The name does not imply poverty. It is a statement of law. Nothing here can be possessed for long—not water, not shelter, not a road, not even the shape of the ground beneath one’s feet. A house is borrowed from a crown-shell, a blade from a jaw, a bridge from a root’s temporary willingness. Bodies are borrowed from the living ground and eventually reclaimed by it.

They are human, or near enough that the distinction matters only to scholars on safer continents. They are long-limbed and lightly built, with broad, splayed feet and powerful toes for gripping bark, woven ladders, and springing vegetation. Their skin ranges from deep umber to a coppery black and bears pale, branching scars: deliberate inoculation marks left by symbiotic fungi cultivated during childhood. These organisms break down common spores and soften certain plant toxins before they enter the blood. The scars change color when the body is dehydrated or infected, making illness visible before other symptoms appear.

Their ears are ridged and unusually mobile. Behind the jaw lie small resonating cavities sensitive to the low calls of herd animals and the first subterranean groans of walking forests. They cannot hear the deepest sounds exactly. They feel them as pressure in the teeth, sternum, and inner ear, and describe this faculty as listening with the bones.

They are not a numerous people. The plateau permits abundance, but it punishes concentration.

A settlement commonly holds between twenty and sixty inhabitants, distributed across several crown-shells or stone ledges so that a closing leaf, internal fire, predator migration, or sudden displacement of the host tree cannot destroy everyone at once. Larger gatherings occur for only a few days. Afterward the gathering place is stripped, cleaned, fed with ash, and abandoned before the ground begins to recognize the repeated warmth of bodies.

To remain too long is to become food.

### The inhabitants of the crown-shells

The oldest permanent communities occupy the dead basal cups left where enormous branches once joined the trunks of column trees. These hollows may lie hundreds of meters above the root plain. Their floors are lined with overlapping woody ribs, each kept separate from the trunk by rollers, sliding pegs, and beds of soft fungus. Without this articulation, a growing tree would crush a dwelling in a few seasons.

Everything flexes. Floors tilt slightly during strong winds. Ladders lengthen as the trunk swells. Platforms migrate around their host by dismantlement and regrowth, so that a village may complete one circuit of its tree in several generations. The people reckon local history by position: the war on the sunward face, the birth years above the white scar, the famine when the village crossed the windward groove.

From below, such settlements are difficult to see. Their screens repeat the bark’s pale plates; their water vessels resemble fruits; their suspended pathways disappear among genuine vines. Bright colors are confined to the inward-facing walls. There, people paint with resin dyes: storm maps, migrations, kin lines, the opening sequences of flowers, and portraits of shelters that no longer exist.

Children learn to climb before they learn to walk on broad ground. Infants are carried in sling-nests made of insect silk and fibrous bark, tethered at three points so that a single broken line does not drop them. Everyone wears a waist cord from which small hooks, wedges, water bulbs, and scent masks hang. Falling is feared, but not as much as becoming stranded below when the heat rises.

Most crown dwellers descend before dawn. By noon they have returned to shade, carrying clipped shoots, insects, shellfish from leaf-cups, resins, medicinal molds, and water. Work resumes in late afternoon and often continues through the warm night, when climbing is easier and many dangerous fliers settle.

They sleep in hanging frames rather than on floors. This is partly for comfort, partly because exploratory roots respond less readily to suspended weight. Bed-lines are inspected each evening for tendrils.

### Water keepers

No occupation carries more authority than the keeping of water.

A water keeper does not merely guard filled vessels. They remember the plateau’s scattered rooms: the cup beneath a split sheath, the shaded cavity in a herd beast’s abandoned armor, the cool cistern inside a reservoir leaf, the breathing root that sweats at dawn. Their maps cannot be fixed maps, because vessels tilt, trees walk, and paths are carried sideways. Instead they maintain chains of relationships.

One cup lies two days beyond the place where the red-column herd drinks in the hot season. Another can be found by following a particular insect chorus until its pitch descends. A third exists only when three low flowers face away from one another. Directions are sung because rhythm preserves conditional knowledge better than marks on bark.

Water keepers taste almost nothing directly. They use captive filter-creatures kept in small pierced gourds. A sample is fed to these pale, many-mouthed organisms; changes in their color, posture, or secretions reveal dissolved toxins, predatory larvae, infectious spores, and ownership by larger beings. Perfectly clear water is approached with special suspicion. Water without debris is being cleaned for a reason.

The most respected keepers know how much can be taken without provoking the vessel that holds it. Some reservoir plants tolerate a daily cup but close around anyone who draws a second. Certain great grazers allow water to be siphoned from their back-gardens if their cooling fans are first cleared of parasites. Other sources must be repaid with salts, dung, blood, pollinators, or shade.

Thus water is not traded as a substance alone. It comes burdened with obligations.

### Those who walk below

The ground-going people are leaner still, scarred at shin and forearm by cutting mats and defensive stems. They travel in groups called strings, usually tied loosely together through dangerous country, though the line is designed to part under sudden strain. Their sandals are broad lattices grown from springwood. These distribute weight, obscure the warmth-pattern of footsteps, and prevent the wearer from sinking into deceptive moss.

A skilled traveler rarely looks directly at the path. They watch the surrounding vegetation: the lean of hair-fronds, the closing of pores, the direction of dust shaken from high bark. They listen for silence inside the greater noise. When insects cease together, no one asks why. The nearest climbable thing becomes the path.

Strings carry portable shade—folding fans of reflective leaf membrane—which can be planted around an injured traveler. They also carry bitter pastes to defeat the ground’s anesthetic secretions. If someone lies unconscious too long, companions must keep shifting the body, warming one patch of substrate and then another, confusing the roots about where flesh has settled.

The dead are never buried. Burial is unnecessary and dangerous, as cutting deeply into the living substrate can open heat pockets or dry fire. Most bodies are offered at designated feeding gardens some distance from habitation. Family members paint the bones with mineral resins that plants cannot absorb quickly. For years afterward, pale branchings may appear in the new growth, preserving the rough architecture of the dead.

These patterns are visited like graves. Eventually they blur.

The Borrowers say this is the second death: not when the body is consumed, but when its shape can no longer be recognized in the garden. It is mourned, though not bitterly. A geometry that vanishes in one place has entered countless mouths elsewhere.

### Herd companions

Some people live almost entirely among the great grazers.

They do not ride them in the simple sense. The animals are too tall, too heat-sensitive, and too governed by routes older than any human lineage. Instead the companions inhabit the miniature landscapes on their backs. They tend parasitic shrubs so they draw sap without sickening the host, prune cooling fans, clear blocked skin vents, and protect flowering lures from creatures that consume rather than pollinate.

In exchange, the herd carries them across distances that would be lethal on foot.

Back settlements are small—often one extended family per animal—and built in hollows of armor. Their roofs are low and flexible, their possessions secured against the host’s sudden shuddering. Water is stored not only in fruits but in cultivated bladder flowers rooted directly into the back-garden. Cooking is rare. Food is sliced thin and dried on warm plates of armor, fermented in sealed pods, or softened in mildly digestive flower cups.

The companions know their animals individually. A grazer’s identity is read in the arrangement of armor, scars, fan growth, parasites, and voice. Names are long bass melodies rather than words. Humans cannot produce them at full depth, so they play them through hollow chest-drums braced against the animal’s hide.

A herd companion’s year is a migration through weather, fruitings, births, predation grounds, and temporary rivers. They arrive at fixed settlements carrying distant pollen, medicines, gossip, tools, marriage partners, and warnings. They also carry disease. For this reason they camp downwind until local fungi have sampled their breath.

When a host dies, the family seldom leaves immediately. The body is a hill, then a garden, then a thicket. They may remain through the first enclosure, guarding valuable armor and guiding the succession of scavengers. Some founding settlements began this way: as mourners living on an animal that slowly became landscape.

### The flower-bound

Not every intelligent inhabitant is human.

Within the largest blossoms live small six-limbed beings called, approximately, **chamber folk**. Adults are no longer than a hand. Their bodies are soft, pale, and sparsely shelled, with broad feet adapted to clinging to wet inner petals. They live in colonies around the warm reproductive chambers of flowers and feed upon nectar yeasts, pollen grazers, and secretions they encourage by stroking responsive tissue.

To them, the flower is world and calendar.

Its opening brings light, heat, predators, and exchange. Its closing encloses the colony in humid darkness that may last for weeks. As the blossom matures, whole districts change function: a feeding fold dries into a roadway; a translucent wall darkens; a nectar pit becomes toxic; a ceiling opens and releases golden weather.

The chamber folk build from wax, hairs, shed shell, and hardened pollen. They farm mites. They dam nectar channels. They use the flower’s own electrical responses to signal one another by drumming coded patterns into sensitive veins.

When pollinators arrive, the event is both festival and catastrophe. Winds roar through the opening. Giants descend trailing fur, legs, and feeding organs. Pollen buries structures and clogs breathing pores. Some chamber folk climb aboard these visitors deliberately, sealed inside wax capsules. Most perish during transit. Survivors awaken in another flower, carrying tools, spores, and lineage marks.

Human communities sometimes negotiate with them. A person places patterned droplets near a flower’s rim; the chamber folk answer by arranging dark mites into signs. From them, humans obtain concentrated medicines and precise knowledge of flowering chains. In return they supply salt, cutting splinters, and evacuation during premature wilting.

Whether chamber folk understand humans as people is uncertain. In their images, a human is usually depicted not as a body but as a sequence of floods, shadows, and gifts.

### Lacquer colonies

Deeper down live the pale quarrying colonies.

An individual quarry-creature is scarcely more intelligent than an insect, but a mature colony behaves with memory, caution, and sometimes negotiation. It excavates dead layers of the living ground, consuming softened fiber and reinforcing passages with glossy pulp. The result may extend for kilometers: cool tunnels, ventilation chimneys, brood vaults, fungus chambers, waste furnaces, and resin dams holding back exploratory roots.

No single creature directs this architecture. Decisions pass through scent, vibration, temperature, and the shape of previous work. Yet colonies recognize recurring human groups. They can learn exchange sites and maintain them over generations.

People leave cracked shell, salt, ash, or infected wood beside a tunnel mouth. In return the colony may expose hardened lacquer sheets, edible fungus nodules, or cool passage into another district. A generous colony creates safe sleeping shelves. A hostile one seals entrances and heats the wall until poisonous vapor is driven outward.

Some human settlements keep a colony beneath them as one might keep livestock, sanitation, foundation, and oracle all at once. The quarryers consume dangerous rot, detect subterranean fire, and thicken weakened supports. Their behavior also reveals distant disturbance. If they wall their eastern galleries, something is approaching from the east below.

The relationship can turn abruptly. Blood spilled near a vent may provoke harvesting behavior. A colony that has tasted too many corpses can begin to classify sleeping humans as damaged material.

### Predators and their imitators

The plateau’s most successful hunters rarely chase.

Seed mimics crouch among stalk fields with their limbs folded into angular husks. Their outer shells even split in heat like genuine pods. When touched, they invert: what seemed to be a seed opens into a ring of grasping blades. Young travelers are taught to throw pebbles, but some mimics have learned that vibration and ignore anything without body heat.

Directional shriekers hunt in the column groves. They are low, fast creatures with asymmetrical skulls and throat chambers that project sound in a narrow beam. The cry stuns small prey and confuses climbers by creating false echoes. Human hunters wear mismatched ear covers against them—one dense, one hollow—so the skull does not resonate evenly.

Above, predatory ribbons steer by twisting their entire bodies. They resemble streamers of dark translucent tissue until they wrap a sail-creature and constrict its membranes. They sometimes trail migrating flocks, and the people harvest their hardened mouth-hooks from carcasses.

More feared than any obvious hunter are the **kindly leaves**: low shelter plants whose undersides are cool, dry, and free of biting insects. They accumulate evidence of safe use—shed hair, dung, discarded shells—because they may remain harmless for years. Only after sufficient animal traffic do their veins turn outward. Then the leaf closes during the hottest hour, seals its edges, and begins to digest everything beneath it.

This is why comfort is treated as information, never proof of safety.

### The old pillars’ tenants

At the cliffs, where ceramic pillars whistle in storms, live colonies of dark, narrow-winged birds. Each pair occupies one hand-sized hollow, and each hollow produces a different note as wind passes across it. The birds modify the tones by adding mud, shell, feathers, and resin to the cavity.

Over generations, a colony tunes an entire pillar.

No one knows whether the original hollows were intended as instruments, dwellings, vents, or writing. The birds have made the question harder. Their tuned pillars sing before shifts in pressure, distant storms, internal collapses, and major movements of heated air through the plateau’s deep chambers. Cliff communities listen closely.

Certain families devote their lives to interpreting the chorus. They insist the birds are not merely altering whistles at random. Colonies repeat phrases, answer neighboring pillars, and preserve tonal patterns after old birds die. During severe weather, thousands enter and leave their hollows in sequences that resemble the insect fronts of the interior.

The listeners call the birds **the Remembering Mouths**.

They feed them, but never tame them. A pillar abandoned by birds is considered more ominous than a silent forest.

### What lives deepest

Beneath the highest swell, in chambers no person has fully entered, something large shares breath with the plateau.

It may not be one creature. The rising and falling surface could be caused by heated air, flexing root vaults, fungal metabolism, or the synchronized inflation of buried organisms. Explorers have lowered weighted lines through vapor cracks and found no bottom. Lines return coated in sweet resin, blood-warm moisture, or tiny translucent eggs. Sometimes they return shortened without being cut.

The deep exhalations carry chemical signals that alter life above. Flowers open. Migrating trees turn. Insects emerge from dormancy. Herds become restless and begin moving toward regions where rain has not yet formed. Human inoculation scars darken hours before the vents open.

The Borrowers do not worship the deep chambers, though outsiders often mistake caution for worship. They speak of them as one speaks of weather, ancestry, hunger, and a sleeping animal in the same room.

They do not agree on what is below.

Some say the chambers are lungs formed accidentally by the grafting of roots.

Some say an ancient species lies there, neither plant nor animal, and the plateau has accumulated upon its back.

Some say the emptiness itself is alive, and everything above exists to feed it warmth.

The oldest crown communities tell a different story. They say there is no being beneath the land. There is only the land’s habit of answering itself. Root warns fungus, fungus warns tree, tree turns herd, herd carries seed, seed changes river, river cools chamber, chamber breathes cloud. Each response becomes the cause of another until the circle grows too wide to see.

Give that circle enough centuries, they say, and it begins to resemble thought.

Whatever the truth, the inhabitants live inside its consequences. They prune and are pruned. They hunt and distribute seeds. Their waste feeds the bridges that carry them; their dead rise as mineral patterns in leaves. Even their oldest settlements migrate around living trunks, never returning to quite the same place.

From far away, the plateau seems a single green country.

From within, it is an immeasurable multitude—every creature sheltering in another creature, every home digestible, every road alive, and every inhabitant, however clever or enormous, borrowing a little space before the space awakens.
