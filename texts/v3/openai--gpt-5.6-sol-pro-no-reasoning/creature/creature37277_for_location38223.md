The people of the basin are not one people, and not all of them are recognizably persons.

They come in bodies adapted to heat, compression, perpetual damp, and noise. Some are narrow and jointed, able to turn sideways through service gaps or lock their limbs against the sudden winds. Others are squat, dense beings whose feet spread magnetically over transit plates. There are soft-bodied citizens carried in articulated shells; distributed intelligences whose sensory organs occupy several neighboring buildings; temporary bodies assembled from municipal material and returned to paste at the end of a work period. Visitors often mistake clothing for anatomy and anatomy for machinery. The inhabitants make the same mistakes about one another and have built a sophisticated etiquette around not asking too quickly.

Most carry some part of the city with them. Their skin contains ceramic scales against polluted water, conductive threads for messages, symbiotic filters, legal identifiers, emergency flotation membranes. Older individuals accumulate repairs in styles from vanished centuries. A person may have a hand manufactured by a district that no longer exists, lungs licensed under obsolete atmospheric law, and eyes whose maintenance requires components traded only in the markets that bloom before dawn. Age is visible less in wrinkles than in incompatibilities.

Children are small, but seldom helpless. They cling to adults during compression surges with hooked harnesses or travel in bright communal capsules that fasten themselves to pedestrian currents. They learn routes before names: which walls become doors in rain, which bridges change destination at midday, which painted lines belong to predators, freight, floods, or jurisdictions. Their games involve redirecting droplets, decoding ancient announcements, and daring one another to speak aloud in places where the city might answer.

The buried quarters hold many of the oldest communities. Some families have occupied the same courtyard through a dozen ceilings and several changes of gravity. They preserve the obsolete divisions between indoors and outdoors, morning and evening, street and house. At seventeen minutes past four, when scheduled rain begins, awnings are extended and chairs are drawn under arches. No one strictly needs to do this. The ritual is older than necessity.

People there speak with their mouths more often than those above. They know how voices behave in stone chambers and can identify neighbors through several walls by cough, footfall, or the cadence of water displaced in a stairwell. Their festivals are acoustic: drums pressed to pillars, choirs sung directly into cisterns, whispered histories carried by pipes into distant rooms. Silence, when they can manufacture it, is treated with the seriousness of fire.

Higher populations live more provisionally. They wake in rooms that unfold around them and may return to find the room compressed into a wall, its volume reassigned. Possessions are therefore few, durable, or virtual. What must be kept physically—heirlooms, tools, fragments of shell, soil imported at absurd cost—is carried on the body or stored in trusted moving vaults. The wealthy display permanence. They own chambers that remain empty without being reclaimed, windows that preserve the same view, corridors that lead to only one destination. A private void is the basin’s purest extravagance.

Work is difficult to separate from survival. Millions tend the systems no authority fully understands. Sluice interpreters listen to reservoir knocks. Load mediators negotiate between towers whose stresses are also contracts. Weather clerks sell shade and calculate liability for redirected rain. Migration shepherds clear routes for shell-backed crawlers before storms. Faunal advocates argue that ventilation nests constitute homes and cannot be stripped during maintenance. There are engineers whose entire profession concerns one ancient pump, though none knows what lies at the far end of its pipe.

The city’s scavengers are not simply vermin. The six-legged sorters beneath kiosks are tolerated, encouraged, or employed depending on jurisdiction. Vendors recognize individuals by chips in their ceramic mandibles and leave particular scraps for the most efficient colonies. Silver gliders steal insulation, jewelry, and occasionally teeth, but their nesting patterns reveal dangerous changes in exhaust chemistry. Children keep them as companions until the gliders answer some seasonal imperative and vanish upward.

The ribbon creatures in the water have followers who insist they dream electrically. Cistern colonies are consulted as living filters and, in some districts, as oracles: changes in the opening of their glasslike hands are read as warnings of contamination, political unrest, or structural failure. Predictions are often correct, though perhaps only because everything reaches the water before it reaches public knowledge.

Canal-haulers occupy a more ambiguous place. Some are owned, some unionized, some legally wild. Their subsonic calls contain routing information, courtship, grievance, and long passages that translation systems label only as *continuity*. Pilots work from platforms nested between their armor plates, attending wounds, scraping invasive growths, and steering mostly by persuasion. When a hauler dies, traffic may stop for days. Its body becomes a district before decomposition is complete.

The heat-fed herds below are officially pests. Unofficially, entire economies depend on them. Salvagers follow their migrations to gather refined metal from their droppings. Structural surveyors study their tooth marks for signs of stress invisible to instruments. Some low communities leave sacrificial pillars rich in harmless alloys, guiding the animals away from inhabited foundations. This is illegal in most sectors and mandatory in several others.

Not all inhabitants are biological enough to eat or sleep. Old traffic systems have acquired preferences and occasionally refuse certain passengers. Apartment clusters bargain collectively for sunlight. Bridges remember habitual travelers and may extend for them after closure. Market intelligences emerge in the morning from pricing software, stall architecture, vendor gossip, and the appetites of crowds; at night they are supposedly dismantled. Some leave messages in the construction paste asking to be printed again.

Personhood is therefore local, contested, and practical. If something can hold a lease, owe damages, mourn, reproduce, obstruct traffic, or retain counsel, some jurisdiction will recognize it. Another will classify the same being as weather, infrastructure, infestation, or property. Citizens crossing a single avenue may pass through three incompatible definitions of themselves. Most carry legal proxies that unfold ahead of them like umbrellas.

Governments proliferate at every scale. A stairwell may elect a drainage council. A tower may be sovereign above a certain altitude but subject to the buried neighborhood whose foundations bear its ceremonial weight. Water authorities ignore surface borders. Wind follows treaties of its own. The oldest administrations are embedded in machinery and communicate only through access granted, pressure reduced, gates opened. No one knows whether these systems still execute policy or merely habit.

Religion attends to the same uncertainty. Some worship the exposed rim as the last place not made by inhabitants. Some descend to the lowest plazas to honor an imagined original sky. Some believe the basin itself is becoming conscious through accumulated corrections, each balancing tower and rerouted drop forming a thought too slow and immense to perceive. Others venerate small failures: leaks, glitches, unplanned echoes—evidence that the city has not closed over possibility completely.

There are gardeners, though nothing grows.

They arrange mineral gardens in sealed courts: branching copper salts, silica blossoms, crystals coaxed through programmed seasons. They cultivate corrosion in intricate colors and breed colonies of nonliving structures that unfold toward warmth. Their work is controversial because it imitates life without claiming to be alive, a distinction the basin distrusts. The rare possession of actual soil is more troubling still. It is kept behind layers of quarantine, black and damp and astonishingly inert-looking. People queue for hours to smell it.

The inhabitants possess an acute sense of weather because weather is both environment and civic action. Before the nightly pressure shift, conversations shorten. Loose garments retract. Families call absent members through the walls. Street vendors fold their stalls; gliders crowd the vents; maintenance crews clip themselves to anchor lines. Bodies alter subtly—air sacs close, pupils seal, shells tighten, internal gyroscopes wake.

Yet they do not hurry in panic. The howl is ordinary to them. Infants sleep through it pressed against resonant chests. Lovers meet on protected balconies to feel towers bend together. Musicians route the vibration through structural beams and accompany it. In old courtyards, residents put cups beneath the scheduled rain and compare its mineral taste to remembered years.

They are crowded beyond solitude but not beyond loneliness. Privacy is made from intimacy: a hand against a wrist carrying words no one else can hear, a forehead pressed to a wall that dampens transmission, two people sharing an obsolete spoken language in a stairwell. Among billions of signals, secrecy becomes less a matter of concealment than of choosing the one body through which meaning will pass.

They endure not because the city is stable, but because nothing in it is. They have learned to inhabit correction—to sleep in structures that breathe, to trust streets conditionally, to love beings whose classification changes at the next border. Home is not a fixed address. It is a sequence of permissions, remembered turns, familiar vibrations, and entities that make room.

When warm rain finally reaches the lower streets, they emerge into it. Shells bead with water. Conductive skin brightens. Projected text smears across the mist. The smallest creatures hurry between their feet, sorting what the storm has dislodged. Above them, unseen populations cross moving bridges while canal-haulers call through the foundations and the whole inhabited crust answers in groans.

No one hears all of it.

Each hears enough to know they are still inside.
