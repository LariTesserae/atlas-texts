The people who live here have made themselves difficult to distinguish from maintenance.

They are few, perhaps, or merely dispersed. A census would fail because an address counts a volume, not a body, and both may move between one counting and the next. Names remain attached to rooms after their occupants have gone elsewhere. Families may inhabit the same house on different nights without meeting, each closing the threshold behind them and thereby drawing the room out of the municipal drift. A stranger could sleep one pace away in a chamber acoustically displaced across the city.

They own almost nothing that cannot be carried close to the body. Cord, cups, thin tools, folded membranes, packets of salt, resonant tokens used as keys. Clothing is close-wrapped and porous, designed for heat and for the long cool descents along the chamber walls. Hard ornaments are avoided because they fasten nearby space. Instead, people decorate themselves with knots, scents, mineral stains, and small tuned slivers that identify them by vibration. A household announces itself not with a crest but with a chord.

Their feet are broad and sensitive. Whether this is inherited or trained is hard to say. Children learn the paving before they learn signs: the pressure grammar of crossings, warnings, district boundaries, and approaching transit. Adults pause with one heel raised, listening through the other leg. Conversation is often conducted by fingers against railings or wrists pressed together. Spoken language is reserved for intimacy, argument, and places where the city’s noise has become too dense for finer signals.

They do not think of darkness as concealment. To them, a person is hidden when they make no vibration.

Silence is therefore dangerous. During pump failures, the inhabitants emerge carrying hammers and weighted cords, striking columns in measured succession. They keep the sounding animals aloft, but they also keep streets legible and drifting rooms from becoming wholly lost. In the deepest outages, whole neighborhoods gather around tuned plates and sustain a communal pulse until machinery resumes. No one speaks during this labor. The rhythm itself assigns tasks, records fatigue, and calls for relief.

Their homes are habits imposed on vacancy. Four posts might mark a sleeping place, but the true dwelling is maintained by repeated passage: the same hand along an absent wall, the same cup set at the same distance from a threshold, the same door closed before rest. Beds are hammocks of altered gravity or warm planes that resist the body without touching it. Storage consists of pockets persuaded not to coincide with the street. Cooking is done over heat faults, in vessels hung from rigid intervals.

They sleep lightly. An unobserved room may migrate, but dreams do not count as observation.

Some households appoint a watcher who remains awake beside the door. Others keep lizards, whose unwavering attention is believed to stabilize small spaces. The lizards may in fact do nothing. Still, old dwellings often contain a warm stone niche near the threshold, and a family whose animal vanishes will not sleep until it returns or a new room has been established.

Children are taught never to stare continuously into an open doorway. Observation fixes what is beyond, but it also fixes the observer in relation to it. There are stories of stubborn children found years later still watching a room that had begun to move, unable to turn away without releasing it into some unknown district.

The inhabitants distinguish many professions that outsiders would call one thing: keeper.

There are **intervalwrights**, who inspect the tensions holding stairs, bridges, and hollow towers in shape. They carry loops, plumb threads, and sealed measures of nothingness. Their work is done by touch. A damaged span may feel soft, crowded, or faintly tilted even when it appears unchanged.

There are **listeners of return**, who map the chamber by echoes and detect new fractures in the ceiling. They can tell whether a detonation has passed around the eastern wall or through a relocated room. Before storms, they climb the highest arcs and wait for the first alteration in the stone’s voice.

There are **water stewards**, who sweep cisterns, ration the brief rivers, and wake the ceramic veins after rain. They harvest the black spores of the pale reeds before the stalks collapse. Spore paste, bitter and mineral-rich, is one of the city’s few native foods.

There are **door tenders**, called when a neglected volume must be recovered. They follow traces of heat, damp, scent, and misplaced reverberation, opening and closing thresholds in carefully timed sequences until the lost room intersects the city again. Sometimes its contents return intact. Sometimes it contains years of accumulated condensation, an unfamiliar stair, or the belongings of someone not yet missing.

There are also custodians of the hospital’s sterile air. They patrol an invisible district with bare, washed hands held before them, feeling for dust. Illness here is treated by moving the patient rather than the medicine: fever rooms are brought near cool wall currents; wounds are stabilized in spaces with little internal change; contagious people are placed inside volumes whose thresholds are allowed to drift away from inhabited routes. Recovery depends on finding those rooms again.

The sounding animals live closest to the machinery and are regarded neither as pets nor wildlife. The people call them by a word that also means *carried message*. Their ribbon bodies are nearly weightless, with rows of pressure-sensitive pits along their sides. When the city thunders, they ride the expanding fronts in loops and sudden angles. Certain individuals become accustomed to particular civic rhythms and migrate between pumps, gates, and transit conduits. Listeners watch their flight to diagnose failures. A ribbon sinking in an active street is a grave warning.

They breed in abandoned towers, laying strands of dark eggs along gradients of tension. The young are transparent and can remain aloft on footsteps. Children run beneath them during wet season, stamping elaborate patterns to draw them lower.

The warm lizards belong to another tempo. They gather heat for weeks beside the faults, digesting almost nothing, their skin pink-gray and faintly translucent. When the temporary insects hatch, the lizards wake together. Their migration crosses stairs, private rooms, transit platforms, and bridges without regard for ownership. No one stops them. Their routes are older than several districts, and buildings subtly rearrange to leave those routes open.

Food arrives from more places than can be seen. Deep root systems draw minerals and water from aquifers. The two silver trees produce small hard fruits at irregular intervals, each containing a dense white kernel. Fungal sheets are cultivated on the undersides of drainage channels. Insects are gathered after mating flights. Warm cisterns hold blind crustaceans. Some transit capsules carry compressed provisions from surface stations, though no one admits to operating those stations. A capsule may arrive after months of silence, open onto an empty platform, and contain grain, machine parts, or only desert sand.

The people treat such deliveries as municipal weather.

They measure time by sound cycles rather than daylight: pump-shifts, gate returns, cooling reports, the seventeen-minute exchange beneath the plaza. Longer time is marked by water. Children born between seepages are said to be of one rain, even if years divide them. Age is counted in the great wet seasons when the central bowl fills and the lens rises over the aperture.

During those hours, nearly everyone becomes visible.

They come down the sloping avenues carrying cups, cords, seed packets, tools, and doors without walls. The nine stations around the plaza are opened, though they remain officially unoccupied. Each receives a different civic guild. The people stand barefoot in the accumulating water and feel the machinery change through the polished stone. Sounding animals spiral above the black aperture. Reeds split the mineral crust.

No speeches can survive the thunder, so the gathering is conducted through contact. Hands join along rails. Messages pass through the crowd as pressure and rhythm. Debts are released. Threshold claims are renewed. The names of the missing are struck into the paving, not as words but as recognizable patterns of absence.

When the water lifts into its trembling lens, the inhabitants hold their containers upside down. To catch the first fall would be selfish; that water still belongs to the city. They wait for the collapse upward, for the chamber roof to receive what the floor has gathered.

Then the second rain comes.

For those few moments, every hidden volume is outlined by water. Droplets break over invisible roofs, run down absent walls, bead along bridges no eye could follow, and hang from the edges of rooms thought lost. The entire settlement reveals itself as a glittering anatomy of engineered emptiness.

The people look up.

This is one of the rare occasions when observation is communal enough to hold the whole city still. Lost houses intersect old addresses. Wandering corridors return. Doors open onto their intended rooms. Figures separated by acoustic distance find themselves suddenly adjacent.

Then the rain thins, the outlines vanish, and the inhabitants begin again—drinking, gathering spores, repairing intervals, releasing what must be allowed to drift.

By the time the streets are dry, almost everyone has disappeared.

Only a warm cup remains on a sill without walls, placed there deliberately: a small, ordinary anchor, holding one corner of the city in place.
