The basin is inhabited chiefly by a low, collective life: moss, liverwort, sedge, algae, fungus, and root. No single plant dominates by stature. Together they make something like a horizontal organism, thin but immense, stretched across terraces and spoil fields, filling wheel ruts, sealing ditches, testing every fracture in the mine.

Its oldest body is buried beneath itself. Each living green surface rests on generations of stems that the cold prevented from rotting. The dead remain useful: pressed into dark felt, they hold water, catch coal dust, and give purchase to new roots. Thus the inhabitant slowly manufactures the soil it lacks. It advances without visibly moving, raising the floor by fibres and fractions.

The moss is its skin. It is dark, compact, and tougher than it appears, silvering beneath shaft light and releasing fine dust when pressure booms through the roof. Drops from the frozen slab perforate it with tiny wells. Around warm pipes it brightens; near the strongest ducts it grows flattened in the direction of the forced air, as though combed by a permanent storm.

Liverworts occupy the wet seams and drainage channels. Pale grasses lie close to the ground, unwilling or unable to rise against the cold current. Horsetails stand at the black ponds in sparse, jointed ranks. Beneath the ice, algae persist as green veils, lit so faintly that they seem less to grow than to remember light.

Fungus inhabits what appears empty. Its threads pass through shale cracks and compressed peat, joining isolated patches beneath bare surfaces. Root hairs accompany it into coal dust, around buried bolts, and under concrete lips. What looks like open ground is often only the exposed back of a hidden network.

Most remarkable are the willows above. Their trunks belong to the northern plain, but their roots have entered the mine and become another kind of forest. They descend through roof fractures, pale and branching, then fuse into hanging groves, walls, ramps, and shelves. They clasp handrails and cable supports. They pass through conveyor trusses and around pipes, accepting the mine’s geometry as a trellis. In places they are strong enough to carry a person, though they tremble constantly with the fans.

This plant world has no intention, but it has habits. It follows seepage. It thickens where condensation falls. It retreats from bare, poisoned stone and returns after moss has filtered the runoff. It stabilizes some walls while prying others apart. It blocks culverts, reinforces spoil slopes, catches industrial dust, and turns abandoned machines into damp islands of shelter. It is both caretaker and obstruction.

Among it live smaller, more distinct creatures.

Springtails gather in such numbers at meltwater margins that the ice appears peppered with soot. Pale moths haunt transformer cages and electrical boxes, resting with folded wings in the little halos of waste heat. Beetles move through the peat with extraordinary slowness, black and lustrous, nearly indistinguishable from droplets of oily water. They survive on fungal growth, softened plant matter, and whatever minute bodies arrive through the shafts.

A vole sometimes comes down from the plain along a root curtain. Its presence is known less by sight than by marks: paired tracks crossing frost, a grass blade bitten cleanly through, a hollow pressed into moss near a warm conduit. It may live below for days or weeks, navigating by root walls and concrete edges, before climbing back toward weather.

Birds arrive by accident. A wren driven into a shaft by winter can find insects around the lamps and nest in an empty junction box. It sings because it is a wren, though the basin cannot hear it. Its beak opens, its throat pulses, and the fan roar removes the song before it travels even the length of its body. Such a bird learns to watch vibration rather than listen. It freezes motionless when louvers open, then resumes its quick, private life.

The fox of the western galleries may be real. If so, it is a lean transient rather than a subterranean sovereign. It follows maintenance roads and root ramps, drinks from iron ponds, and sleeps in the insulated lee of abandoned huts. Its tracks endure for weeks in the frost, so every crossing becomes several stories: one animal multiplied by time, glimpsed nowhere but inferred everywhere.

Humans live here only temporarily and by procedure. They enter wrapped in thermal layers, faces enclosed by respirators or hearing protection, lamps fixed to their helmets. Gas meters blink on their chests. Radios turn their voices into close, artificial speech while the open air annihilates ordinary conversation. They travel in pairs through numbered gates, pausing at valves, pump housings, transformer cages, and measuring points.

Their true habitat is not the whole basin but a narrow web laid across it: amber lamps, inspection roads, handrails, relay stations, emergency cabinets, and painted doors. Outside that web they are visitors. Even within it, they appear fragile—brief sparks passing through a scale of darkness they maintain but do not command.

They tend the greater mechanical inhabitants.

The fans occupy chambers around the perimeter, hidden behind grilles and ducts, yet their presence fills every part of the mine. They do not resemble animals, but the basin experiences them as immense respiratory organs. They draw, press, expel, and draw again. Their motors are replaced; their bearings wear; their housings acquire welded braces and generations of grease. No individual fan is immortal, but the breathing continues.

That breath shapes all life below. It carries gases out and carbon dioxide, dust, spores, and faint seasonal warmth inward. It bends grass into fixed arcs, shakes droplets from roots, and roughens pond ice with continuous trembling. It teaches moths where warmth is, dries some moss beds, feeds others with condensation, and determines where a small creature can rest without being battered by moving air.

The pumps are slower creatures. They thud in distant houses and keep drowned levels from rising into the maintained world. Pipes carry their hidden circulation through curtains of roots. Transformers emit blue light and heat, drawing insects into their cages. Louvers wake at intervals with a metallic concussion, changing the pressure of the entire basin like a vast involuntary reflex.

Machine and plant have become difficult to separate. Roots reinforce cable trays. Moss insulates old concrete. Peat climbs pump-house steps. A bench at the Square has ceased to be merely furniture: green shoots rise through its drainage holes, and its legs descend into compressed vegetation rather than soil. Conveyor trusses support willow tissue; willow tissue supports corroding trusses. Neither system designed the arrangement, but each now bears some portion of the other.

Still, the deepest inhabitant may be the basin itself—not conscious, not unified, but active across timescales too different to notice one another.

Its stone holds cold. Its roof sweats. Frost enters cut faces and loosens them grain by grain. Water rises where pumping stops. Coal absorbs light. Peat accumulates. Metal creeps beneath vibration. Roots thicken around it. Engineers alter a line, and decades later sedge redraws the wet ground around that decision.

At the drowned edge, life becomes narrow and hesitant. Moss advances to the black water and stops in a bright, thin margin. Algae continue beneath the ice, but the rooted world cannot follow. Below lie the preserved shapes of another habitat: stairs descending nowhere breathable, pipes full of still water, signs facing blind galleries, drill marks untouched by frost or growth.

Nothing visibly lives there, yet the flooded workings are not empty. They contain cold, minerals, darkness, and whatever microscopic life can endure without light. They hold the mine’s younger geometry in suspension. Above them, drops continue to fall; the fans maintain their enormous note; roots tremble in the roof.

All the inhabitants are slow except the machines, and even the machines serve slowness. They prevent a sudden end. They keep the basin in the long interval during which moss can become peat, roots can become architecture, and an excavation can acquire a living surface without ever becoming a forest.

What lives there is therefore not one hidden species or isolated community. It is an unfinished ecology of pressure and patience: plants making ground, machines making air, people preserving the machines, insects gathering around their warmth, occasional animals borrowing the routes, and water waiting below to inherit whatever maintenance finally releases.
