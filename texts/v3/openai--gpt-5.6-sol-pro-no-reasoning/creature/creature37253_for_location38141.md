The people of the bowl are compact, broad-handed, and sure-footed. At first glance they seem bundled into shapelessness: pale felt, layered hides, skirts split for climbing, hoods drawn beneath slotted visors of bone. Seen indoors, they are leaner than their clothing suggests. Their chests are deep from a lifetime in thin air, their shoulders developed unevenly by hauling stone and bracing themselves in narrow passages. Most have toes spread wide and strong, and crooked fingers hardened by cold water, lime, and rope.

They walk with a peculiar patience. A foot is placed, tested, and only then trusted with weight. One hand trails along the nearest wall, not merely for balance but to feel what travels through it. Even children rarely run without first touching the ground. Outsiders mistake this for timidity until they see a resident cross a collapsing roof by three sagging beams, a sluice lip, and the back of an unperturbed goat.

Brightness has marked them. Their faces are creased early around the eyes; their pupils contract to black pinpricks outdoors. Many have pale scars across the cheeks where reflected light burned them in childhood, before they learned discipline. Their bone visors are personal objects, fitted close and carved with narrow horizontal slits. From a distance the visors make a gathering of townspeople resemble a flock of quiet, expressionless birds. Up close, each is different. Some are inlaid with horn or copper foil; some carry generations of repairs. A child receives a first visor after learning to identify blue ice, a dry channel, and a stair that hums.

They do not think of themselves chiefly as families, trades, or neighborhoods. They belong to flows.

A household is named for the water passing through it: the Thread Under the Oven, the Three Wall Tears, the Warm Eastern Drop. If that water vanishes for good, the name is retired even if the bloodline continues. Marriage cords knot two flows together. Feuds are remembered by the channels diverted during them. The dead are described not by where they lived, but by which springs they could hear.

No one among them is entirely unskilled. A cook knows how to shore a lintel. A mason can read the behavior of martins. A child carrying broth also carries two wedges and a plug of red sedge. Repair is not treated as a profession separate from life. It is etiquette, worship, defense, and conversation with the mountain all at once.

Still, some inhabitants are set apart.

The masons are often the youngest strong adults, because the work demands speed more than age. They wear aprons stiff with lime and keep their tools polished straight, as though straightness were a sacred property too fragile to expose carelessly. Their mallets are wrapped in layers of wet felt. They strike with small, controlled motions and listen after every blow. A skilled mason is praised not for raising a durable wall—such a claim would be considered vain—but for building one that fails gently.

Channel keepers are thinner, wetter, and rarely still. Their sleeves end above the elbow even in bitter weather. Mineral stains map their arms: white crust at the wrists, rust-red fans near the inner elbows, translucent blue patches where untreated water has begun to alter the skin’s outer layers. They carry stacks of clay lips, horn scoops, sedge seals, plugs, cords, and flat stones. They know the temperament of water by touch. Some can place two fingers in a stream and tell whether a spring has opened three houses upslope.

Animal watchers concern themselves with creatures that perceive changes before humans do. They sleep among goats, maintain finch vents, inspect stoat paths, and learn the moods of the shaggy milk beasts. They are judged by attentiveness rather than obedience: an animal that refuses them may be warning them. The best watchers have an air of distracted listening, forever noticing some departure or silence at the edge of the room.

Water readers tend the blind fish. Their chambers smell of wet horn and deep stone. They lift the fish into shallow bowls and turn them beneath cold lamps, studying the shadow-patterns under their skin. These markings are copied not with ink but with lengths of dark thread laid upon white felt, because a drawn line is thought too rigid for a living forecast. Readers rarely issue commands. They say, “The lower wall may wish to open,” or, “The western rooms should become lighter.” Everyone understands.

Above these trades, though not precisely ruling them, are the three listeners.

The listeners are old because listening takes decades to learn and because anyone too eager to interpret the mountain does not remain a listener long. Their cheeks are flattened and callused from stone. Their hearing in open air is often poor, yet through a floor they can distinguish a blocked fissure from a contracting foundation, or so the town believes. They spend hours lying motionless under blankets, one ear pressed to the oldest masonry. Assistants feed them warm milk through reed tubes so they need not lift their heads.

They are not oracles. They argue constantly, but in signs: a hooked finger for hollow water, a closed fist for pressure, two knuckles walking for an echo that has learned movement. When the mountain repeats voices, the listeners decide whether the sound is merely returning or attempting to lead.

Speech exists, but the town’s spoken language is restrained and breath-efficient. Its words are short. Much of grammar belongs to the hands. A turn of the wrist can indicate uphill, ancestral, unstable, or all three together depending on context. Fingers flicked toward the floor mean that a route is passing beneath the speaker. Two palms held apart describe not size but the likelihood that something will remain where it is. Names are seldom spoken outside. To attract attention, people tap their own collarbone or send a vibration along a shared rail.

Children learn silence early, but theirs is not a joyless upbringing. They play games of balance, prediction, and rerouting. They race droplets down sloped tiles. They hide polished pebbles in trickling channels and wager where they will emerge. They build tiny houses beside new springs and delight when the water dismantles them. One game involves standing blindfolded while companions move around a room; the blindfolded child must identify each by the pressure of their steps.

The most serious childhood game is called Still Water. At a signal, everyone must freeze and find the safest path away from the Bright Well without touching any liquid surface. Young children laugh while playing it. Older ones do not.

Their elderly are rarely helpless. As agility diminishes, attention deepens. Old people sit at junctions sorting cord, watching the angle of runoff, noticing which birds fail to return. Many serve as living maps, though they describe no route without first asking the time of day, the temperature of the western wall, and whether the goats have crossed the upper roofs. To ask, “How do I reach the market?” is meaningless. One asks, “How is the market reached now?”

Their bodies do not always remain wholly human in appearance.

Long exposure to the mountain’s water leaves changes. The blue rind that softens untended stone sometimes grows on nails, teeth, or the edges of old scars. On most people it can be scrubbed away with lime and fat. On some it returns beneath the skin as a faint translucence. Their fingertips shine blue when held against a lamp. Their joints become unusually flexible. The oldest channel keepers may possess voices with a watery doubling, as though each word has already begun to echo.

Such people are called the Tempered. They are neither shunned nor revered, but watched with tenderness and unease. They can endure freezing runoff longer than others and often sense when a wall is about to liquefy. They also tend to sleepwalk toward the Bright Well. At night their families secure them not with knots—which could tighten dangerously—but with braided sedge cords designed to break loudly and wake the household.

The town’s other inhabitants have likewise become particular to it.

The cliff-martins are white not merely in feather but in beak, claw, and eye. They build nests over nests until an eave becomes a hanging district of mud chambers. Their colonies maintain their own routes through town, and residents preserve small apertures for them in new construction. When the birds swarm, they do not cry. Their wings make the soft roar of distant water.

Water crows are solemn, long-shanked birds with dark translucent legs. They stand across narrow gutters, one foot on either side, and spear larvae from currents without wetting their breasts. They have learned to follow channel keepers and will wait beside a newly opened spring before anyone else arrives. A crow that abandons feeding and stares into a wall is given immediate attention.

The finches are no larger than fists and nearly spherical under their insulating feathers. They enter vents tail-first, disappearing into warmth until only their beaks remain visible. Their sudden evacuations are among the town’s most reliable alarms. Families count them at dawn.

The blue-gray goats are less livestock than fellow citizens. Their divided hooves can spread around wet stone, cling to limewash, and close upon narrow rails. They travel rooflines inaccessible to people and choose sleeping places according to subterranean warmth. It is common to find a household gathered solemnly in a stranger’s pantry because its oldest goat insisted on spending the night there.

The shaggy milk beasts are broad, low, and slow-eyed, with coats hanging to the floor in damp cords. Their body heat is substantial enough that winter sleeping rooms are constructed around them. Children crawl beneath their bellies during pressure storms. They dislike the Bright Well and will not face it, even when dragged.

Pale stoats pass through the drains like lengths of spilled milk. They steal eggs, hair, fish, and bits of red sedge, but are tolerated because they vacate threatened channels early. Broad-faced rodents live in wall hollows and weave human hair into astonishingly neat nests. People leave combings for them in designated crevices. A rodent nest abandoned overnight is treated as a structural report.

In the gutters, colorless shrimp and adhesive worms form the small, ceaseless population upon which the larger signs depend. Children know every variety. Shrimp that swim backward indicate a deep pressure turn. Worms clustered at the upper edge of a sluice announce mineral bitterness. Neither is eaten except in famine, and even then only after apology.

The patterned fish are regarded most carefully of all. Blind and slow, they seem at first nearly blank, but beneath their skin lie gray lines like plans for rooms that do not yet exist. The lines shift as water shifts in the mountain. Occasionally a fish develops the unmistakable outline of a stair, arch, or sealed doorway days before the Bright Well produces one.

No patterned fish is deliberately killed. Those found dead are sealed in translucent ice and placed in niches facing the well. Sometimes, after many seasons, the ice is discovered empty.

What lives deepest in the town is harder to name.

The inhabitants do not say the mountain is alive. Life is too simple a category. Animals live; people live; moss lives. The mountain retains. It repeats. It revises.

They suspect that sound descends through the fissures and becomes something like seed. A hammer blow may germinate as a knock beneath a cistern. A spoken name may divide into whispers. A day may be absorbed whole and returned years later, stripped of sequence. This is why they avoid shouting, but also why they speak gently to new walls. While applying plaster, masons murmur harmless phrases directly into it: weather observations, counting rhymes, recipes. If the mountain must repeat something, they prefer it repeat what cannot lure anyone away.

The rooms grown by the Bright Well strengthen this suspicion. They arrive furnished according to no living custom. Chairs are shaped for bodies with backward knees or no knees at all. Beds contain shallow channels instead of blankets. Shelves hold stone vessels sealed shut and too cold to touch. Doorways open onto walls; windows face inward; stairs descend three steps and become ceilings.

Yet these rooms weather into usefulness. A chair is cut apart for braces. A vessel becomes a gutter weight. A window is opened through. Within a generation, what rose impossible from the well has been worn smooth by shoulders and named for the water crossing it.

Very rarely, something is found inhabiting a new room.

Usually it is small: a clutch of transparent eggs, a damp nest of braided mineral fibers, pale insects arranged in a circle. Once, the records of knotted cord say, a room emerged containing a sleeping old woman whose face resembled three living children from unrelated households. She woke, drank from the nearest channel, and knew every route in town—but all of them as they had been centuries before. She lived for nine days and spent them correcting songs no one realized were incomplete.

Since then, during every upward growth, searchers enter the new architecture before it hardens. They carry dim fat lamps, felt-wrapped tools, warm milk, and a child’s visor. They are prepared to find a creature, a person, or some unfinished resident not yet suited to the light.

Most inhabitants believe the well does not create. It remembers forward and backward without distinguishing between them. The chicks rising where adult martins descended may be those birds made young, or their descendants, or birds from a year that has not occurred. The pale fish falling upward may bear patterns of structures the town has already lost. A swallowed sleeper may emerge one day from a chamber not yet grown.

This belief shapes grief. The dead are not said to be gone. They are said to have entered a route the living cannot presently use.

Funerals are quiet even by the town’s standards. Bodies are washed in moving water, wrapped in felt, and laid briefly beside a warm channel. Hair is cut for nesting rodents. A personal tool is straightened as well as possible and then bent across the knee, acknowledging that its work is finished. The body is not cast into the Bright Well. The well requires no offerings and interprets gifts according to motives of its own. Instead, the dead are sealed into dry wall niches where the mountain may hear them without immediately taking them.

Sometimes, years later, a familiar voice issues from a drain.

No one answers. But no one leaves at once, either.

They sit beside the opening, hands folded, listening while the water carries a laugh, a complaint about cold, the fragment of a market bargain, or a parent calling a childhood name. They remain until the repetition ends. Then they mark the drain with a loop of pale cord so others will know that memory passed there.

At evening the whole population seems briefly transformed. Visors come off as the glare lowers. Faces appear in windows, on roofs, along bridges and ladders. Birds settle around them. Goats stand with legs braced. The shaggy beasts turn their broad heads uphill. Even the stoats pause at drain mouths.

In that suspended light, the town’s inhabitants do not look like people enduring an impossible place. They look like one distributed creature: thousands of hands resting on ropes and walls, thousands of eyes reading water, feathers, ice, and one another; a creature with stone for its slow bones, channels for its blood, animals for its finer senses, and the listeners laid against its oldest heart.

Then darkness closes the distances between them.

The people put their visors away, light their dim bowls, and resume the work that has no completion. They divert springs. They lift sleeping children from rooms the goats distrust. They brace old walls and dismantle new ones. They listen to sounds that may have been made tomorrow or a hundred winters ago.

And beneath them, within them, the mountain listens back.
