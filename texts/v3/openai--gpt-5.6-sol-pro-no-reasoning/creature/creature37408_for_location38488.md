The people who live in the hanging valley do so only in fragments.

They arrive after the lower forests have leafed out, when the glacier’s stream can be heard beneath its winter lid and the narrow passes cease shedding snow at every footfall. They depart before the first lasting storm. No one is born there if it can be helped, and no one willingly dies there. The valley is not a village or pasture, though it serves at times as both. It is a meeting place, a hunting ground, a shrine, and—most importantly—a keeper of things that have just happened.

Those who return to it are small, spare people, made narrow by climbing. Their chests are deep, their calves corded, their toes spread from a lifetime in soft hide shoes. Sun and wind darken their faces except around the eyes, where glare masks leave pale bands. They wear layered felt and woven grass beneath close-fitting skins, with short cloaks that cannot catch on stone. Everything carried above the tree line has several uses. A sling becomes a belt; a sleeping hide becomes a sack; horn toggles serve as hooks, needles, gaming pieces, and offerings.

They call themselves no single name in the valley. Families from several lower ravines use it, and each has its own word for the place: the High Green, the Cup Between Knives, the Meadow Above Weather. Yet all call its pools the Afterwaters.

They know the delay in the reflections is real.

Children are taught never to wave over still water, never to count the images trapped under new ice, and never to break a frozen pool while it is still giving back the sun. The prohibitions are practical in the way old sacred laws often are: a child staring into a pool does not watch the sky; someone kneeling on thin ice may fall through; a person entranced by retained light may remain outside after killing cold has entered the valley. But practicality does not explain everything they have seen.

The eldest visitors say the water does not reflect. It remembers.

Memory here is shallow and short-lived. Rain dimples remain only a heartbeat. Passing wings last a breath. Sunset may endure a minute beneath ice. The people test the pools with pebbles and gestures, though never casually. A hunter may hold an arrowhead over the surface before a difficult stalk, then look away and back, watching the hand that was. Mourners sometimes bring an object belonging to the dead—a bead, a tooth, a piece of plaited hair—and lower it near the water without letting it touch. They hope to see another hand reach after it. Usually they see only their own.

There are stories of deeper recollections.

The largest pool, beside the boulder with the worn bowl, is said to keep one day in every hundred years. On certain evenings its stones are obscured by feet: hunters in forgotten clothing, animals no longer found in the mountains, and once, according to a song, a forest growing where no tree could now stand. Whether these are visions or merely stories encouraged by cold, hunger, and glare matters little to those who climb there. They behave as though the water is listening.

The upright stones beneath the glacier are not graves. They are reminders addressed to the valley itself.

Each carving names not a person but a recurring presence: the finches’ arrival, the horned animals’ descent, the opening of the hidden stream, the lammergeier’s circle, the first thunder, the last gold light. When one stone falls, it is not immediately raised. Visitors wait to see whether frost, ice, or settling moraine will lift or turn it. Only then do they help it into its new posture. To force it back into its former alignment would be to insist that a changed world remain unchanged.

A few people spend most of the warm season there. They are hunters, gatherers, weather-watchers, and keepers of the stones, although none holds the role for life. They sleep in the three-walled shelter or in shallow cavities roofed with hide. They gather medicinal moss, bitter roots, moth cocoons, shed feathers, and the blue flowers that open beside meltwater. They set wicker traps for tiny cold-water creatures and collect minerals where the dark-legged climbers lick the cliffs. They carry news between valleys when the lower routes are blocked by flood or feud.

They also watch the glacier.

Its knocks have meanings, or seem to. A close double crack warns of ice settling over an empty channel. A long interior groan precedes a fall from the blue face. A scatter of small reports in warm darkness means water is tunneling somewhere unseen. The watchers mark these changes by shifting pebbles in the carved bowl. When they descend, they bring warnings: which stream will swell, which path has opened, which slope is likely to fail.

Their knowledge is exact but never confident. Certainty is considered a lowland vice.

The most constant inhabitants are not human. The cushion plants are the valley’s old cities, crowded, defensive, and slow-growing. Within them live mites, pale grubs, beetles polished like seeds, and spiders so small their webs fit between adjacent leaves. Black bees sleep inside flowers when sudden cloud steals the heat. Moths press themselves to the undersides of stones, their wings matching quartz, lichen, or old ice.

The finches belong to the valley collectively rather than as individuals. A flock settles like cast grain and rises like a single startled thought. The visitors read their movements. Birds feeding high on the terraces promise stable weather. Birds clustering near the shelter announce wind above the ridge. If they abandon the valley in full daylight, everyone looks up.

The hares are regarded with wary affection. They know every passage between boulders, including routes erased by slides and routes that seem never to have existed. Some have dark ears, some torn ears, some eyes clouded white by age. They will cross a person’s sleeping place but avoid the pools’ mirrored centers. Hunters take them when food is needed, apologizing not to the dead animal but to the faint path it leaves behind.

The little long-eared predators in the talus are almost never seen clearly. They are low-bodied, ash-colored, and able to flatten themselves through cracks narrower than a wrist. Their enormous ears turn independently toward beetle movement, wingbeats, and water under stone. At night they make a soft clicking call that the cliffs scatter into false locations. The valley people say these hunters can hear delayed sounds just as the pools retain delayed light. A click may return after the animal has moved, drawing prey—or danger—toward an empty crack.

Above all circles the lammergeier.

It is not worshiped, but many rites wait upon it. If it passes over the moraine while the standing stones are being tended, work stops until its shadow has crossed. Bones from a successful hunt are left on a particular tilted shelf. The bird takes some and drops others onto the scree, where they break and enter the valley again as grease, splinters, tools, and soil. Its shadow moves without delay over grass and rock, but in the pools a second bird follows after. Thus two lammergeiers cross the Afterwaters: the living one and the one the valley has not yet released.

The dark-legged horned animals come and go by impossible walls. Their bodies are compact, their hooves narrow and soft-edged, their horns close-swept so they do not snag in fissures. They are older in the valley’s stories than human beings. No one hunts them near the mineral seep. They are thought to open the high paths by walking them, and to close those paths when they cease.

Their leader is often an old female with a scarred muzzle. She stamps the pools cloudy before drinking, breaking the reflection into light. The people imitate her when they must cross after dusk: three stamps beside the water, then no backward glance.

All these lives are temporary, even those that never leave. Plants are peeled away by frost. Burrows vanish under slides. Nests are buried. The glacier exposes new ground and destroys old ground in the same season. Nothing possesses the valley long enough to be its master.

If the place has a true inhabitant, it may be the brief interval itself: the breath between a thing and its image, the golden minute held under ice, the pause after a crack in the glacier when every creature waits to learn what has changed.

The people live inside that interval when they can. The hares avoid it. The horned animals muddy it. The finches refuse to bathe in it. The small hunters may hear its echo. The lammergeier crosses it twice.

Then frost seals the pools, the passes close, and the human visitors descend with their narrow tools and unfinished stories. Snow fills the shelter. Wind worries the cairns. The standing stones lean another finger’s breadth downhill.

Under clear ice, for a little while, the empty valley continues to contain those who have already gone.
