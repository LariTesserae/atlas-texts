The plateau is inhabited less by separate species than by a single, dispersed commonwealth of organisms, each life folded into the work of another.

Its most influential inhabitants are the ironwoods of the central marshes. They stand in widely spaced groves, low and broad-crowned where storms have repeatedly broken their upper limbs. Their bark is dark, plated, and extraordinarily dense, its fissures filled with reddish mineral salts. Old individuals contain enough accumulated metal that falling branches strike their trunks with a muted bell note. Their leaves are narrow and leathery, with conductive veins that gather atmospheric charge. Before a storm, their flower heads lift and bristle; blue sparks pass among them, briefly joining entire groves into one luminous crown.

Belowground, an ironwood is difficult to distinguish from its neighbors. Their roots braid through saturated soil and fuse where they touch, forming conductive rafts beneath the marsh. Fungi sheath these roots in pale, cartilage-like tissue. The fungi regulate water and pressure, sealing damaged passages, opening others, and directing charged fluids toward colonies of metallic bacteria. Thus an ironwood grove is also a reservoir, a circuit, and a pump.

The bacteria inhabit mineral seams too narrow for roots. Some draw iron and copper from groundwater and deposit them as branching films; others feed on the resulting electrical gradients. Together they produce delicate metallic threads that reinforce the mud and redirect seepage. Their colonies glow dull amber when groundwater rises through them. A brightening beneath the marsh indicates pressure accumulating somewhere below; the light fades after a vent opens, sometimes in a traveling wave across an entire basin.

Among them live black algae whose floating mats turn slowly on quiet pools. By day they orient toward the sun, storing energy in oily tissues. At night they release sugars and reactive compounds into the water, feeding the chemistry underground. Their rotation is maintained by uneven growth, gas bladders, and minute differences in water temperature, yet its regularity gives the pools the appearance of enormous, silent gauges.

Fungi occupy nearly every boundary in this system. Thin forms line mineral veins. Tough, valve-like growths swell shut when underground pressure rises and relax after discharge. Others form luminous rings around ironwood roots, leaking stored lightning upward as cold blue light. Canopy fungi consume leaves before they fall; aquatic fungi soften submerged wood; minute symbiotic species live inside the waxy leaves of marsh plants and help them tolerate metal-rich water. They are the plateau’s regulators and recyclers, seldom visible except as pale membranes, fruiting shelves, or sudden nocturnal light.

The open wetlands belong to herds of small browsers. They are delicately built through the shoulders but carry unusually broad feet, each toe capable of spreading over reeds and floating vegetation. Their hides are dark umber or moss-gray, broken by pale vertical markings that resemble wet sedge stems. They feed on waxy herbs, young ironwood shoots, and mineral crusts exposed when pools contract. The metal in their diet stains their teeth and gives their antlers or brow prongs a faint greenish cast.

They move quietly, testing the ground before committing their weight. When startled, they do not bound far. Instead, the herd separates and stands motionless among the reeds, relying on the resemblance between their narrow legs and the vegetation. They sense storms through their feet, becoming restless when electrical pulses begin to travel through the root mat. By shifting from one basin to another, they carry fungal spores in the mud between their toes and seeds in their coats.

The heavy cats that hunt them are solitary, compact predators adapted to wet footing. Their legs are shorter than those of lowland cats, their paws broad and heavily webbed. Dense, oil-rich fur keeps water from the skin and muffles their passage through dripping undergrowth. Their coats tend toward charcoal, deep rust, or black patterned with markings visible only when wet. They rarely pursue prey at speed. Instead, they use the plateau’s hidden channels as paths and wait near stable crossings in the bog.

The cats announce themselves chiefly through absence. Birds stop calling. Stem-borne insects cease their vibrations. Browsers raise their heads without yet knowing why. A cat may remain half-submerged beside a root mass for hours, its nostrils barely above the tea-colored water. Their territories follow watersheds rather than ridges, and individuals leave scent on warm basalt shelves where rain cannot immediately wash it away.

Long-legged hunters patrol shallower pools. Some are birds, slate-winged and copper-beaked, walking with each foot placed carefully between submerged mineral filaments. Others are narrow-bodied reptiles that balance on reed mats and strike downward through them. They gather wherever armored fish migrate.

Those fish are among the plateau’s most durable inhabitants. Their bodies are enclosed in overlapping plates, smooth enough to slip through moss but strong enough to withstand abrasive rock channels. Paired fins end in thick, jointed rays, allowing them to haul themselves over saturated ground when pools become isolated. During these journeys they breathe through wet, folded chambers behind the jaw. At night, whole groups creep through the moss in silence, leaving parallel furrows that fill immediately with water. Eggs adhere to bromeliad cups and flooded root hollows, where the young remain until heavy rain reconnects them to larger pools.

Pale crabs live much higher on the escarpments. Almost colorless and no larger than a hand, they occupy hanging springs separated from continuous waterways by hundreds of meters of cliff. Their flattened bodies fit beneath sheets of running water, and hooked legs anchor them to mineral films. They graze on bacteria and capture larvae brought down from moss beds above. Each isolated spring supports a slightly different form: some blind, some blue-eyed, some with reddish shells stained by iron. During the wettest weeks they cross vertical rock beneath water veils, moving between springs along routes no terrestrial animal could use.

The escarpments also house swifts. They nest behind the warm curtains of falling water, attaching shallow cups of fiber and saliva directly to sheltered basalt. At sunrise they drop from the cliffs, wings closed, then open them in the rising air. Their long flights carry insects—and the spores clinging to those insects—between the plateau and the hidden lowlands. When storms approach, the flocks gather unusually high, tracing the invisible columns of heat that feed the clouds.

On sunward ledges live dark, heat-loving bees. Their combs hang directly against basalt and soften slightly each afternoon, so workers continually reshape them. The bees forage on ironwood flowers, orchids, mineral-loving herbs, and pale blossoms that open only after lightning. Metallic dust adheres to their bodies and becomes incorporated into the wax. Their honey is dark, sharp, and faintly smoky from compounds produced by flowers growing over thermal seams. The colonies endure heavy rain by sealing the outer comb into a water-shedding crust.

Within the forest canopy, bright lizards occupy the boundary between heat and shade. They emerge onto black stone whenever the sun appears, flattening their bodies to absorb warmth. Their colors intensify with temperature: dull green in cloud, brilliant blue or orange on heated basalt. The moment shade returns, they retreat into wet cracks and become nearly black. Some possess ridged scales that collect condensation and channel it toward the mouth. They eat insects, orchid nectar, and the electrical gnats that swarm around charged ironwood flowers.

Gliding mammals move between wooded ridges at dawn. They are small, nocturnal leaf-eaters with membranes extending from wrist to ankle and tails fringed for stability. Their fur hosts green algae, making resting animals resemble mossy growths against the trunks. They sleep in hollow branches warmed by fungal decay and emerge while mist still fills the gullies. Their glides carry seeds across ravines that ground animals seldom cross. In storm weather they cling low on the lee sides of trees, sensing charge through the pads of their feet.

Much of the animal life is heard through solid matter rather than air. Insects tap root, reed, and bark in patterned pulses. Some have enlarged legs that drum on floating leaves; others send vibrations through ironwood trunks, using the whole grove as a signaling surface. Predators detect these messages through whiskers, feet, or jawbones held against branches. To a human ear the marsh may seem quiet while beneath that quiet it is dense with courtship, alarm, territorial dispute, and orientation.

The loud voices belong to a few specialists. Hollow-throated canopy birds call across ridges in single resonant notes, leaving long intervals in which an answer can travel through mist. Their beaks are deep and chambered, and each bird’s note is subtly shaped by minerals accumulated in the bill. Frogs gather in flooded hollows and begin together when falling pressure stretches the membranes of their inner ears. Their choruses end just as abruptly. Eggs are laid in temporary foam masses among charged roots, where weak electrical fields inhibit some parasites.

Every surface supports smaller inhabitants. Bromeliad cups hold mosquito larvae, translucent worms, tiny predatory tadpoles, and mites that never descend to the ground. Orchids carry moss; the moss holds algae; the algae harbor rotifers and mineral-feeding bacteria. Water films on leaves contain entire grazing communities. Fallen branches become nurseries for beetles and fungi while still suspended in vines. Even persistent droplets have resident microbes adapted to the cycles of dilution, sunlight, and evaporation.

No creature commands the plateau’s living industry. The closest thing to a central organism is the network itself: ironwoods gathering charge, fungi opening and closing passages, bacteria laying down conductive minerals, algae supplying stored sunlight, animals moving spores and seeds, roots catching what erosion loosens. Its inhabitants do not merely live upon the plateau. Their bodies extend its drainage, circuitry, memory, and repair.

Seen at night, the relationships become briefly legible. Amber bacterial light traces the buried water. Blue fungi outline root junctions. Pools show the faint revolving sheen of algal mats. Above them, browsers pass as dark interruptions, frogs pulse at the waterline, and a cat’s eyes appear once before sinking out of sight. Then a pressure chamber empties somewhere underground. The marsh exhales, the lights dim along one branching vein, and everything resumes its quiet labor.
