This city belongs first to creatures that can live on margins: between salt and fresh water, flood and pavement, waste and shelter, machinery and neglect.

The shorebirds are its true multitude. Sandpipers, plovers, stilts, knots, and other small waders cover the exposed mud in close, trembling congregations. They move with the waterline, probing wherever a retreating film of water leaves worms, larvae, and small crustaceans within reach. Their occupation of the city is tidal. At low water they spread across the basin; as the channels fill, they compress onto dikes, roofs, gantries, and concrete barriers until whole structures appear feathered. When startled, they rise almost simultaneously, becoming for a moment a single turning body before breaking apart and settling around another shallow basin.

Gulls hold the higher architecture. They line parapets, lamp standards, crane jibs, cable trays, tanks, and warehouse ridges, watching both water and industry. Grain spills, fish offal, uncovered refuse, drowned vermin, and the aftermath of storms all belong to them. They are patient around active machinery and quick to recognize a stopped conveyor or unattended loading dock. Their nests occupy gravel roofs and sheltered recesses where the heat is intense but terrestrial predators are scarce.

Herons and egrets stand apart from the crowds. They wait on culvert mouths and narrow concrete ledges, nearly motionless in the haze, striking at fish driven toward warm outfalls. Cormorants claim rusted rails and half-submerged structures, diving in canals whose salinity and oxygen change from hour to hour. After feeding, they spread their dark wings beside warning signs and corroded ladders.

Pigeons inhabit the hollow factories. They roost on overhead beams, broken offices, and the upper floors of turbine halls, descending through shafts of dusty light to drink from roof drains and flooded loading bays. Wherever maintenance ceases, they enter almost at once. Their droppings pale the machinery; their feathers collect against mesh and floor drains. Cats follow them into the dryer buildings, living in substations, workshops, and elevated storerooms where electrical warmth persists through the night.

Beneath bridges and inside expansion joints, bats sleep through the hot day. At dusk they stream out over settling ponds and drainage canals, feeding on the enormous insect population. Mosquitoes breed in every protected volume of standing water: basements, wheel ruts, blocked gutters, cooling trays, abandoned foundations, and the hidden cavities beneath tilted paving. Their numbers help determine human movement as effectively as heat or tide. After sunset, unscreened rooms and bare skin are rapidly surrendered to them.

Rats possess the enclosed underside of the city. They nest around steam pipes, cable galleries, food stores, drains, and wall cavities warmed by surviving equipment. At dusk they emerge in purposeful lines, using pipe racks and raised conduits as streets above the wet ground. Flooding does not remove them; it merely lifts them. During storms they climb ladders, cable runs, shelving, and machinery, waiting above the brown water until routes reopen. They eat spilled grain, fish waste, insects, eggs, refuse, and one another.

The canals contain a similarly opportunistic population. Mullet graze in turbid shallows. Eels move through drains and floodgates, entering yards when storms join one body of water to another. Small silver fish school beside warm discharges, enduring low oxygen and sudden changes in salt. Crabs shelter beneath sheet metal and broken concrete, then emerge onto warehouse floors when the tide or rain reaches them. The water may be oily, mineral-rich, overheated, or nearly stagnant, but it is productive. Heat and nutrients concentrate life wherever currents remain.

Feral dogs range across the open districts. They know the rail bridges, service ramps, shaded pipe corridors, and the few reliable freshwater leaks. Their packs are loose rather than constant: animals join and separate according to food, floodwater, and breeding. At noon they sleep beneath tanker cars or in the shadows of loading platforms. At night they cross kilometers of empty road, calling from one industrial island to another. They enter any building left open long enough and search storm debris after the birds have taken the easiest food.

Human beings survive here by behaving less like owners than custodians of defended enclosures. They occupy upper stories, artificial ridges, elevated corridors, and rooms that can be cooled, screened, and sealed. Their homes begin above flood marks. Doorways have raised thresholds; vents are meshed; shutters fit tightly; food is kept in hard containers. Boots, nets, fans, repellents, traps, and pumps are ordinary domestic equipment. Children learn which paving conceals soft mud, which handrails are weakened by salt, and which apparently shallow pools connect to open drains.

The remaining workers tend systems built for a much larger population. They operate pumps, turbines, ice plants, canneries, rail switches, repair shops, and fertilizer lines amid acres of inactive machinery. Their labor is concentrated and repetitive: clear a screen, seal a conduit, drive birds from an intake, remove crabs from a pump housing, raise stock before rain, restart a motor in a distant hall. Much of their work consists of preserving narrow zones of human intention against water, salt, heat, and animal occupation.

They travel between enclaves along elevated roads, covered walkways, and rail lines. During the hottest hours, they withdraw into control rooms and service tunnels while the exposed city passes to the animals. Their clothing is light but covering; their movements are measured. They recognize alarms by tone, storms by smell, and changing groundwater by the behavior of drains. They do not trust a dry floor simply because it is dry today.

No species wholly rules the city. Occupation shifts with height, temperature, tide, and attention. People hold the cooled rooms and maintained platforms. Pigeons and bats possess the upper cavities. Rats command the heated interior passages. Dogs range over the causeways. Fish and crabs enter wherever water opens a path. Birds gather on the immense exposed surfaces, then retreat upward as the tide erases them.

Together they form a population organized around leakage: leaked heat, grain, water, shelter, waste, and time. The factories feed colonies they were never designed to sustain. The drains serve as estuaries. The roofs become islands and cliffs. Every abandoned space is claimed, and every maintained space remains human only through repeated effort.

From far away, the city can look vacant because its inhabitants are dispersed through scales the architecture conceals. They are under the paving, inside the joints, above the ceilings, along the water’s edge, and massed on distant roofs. Only when the tide rises—or a siren, train, or sudden shadow disturbs them—does the full population reveal itself. Then the structures darken with arriving birds, dogs emerge onto the embankments, rats climb above the flood, fish pass through the gates, and the apparently empty basin becomes visibly, overwhelmingly alive.
