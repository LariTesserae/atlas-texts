The high country is inhabited by water.

Not merely filled with it, but occupied by it in the way another land might be occupied by a people: unevenly, purposefully, according to routes, customs, and ancient obligations. It gathers in courts. It queues in narrow channels. It waits behind gates and leans against walls. It travels beneath the paving in numbers too great to see, then emerges alone as a bead trembling from the lip of a spout.

Each drop appears simple. Together they possess something like intention.

They prefer incisions to slopes, fitted joints to open faces, basins to accidental hollows. A drop stranded on an unmarked slab may remain there for hours, round and undecided, though gravity offers it several directions. Touch it with the point of a chisel, extending one of the engraved lines beneath it, and it gathers itself at once. It enters the mark and runs. Where the line ends, it pauses again.

Thus the water can be guided, though not commanded. The distinction may have governed the makers’ entire existence.

Deep below the plateau live the reservoirs, if living is the word for such vast, unlit bodies. They do not resemble lakes. Lakes have shores, beds, winds, and surfaces open to weather. These are volumes confined within successive courses of stone, shaped into chambers whose limits no one above can determine. They press against floors and foundations with immense patience. Their slightest changes pass upward as dampness, mineral bloom, a lifted paving block, or the low pressure-sound heard through stone.

They may be the oldest inhabitants. Perhaps they were enclosed before the upper country was raised. Perhaps the whole plateau is only the elaborate lid of their dwelling.

Nearer the surface, water divides into smaller temperaments.

There is stair-water, thin and quick, descending each flight along the shallow wear at its center. It never spreads far onto the treads. At landings it collects itself in square sockets, then chooses the next stair, sometimes passing over an ascending flight to find one that leads more deeply into the works.

There is wall-water, slow enough to leave fans of salt. It emerges without openings, beads along hammer scars, and travels sideways as readily as down. It seems especially attentive to unfinished masonry. Red guide lines often draw it. A wall marked for cutting will become wet along the intended cut before the surrounding stone darkens, as if the pigment has preserved not merely a measurement but an instruction.

There is vessel-water, held in ceramic jars, troughs, gauges, and roof tiles. It acquires the shape of these things with unusual fidelity. Poured out, it may retain a curved lip or angular corner for several breaths before collapsing. In cold districts it remembers even longer. The clear plates of ice that fall from the high overhangs are its most exact recollections: temporary casts of molds, gutters, joints, and sockets, released intact into the courts below.

There is also mirror-water.

This is the most difficult kind to approach. It lies in the smallest basins and the deepest cisterns, wherever a surface has been deliberately leveled and enclosed. It reflects neither the observer nor the luminous sky directly above. Instead, it shows masonry: nearby on some days, impossibly distant on others. A palm-sized pool may contain the image of a stair with a hundred steps. A trough under an unfinished wall may show that wall completed, roofed, and joined to towers that have no foundations yet. Sometimes the reflection is exact enough to reveal individual tool marks.

These images are not passive. They are the nearest thing in the high country to offspring.

A reflected structure enters the world first as light held upon water. Then condensation gathers along the corresponding guide marks. Mineral powder drifts toward damp joints. Loose wedges settle. A gate elsewhere opens, sending a measured runnel through a district that had remained dry for years. The water carries ground stone and salts into prepared gaps. Deposits harden by imperceptible layers. Balance encourages balance. Weight calls weight into place.

What builds here now is not a hidden race of masons. It is a distributed creature whose body includes reservoir pressure, engraved grooves, mineral cords, counterweights, reflections, and time.

Its movements are too slow to look like movement. Its metabolism is the cycling of districts: filling, tipping, flooding, draining, drying. Its pulse is the subdued rush beneath the paving when an unseen gate opens. Its growth appears as a tightened joint, a cemented peg, a lintel newly inclined toward its seat. Its memory is stored in repetition—not perfect repetition, but hand-shaped recurrence, every dimension inherited with some small error.

That imperfection may be essential. An exact copy would close the pattern. The thumb-width narrowing of a channel alters the speed of flow; the altered flow fills a different basin; the basin reflects a terrace at another angle. From tiny errors, the plateau obtains new instructions. It does not reproduce itself like a crystal. It elaborates.

The vanished builders, if they existed, may therefore remain present in a dispersed form. Their measurements survive in the water’s habits. Their gestures persist in the bias of a stair, the reach between lever and counterweight, the height at which a person—or something shaped much like one—would have tied a mineral rope. Yet no individual ghost walks the courts. Nothing waits behind the pivoting doors. The arrangement itself is what remains of them, as a shell remains of an animal, except that this shell continues secreting its own further chambers.

At the eastern rim, where construction is youngest, the inhabiting force is almost visible.

Cloud presses against the raw tiers from below. Water condenses on keen edges and runs into channels that stop over brightness. There it hangs, forming long clear pendants. These swell without falling. Within them appear reversed fragments of architecture: a landing, a parapet, the first few blocks of a tower. When the drops finally break, some descend into the cloud. Others seem to withdraw upward along the channel, leaving a line of wet stone behind.

Afterward, a slab may be found shifted.

A tile stack may contain one additional tile.

A staircase that ended in open air may possess another step.

No single change proves anything. The high country offers no dramatic act of creation, no visible hand and no moment when absence becomes form. It works by accumulations smaller than certainty. The witness turns away, rain passes, a district fills and empties—and the place is fractionally more complete.

If there are minds here, they are the basins.

Each is a shallow attention held open to the luminous sky. Each receives an image, keeps it trembling for a while, and passes some consequence onward through a notch. Larger reservoirs may contemplate whole districts. The buried cisterns may hold visions of the entire plateau, including structures not yet raised and chambers long sealed beneath later courses. Their thoughts would be hydraulic: pressure, level, reflection, release.

They would know no distinction between remembering and designing.

They would not understand emptiness except as a place prepared to contain water, nor distance except as the sequence of channels required to reach it. Time would be measured by saturation: the filling of a chamber, the hardening of a mineral seam, the settling of grit into load-bearing stone. A thousand years might be only the interval between two drops striking the same hollow.

So the high country is not abandoned. Abandonment belongs to places that wait for former inhabitants to return. This place waits only for completion, and perhaps not even that. Completion may forever retreat into the next reflection.

What lives there is an architecture dreaming through water: blind, patient, innumerable, preserving the touch of absent hands while teaching stone to continue without them.
