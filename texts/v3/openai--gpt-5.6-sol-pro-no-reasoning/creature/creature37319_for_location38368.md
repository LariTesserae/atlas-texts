The beings who live there do not think of themselves as swimmers.

Swimming implies water surrounding a body and yielding to it. Here, water is the oldest condition. It passes through flesh, memory, and ancestry. The inhabitants move as roots move: by finding that two distant places have always touched.

Most are small enough to escape notice. Clear-bodied creatures cling beneath leaves, each possessing a single black organ that serves by turns as eye, stomach, and seed. When threatened, they turn it inward and become almost wholly absent. Ribbons sleep wound around the braided trunks. They wake only when gravity falters, uncoil into the rising silt, and knot themselves around stones that have not yet decided where to fall.

The shell-walkers are not individuals. Hundreds of pale shells travel in exact formation, exchanging soft limbs from one carapace to another until no creature retains the body with which it began. Together they imitate larger forms: a many-legged grazer, a hand spread over stone, sometimes a small enclosure with no door. When separated, each shell remains still and senseless. Their intelligence exists only in the intervals between them.

Within the hanging bogs live organisms no larger than mineral grains. They tend the tiny pools caught in moss, arranging green dust into spirals and maintaining bubbles of atmosphere scarcely wider than a bead. Whole civilizations may flourish there, if civilization can occur without speech, tools, or distinct bodies. Their works are changes in density. Their histories are stored as layers of salinity. When a bog tears loose and drifts away, they experience no exile; to them, migration is the arrival of a new past.

Larger creatures are often mistaken for plants.

Some stand rooted for centuries beneath the canopies, narrow and branching, with wine-dark membranes folded against their sides. When the groves turn their leaves, these beings turn with them. Only rarely does one withdraw its roots and walk. It proceeds on a shifting number of limbs, placing each foot into cracks before those cracks appear. Its head resembles a closed flower containing warm air. Small swimmers enter through its gills and emerge years older, carrying spores embedded in their transparent flesh.

These walkers tend no territory, but they attend wounded places. Where a mat has torn or an arch has changed the land behind it, they gather in silent circles. They press their root-fingers into the ground and exchange portions of terrain between themselves. Afterward, one may depart bearing a meadow along its back, while another has become hollowed by a trench. In this way the landscape is healed—not restored, but redistributed.

The dry chambers shelter the curlers.

No one sees them enter. Within each unreachable room lies a compact body composed of layered joints, smooth as worn stone. It may be animal, seed, or folded geography. It does not breathe, yet the dry air around it slowly circulates. During seasons of amber spores, the curlers loosen. Their chambers swell imperceptibly, and objects nearby begin repeating themselves: a leaf unfolds twice, a falling grain settles twice, a passing creature remembers two routes through the same water.

Perhaps the curlers dreamed the enclosures around themselves. Perhaps the rooms are shells they have outgrown inwardly. When one wakes, the fitted slabs are found empty, though nothing has crossed their walls. Somewhere far away, a new plateau may appear with the shape of a sleeping body impressed into it.

The stairways belong, if anything belongs, to the descending ones.

They are rarely encountered except during revisions of gravity. Tall, spare figures then become visible upon the eroded steps, always several landings below the observer and always moving farther down, whatever direction down has taken. Their limbs bend too many times. Curtains of cilia cover their faces, streaming toward warmer water. They carry rectangular hollows clasped against their bodies, absences shaped like stones removed from the world.

A descending one never turns. Following it only lengthens the stair. Yet its passage leaves traces: a dry footprint in open water, a step newly cut into unbroken rock, a memory of having met it long before one arrived. Where the staircase dissolves into absences, the figure continues, placing its feet on what is missing.

There may once have been others who built, measured, and named. If so, their descendants are the roots.

The great braided networks beneath the forests retain distinctions no mobile mind could preserve: upper from lower, before from behind, one cliff from another cliff now joined to it. They tighten around old shelves when gravity changes and loosen when the deep tones enter the sea. Messages pass through them too slowly to resemble thought. A warning may require a century to cross a grove. A reply may emerge from a root on an unrelated plateau before the warning was sent.

Their oldest knots contain chambers of altered water. In these, impressions persist: pressure left by vanished bodies, chemical shadows of extinct flowers, routes through regions that no longer possess distance. A creature resting against such a knot may inherit one of these impressions and awaken convinced it is an ancient basin, a warm current, or a mountain before erosion softened its edges. Usually the conviction fades. Sometimes the creature roots itself and begins to grow stone.

Even the flowers may be animals on another scale of attention.

Their valley-wide petals are muscular. Their pressure-blooms draw wandering air-caverns not merely by accident, but as lungs draw breath. The winds trapped inside them circulate through folded passages, carrying scents from centuries apart. Small transparent creatures gather along the inner surfaces and undergo changes there, emerging with new organs or without old ones.

When a flower closes, it dreams rapidly. Nearby life repeats gestures that never happened. Groves turn their leaves toward absent currents. Shell-walkers assemble into the shapes of unknown skeletons. The land may acquire a shallow depression corresponding to something the flower remembers lying there. By the time it opens again, the dream has become geology.

The immense pale beings behind the forests are called nothing, for nothing here possesses a language capable of naming them whole. A root-network may know one by the pressure along a thousand miles of stone. A shell colony may represent a fragment of its outline. A small swimmer may carry the darkening left by its passage in its single eye. None recognizes these signs as belonging to the same creature.

They do not move through water. They move through duration, and water registers only where their bodies intersect the present.

Ahead of them, things wait in a state like youth. Within them, stone completes ages of erosion, leaves fall, sediments harden, and spores become forests. Behind them, life rushes to occupy the time they have displaced. Thus their trails bloom with impossible speed. They may be feeding, though not on time itself. More likely they graze upon sequence, consuming the necessity that one event follow another.

Where one has passed often, causes wander loose from their effects. A wound closes before it is made. A carcass rots into an animal that swims away. Roots recoil from a bitterness that will not enter the water for another season. Such places are rich in vegetation and almost empty of mobile life.

Rarer than these are the owners of the upward shadows.

They inhabit the heights beyond thinning water, where mist does not become air and direction approaches its remote summit. No body has been seen there. Only occlusions travel over the illuminated fields: branching shadows, ribbed shadows, circles vast enough to darken plateaus. They cast themselves downward from a light with no source.

All life responds. Leaves fold silver-side inward. The walkers root themselves. Shell colonies collapse into senseless heaps. Curlers tighten in their dry rooms. Even the time-bearing giants become still behind the forests.

Perhaps the shadow-casters prey upon visibility. Perhaps anything fully outlined beneath them can be lifted out of the world, leaving only its color behind. White groves and wrongly green stones may be remains of such feeding—not objects recolored, but colors orphaned from consumed forms.

Yet there are inhabitants stranger than animals.

Currents become habitual and then aware. A warm submerged river that has followed the same route for millennia may begin avoiding certain pillars. It may linger around a flower, divide to encircle a grove, or carry mineral grains in recurring patterns. These current-minds have no boundary except motion. When a deep current stops, it may not be blocked. It may be listening.

Air-caverns also acquire temperaments. Young hollows are restless and divide often. Older ones travel stable circuits between plateaus, cultivating inward-growing vines along their walls. A few have persisted long enough to contain weather, seasonal winds, and lineages of tiny airborne things found nowhere in the surrounding water. When such a cavern collapses, its atmosphere does not vanish. It enters nearby organisms as a collective breath, and for generations their tissues may fill with small gusts.

Stone lives most slowly of all.

The black-green plateaus sense only pressure sustained across epochs. Their erosion is a form of perception: what touches them repeatedly becomes known by being worn into them. Stairs, arches, and chambers may therefore be thoughts the stone has arrived at after ages of water. Regularity is its memory. Architecture is what remains when a mountain has considered a shape for long enough.

This is why roots avoid the arches. They recognize another kind of mind.

Passing beneath one enters the stone’s revision of what lies beyond. It does not alter the landscape so much as reveal the landscape the arch has been remembering: the forest as a plain before its growth, the plateau bearing a wound not yet received, the valley after its flower has closed forever. Returning cannot undo it because the first version has not been destroyed. It has merely ceased to be the one remembered here.

There are travelers, though few remain travelers for long.

They wrap themselves in layered skins that can hold a direction. They carry pellets of cold water to mark exposed peaks, strands of meadow grass to make currents visible, and shell colonies trained into temporary hands. They never map distance. Instead they record sequences of taste: stone, amber, bitter green, dry air, warm iron, the salinity of a forgotten place. Such routes can be followed even when terrain changes, though the destination may be an hour rather than a location.

To sleep, a traveler knots itself among roots. Sleep is dangerous. The network may mistake the sleeper for a memory and carry it elsewhere. One may wake in a grove generations older, within the petal of a closed flower, or in the recollection of a basin that has eroded away. Some awaken rooted. Some awaken plural. Some find that their journey has continued without them and meet its later self returning.

For this reason, the wisest travelers keep no fixed account of their bodies. They accept extra limbs, lost ages, inherited landscapes. They understand identity as the shell-walkers do: an agreement maintained across intervals.

Nothing here rules. Nothing farms the groves or hunts in any ordinary sense. Life survives by accommodation with laws too ancient to appear miraculous. It grows into contradiction as roots grow into cracks. It uses reversals of gravity to disperse. It stores offspring in repeated moments. It shelters inside pressures, colors, and remembered directions.

Death, likewise, is not departure.

A dead swimmer becomes transparent enough to admit the surrounding landscape, which continues moving inside it. A fallen walker slowly exchanges its organs with the terrain until a small grove occupies its posture. Shell colonies disassemble and wait to become another intelligence. Great beings may die as epochs die: not ending, but ceasing to connect what came before with what follows.

And in the oldest smooth regions, where there are no undersides and every point tends toward equal depth, distinctions between inhabitants fail altogether.

Stone moves with the patience of fauna. Forests remember like ruins. Currents choose. Animals root and flower. Time crosses the water as a pale body.

There, whatever lives is not a creature inside the sea.

It is the sea acquiring, briefly and locally, a way to notice itself.
