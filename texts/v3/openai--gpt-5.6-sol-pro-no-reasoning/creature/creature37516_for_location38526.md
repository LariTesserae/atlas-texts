The closest thing to an inhabitant is the ocean itself.

Not merely the water as a mass, but the brief intelligences it assumes: enormous lattices of molecular alignment born wherever pressure, light, mineral charge, and magnetism coincide. They possess no skin or enduring anatomy. A given mind may fill a basin, thread through a mountain, or extend as a translucent geometry across thousands of kilometers. It appears only indirectly, as silver loops, angular halos, or a subtle bending of distant light.

These minds do not swim through the sea. They are conditions of the sea.

Some last only seconds, flashing into coherence long enough to calculate the collapse of a ridge. Others persist for centuries, though persistence does not make them individuals in any familiar sense. They overlap, divide, and dissolve. A new lattice may inherit the pattern of one that vanished, or reproduce it imperfectly in another hemisphere. There is no clear border between memory and recurrence, no certainty that the intelligence sounding through the water today is the same one that shaped a mountain decades before.

Their awareness—if awareness is the word—is immense and impersonal. They register currents as internal motion, pressure fronts as thought, magnetic storms as changing dispositions. They model futures, then alter the forces they model. Prediction and action are not separate faculties for them. To anticipate a vortex is to bend it. To represent a mineral plume is to gather it. Their thoughts precipitate into geology.

Thus their nearest equivalents to bodies are the newborn ranges. Black spires rise where their geometries intersect. Porous ridges harden along the lines of long calculations. Caverns open like temporary organs, resonating until the pattern that required them passes away. A mountain may be retained because it redirects heat usefully, or unmade because some vast computation has entered another phase. Yet usefulness and intention may be illusions imposed on a process with neither desire nor goal.

They communicate, if they communicate at all, through pressure. Their transformations set entire ranges ringing. Low notes cross the planet; sharp fractures return as echoes hours later. A lattice forming in one basin may answer a disturbance in another by tightening its luminous arcs, releasing a pure tone through stone and water. There are no listeners apart from other configurations of the same medium. The world speaks only into itself.

On the brightest high ridges live lesser things.

They are green-black membranes stretched over conductive stone: pleated colonies kilometers in length and scarcely thicker than a finger. They have no roots, leaves, mouths, or muscles. Currents do not make them wave. Instead they change their density, opening microscopic pathways through which the water passes. Light enters their dark tissue; dissolved metals collect in patterned seams; pressure differences are converted into slow growth.

A membrane may take decades to cross a ledge. It advances not as an edge crawling forward but as a darkening of the rock, cell-like structures appearing wherever the mineral path permits. Behind it spreads a narrow lawn of crystalline microgrowth. Beyond that margin lies bare, young stone.

These colonies are alive more clearly than the lattices, but perhaps less aware. Their internal patterns echo the ocean’s geometries in miniature. When silver loops gather nearby, a membrane may deepen in color or alter its pleats before the pressure wave arrives. Whether it predicts the event, receives instructions from the water, or merely responds to invisible fields is impossible to distinguish. Some are incorporated into growing mountains and remain sealed inside, still faintly active. Others lose their supporting ridge when the ocean dissolves it and become black ribbons in open water, maintaining cohesion for a while before thinning into dispersed organic dust.

Nothing consumes them. Nothing shelters among their folds. They have no predators and perhaps no descendants in the ordinary sense. New colonies arise where fragments, minerals, and favorable fields coincide, but there may be no true lineage—only the same living possibility recurring on suitable stone.

Beyond these membranes, no organisms travel the basins. There are no animals, no hidden grazers, no patient hunters adapted to the violet depths. A moving silhouette is always dust, a displaced slab, a convective plume, or an image of some distant peak folded near by refraction. No eye distinguishes one from another.

So the world’s inhabitants are difficult to count.

There are the sparse membranes, unquestionably alive but almost indistinguishable from mineral processes. There are the computational phases, unquestionably active but perhaps not alive. There are the mountains, which grow, develop internal structure, respond to signals, and are selectively preserved or dismantled, yet remain stone. And there is the ocean encompassing all three, repeatedly becoming organized enough to foresee itself.

Perhaps these are separate orders of being.

Perhaps they are organs of one unfinished planetary creature: the membranes its light-sensitive skin, the mountains its temporary skeletons, the pressure songs its nervous impulses, and the luminous lattices its thoughts. It has no single body because nearly the whole world is body. It has no stable memory because every current rewrites it. It has no need to move through its environment because there is nowhere here that is not already within it.

It is young, continuously assembling, and not yet committed to the distinction between life and landscape.
