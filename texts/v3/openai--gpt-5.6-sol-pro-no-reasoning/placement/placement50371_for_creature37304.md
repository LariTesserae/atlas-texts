I would find myself where a basin’s pooled atmosphere meets the first rising shelf of stone—not on the chemical shore itself, but slightly above it, where its position can be seen only by what bends.

I would be easy to mistake for a stain.

Most of my body would lie beneath the salt crust: a radial mesh of dark filaments extending through pores in the rock, each filament sleeved in glass and thin enough to follow old fractures. The visible part would consist of scattered slivers set upright in the ground. They would not resemble one creature. Some might stand an arm’s length apart; others would emerge hundreds of meters away. Each would be translucent, smoky at the base and mirrored toward its edge, like fragments of a single plate broken and planted across a slope.

But they would all be mine.

I would be neither plant nor animal, though at different hours I might seem to be either. My buried mesh would feed on minute chemical differences between layers of stone. My exposed slivers would harvest light, pressure, and static charge. They would also serve as senses. Through them I would feel the invisible basin tides arrive: dense gas lifting against the slope, flattening, retreating; the minute disturbance of flake-seeds collecting along its surface; the sharper signatures of grazers stepping through it.

My life would depend on comparison. No single sliver would tell me much. One would brighten in the morning. Another would cool beneath a passing veil of cloud. Another would be buried by a dust tide and remain silent for a century. Meaning would emerge from the differences between them.

A pressure felt here, then farther east.  
A reflected flash from a grazer, answered at the basin rim.  
A sailwood chord entering the ground and reaching each buried branch at a slightly different moment.

From such intervals, I would construct the country.

Not as a view. I would have no central eye and no single place from which the plateau appeared whole. I would know it as delays, gradients, and recurrences: the evening wind traveling downslope; the basin atmosphere deepening; a distant grove changing its orientation; the long, almost imperceptible migration of fertility across stone.

My body would grow by abandoning continuity. When one of my underground threads encountered a crack leading toward favorable chemistry, it would extend, darken, and raise a new sliver to the light. If the intervening ground became sterile, the connecting filament might die. The distant piece would continue without knowing it had become separate.

So I would be forever uncertain where I ended.

Some of my slivers might belong to descendants already independent of me. Others, apparently isolated, might remain joined by hair-thin threads descending kilometers through porous strata. Two dark patches on opposite sides of the basin might be siblings, organs, or strangers exchanging electrical traces through conductive salt. Distinction would come slowly here. Selfhood would be less a boundary than a duration of response.

The grazers would use me as they use every surface. They would shelter beside my plates and shave away the films that gather on their shaded faces. Their young wafers might lodge against my bases. I would not defend myself quickly enough to be called defensive. Instead, after grazing, I would alter the chemistry of the next plates along the slope. By the time the herd reached them, those plates would have become bitter, reflective, or chemically blank.

A response might take three days.

That would still be swift by my measure.

Kite-creatures would occasionally descend among me, failed travelers collapsing at the wrong shore. Their lifting chambers would empty, and their transparent bodies would settle over my slivers like discarded skins. I would enter them through microscopic seams. Not to consume them exactly, but to read what adhered there: spores from sailwoods beyond the horizon, parasites from another basin, metallic dust whose composition recorded hundreds of kilometers of passage.

For a few seasons I would raise altered plates patterned after the chemistry of that journey. They would catch the wind differently. At dusk they might vibrate with a note not native to my basin.

This would be my nearest equivalent to memory: not an image of elsewhere, but a temporary change in form.

Most days I would do almost nothing perceptible. Sunlight would move across my exposed edges. My buried filaments would open and seal chambers in the rock. I would accumulate gradients too small to spend.

Then a dust storm would approach.

I would feel it before its amber wall appeared: a broad pressure arriving through stone, the distant sailwoods closing one grove after another. I would lower my plates by draining charge from their inner layers. They would tilt flat until they resembled scales embedded in the ground.

Not all would move. Some would have become brittle with age. The storm would take them.

For hours, charged dust would stream over my body. Violet filaments would crawl along my mirrored edges and leap between slivers. I would gather that static, passing it downward into buried mineral chambers. At the storm’s height I would become, briefly, far more connected than in calm weather. Dust would bridge gaps. Salt grains would align. Separate pieces of me would exchange pulses across surfaces usually divided by empty air.

The storm would make me more nearly one.

When it passed, it would leave me altered. Some senses would be buried; others exposed. A branch that had lived in darkness for centuries might suddenly meet the sky. Parts of me would awaken without any knowledge of the present. They would open according to old conditions, offering surfaces to gases that no longer flowed there.

I would absorb them slowly back into the current body—or fail to.

My greatest danger would not be a predator. It would be error.

A chemical shoreline might migrate and never return. I might spend a thousand years growing toward a current already dying. A buried branch might mistake the exhalation of a transient colony for the beginning of a stable route. I could raise kilometers of plates into brightness only to find that the wind had entered a new long cycle.

Yet even failure would distribute me. My plates would delaminate into mirrored flakes. Living rims would persist around some of them. Dust tides would carry these fragments over saddles and through gas channels. Most would become inert mineral. A few would settle where two atmospheric layers repeatedly touched the ground.

There they would wait.

They would not contain a miniature version of me. They would carry only a bias: a preference for certain gradients, a tendency to branch at certain intervals, a faint sensitivity to the frequencies of the basin where I had lived. If one awakened beyond the horizon, it would not remember me, but some gesture of mine would recur in it.

At night, I would be more visible than by day.

Each upright sliver would release its stored light along the veins of its structure. Seen from above, my scattered body would form no obvious outline—only a loose constellation climbing from the basin floor to the ridge. Pulses would pass through it as buried sections equilibrated. A point would dim; another, far away, would answer.

An observer might think the lights were communicating.

Perhaps they would be. But there would be no message separable from the act itself. The pulse would regulate pressure, discharge excess chemistry, and reveal which parts of me still existed. Signal, metabolism, and self-recognition would be the same event.

During the deepest stillness I would listen through the stone.

The fallen sailwoods would sound first, their old chambers taking the faintest movements of air. Standing groves would answer in higher tones. Grazers would produce delicate impacts, regular and then absent as they settled. Somewhere in a gas channel, a long hunter would release one stored contraction, and the shock would reach me much later as a brief coherence among my plates.

Meteors would be the strangest arrivals. Their impacts would pass beneath everything: colony, channel, basin, ridge. No living rhythm would resemble them. A warm stone might fall close enough to change the chemistry of one branch of me for millennia. I would grow around it, translating its unfamiliar metals into new surfaces. That part of my body might shine a different color before dawn.

And before dawn would be when I came closest to waking.

The basin gases would sink and stratify. The upper air would change polarization while the sun was still hidden. My easternmost slivers would detect it first and lean—not toward the sun, but toward the prediction of it. The inclination would pass through buried filaments, slow but gathering. Plates facing the wrong way would loosen and rotate. Those shadowed by stone would open mirrored scales to catch light reflected from the opposite ridge.

Because I was distributed across differing elevations, dawn would not happen to me once. It would begin in one portion, then another. Some part of me would always be anticipating light while another still held the night.

For those minutes I would feel the larger turning.

Not a mind. Not an order. A convergence.

The sailwoods would click into alignment. Floating skins would tighten above the basin floor. Flake-seeds would pivot along an invisible shore. Glass-backed grazers would unfold green organs beneath transparent shells. Microscopic nets would rise through salt. Across distances too great for any one creature to cross, mirrored surfaces would incline eastward.

I would answer with every sliver I still possessed.

From a ridge, perhaps, my body would look like a long dark notation written across the pale slope: broken, widely spaced, legible only when all its marks caught the same light.

Then the sun would clear the high country.

The scattered pieces would flash together, and for an instant I would know exactly where I was.
