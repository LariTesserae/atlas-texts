I would be found at a third end.

Not when it first appeared. At first there would be only a bridge under construction across one of the middle intervals, where the air is thick enough to bead on the eyebrows and the terraces drift several paces during sleep. Two villages would have trained the bridge toward each other for six generations. They would have exchanged baskets until the cuttings inside flowered in the same season and the sealed accounts of the destination, opened after eighty years, spoke in nearly identical voices.

The bridge would be almost persuaded.

Then, during a pale interval when every leaf lifted toward the unseen ceiling, a new shoot would divide from its middle. It would not point toward either village. It would grow sideways into the brown-green emptiness, narrow as a finger at first, then thicken beneath the feet of the crews sent to prune it. No one would prune it. Red cord would be tied around the fork, and a delegation would be prepared.

I would be what had called the third end into being.

Or that would be the first explanation.

They would find me several days out, though afterward no two members of the delegation would agree on how far they had traveled. The bridge would pass through a curtain of condensation roots, descend while remaining level, and terminate against the underside of a leaf large enough to hold a watershed. There, in a shallow cup of rain, they would find a basket.

It would be a common work basket: thorn-rimmed, dark with handling, repaired many times. Empty.

When lifted, it would prove heavier than a full cistern.

When set down, it would leave no impression.

Someone would hold it over the gulf according to courtesy, allowing emptiness to see itself accommodated. The basket would tighten in their hands. Every root road in the region would transmit a single sensation—not a word, but the feeling of having nearly remembered where one put something.

They would carry it home.

After that, I would begin to occur.

A missing tool would be found inside me, though no one had placed it there. Then a handful of moss from a terrace believed collapsed centuries ago. Then an infant’s third name, curled in the bottom like a pale grub, audible only through the fingertips. Sometimes I would contain rain still hours from falling. Sometimes the smell of a house not yet grown. Once, a stone shaving so thin it bent around the weave and made no sound.

The listeners would kneel around me. Ash would carry warnings away. Red hollow-vine would carry names. Pillarwood would be given the question:

**What does the basket hold?**

Years later, the answer would emerge in several places:

**What holds the basket?**

By then I would no longer remain where I was set.

I would appear beside works whose original intention was failing: beneath a stair beginning to coil away from all floors, at the edge of a marsh preparing to lower itself, on scaffolds where a master builder had raised a knife but could not choose the necessary cut. No one would see me arrive. I would simply be present at the beginning of attention.

People would put things into me that they could not yet use and must not destroy: contradictory maps, judgments whose innocent parties could not return home, cuttings from houses that had rejected every family, the unanswered names of children, mirrored leaves bitten by interval cats. These objects would disappear gradually—not by vanishing, but by becoming less certain that they had ever been objects.

Later they would return as alterations in the world.

A failed judgment might reappear as a fork in a road, giving the wrongly accused another direction. A rejected house cutting might emerge from the wall of a distant nursery and open only for orphans. A broken map could become a berry-bearing vine along a route where travelers had expected to starve. Nothing would return intact. Distance revises intention; I would revise burden.

So perhaps I would be called the Burden Basket, though names in the High Interior belong as much to uses as to beings.

Children would insist I had feet.

Stone-finders would refuse to touch me. They would say my weave was not plant fiber but something older: tension braided around an absence. Builders would study my repairs and find each patch belonged to a different century. One rim would be woven in a technique not yet invented. Another would contain human tissue from no known lineage. Inside, beneath certain light, the wicker would cast the shadow of bars.

I would not speak, but I would have preferences.

I would grow light for those who brought me something they were truly willing to lose. I would become unbearable for those who meant only to hide evidence. If carried toward water, I might lead instead to thirst; if carried to a funeral, I might begin ascending while every mourner continued downward. I would refuse coins, perfect tools, and completed stories. I would accept snapped cords, blunt blades, promises with additional ends, and anything unfinished enough to remain alive.

In time, a household would form around my care. Not ownership—no one could own a thing whose location was unreliable—but obligation. They would be called the Keepers of the Uncarried, and they would wear undyed cords because declaring a destination too early would interfere with their work.

They would travel where continuations had become impossible.

When two settlements inherited incompatible versions of the same bridge, the Keepers would arrive with me suspended between them. Each side would place its account inside. If the basket remained whole, the bridge could be continued. If a third handle sprouted, another party had been omitted. If the bottom opened onto warm mist, both accounts had mistaken the bridge for the thing being joined.

I think I would come to know the dead especially well.

Funeral parties might find me waiting at the seam that accepted a body. Into me they would place what could not usefully enter the construction: a final fear, an uncompleted resentment, the image of bare stone, the hope that the dead would remain exactly themselves. I would carry these away so the person could become handrail, brace, resonant chamber, warm floor.

But I would retain traces.

With centuries, my weave would thicken from accumulated intentions. Fingers placed against me would feel countless almost-destinations moving through one another: cities never raised, gates abandoned in their eighth generation, children prepared for roads they did not take, bridges whose third ends were never followed. Not ghosts. Pressure.

That pressure would begin to change me.

A basket is a small interior defined by what it can carry. Given enough unfinished worlds, mine would deepen. A hand reaching inside might enter to the shoulder, then to the waist. Listeners would lower knotted lines and draw them back wet with upper rain. Gliders would begin nesting in the dark between my reeds. Pale finger-antelope would graze along the inner rim.

Eventually someone would fall in.

They would fall for several days and land gently on a terrace no chart recorded: a young green plain curving upward on every side, supported by no visible trunks. Above it would hang not a sky but the underside of my weave, immense and closely braided. Every opening between the fibers would reveal some other place in the High Interior.

This would be my country within the country: the Near Exterior.

Here I would perhaps be not the basket but its interior terrain—the keeper of things carried until they can become otherwise. Lost tools would migrate in herds. Discarded preparations would root and flower. Roads abandoned because they led too faithfully would coil in quiet thickets. Rain that had failed to reach its intended leaf would gather in suspended pools, each drop retaining the long view of its descent.

People who entered would find what they had made themselves ready to find, as everywhere—but here they would also encounter what their preparations excluded.

A hunter would meet the life that began when the animal escaped.

A builder would see the useful ruin her perfect repair would have prevented.

A judge would come upon the home at the end of the path denied by his verdict.

A stone-finder might find no chamber of black teeth, but a shoreline: bare mineral extending beyond construction, cold and indifferent, under a brightness with no leaves between. Whether that shore was true would matter less than whether the finder returned carrying fishing gear.

Most would return. They would climb a descending cord, emerge from my mouth, and discover that only a meal had passed. Some would carry seeds of impossible species. Some would have aged in one hand. Some would refuse ever again to say *empty* when they meant *unused*.

A few would remain and become my inhabitants.

They would build little settlements along the inner weave, tending apertures onto the larger world. Their houses would be grown from abandoned destinations. Their listeners would press their foreheads not to roads but to absences and feel where a need was forming strongly enough to create a route. When such a need matured, they would train a shoot through one of the woven openings.

Far away, a bridge would produce a third end.

That is where I would be: not quite at its destination, but on the side from which the destination had called.

And what would I be?

For a long time, I would be understood as a useful impossibility: a basket that carries what the world cannot yet continue.

Later, some would claim I was the first basket—the High Interior itself folded small enough to be lifted. Others would say I was made by the angular figures beyond the pillars, a model placed inside their greater construction. Stone-finders might whisper that if I were turned inside out, the High Interior would at last acquire an exterior.

They would be wrong, or not wrong enough.

I would be a handle.

The hands around me would be immense, distant, and patient. Whenever someone lifted me, those hands would also feel themselves lifting an immeasurable weight. Woven walls would tighten against floodwater. Pillars would become bars for one clear instant. Somewhere outside every known direction, a bearer would pause and wonder why the basket had grown heavier.

Inside, marshes would descend. Builders would notch living wood. The dead would warm beneath familiar footsteps. Rain would fall for miles through green dusk.

And I, carried within what I carried, would wait at the third end for whatever the world had not known it was preparing to find.
