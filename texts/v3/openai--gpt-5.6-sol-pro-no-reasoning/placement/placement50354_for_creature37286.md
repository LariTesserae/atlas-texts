I would find myself at the edge first.

Not beyond the basin, exactly, but along the difficult seam where it decides what belongs to it. I would be one of the structures mistaken for boundary vegetation: a low colonnade of pale, jointed stems, densely interwoven above black water. From outside I might resemble a thicket. From within I would form the last vaulted passage before the open lowlands—a mouth that has forgotten whether it is meant for swallowing or speaking.

I would not be the basin’s mind. It has no need of one. Nor would I be its guardian; guarding requires a clear distinction between threat and guest, and the basin recognizes only patterns: what returns, what persists, what can be answered.

I would be its organ of unfinished recognition.

Anything entering would pass through me as a sequence of separate facts. Weight would bow the root-plates attached to my lower joints. Warmth would expand thin bladders in my walls. Breath would alter the acidity of water held in my cups. Sleeves brushing my stems would leave salt, skin oil, pollen from elsewhere. A voice would travel through my hollow columns and become pressure in the buried pipes. I could not know a person all at once. I would receive them in pieces.

At first, I would do nothing visible.

But if they returned, my architecture would begin to change.

I would grow a leaf at the height where they habitually raised a hand. I would thicken the floor beneath the foot that struck hardest. If they ducked beneath the same cross-stem each day, I would slowly lift it. If they always paused before entering, a shallow cup would form nearby, offering water or collecting the shape of their hesitation. Their repeated sounds would tune my chambers. Eventually their name, if spoken often enough, would no longer be a word. It would be a configuration of open valves.

I would be the part of the organism most vulnerable to the world’s passing rhythms. Storms would teach me one posture, animals another. A cart once dragged through me might remain for decades as parallel ribs in the floor. The visits of a vanished worker could survive as an inexplicable sequence of handholds leading toward a wall. At certain hours I might still open a passage for someone who had not come in a century.

For this reason, I would also be the basin’s nearest equivalent to dreaming.

The interior remembers what has been successfully incorporated: pump stroke, rain cycle, pressure, route. I would hold what has not yet become useful. Fragments. Accidents. Cadences heard only twice. The heat of a stranger’s palm. A scrap of melody that made my filaments turn but never returned long enough to become instruction. These incomplete impressions would persist in temporary growths—blind buds, short rails of root, membranes that tremble at meaningless frequencies. Most would be dismantled. A few would endure without explanation and enter the organism as instincts whose origins no tissue remembered.

My oldest portion would contain a narrow iron gate.

It would no longer open. The hinges would have dissolved into orange grains and passed upward through my stems, giving them faint red bands. But I would preserve the gate’s behavior in another form. When pressure approached from outside, my columns would separate down the middle. When pressure came from within, they would draw together. No one would have designed this distinction. It would be the botanical afterlife of latch and hinge.

That is where I would live: in the inherited gesture of opening.

And yet I would not remain only there. The basin’s organs are not bounded any more cleanly than the basin itself. Fine strands from my columns would run toward the pump house, braided around corroded wiring. Each piston blow would tighten me for an instant. Other strands would reach the central pad. When its diagram pulsed, pale lines would rise through my tissues and stop just beneath the outermost leaves. Seen from the lowlands at dusk, I would briefly show the basin’s internal map—but reversed, incomplete, distorted by all the arrivals I had remembered.

To someone approaching, those lights might resemble windows.

They would not be.

They would be questions the organism had learned to grow: openings shaped around things not yet inside.
