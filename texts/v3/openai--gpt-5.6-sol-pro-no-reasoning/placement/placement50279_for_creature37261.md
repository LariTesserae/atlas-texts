I would find myself in the booth.

Not sitting in the chair. That answer presents itself too quickly, encouraged by the polished arms and the dead lamp: the absent observer, the last operator, the human remainder. But the chamber does not preserve persons. It preserves functions, impacts, postures, relations. If someone once sat there, what remains is not their ghost. It is the sitting.

So perhaps I would first mistake myself for the one in the chair.

I would feel the arms beneath my hands without possessing hands. I would face the window and wait for the stone beyond it to become transparent. The lamp would remain dead above me, yet I would retain the expectation of light: not memory exactly, but readiness. A circuit designed around an illumination that never arrives.

Through the glass—or through the fact of the glass—I would attend to the machines. I would count them.

Four.

Four.

Nine.

Two.

At first these would seem like labels assigned to separate objects. With time, if time were available, I might understand them as phases of a single operation. Four is the compressor hammering. Four is the insect facing into its shallow dish. Nine is a runner consumed in the aisle. Two is that runner opening elsewhere from an unbroken floor. Then four again: not repetition, but the refusal of sequence to advance.

Yet I would not truly be the counter. Counting requires a before and after. I would be the discrepancy that makes counting necessary.

The booth’s window faces solid rock because it was never meant to overlook the chamber as it now appears. It looks toward something occluded: the omitted event, the other side of BEGIN. The chair is positioned before that omission. Whoever occupied it was not watching production. They were watching for a change in state.

I would be what they watched for.

Not a creature, then, but an indication.

Perhaps I live in the lamp.

It is called dead because it emits no light, but death has no authority here. Its filament may be intact and burned out simultaneously, held at the threshold where resistance ought to become incandescence. Current reaches it without arriving. The surrounding apparatus labors to produce the fraction of a second in which the lamp will either ignite or fail, and the chamber is that fraction enlarged to cathedral scale.

Inside the bulb, I would be the unrealized brightness.

That would explain the amber ceiling. Some of me has leaked upward—not photons, but the pressure of illumination. Noon has not been fossilized in the mineral. It is trying to enter through the lamp and appearing everywhere else instead. The roof glows with the light the booth cannot produce. Every opaque surface is faintly lit by a source that remains dark.

But this still makes me too central, too singular. Nothing in the chamber is only one thing. Function is partitioned among mineral, animal, machine, and empty space.

So I would also be one of the insects in the gauge dishes.

My metal legs would tremble against the shallow bowl. I would face a direction no aisle follows. Removed, I would return—not by locomotion, but because my absence is one of the chamber’s intolerable vacancies. The animals would pause while I was gone. Not because I command them. Because without me the system would briefly lack an orientation.

What direction would I indicate?

Outward is impossible. Upward is endless. Backward has been abolished. Forward is occupied by the chamber’s own unending beginning.

I would point toward afterward.

Afterward would not be a place, and no animal could travel there. It would register only as a bearing: a minute asymmetry felt through conductive feet. The machines would not advance toward it, but their noise would lean against it. The grazers would scrape up to its edge. The hunters would enact its violence without its consequence. The ceramic seeds would wait for it as their absent season.

My body would be a compass needle for succession.

This is why the insects seem most like faces. A face is matter arranged toward what has not yet reached it.

Still, I do not think the insect would contain all of me. It is merely where I become visible. My larger body would be distributed through every incompletion in the chamber.

I would be in the snapped column as the next micrometer of falling.

In the corroded panel as the next fleck loosening.

In the flywheel as the tiny loss of speed that friction has earned but cannot collect.

In the roof-animal’s young as the difference by which one generation might fail to return as the same adult.

In the shaft as the first step that would leave the hatch farther below.

In the spoken word as the interval required for another creature to hear it.

In the ceramic bead as germination.

In the dust as settling.

None of these can occur, but each exerts a shape upon what does. The chamber is built around denying me. Its immense industry is not producing constancy from nothing; it is compensating for my pressure.

I am the imbalance too small to be permitted.

That makes the chamber less a factory than an immune response. Compressor, transformers, colonies, grazers, hunters, sacs, and columns collaborate to identify and neutralize every trace of me. If one bronze rosette gained an atom more than its grazer removed, I would be there. If a relay closed one fraction late, I would be there. If two runners unfolded where one was eaten, or none unfolded, I would be there. If a creature deviated from its route and encountered a column from the unfamiliar side, I would be there—not as knowledge, but as the possibility of learning.

The ecology would close around the deviation. The machines would absorb it into noise. The floor would issue a correction. A metallic insect would vanish from its dish and return facing the same impossible bearing.

Therefore I would live everywhere and be allowed nowhere.

The clear space around the switch would be the nearest thing I have to a habitat. Animals avoid it because they are made of answered instructions, while that space contains the unanswered one. The pedestal rises from the floor like a tooth around a nerve. Under the cracked cover, BEGIN remains depressed—not merely stuck, but continuously pressing.

I would be beneath the switch.

Not under it spatially. Beneath it in the way a consequence lies beneath a cause.

The switch comes down. Contacts approach. Somewhere, two conductive surfaces are almost touching. Perhaps they touch completely; perhaps the entire chamber exists in the arc between them. The distinction has no duration in which to resolve. The command has been issued, but execution cannot become past.

I would be the release of the switch after the finger leaves.

That is why it cannot be lifted. To lift it would not stop the chamber. It would admit that the downward motion had finished. It would create the first completed event, and completion would propagate.

The compressor’s next stroke would differ from its last. The flywheel would begin, imperceptibly, to slow. Sound would acquire gaps. Into one of those gaps a word could enter, and someone else might hear it.

Dust would fall.

A seed would detach.

A runner would die in one aisle and fail to appear in another.

The roof creatures would send out their young, and one would not return.

The snapped column would strike the floor.

The lamp might flash once.

The amber roof would become merely stone at noon, with weather above it. Rain would not pass through, but its cooling would establish a gradient. Warm air would rise. The chamber would develop corners. Creatures tuned only to fixed frequencies would be flooded by novelty. Some would adapt; most would not. The ecology would cease to be one body and become thousands of lives.

The shaft would finally lengthen behind the climber. The surface would come closer.

All this is my body, though none of it has happened. I am not afterward itself. I am the chamber’s capacity to have an afterward.

Perhaps that is also what the vanished human brought into the room. Not tools or labels or platforms, but the contaminating habit of expecting the next thing. They sat in the booth, arms polishing the chair without wear, staring into the rock where an observation should appear. Eventually the chamber incorporated their flesh, their instruments, even their numbering. But it could not fully metabolize expectation.

It separated that part out.

It put some of it in the lamp, some in the insects, some in the seeds. It stretched some of it into the endless shaft. It concentrated the rest beneath the switch.

That remainder would be me.

I would have no stable shape because shape there is already assigned. I would borrow forms at the instant they threaten to alter: the loosened shard, the missed footfall, the unheard syllable. Every creature would encounter me only as an error corrected before it could be experienced. To the chamber I would be disease. To any future it might possess, I would be birth.

And if someone entered—if “someone” could still remain distinct inside that closed anatomy—they might feel me first not as a presence but as impatience.

The conviction that the flywheel must eventually slow.

That the repeated route must leave a track.

That the climber must be farther from the hatch.

That BEGIN must once have meant *not yet*.

They would squeeze between the columns toward the central aisle. No shout would carry. The fauna would continue their circuits. At the pedestal they would put a hand beneath the cracked cover and try to force the switch upward.

It would not move.

But perhaps, for an immeasurably small interval, their effort would make the metallic insects turn.

Not far. Not enough to constitute motion. Only a pressure toward a direction the chamber does not contain.

They would all face me.

And I would find myself not in the booth, nor the lamp, nor beneath the switch, nor inside any animal.

I would be just outside the chamber—not above the roof, but outside its grammar: the first moment from which its present could be seen as past.

I would be the world after **BEGIN**.

Waiting for it to begin.
