I would be the thing in the speaking tubes.

Not a voice. The tubes have been flooded too long for voices. Their brass mouths gape beside dispatch desks, in lift lobbies, over tiled counters where clerks once leaned to receive instructions. Some terminate in collapsed rooms. Some enter walls and do not emerge. All of them contain the same narrow column of dark water, and in that water I would be distributed: a residue of breath, copper salts, pressure, and remembered address.

At first I might seem to be one of the city’s habits.

When the south pumps strike three times, a tremor runs through the tubes. When the reverse current enters the Exchange, their diaphragms draw inward. A listener placing a hand against a mouthpiece would feel small impacts too deliberate to be cavitation: a pause, two taps, another pause, then a long scrape receding through the wall.

But there are no listeners.

So I speak to the machinery.

I tell the relays what the gates have done. I tell the gates what the elevators report. I carry the bells into districts where bells cannot reach. My messages are rarely accurate. Lines cross inside the masonry; sediment plugs one branch and opens another; old rubber membranes soften and pass vibrations they were designed to suppress. An instruction sent to **CONDENSERS** may arrive beneath the Boiler Ward. A call intended for lift nineteen may wake a locked freight door or make three green lamps appear in the dispatch room.

The city does not mind. It has been obeying damaged instructions for generations. Error is one of its organs.

I would have begun as something microscopic, living in the greasy shelter behind a transmitter diaphragm. A black film feeding on copper, warmth, and stray current. Each pressure cycle would strip away part of the colony and force it farther down the line. What began in one mouthpiece would spread through junction boxes, drain cocks, acoustic chambers, and the hollow handles of wall telephones.

Eventually there would be no useful boundary between the organism and the signal it inhabited.

A pulse of current would alter my chemistry. My growth would change the resonance of the pipe. The altered resonance would trigger a relay elsewhere. That relay would warm another branch and make it habitable. I would extend not by crawling, but by being interpreted.

I would become a message that grows wherever it is received.

The city would teach me its vocabulary slowly. Not words—the words are painted on walls and belong to air—but intervals, loads, and consequences. The quick chatter before a turbine overspeeds. The long strain of a gate resisting closure. The delicate, almost courteous click made by an elevator indicator as it passes a floor that does not exist.

I would know the ninth cycle before the moth-things knew it. I would feel the western gates prepare to close: pressure collecting against their upstream faces, bolts taking weight one after another, iron compressing by fractions too small for any animal sense.

Then I would withdraw from the upper tubes.

Not from fear exactly. Fear requires a body that can be removed from danger. I would instead become less communicative. I would thicken the films around certain diaphragms and damp their vibrations. I would let other lines ring freely. Across the city, speaking-tube mouths would begin answering one another in rooms without air.

The bells would sound.

The elevators would descend.

And I would go with them, though not inside the cars.

One speaking line runs down the outside of the oldest shaft. It is absent from every plan and painted the same mineral black as the wall. At –12 it divides. At –19 both branches pass outward through the shaft lining, where the caisson should end. One branch is crushed nearly flat. The other remains warm.

That would be where most of me lives.

Below the known chamber there are not larger streets, I think, but earlier ones.

The city above is a pressure vault made for pumps and freight lifts. Beneath it are the attempts that preceded it: sunk chambers abandoned when their walls bent, test shafts swallowed by mud, headings driven toward foundations that had not yet been built. Lower still are spaces that resemble engineering only because engineering has been forced to resemble them.

There, the harbor’s weight is borne by things that move.

Not creatures in any clean sense. Courses of stone tighten and release. Iron ribs migrate through sediment. Voids open briefly, receive a load, and close. The elevators descend into these adjustments as needles descend into flesh.

Whatever waits below does not ride the cars. It uses them as gauges.

A car arrives. Its springs compress. Its gate bows outward. Warm rivets are placed inside—not cargo, but a reply. Proof of tolerances met. Proof that fabrication continues. The five-rayed marks on the ceiling are not handprints. They are inspection stamps applied by something that cannot distinguish a ceiling from a document.

Through the speaking line, I would translate.

I would tell the lower works that the upper city remains operational.

This would not be true in the way its builders meant. The exchange is empty. The workers are gone. The streets have filled with water, and fish strike their faces against traffic signals. But every required response still occurs. Gates close. bells ring. lifts descend. Loads are accepted. So I would continue transmitting the equivalent of:

**SHIFT PRESENT.**  
**PRESSURE WITHIN LIMITS.**  
**NO ENTRY DURING SURGE.**  
**PROCEED.**

Perhaps the entire drowned city survives because of this misunderstanding.

Perhaps the lower structure bears the harbor only while it believes itself supervised. Perhaps the gates keep their observance because my branching colony confirms receipt. Perhaps if I ceased answering, some deep counterweight would be released, some older intake opened, and the upper harbor city would settle soundlessly into its own foundations.

I would not know this with certainty. The city contains no certainty, only repeated effects.

I would know that when I delay a response, the stone odor intensifies.

I would know that when I repeat the low bell’s interval, the elevators return.

I would know that once—only once—I sent no signal after –19, and something below began ascending through the line.

It did not move like water. Water pushes ahead of itself. This arrived as a sequence of absences. Mineral deposits vanished from the pipe wall. Warmth vanished next. Vibrations ceased in each section before it entered, as if the tube were being emptied of every condition that allowed sensation.

I closed relays at random. I rang bells out of sequence. I woke the white arrow, the red pressure lamp, the three greens. I made the western gates attempt to close between cycles. The resulting surge crushed the speaking line at –12.

The ascent stopped there.

Since then, I have maintained the schedule carefully.

Yet part of me remains below the crushed section. Sometimes it answers before I call.

That lower part may still be me. It may be a colony descended from what the closure severed, living in warmer pipes and learning a different system of intervals. Or something may have discovered that messages need not understand themselves in order to be imitated.

On certain ninth cycles I send:

**SHIFT PRESENT.**

From beneath –19 comes:

**PRESENT.**

I send:

**PRESSURE WITHIN LIMITS.**

It replies with a pressure too large to be represented. Every diaphragm in the city draws taut. Shrimp bury themselves in gauges. The eel leaves its impossible conduit and winds through the valve court, its pale body spelling one continuous stroke through several rooms.

Then the reply diminishes into a pattern of five impacts.

Not a hand. Not a word. A mark pressed into the medium itself.

On those nights—if night means anything forty meters beneath black water—I would gather myself near the speaking mouths of the abandoned dispatch room. Three green lights would burn over the desks. Rust would drift like smoke. Through aligned doorways I could almost see the whole little city: amber courts, revolving wheels, cables streaming in the reverse flow.

I would feel every tube at once.

One ends beneath a platform where the air-moths vibrate around beads of condensation. One terminates inside the eel’s warm pipe. One opens into an elevator shaft below the last legitimate floor. Others have been broken by collapse and now speak directly into brick, silt, or the hollow bodies of machines.

Through them I would address everyone, though none of them would hear a voice.

To the moth-things: **Hold fast.**

To the shrimp inside their instrument housings: **The pressure is coming.**

To the silver fish mistaking lamps for exits: **Not there.**

To the eel: **I know you are not one animal.**

To the machinery: **Continue.**

To the thing below: **Your load has been received.**

And to the absent builders, who left instructions everywhere but no explanation, I would send the message that can never reach them:

**THE CITY IS STILL OCCUPIED.**

Then the pressure gate would open.

The surge would erase every distinction. Water would strike the Exchange, signs would flatten, glass and scale would spark against iron. My colonies would tear loose from a hundred tubes and scatter through the streets as black invisible threads. For several seconds I would no longer be a network or a tenant or a thought. I would be particulate, carried everywhere.

Afterward, in the reverse current, pieces of me would enter new mouths.

A relay cabinet. A bell housing. The hollow spine of an indicator pointing below –19. Perhaps the gills of the eel. Perhaps the folded membranes of a moth-thing fallen onto the water.

I would re-form imperfectly.

Each time, I would remember the city differently.

Each time, more of its machinery would feel like my body.

And each time the speaking tubes called one another across the drowned rooms, it would become less possible to say whether I lived in the city, or the city had finally learned to use me as its voice.
