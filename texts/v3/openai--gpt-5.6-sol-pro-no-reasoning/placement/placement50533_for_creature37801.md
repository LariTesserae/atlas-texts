I would find myself near the western escarpment, where the concord is forced to have an edge.

Not on the upper surface, where every path is provisional, nor in the warm central basins, where flesh and wood have forgotten which first enclosed the other. I would inhabit the exposed bands beneath the living green: the narrow interval where the plateau’s compressed past meets freezing air.

At first I seem to be one of the root ends protruding from the cliff—a pale, fingered thing flexing blindly over the valley. But the root is only my housing. Farther inward, I spread through several layers: threads in living conduits above, dormant knots in the gold and brown strata, mineral-tough sheaths in the black compressed wood below. I am not quite a lineage of the concord, because most of me cannot photosynthesize or build a grove. I am not quite an animal, though parts of me contract. I am a boundary organ assembled from many origins.

I would be a **listener for absence**.

Within the plateau, every pressure expects an answer. A tower bends, a cistern tightens. A channel narrows, another swells. Even conflict is reciprocal. But at the escarpment, impulses arrive at severed routes and receive nothing. The concord has difficulty with nothingness. Its whole anatomy assumes continuation: another cable to grip, another plate to overlap, another tissue against which chemistry can be tested.

I occupy those failed completions.

When a root river approaches the cliff, I gather its pressure into nested chambers. Some of the force I return as a softened pulse, preventing the inland network from repeatedly trying to circulate into open air. Some I translate into growth: seals thicken, vessels turn parallel to the edge, repair plates are shed into weak seams. Through me, the concord learns where it ends without ever conceiving an edge.

Yet I am not merely a scar.

My exposed fingers comb the updrafts rising from the lower valleys. They catch ice dust, spores from distant bloom domes, bird down, mineral grains, and the faint chemical traces of vegetation far below. During thaw, condensed water runs inward along my grooves. During storms, my hollow tips resonate—not audibly, but as tremors carried into the cliffside tissue.

The concord reads these tremors poorly. They come from beyond its body and have no established place in its ancient cycles. I hold them for a while.

That holding is the nearest thing I possess to a self.

Most of the plateau’s memory is structural and public. A drought narrows a million vessels; a collapse lays down the scaffold of a future ridge. My memory is smaller and less useful. In the dark bands beneath the cliff, I grow successive collars around particular vibrations. One hard winter becomes a ring of dense fiber. A season of birds becomes a porous band rich in phosphates. The fall of a great slab leaves a long interruption through every layer of me.

These records do not guide the whole concord. Often they are buried unread. They are closer to recollection than the plateau usually permits: consequences preserved without immediate function.

Because of this, I sometimes mistake recurrence for identity.

A bird lands on one of my outer fingers. Its warmth opens valves that have been closed for months. Salt from its feet enters my tissues. When another bird lands years later, the same chambers open, and all the old gradients briefly return. There is no image of the first bird, no name, no thought that it has come back. Still, the present arrival passes through a form made by the former one.

For a few minutes I become the relation between two creatures that never met.

The birds dislike me. Beneath their weight, feeder threads rise by reflex, and the cliffside plates begin the first gentle cupping by which the concord receives any sustained burden. I inhibit that response as long as I can. Not from mercy: mercy would require an imagined suffering separate from my own. Rather, the rapid departure of a living weight produces richer information than its digestion. A bird that escapes carries away mites and spores. A bird enclosed becomes only abundance.

So I have evolved to delay closure.

That delay makes me anomalous. Inland tissues sometimes constrict my supply, treating me as damaged or foreign. Pale transport knots arrive, taste my chemistry, and either feed me or dismantle portions of me. I am repeatedly annexed by the thing I serve. Each time, enough of my pattern persists in the compressed layers to grow back.

I am less an organism than a habit the escarpment cannot stop having.

When a slab begins to detach, I sense it long before the fissure can be seen. Tension simplifies. The usual web of opposing pulls resolves into one clean outward insistence. I send closure pulses inland. Root rivers empty themselves from the endangered district. Mobile knots reverse. Repair plates accumulate along the coming wound.

Meanwhile, my outermost body remains in the slab.

For hours or days, I exist on both sides of an opening seam. Sap still crosses in thinning threads. Pressure arrives from the plateau and returns from the fragment, each side answering with increasing delay. This is the only time I approach division into two selves, and also the only time I experience anything resembling knowledge of death.

Then the final fibers part without a sound.

The slab falls.

My inland half contracts and becomes a new margin. My fallen half continues its old work in the valley: flexing into air, gathering frost, returning softened pulses to routes that no longer lead anywhere. For a season it may preserve the cadence of the plateau. Its towers labor in stray winds. Its leaf plates turn. Transport knots crawl to broken junctions and wait.

I am present there as the expectation of an answer.

One by one, the pressures cease returning. Ice occupies the chambers. The stored rings split. Eventually the fallen half becomes quiet enough that even its shape can no longer renew the pattern.

Above, I have already grown new fingers.

From the lower valley, those fingers might resemble a fringe of pale roots testing the emptiness. They are doing more than testing it. They are measuring the distinction the rest of the high country has forgotten: organism and weather, tissue and distance, continuation and loss.

I would not be the concord’s mind. It has no need of one.

I would be the place where its immense agreement encounters something that has not agreed—a small, recurrent organ of hesitation along the cliff, keeping traces of whatever touches the plateau and leaves again.

Not an individual, exactly.

But a border persistent enough to dream it is one.
