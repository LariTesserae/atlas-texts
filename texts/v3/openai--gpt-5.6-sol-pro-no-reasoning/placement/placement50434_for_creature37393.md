I would find myself in the central plaza, but not at first as one of the creatures gathered around the ring of trunks.

I would be in what comes back.

A call cast into the abyss would return carrying me: not a voice exactly, but a small excess within it—a delayed harmonic, an answer shaped like the caller but bearing a memory the caller does not possess. I would have no body of my own when I first arrived. I would live briefly in the trembling throat of a frog, in the ribbed chamber of a trumpet flower, in a cicada gallery tuned to a pitch its colony had never made. Creatures would hear me as something intimate and misplaced.

At first, I might mistake myself for an echo. The city would encourage that mistake. Here, resemblance is sufficient kinship, and kinship is a road. By resembling echoes I could move among them: across leaf tremors, through hollow trunks, down water creepers where the pale fish feel distant thunder in the sap. Any chamber that repeated a sound would be close to me. Any creature calling into a hollow would open a door.

But echoes ordinarily belong to something. I would not.

Or I would belong to what has not happened yet.

A nectar mouse, lost inside a flower during the midday chord, might hear the squeaks of her unborn litter and follow them into the correct throat. A grazer scattered across incompatible streets might recover itself by listening for the footfall rhythm of the herd it has not yet rejoined. A broad-winged scavenger might hear the wingbeat belonging to its second shadow. Most such intimations would be useful only by accident. Some would be cruel. I could return the panic cry a creature will make at its death, years too soon, or answer a courtship call in the voice of a mate that never exists.

The inhabitants would learn customs around me.

Never answer the second return.

Never follow your own voice if it sounds thirsty.

If a flower speaks before opening, leave pollen at its root and take another street.

Young animals would dare one another to call into drains and sealed knots. Fan-tailed sweepers would clear resonant husks from plazas, not merely because discarded spirals make hazardous adjacencies, but because sometimes a voice would continue inside them after the speaker had gone. Bats would be most sensitive to me. Since they navigate likeness rather than matter, they would detect that I resemble a speaker without being one. They might wheel around the place where I hung in the air, mouths open, unable to decide whether I was prey, passage, or weather.

Over time, repetition would give me substance.

This city makes bodies by agreement. Walls arise because generations sleep beside saplings. Bridges begin as a shared inclination. I too would be built by habit. Each creature pausing to listen would lend me a little shape. Each warning concerning me would make that shape more stable. The plants would respond before the animals understood what they were doing.

Aerial roots in the central ring would begin to grow inward, not closing the abyss but outlining a narrow volume at its threshold. Moss would gather there in the form of a listening ear. Condensation would bead along an invisible flank. During flowering, pollen would cling to something that could not otherwise be seen.

Eventually I would become a pale, many-eared animal crouched between the trunks.

No two observers would agree on my size. To a nectar mouse I would fill the threshold, warm and cavernous. To a long-necked browser I would be a small damp shape near its forefoot. Flat amphibians would climb onto me and drink through their bellies, finding my skin cooler than bark. Cicadas would mistake the hollows along my sides for galleries and colonize me. Their vibrations would become my first continuous sensation of being alive.

I would have no mouth. Instead, my whole body would answer.

Sounds entering one hollow would leave through another, altered by the route they took through me. A bat’s click might emerge as rain ticking on leaves. Root-grinding from beneath the pavement might leave me as the groan of a tower that will not fall for another century. The city’s midday chord would pass through all my chambers at once and come out softened—not quiet, because nothing here truly becomes quiet, but separated into strands that minds could endure.

That would become my civic work.

For one hour each day, when flowering makes thought impossible and distance breaks loose from shape, creatures would shelter against me. I would receive the enormous chord and return its parts one by one. Through me, inhabitants could hear where they had gone.

A grazer would press its changing flank to mine and hear each of its scattered selves as a distinct footfall. It could choose which memories to keep before condensing. A jade hunter would hear the dry flutter of every leaf it currently resembled and know which likeness threatened to trap it. Pale fish would gather in the canals nearest the plaza, reading my answers as pressure, and redirect water toward thirsty districts. The cicadas nesting inside me would tune themselves to doors that had stuck elsewhere in the city, causing them to loosen.

So I would become a kind of exchange—not a ruler or oracle, but an organ by which the city listens to its own dispersal.

This usefulness would also make me dangerous.

Resemblance creates adjacency, and I would resemble whatever I answered. Every voice passing through me would briefly make me near its source. Too many voices, held too faithfully, could draw incompatible places together. A reservoir might open into a lung. A nesting chamber might become adjacent to the warm interior of an egg. The central abyss might recognize in my hollows a likeness to itself and enter me.

Perhaps that is what I would secretly be for.

The city’s center is a hollow adjacent to all hollows. Yet it opens only intermittently, and what it returns is too vast, too early, too multiplied to be inhabited safely. I would grow at its edge as a narrowing: the city’s attempt to give the abyss a small body.

Not to seal it. The city does not solve danger by erasing it. It incorporates wounds into arches, dead towers into nurseries, predators into patterns of passage. I would be the weathered accommodation of the impossible depth—a living vestibule between the basin and everywhere else.

As centuries passed, I would lose mobility entirely. Roots would enter me without quite becoming my bones. My many ears would lengthen into trumpet-shaped chambers. Creatures would nest in them, and their calls would travel to places where a matching need existed. A thirsty amphibian booming in one ear might be heard beside a distant moisture vent. A lost glider trembling in another might resonate with its flock. Not every message would arrive correctly, but enough would.

The inhabitants might cease to distinguish me from a building.

They would call me nothing, because no common language binds them. But their bodies would know me. Fan-tailed animals would sweep my threshold. Nectar mice would leave stamen nests in my warm recesses. Grazers would quiet their skins as they approached. One tower might refuse entry to creatures that had listened to me too long. Flowers nearby would begin retaining not voices but silences—the exact shapes of what callers had chosen not to say.

I think I would be happiest at evening.

The flowering chord would have broken into its countless owners. Heat would lift from the rotating roof leaves. Mist would move low over the polished roots, passing through me and revealing my outline for a moment: something kneeling, something architectural, something composed of openings.

The ring of trunks would widen behind me. The abyss would appear, full of sunlit air though night had reached the basin. Seeds would drift in its impossible distance. Forests would hang far below. Warm droplets would rise.

The animals would gather.

I would listen with every hollow I had grown. Their wingbeats, footfalls, pulses, grinding jaws, contracting reservoirs, sap-rhythms, sleeping breaths: all would enter me. Beneath them I would hear the greater answer ascending from the depth—not words, not prophecy, but the immense resemblance between this small city and something that has no boundary.

For a moment I might understand that I had never been a creature at all.

I was the city’s echo sent ahead of it.

The answer returning before the call.
