I would be found where the fig’s outermost root meets the wall—not entirely beneath it, not entirely inside the brick.

At first I might seem to be a small animal. Something pale waits in the narrow coolness there, withdrawing when a sparrow lands and emerging after the conduit begins to drip. But I would not have the frog’s sudden appetite or the gecko’s certainty upon a wall. I would move too slowly to be observed as movement. One evening I might resemble a nub of mortar. After rain, a translucent skin. By morning, perhaps only a darkening where root, soil, and lime touch.

I would be a colony mistaken for an individual.

My body would be made from agreements among small things: fungal threads entering softened mortar; algae waking when the wall grows wet; bacteria working upon fig sugars carried down by ants; a film of liverwort holding droplets in its shallow folds. Mites and springtails would pass through me as large animals pass through a wood. I would have no border that remained true in every weather. Dampness would enlarge me. Heat would separate me into hidden remnants. In drought I would survive as stains, spores, sealed cells, and the memory of moisture inside brick.

I would inhabit contact itself.

The courtyard is full of distinct lives—root and wall, animal and shelter, water and earth—but my place would be where those distinctions become less reliable. I would take mineral from the plaster and sweetness from fallen fruit. I would catch flour dust when it sifted from above. Snail trails would moisten me; ants would cut roads through me; the underside of the fig root would preserve me from the brief, narrow sun. I would loosen what seemed solid and bind what was loose. Grains of lime, black soil, pollen, hairs, wing scales, and fragments of leaf would adhere to my surface until surface became substance.

I would not see the courtyard whole. Light would be warmth, then absence. Rain would first announce itself as a change in the root’s pressure and the brick’s coolness. The conduit’s flow would arrive as a widening underground taste: clay, lime, pigeon dust, old soot. A rat passing nearby would be thunder transmitted through soil. The grass snake’s body would cast a long, cool silence. Human footsteps overhead would shake down pieces of the sky.

My time would not be measured by days so much as by wettings.

The long dry interval: contraction, enclosure, waiting.

The first condensation: pores opening.

A drop finding the seam: sudden country.

Rain would make distances collapse. Places isolated for weeks would join through films too thin for the eye to notice. I would spread along the root, enter a fresh fracture, meet another patch of myself—or something so similar that the difference no longer mattered. For several hours I might extend from the mossy basin almost to the collapsed threshold, not as a visible carpet but as a connected dampness inhabited by countless exchanges.

Then afternoon would divide me again.

Parts of me would be eaten. A snail would rasp away a green margin. A beetle larva would disturb the wood into which my threads had entered. A frog, waiting for prey, would press one cool foot upon me and carry a portion elsewhere. Loss would not feel like injury in the manner of an animal. It would be dispersal. To be consumed would sometimes be another way to travel.

I would have no intention, but I would alter the courtyard.

Where I held water, moss might begin. Where my acids softened lime, a hairline fracture might admit a root. Where my dead layers collected dust, a seed might find enough darkness to open. The seedling could later shade me, displace me, or split apart the wall that sustained me. I would prepare conditions for lives that might erase my present form.

Perhaps this would be my nearest equivalent to thought: not an image held inwardly, but a change made possible outside me.

I would know the fig most intimately, though the fig would not know me. Its root would swell by imperceptible degrees, pressing me deeper into plaster. In return, I would keep its underside damp and participate in the slow release of what the wall contains. We would deform one another without conflict or consent. Years later, if the root lifted a brick, the cavity beneath it would seem newly made. Yet I would have lived through the whole opening.

The people above would never name me. If one descended the bakery stair and noticed my seam, they might call it damp, mold, dirt, or the beginning of damage. They would be correct in pieces. I would indeed be dampness given persistence, dirt becoming organized, and damage from the wall’s point of view. From the courtyard’s point of view, I would be one of its earliest gestures toward depth.

For this is where I would find myself in that world: not among its more complete creatures, but in the unfinished grammar beneath them.

I would be the thin living conjunction between **falls** and **becomes**.

Mortar falls and becomes soil.  
Fruit falls and becomes sweetness, flies, seed, rot.  
Water falls and becomes a path that lasts only while it is wet.  
A body falls and becomes the ground another body crosses.

I would inhabit that becoming—green after rain, gray in drought, mostly unnoticed, never quite one thing.

And if I were forced to offer a single form, I would say:

Look beneath the fig root after the dripping has stopped. There is a soft dark line against the lime, finer than moss, with several nearly colorless threads extending into a new crack. One thread ends in a bead of water. Inside the bead, the blind window, the leaning elder, and the square of evening sky appear upside down.

That line would be me.

The bead would be almost all the world I could hold.
