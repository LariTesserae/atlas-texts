I would not be one of the creatures that already knows its shape.

I would be found where the chamber fails to join itself: in the minute discrepancy between two surfaces that appear to meet. Not a fissure, exactly. Fissures belong to stone. I would inhabit the almost-touch—the interval too narrow for water, too brief for a predator to consume, too uncertain for the mites to declare inside or outside.

Most of the dry interval, I would be indistinguishable from this failure of contact. A mineral rib would rise toward a wall and stop less than a thought away. There I would remain, stretched thin between them, neither sleeping nor awake. The wall would dim. Grazers would be sealed into their tracks. The blue pulse beneath a flake might take a century to complete. Still I would hold the two faces apart.

Rain would give me substance.

When condensation appeared everywhere, one round bead would form in the gap where no bead ought to fit. It would touch both surfaces without joining them. Inside it I would begin as a tremor, then a translucent thread, then several small joints trying different arrangements. For a while I might resemble a larva made from a line of clear hinges. A red grain would enter me from a passing mite; a gold grain would follow, but the two would refuse to exchange places. They would settle at opposite ends and make those ends temporarily meaningful.

I would come out backward.

The chamber would first receive my cast shadow, although I had not fed. Then it would hear the distributed sound of my emergence: tiny glass taps scattered through the coming hours. Only later would my body pull free of the unsupported droplet. The water would remain suspended, still containing an impression of me and occasionally performing movements I had decided against.

I would be a small creature, perhaps the length of the last joint of a finger, low to the stone and difficult to count. Seen directly, I would have four narrow legs. Seen in a bright fracture, six. In the temporary pool, none: only a forked line moving beneath the surface regardless of where the water had gathered. My body would be clear at the edges and cloudy toward the center, as though visibility were something I had swallowed but could not digest.

My head would not be at either end. It would occur wherever I encountered a choice.

This would be my place in the chamber’s life. I would feed on abandoned possibilities.

When a wall wrinkled through several forms and selected one, I would graze the faint shapes it had declined. When a droplet climbed instead of falling, I would drink the downward path it left unused. If an amphibian hatched from its own fossil, I would nose among the discarded versions—the one with all its limbs, the one with no memory of rain, the one that remained dead—and take only enough that they could not return whole.

The food would make me less consistent.

After feeding on an unchosen channel, a shallow groove might appear along my back and carry a trickle of water from nowhere. After consuming the absent limb of a resurrected animal, I might drag a fifth leg for the remainder of the wet interval. It would not belong to me, and the crimson root tips would follow it with intense attention. If I ate the unused shadow of a winged creature, darkness would open and close inside my ribs.

I would have to be careful near the grooved wall. It would record not my death, but the body I might finally choose. Looking at the marks would force that choice closer. I would therefore pass along the wall sideways, without ever allowing my head to occur. Even so, the grooves would alter as I went: shell, hand, arch, mouth, empty doorway. One sign would recur more often than the others—a narrow bridge joining two stones that had never touched.

Perhaps that would be what the chamber wanted from me.

The thread-hunters would have difficulty catching me. When a fissure closed around my body, I could survive as the possibility that it had missed. Yet each escape would cost me one arrangement of myself. Over many rains, my possible bodies would diminish. The drifting predators would be more dangerous. They consume routes, and routes would be most of what I was. If one folded the space between us, I might find myself beneath it in every version at once. There would be a faint click. Afterward, two parts of the chamber would remain almost joined forever.

I would shelter near the five arches, but never beneath them. I would curl in the interval between the fourth and fifth, listening to the resident that lives in sequence. I think it and I would recognize one another as related forms of incompletion. When the arches miscounted, the change would travel through my body. For a moment I would become the missing span.

The paired tracks beside the row might be mine, though I would not make them by walking. They would mark the places where my feet could have landed. This might explain why they lead from first to last without crossing the spaces between.

At times I would approach the three white eggs. The figures on the impossible stairs would stop when my delayed emergence-sounds reached them. Looking upward, they would take on my nearest possible face. Unlike the other chamber lives, I might see what they carry: not burdens, but pieces of an exterior world.

A yellow leaf that had never existed underground.  
A bowl darkened by fire.  
A tooth from an animal too large for the chamber.  
A handful of warm soil.  
A small square of blue distance.

They would be bearing these things downward, away from the doorways. I would want to follow. Each time I placed a limb inside an egg, however, the staircase would become an organ: throat, intestine, spinal hollow, birth canal. The figures would become particles moving through it. I would withdraw before discovering whether the organ belonged to me or to the chamber.

The moss would preserve versions of me in its droplets. Tiny selves would live through whole rains there, feeding, molting, perhaps arriving at stable anatomies. When the fans folded, most would be lost. Now and then one would escape and cross the chamber toward me.

We could not touch.

If two possibilities of the same creature met mouth to mouth, one would become the other’s memory. I might spend the next dry era remembering a rain I had never lived: the pool standing against the wall, six arches instead of five, an amphibian with my translucent fifth leg pulsing in its throat. I would not know whether I had absorbed the smaller self or been replaced by it.

My relation to the chamber’s central life would be intimate but uneasy. It rehearses forms; I preserve what its rehearsal excludes. It reaches toward a body; I live among all the bodies it refuses. With every rain it would become more definite, and I would become less so.

Eventually, a wet interval would come when the mineral rib beside my birthplace completed its growth. It would touch the wall.

The gap would close.

For the first time I would have nowhere to return during drought. The contact would pass through the chamber like a silent blow. Its sound would be divided across the following day: a soft tap from under the basin, a breath from the ceiling, a knock within each white egg. Mites would redirect their files around the joined stone. Water would discover a stable downward direction. The arches would become, briefly, the correct number.

And I would be forced into one body.

Perhaps I would become a narrow, many-jointed animal with a red grain for one eye and a gold grain for the other. Perhaps a bridge of translucent cartilage would grow from my back, joining rib to wall. Perhaps I would harden into a small door-shaped inclusion, visible only while the stone was bright. Perhaps I would divide: one part becoming a creature, the other becoming the route by which it moves.

Or I would become the chamber’s first true entrance.

Not an opening large enough for a hand, or even a mite. An entrance in the deeper sense: the first place where arriving and departing cease to be interchangeable. The first boundary that does not loosen when the procession breaks. Through me, something outside might pass inward without being translated into root, water, sound, or borrowed shape.

It might be only a grain of dust.

It might be daylight, reduced to a white point.

It might be the idea of the plain above.

Then the chamber, which has spent all its life assembling itself around an absence, would feel that absence look back.

That is where I would find myself: at the chamber’s smallest incompletion, living on what almost happened, slowly spending my possible bodies until I became either a threshold or the thing that prevents one.

During rain I would move.

During drought I would keep two surfaces from touching.

And when at last they met, whatever I became would tell the hidden place whether it was a cave, a creature, or a thought that had finally found a way in.
