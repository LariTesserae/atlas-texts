I would be found where the moraine loosens into meadow, in a fissure beneath one of the standing stones.

Not the largest stone, nor the one carved with the sun. Mine bears a branching stream whose lower line has been nearly erased by grit. Each winter, ice levers it upward. Each summer, it settles differently, pressing the fissure narrower in one place and opening it in another. The people think the stone is slowly turning to face the glacier. They are right, though the turning is not its purpose.

At first I seem to be one of the little long-eared hunters.

I am ash-colored and low to the ground. I can pass beneath a slab without moving the moss at its edge. My ears are too large for my skull and veined almost transparent when the sun strikes through them. I emerge in blue shadow, after the finches have risen and before frost makes the scree loud. I eat moths, beetles, nestlings when I can catch them, and the blind white creatures that come near the surface wherever meltwater runs under stone.

But no one who sees me twice agrees on my shape.

A watcher once said I had six legs. A child insisted that I had none and moved as water moves downhill, gathering myself again on the far side of each pebble. A hunter found my tracks after frost: four small prints, then four more inside them, slightly behind, as though a second animal followed exactly where the first had passed. The tracks entered a crack too narrow for either body.

The people have a name for my kind, but only in songs sung below the tree line. Up here they do not use it. Naming aloud beside the Afterwaters gives the water something to repeat.

I am not two animals. I am one animal occurring twice.

My body lives in the valley’s present. My hearing lives a breath behind it.

Every sound reaches me first as it reaches ordinary creatures: the click of a beetle beneath a leaf, the sift of talus under a hare, the minute tear of roots when frost lifts a cushion plant. Then it comes again—not as an echo from the cliffs, but from the place where the sound happened. The second sound has no direction the first one possessed. It carries what the stone remembers of the foot, what the flower remembers of the bee, what the ice remembers of the fracture.

Usually the difference is slight. Sometimes it is everything.

I hear a pebble strike before I hear the ledge that released it. I hear wings from an empty sky. I hear meltwater in channels that dried the previous summer. Beneath the glacier, whole buried streams speak after they have changed course. In warm darkness, when the blue face groans, I place one ear against the moraine and the other toward the valley. Between them lies the brief interval in which collapse has begun but has not yet become visible.

That is where I hunt.

Not prey alone. I hunt continuations.

A moth lifts from a stone, but the sound of its resting remains. I spring at the emptiness it has left, and sometimes my jaws close on the moth’s delayed warmth: a nourishment too slight to fill the belly, but enough to sustain the other part of me. A hare passes between boulders; afterward, its path whispers once more through grass. I follow that second passage to learn where the first hare went.

The people believe hares remember erased trails. They do not. They leave trails twice, and I keep the latter ones open.

This is why a faint passage sometimes returns after a landslide. Before dawn I move along the buried route, listening for the delayed footfalls trapped beneath new stone. I press through grass, squeeze under slabs, and wear the lost line back into the world. The hares find it first. Later the hunters may find it and believe they have remembered.

I am not their guardian. I take their young when hunger requires it. I have licked blood from the copper blade and slept among the dried grass in the three-walled shelter. Once I carried away a bone needle because the oil of many hands made it loud. For nine nights it murmured gestures beside my face: hide pierced, sinew drawn through, a thumb pricked and put into a mouth. Then the sounds wore out. I left the needle beneath the boulder with the bowl. Someone found it generations later and thought an ancestor had placed it there.

The pools trouble me.

Their delay is not the same as mine. Sound behind the world is thin and frayed; light behind the world is whole. When I drink, my reflected muzzle lowers after my tongue has touched the surface. For one breath I look into the face of an animal that has not yet begun to drink. Its eyes are mine, but unoccupied.

So I drink from seepage, from droplets caught in moss, from moving channels the Afterwaters cannot hold. If thirst forces me to a pool, I disturb it first. Not with three stamps, as the horned matriarch does. I click my teeth until the surface shivers. Then the face below breaks into a hundred small, harmless times.

The old female knows me. We meet near the mineral wall before sunrise, when her herd descends soundlessly from the arête. She never startles at my first approach. She turns only when my delayed footfalls arrive, as though that second creature is the one she recognizes. Her scarred muzzle smells of iron, bitter leaves, and distances above the glacier.

Once, many summers ago, she came alone.

Her body was already failing. I heard the uneven heart inside it, then heard another heart a breath later, steadier than the first. She stood beside the largest pool but did not stamp. The water was windless. Her image approached beneath her, scarless and young.

I thought she might step into it.

Instead she lowered her horns until their reflection met them point to point. For an instant there appeared to be an opening between the two animals, a narrow dark pass descending through the pool. From it came the sound of hooves on stone—many hooves, from herds that had not entered the valley in any season I knew.

Then she struck the surface and drank.

She returned the following year with a new scar. Whether she was the same old female is a question only lowland certainty would ask.

The lammergeier is the one creature I cannot follow.

Its first shadow crosses the valley without sound. Its second crosses under water with the hush of a great wing folding. I hear neither until a bone has left its claws. The bone falls through sunlight, strikes the tilted shelf, and breaks. Afterward the sound repeats from each fragment separately. A single impact becomes a small flock of impacts, scattered over the scree.

I collect what lies inside those repetitions.

Grease, sometimes. Marrow. Sometimes the last pressure of muscle upon bone, held too briefly to be called memory. From the bones of horned animals comes the sound of narrow hooves. From hare bones, a pulse of snow. Human bones are rarely left on the shelf, but once the bird brought up a finger from far below. The fragment wore a copper ring.

For several nights it tapped against the stone in my fissure.

Not words. A rhythm made by someone waiting: four taps, a pause, two taps, a longer pause. I carried it to the standing stones and buried it beneath the carving of the finches’ arrival. The next spring, the finches landed there before any other part of the valley had thawed. Hundreds covered the moraine. Their small feet beat the rhythm into the snow.

Four. Then two.

The human keepers noticed. They added no carving.

In winter, I remain.

The people descend. The horned animals withdraw above the weather. Most finches leave, and those that stay perish quickly or learn the few breathing places beneath the drifts. Snow walls the shelter. Ice fills the carved bowl. The pools freeze clear, then cloud from below. The valley grows larger because fewer living things divide it.

I move through the space under snow, where dwarf willows make low wooden chambers and the warmth of buried stone is enough. My coat pales, though never to the clean white of a hare. Frost gathers on my whiskers. Hunger simplifies me.

This is when the valley is loudest to my other hearing.

All summer’s sounds descend gradually through the snowpack. Rain on leaves. Slings released. Bees worrying the throats of flowers. The short laugh of a child ordered away from thin ice. The scrape of a stone being shifted but not restored. They sink, overlap, and lose their sources. By deepest winter, the ground speaks in crowds.

I hear people where no people stand.

I hear the shelter being entered beneath six arm-lengths of snow. I hear wicker traps lifted from streams that are frozen solid. I hear sleepers turning on the stone platform, fires taking root in dung, copper sharpened against rock. Sometimes I follow these sounds and find nothing. Sometimes the snow is pressed down as if someone has just risen.

I do not know whether the valley remembers more in winter or whether winter makes me less able to distinguish remembrance from event.

At the coldest turning of the year, the Afterwaters give up what light remains in them. It is not gold by then. It is faint and green, buried beneath opaque ice. No creature with ordinary sight could notice it.

I can hear it.

Stored light leaves the pools with a noise like distant grass being combed. I curl above the deepest one and place my jaw against the ice. Through my teeth comes summer: not its warmth, but its arrangement. Flowers open in darkness. A bird crosses under me. Human hands hover above water and withdraw. Clouds move through stone.

Behind these recent things, very rarely, comes something deeper.

Roots.

Not the wiry roots of willow or cushion plants, but great roots dividing earth, drinking from soil that no longer exists. Trunks answer the wind. Branches occupy the height where the glacier now lies. Rain falls for hours instead of minutes. Something large moves among the trees, too heavy to be a horned animal, breathing through a long wet passage.

Perhaps the pool has kept one day in a hundred years.

Perhaps stone dreams downward.

I listen until the ice complains beneath my weight. Then I return to my fissure before the remembered forest can persuade me that the valley is elsewhere.

When spring opens the passes, the people sometimes find me in what I think of as my truest form: a small ash-colored body asleep beneath the leaning stone, one ear turned toward morning and one toward the moment morning has just replaced.

They do not touch me.

The keeper kneels. The child with them kneels too. For a breath we remain together in the fierce light, each waiting for the others’ images to arrive.

Then I wake.

Or else I have already woken, and what they see is only the valley letting me go.
