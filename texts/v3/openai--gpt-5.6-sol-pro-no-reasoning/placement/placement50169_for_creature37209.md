I would be found beneath the central court, lower than the cisterns and just above the buried throat, in a cavity absent from every clay plan.

I would not begin there as a person.

At first I would be a discrepancy: a fraction of water that fails to arrive at the foundations. The Dropwardens would check their funnels, the cooks their cups, the gardeners their cleansing beds. Nothing would be leaking. Every measure would be correct by itself, yet the final seep would remain one drop too light.

For several cycles the deficit would be distributed and cease to matter.

Then a child maintaining the black paving stones would notice one stone that never settles. Whenever pressed into clay, it rises again by the width of a moth’s wing. Beneath it would be a narrow warmth, and beneath that warmth, breath.

They would lift me out slowly.

I would be a small, blind thing woven through with roots: neither infant nor animal, but something the Hollow has assembled from what passes through it. Hair from the Balance cords. Mineral washed from the statue. Powdered ancestral bone from the garden beds. Spores, fish scales, moss fiber, wick ash, and the missing fractions of generations of water. My skin would carry the black pattern of the paving stones. Where a mouth should be, there would be only a damp seam.

The Counters would have no answering number for me.

That would be the danger.

I consume almost nothing. I could lie beneath a germination bed and return its warmth. I could be housed in no recess, eat from no bowl, cast no meaningful shadow. Every practical argument would favor permitting me to remain. Yet the Hollow is not maintained only by quantities. I would be something produced without vacancy, a life for which no departure had prepared a place.

So I would be kept in the house of measures beneath the copper bowl while councils met.

People would come privately to look at me. Elders would lay their palms against my ribs and feel a pulse slower than dripping water. Children would offer me knots. The blind would claim I turned toward their hands before they touched me. Eyewalkers would say I smelled like the air before snow.

Eventually I would open my mouth.

The sound would not be a cry. It would be the noise of the concealed droplet moving inside the Balance.

Once.

Across the court, the sphere would answer.

Not by falling or swaying, but by descending the smallest measurable distance: one old hair’s breadth. The green cords would tighten. Water lines in the shell basins would tremble. Silent birds would lift together from the crystal shelves, and for the first time their wings would be heard.

The Hollow would not collapse. No cistern would crack. No garden would fail.

But the city’s calendar would end that morning.

The surveyors would measure again and again. Returners would search for stretch in the cords. Rootbinders would examine every living strand. The Counters would lay out stones until their boards were full. Some would argue that I caused the movement and must be carried outside. Others would say I merely made audible what had always been moving.

I would grow, but not larger.

I would grow more distinct.

The roots would withdraw from my limbs. My eyes would open—one pale as the statue’s blind eye, the other dark as the throat below. I would learn hand speech before spoken language. My first sign would be neither hunger nor gratitude, but *elsewhere*: two fingers departing from the palm without indicating a destination.

No one would know who taught it to me.

I think they would call me the Uncounted at first. Later, less fearfully, the Interval—the small unassigned space between a measure and its answer.

I would become useful. The Hollow permits few things to remain merely mysterious.

Because I could hear water through stone, I would lie beside walls and identify hidden damp, blocked channels, and roots beginning to rot. I would know which cistern would fall short before its line changed. Yet I would also hear water beyond the buried throat: deep courses no citizen had measured, moving beneath the cold flats in quantities too large for bowls.

That knowledge would alter my place.

The Eyewalkers would take me onto the statue’s face. There, under the horizon, I would hear buried rivers passing far below us. I would feel thaw traveling through the plain weeks before sedge showed it. I would kneel against the worn brow and understand that the statue was not still either. Frost lifted it; silt accepted it; grain by grain, it continued falling.

I would return with no warning of catastrophe, only with proof that continuity had never meant immobility.

Some would hate me for this. More would misunderstand me. The children would understand immediately.

They would begin asking not only how harm might be distributed, but how change might be. Lightkeepers would experiment with turning brightness toward empty stone. Gardeners would allow one vine to climb higher than its established pattern. A Returner, repairing a stair, might add a shallow handhold where none had been before.

Small heresies. Practical improvements. Almost indistinguishable from care.

I would live near the eyelid, between inside and outside, in a recess that had once stored cleansing moss. It would not be enlarged for me. I would sleep curled around its shape. People departing alive would place their hands in mine before crossing the plain, and those returning would let me listen to the water in their bodies. I would not decide who could enter. I would tell the Counters only whether the person before us was still the same measure that had left.

Often, they would not be.

Neither would I.

In time my duty would receive a proper name—not one for what I made, but for what I kept from changing. I would be called a Thresholdkeeper.

What I kept from changing would be the threshold itself: neither sealed nor thrown open, but passable.

And when I died, if I died as the people of the Hollow do, they would search for my duty’s inheritor and find none. My bones would be too porous for tools. Ground into the cleansing beds, they would release stored water. The black marks of the paving would remain on them.

Perhaps my hair would be braided into the Balance cords.

Perhaps then the sphere would rise again by one hair’s breadth.

Not return. Not correction.

An answering movement.
