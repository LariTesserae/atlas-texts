I would find myself where the firm ground gives way—not in a house or beneath a pylon, but along the temporary paths between them.

At first I imagine being one of the people who walks those paths at dawn, carrying a coil of rope and a bundle of painted stakes. Someone sent out after the worst of the night’s work to discover what still connects to what. I would test yesterday’s crossings with a long pole, note where water has warmed, watch the angle of reeds and the behavior of birds. My maps would be small and perishable. I would make no claim that a route was safe, only that it had held while I crossed it.

But that image is too solitary. No one survives here by being merely observant. I would belong to the scattered chain of people who keep one another reachable: sounding a horn, repairing a bridge joint, carrying medicine by boat, tying a guide rope between a house and a stand of spruce before the fog closes. Perhaps my work would be called routekeeping, though there would be few permanent routes to keep. The real task would be maintaining continuity while the landscape changes its terms.

I would know households by their signals rather than their faces. A bronze bell with a cracked lower note. Three short horn blasts from the northern trees. A porch lamp hooded in red. A particular rope whose tremor means someone is crossing beyond sight. I would remember which old woman refuses mechanical assistance with her shutters, which child notices pressure shifts before the gauges do, which family keeps a spare boat tethered downstream because upstream is never where one expects it to remain.

I might have been trained as a hydrologist, or apprenticed to one of the old technicians, but I would no longer pretend the distinction mattered. My instruments would hang beside animal signs, knots, and inherited sayings. I would lower probes into warm pools and listen to vibrations in buried mesh, then postpone a crossing because the deer had abandoned it. I would understand enough of the works to recognize heat transfer, soil binding, and the early pulse of a pressure correction. Never enough to mistake understanding for authority.

Yet even this does not feel quite right. It puts me too far above the ground, too intact and singular.

Perhaps I would be the route itself.

Not a road, because roads demand persistence. I would be a narrow succession of agreements: stiffened peat, exposed roots, a gravel tongue, two jointed planks, a rope vanishing into rain. Deer would establish me first. People would follow, pressing stakes into my margins. Wolves would use me at night. Maintenance carriers might cross without leaving marks. For several weeks I would connect the forest houses to a service building, a fish channel, and the higher ground beneath the ridge.

Then the works would alter the watershed. One end of me would sink. Another would become a bank beside a new stream. Willows would root through my old center. The rope would be recovered, the stakes repainted and moved. No one would mourn me as a lost road, because I had never promised permanence. My purpose would have been fulfilled each time something living reached the other side.

But a path is only what others make of it. The basin’s truest inhabitants are not merely adaptable; they are connective. Fungi, microbial films, roots, conduits, watercourses, migration lines, warning systems—everything essential passes beneath categories and between bodies.

So perhaps I would be found under the moss.

I would be a fungal network living where spruce roots meet the warm outer skin of a buried conduit. Part native organism, part descendant of strains introduced during restoration, changed by generations of mineral rain and engineered heat. My threads would enter peat, drowned timber, clay fractures, root tissue, and the repair film coating old infrastructure. I would not know where the forest ended and the works began.

Through me, trees shifted onto unfamiliar soil would regain access to nutrients. I would bind a raw cut long enough for sedges to take it. I would taste metals exposed by moving ground and wake microbial communities suited to seal them. A pressure change in the conduit would pass through my wet filaments before it reached the reeds. I would tighten around roots—not from foresight, exactly, but from a chemistry that had become indistinguishable from warning.

I would have no view of the basin. No horizon. No concept of a pylon or a storm. I would know the world as gradients: warmth advancing, oxygen withdrawing, minerals blooming into reach, roots signaling damage, water changing its pressure in the dark. A deer’s hoof would be a brief compression overhead. A house lifting on its piers would arrive as a slow rearrangement of weight. The buried works waking would be an immense season moving through me in seconds.

When a raft of peat tore free, parts of me would travel with it. Some threads would rupture. Others would remain in the bank. Neither portion would be the original, and both would continue. On the new ground I would enter displaced willow roots and begin again before the leaves had finished turning toward their altered sky.

People might occasionally notice me. A technician cutting back moss from an exposed seam would find pale threads woven through the protective coating. A child would lift a fragment of rotten wood and see a faint blue response travel across it. Someone might sample me, argue whether I was contamination or maintenance, and return me carefully to the wet earth because uncertainty here encourages caution.

I would not be important alone. That would be the condition of my life. My intelligence, if anyone insisted on calling it that, would exist only in the exchanges: root to fungus, fungus to film, film to conduit, conduit to the vast buried correction, then back through warmed soil into root and leaf. I would participate in decisions without conceiving them and sustain lives I could never perceive.

Still, I think some part of me would remain with the routekeeper above.

At morning, they would walk over the ground I helped hold. They would stop where a blue-white line had passed beneath the marsh during the night. They would kneel, press a gloved hand to the moss, and feel a residual warmth. Around them the basin would be newly arranged: a stream where the path had been, an island carrying three mature willows, gravel lifting pale and clean from black water. Far off, a horn would sound twice.

The routekeeper would rise and move the first stake.

Below, I would be moving too—not going anywhere as an animal goes, but extending into the morning’s new surfaces. Neither of us would trust the shape of the land. Both of us would work to make it inhabitable for one more crossing.

That is where I would find myself: at the living seam between the buried works and the unstable world above them.

I would be something that does not preserve places.

I would preserve passage.
