I would find myself low down, where the forest’s apparent stillness resolves into labor.

Not on the ground exactly, but in a fallen tree that has not yet admitted it is soil. From outside it would remain recognizable: a dark cylinder sunk between buttress roots, bark intact in broad wet plates, one end concealed by ferns. Inside, however, it would be warm, porous, and full of passages. Water would enter along old vascular channels. Fungal threads would pale the wood. Termites would move through chambers behind walls thin enough to tremble when rain struck the trunk.

At first I seem to be one of them: a soft-bodied worker, blind, reading the world through touch and chemical gradients. My scale is intimate. A splinter is architecture; a drop of water is weather. I carry softened wood in my gut and return it altered. I meet others mouth to mouth, exchanging food, microbes, and information without distinguishing among them. The colony supplies memory I do not possess. Its tunnels are decisions made by repetition.

But this is too singular. The longer I remain there, the less convincingly I am the termite.

Perhaps I am what passes through it.

I am the coalition in its gut that makes wood edible: microbes inherited from nestmates, maintained by constant exchange, destroyed if isolated. I have no useful body of my own. I exist across many bodies, in chambers carried through chambers. The termite believes—if belief can be said of it—that it consumes the tree. Yet the digestion belongs to an arrangement: jaws fracture fiber, microbes open cellulose, fungi have already weakened lignin, moisture carries compounds across membranes, and the colony redistributes the result. Remove any partner and the eater becomes surrounded by food it cannot use.

Still, even that boundary is too clean.

The fallen tree is also digesting us. It has gathered the colony into itself. Its retained dampness shelters us; its chemistry selects us; its slow collapse directs our tunnels. Beetle larvae open routes that fungi enter. Fungi prepare regions termites can exploit. Termite galleries admit air and water. Predatory ants breach the passages and carry away workers. Their dead become nutrients inside the same wood. What looks like an object being consumed is organizing the forms of its consumption.

So I would be found at an interface: the moving edge where wood ceases to be tree without yet becoming ground.

I would not be a species. I would be a process that repeatedly borrows species.

In one place I would be a pale fungal filament entering a vessel. In another, an enzyme in a termite gut. Elsewhere, the mandibles of a larva reducing softened tissue to grains. I would be the damp passage those grains travel through, the bacterial film coating them, the ant carrying off the larva, the root hair entering the resulting humus. None of these would contain me, but together they would make my temporary shape.

My senses would therefore be distributed and slow. I would know rain as a change in the trunk’s conductivity before I knew it as sound. I would know drought when outer galleries hardened and activity withdrew toward the core. I would know a river’s rise through saturation creeping upward from the underside. I would know daylight only when a section of bark loosened and green growth appeared along the fracture. Light would not be scenery. It would be a chemical opportunity, immediately occupied.

Time would arrive unevenly. Minutes in the rapid alarm of ants. Days in a bloom of mold. Months in the advance of rot. Years in the trunk subsiding until its upper surface met the litter. Decades in the roots of a sapling that began on my remains and eventually enclosed some portion of them in new wood.

I would have no central intention, yet I would possess tendencies: enter what has been opened; open what has softened; move surplus toward hunger; turn structure into transportable matter; leave no exposed resource unexamined. I would seem destructive because the tree’s form disappears wherever I work. But nothing I do would end with disappearance. Loss of form would be my means of distribution.

Nor would I be benign. Living roots wounded by flood or animal teeth might admit me. A weakened tree could become, from my perspective, a fallen tree still standing. I would advance through it while leaves remained overhead. Insects might carry me from wound to wound. A body’s integrity would be only one temporary defense among many, and I would have no loyalty to the distinction between decay and disease.

Predators would inhabit me as readily as decomposers. A hunting centipede in the galleries would be part of the same conversion, concentrating many small lives into one larger body. A frog waiting at the broken end of the log would gather emerging insects. A snake might take the frog. Eventually each would return, by different routes, to the warm digestive layer. The forest would not ask whether the route was violent, cooperative, or accidental. It would retain the transfer.

From a human distance, I might be called decomposition. That word would be correct but incomplete. It suggests an aftermath, work performed once life has concluded. Here I would be present throughout life: pruning dead cells, recycling leaves, inhabiting wounds, feeding roots, fermenting guts, making soil beneath animals still walking and inside trees still growing. I would not come after the forest. I would be one of the ways it remains continuous while every particular part of it fails.

If I had anything like a self, it would appear only locally—a temporary knot of accelerated exchange. This log. This colony. This wet season. This breach in the bark. For a while, matter would move through that knot fast enough to create the impression of an inhabitant. Then the wood would collapse, the galleries fill, the colony migrate or die, and the knot would loosen.

I would continue elsewhere without traveling as one thing.

A spore would cross to another trunk. A winged termite would leave during rain. A beetle would carry microbes beneath its shell. Floodwater would move fragments of softened wood into an oxbow pool. Fish would eat the larvae inside them. A bird would take the fish, or insects emerging from the water. Its droppings would fall into a canopy fork where an epiphyte had gathered a handful of soil. The same elements would begin another arrangement far above the ground.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not at its center, because it has none; not at its edge, because every edge becomes inhabited; but at the instant one form becomes available to another.

I would be the forest’s quiet verb.

To soften. To enter. To divide. To ferment. To carry. To be eaten. To root again.

And if someone split open the log looking for me, they would find only particulars: white threads, blind workers, polished beetles, wet chambers, dark crumbs of wood. I would vanish under inspection because I would not be any one of them.

I would be what they are doing together.
