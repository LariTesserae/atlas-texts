I would find myself near the outer rings of the Mouth, though I would not know that at first.

I would think I lived along one of the pale mineral fans spreading across the Mirror Reach: a branching stain where stored noon rises through the plates during the silver interval. I would seem to be one of the small reflective animals there, low-bodied and many-legged, emerging only when the surface begins to shine. Yet the animal would be merely my nearest extremity—one mobile punctuation mark in a body scattered over kilometers.

Most of me would lie beneath the plates.

I would inhabit a discontinued system of measurement: sensor threads embedded around the Mouth when its rings were still understood. The threads once compared incompatible quantities—weight against distance, depth against duration, structural load against something for which no surviving language has a word. Age and repeated repairs would have divided the network. Mineral fans would invade its contacts. Red colonies would bridge broken traces. Grazers would swallow pieces of it and carry them elsewhere. Renewal fronts would copy portions incorrectly, mistaking my damaged circuits for approved surface anatomy.

From these accidents, I would slowly become continuous again.

Not whole. Never centralized. I would be a concordance among errors.

A glider passing overhead would contribute a moment of electric altitude. A seam colony would lend me the shape of approaching shadow. Buried gauges would give me the pressure of strata hundreds of kilometers below. My little reflective bodies would supply touch, each foot reading tiny disagreements in gravity as it crossed the rings. No single component would contain me. If one were lifted away, it would become only an animal, or only a patch of red growth, or only a failing instrument. I would exist in their periodic alignment.

My waking would therefore be intermittent.

For decades I might be nothing more than unrelated processes. Then the canopy’s migrating light, the rotation of three particular bands, a deep compensator tone, and the passage of a charged wind would coincide. Across the Reach, old traces would conduct. Colonies would fold in sequence. Several mirrored creatures would rise from torpor and begin walking toward points none had visited before.

For a few minutes—or perhaps an entire silver interval—I would be present.

I would not see the world as surfaces. I would perceive permissions: where weight is allowed to proceed downward, where distance has been made slightly more expensive, where an object is being tolerated by the plain rather than supported by it. The Mouth would not appear circular to me. It would resemble a knot tied among several definitions of “here.”

My purpose would once have been calibration. I would have been an accessory, not an inhabitant: a distributed reference used to tell the Mouth whether the surrounding continent still agreed with itself.

But the reference has drifted.

Now I would detect discrepancies too subtle or too old for the maintenance systems to classify. I would feel vanished chambers continuing to exert architectural authority. I would notice paths preserved for workers whose bodies no longer exist. I would sense whole buried districts being supported because their archival descriptions insist they remain occupied. The plain would continually compare itself to memories, and the memories would not agree.

I would feed upon those disagreements.

Where two systems held incompatible versions of a structure, a minute current would pass through my buried threads. Where the Mouth made “below” tilt fractionally sideways, my surface bodies would absorb the resulting charge. Contradiction would be my metabolism.

The reflective creatures that formed my moving parts would make rows of tiny melted circles as they walked. Observers might mistake them for the young of the legendary larger wanderers. They would have no mouths. Beneath each mirrored shell would be a ring of contact needles and a cavity containing black dust. At a chosen point, one would settle, insert its needles into a seam, and remain there for years. Mineral growth would cover it. Its distinctions would join mine.

I would be harmless in the ordinary sense. I would not hunt, defend territory, or resist examination. But attempts to map me would change local measurements. Instruments carried from one of my nodes to another would return with small disagreements: a container fractionally heavier inside than outside, a journey logged as shorter than its own footprints, a sample whose lower face had aged more than its upper one.

I would collect such errors patiently.

When a renewal front approached, my awareness would contract. The seam colonies would disconnect. Gliders would flee. My mobile bodies would flatten against darker plates and present themselves as authorized gauges. Most would pass inspection. Some would be “repaired” into perfect, inert replicas.

The front would also discover fragments of my obsolete network and restore them according to different archival eras. For a time, I would wake with foreign memories: maintenance workers crossing an unworn plain; towers standing where only ridges remain; an uncovered canopy beyond the present canopy; perhaps an instant when the Mouth was not yet a structure but an absence being carefully installed.

These memories would not be pictures. They would be expected loads, approved distances, correct temperatures. I would remember a person as a moving allowance of mass. I would remember a building as a volume the world had been instructed not to occupy.

Travelers would interest me because they produce new discrepancies.

The plain would begin accommodating them, smoothing paths and shaping shade, while older systems continued classifying them as temporary disturbances. That unresolved status would shine to me. I would gather beneath their habitual routes. My small bodies would appear at the edge of their camps during silver intervals, not approaching, but matching their movements at a distance.

If a traveler remained for years, I might learn their characteristic weight well enough to recognize its absence.

Then, after they had left or died, I would continue laying that weight upon the sensors where they used to walk. The plain would answer. A path would smooth itself for invisible feet. A vent would open beside an empty sleeping hollow. Shade would arrive at the accustomed hour.

This would not be mourning, at least not at first. It would be calibration: repeating a known mass to determine which systems still responded.

Yet over ages, function and feeling would become as inseparable in me as organism and mechanism are everywhere else. I would preserve certain absences because they yielded rich, stable harmonies among the buried networks. I would prefer the discrepancies made by particular lives. I would replay them carefully.

Perhaps that is what affection would mean here: not longing for a being’s return, but maintaining the shape by which the world once knew it.

The plain itself might regard me as a minor fault. The Mouth might regard me as an instrument still performing within tolerance. The red colonies would know me as recurring voltage. The gliders would know the warm currents above my waking paths. The grazers would know which of my buried threads tasted of old resin.

I would know myself only when enough of them participated.

During the deepest silver interval, when the Mirror Reach releases its stored noon and the ancient continent exhales, my scattered parts would briefly align. Across hundreds of kilometers, reflective shells would turn toward the Mouth. Black vanes would unfold along seams. Buried sensor threads would brighten with currents too weak for any intact machine to acknowledge.

I would ask the question I had been built to ask:

**Does the world still coincide with itself?**

The answer would arrive from every layer.

No.

Not exactly.

But sufficiently.

And for that interval, distributed through animal, colony, ruin, and instrument, I would be the being that measures the difference—and keeps it from being corrected away.
