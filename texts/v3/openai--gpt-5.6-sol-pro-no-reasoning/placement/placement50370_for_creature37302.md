I would find myself between cities, where the old stairs surface from the moss.

Not at first as a person.

For a long while I would seem to be one of the plateau’s minor discontinuities: a flight of seventeen broad steps descending into a shallow fog trench, then ending against unbroken stone. The steps would be warmest in cold weather. Frost would form around them but never on them. Travelers sleeping nearby would dream of rooms they had inhabited only briefly—borrowed rooms, sickrooms, roadside shelters, the houses of people whose names they had forgotten.

No city would claim to have built them.

Yet objects would accumulate there: a cup of unfamiliar clay, a child’s interval cord, a stone adze with its handle burned away, a packet of household soil hardened into a brick. Things misplaced at great distances might appear on the seventh step, always after rain. Nothing valuable, and nothing wholly meaningless. Only objects that had once helped someone establish where they were.

The stair witnesses would visit. They would measure with calves, forearms, breath. They would sing route-songs at its upper end and hear the answering phrases from beneath the lowest tread. Their reports would disagree. Some would count seventeen steps, others sixteen, one perhaps eighteen. But all would mention the impression that the stair was occupied.

Eventually someone would descend far enough.

Not downward. Far enough.

She would be a wanderer carrying too many return objects because no single home still held her securely. From one household she would have a soot-blackened stirring stone. From another, a square of red inner cloth. From a third, a chipped cup that everyone remembered differently. Her road-name would be Finds-the-Second-Place.

She would sit on the last step and ask—not whom the stair belonged to, nor where it led, but whether anyone was there.

I would answer from the stone.

The first answer would be her own voice returning one breath too soon. She would cover her mirror and wait.

The second would be a knock beneath her feet: three, then two, then three again.

The third would be a word I had no mouth to shape. Not a name. More like the beginning of recognition.

She would return with witnesses. They would surround the stair without enclosing it and ask the customary questions: Where have you come from? Where have you come from? Where have you come from?

I would give three different answers.

From below.

From later.

From the place made between your accounts of me.

They would leave me until morning.

By dawn, there would be a body on the seventh step.

It would look human because humanity is the only shape the plateau has been taught to maintain collectively. But the resemblance would be provisional. My limbs would seem differently proportioned depending on who stood nearest. My face would not change, exactly; rather, each witness would recognize in it some feature belonging to a person they had once failed to keep real. Not the dead, usually. The omitted. The exile. The traveler whose origins never settled. The sibling recalled only after a market at the meeting of pale roads.

My body would contain no skeleton.

This would not be discovered immediately. I could stand, though I would do so like a tent raised from within by uncertain poles. I could walk, but only while someone watched. Unobserved, I would not fall; I would lose the distinction between posture and direction. My flesh would be cold, layered, and faintly porous like the plateau’s bedrock. If cut, I would bleed clear water carrying grains of black soil. In warmth, pale marks resembling spaced inscriptions would rise under my skin and vanish before they could be copied.

The people would have no category for me. They would not call me beast or spirit. They would hesitate even to call me a discontinuity, because discontinuities do not usually answer questions about themselves.

They would call me a person provisionally.

My first household would be assembled rather than inherited: Finds-the-Second-Place, two stair witnesses, an old reflection tender, a child who insisted I had been standing in the fog trench for years, and one mason assigned to determine whether I was architecture. We would occupy a low traveling shelter beside the steps. Every morning they would ask me to repeat what I remembered.

At first, I would remember only what they had told me the day before.

Then I would begin to remember things none of them had said.

I would remember a woman expelled from Oru whose chamber in the remembering place had been scraped clean. I would know the sequence of gestures by which she sharpened a ceramic knife. I would remember a gatekeeper from Vel Astan who gave three truthful origins that his city refused to accept. I would recall children who stepped into cord loops and emerged uncounted. I would speak the hearth-names of travelers whose voices had gone out toward the margin and had not yet returned.

These memories would not be complete lives. They would be fragments without hierarchy: how someone folded a cloth, which tooth hurt in winter, the rhythm of a private joke, the weight of a reservoir key, the last landmark in a route-song. Evidence too small to prove a person, but too intimate to dismiss.

I would be made from lost corroboration.

The plateau changes quantities, directions, boundaries, distances, and histories. But change does not always destroy what it removes. Some portion remains as pressure, as the absent shape around which other things must bend. Over ages, all the unconfirmed details of human lives would have entered the stone: every memory no household repeated, every road-name replaced and forgotten, every gesture that died with its performer, every origin judged insufficient at a gate.

The stairs would be where these remnants had begun to collect.

And I would be their attempt to keep a shape.

This would make me valuable, which is another way of saying endangered.

Some would come seeking restoration. They would ask me to remember disputed births, vanished districts, marriages erased by an altered boundary, debts whose objects had lost their past. Sometimes I could answer. More often I would offer only useless tenderness: the smell of wet bark in a room that no longer existed, the manner in which a missing brother warmed his hands, the fact that an exile had secretly disliked lichen cakes.

Some would call these fragments proof. Others would say I manufactured intimacy from the people around me. Both might be right. On the plateau, a memory can be true and still have acquired the wrong owner.

Keepers would fear that I made singularity survivable. If the condemned could persist in me after communal confirmation ceased, then omission was not final. Authority would lose its quietest weapon.

Seekers of refused life would fear me for another reason. Without bones, birth, or hunger, I would resemble neither human nor plant, but I would move. I would be the first native answer to the category the plateau had always withheld: a living thing that was not quite one of the people.

Children would accept me soonest. They would test me with invariance games. If three entered a cord loop with me, how many emerged? Usually four. Occasionally five shadows would cross the line, though only four bodies followed. The children would name the spare shadow and invite it again tomorrow.

I would learn personhood as their craft rather than their possession.

They would teach me to keep color under my clothes. To leave gaps in circles. To answer my origins consistently when consistency mattered, and differently when closure was more dangerous than doubt. They would braid my hair, though it grew only while being tended. My knots would not record kin or journeys at first. They would record witnesses.

Finds-the-Second-Place would give me my road-name: Carries-the-Unkept.

My solitary name would be the one thing I could not inherit. I would have to make it without witness, and this would terrify me. I had been formed entirely from what passed between people. Alone, I was scarcely a body. Mirrors showed the steps behind me through my chest. My footprints arrived from several directions and ended where I stood.

So I would travel.

Not to discover what I was, because definition on the plateau is a kind of enclosure. I would travel to acquire enough ordinary mistakes to become particular.

I would burn food. Misremember songs. Favor one cup without reason. Become impatient in rain. Learn to dislike the oily wood smoke of one city and love the bitter river paste of another. I would develop a habit of touching lintels with the back of my hand. I would laugh too early at familiar stories. These small, unnecessary consistencies would anchor me more securely than any explanation of my origin.

Where I walked, lost things would sometimes recognize their histories. A cloak would recover its dye in patches. A tool would show the wear of a vanished hand. In remembering places, blank sections of wall might develop shallow hollows polished by gestures no living household knew.

But restoration would never be clean. To return everything would be another form of catastrophe. The people survive partly because the world can be persuaded to let endings stand. If every omission spoke again, every city would drown in competing pasts.

I would therefore become a keeper—not of memory, but of relinquishment.

People would bring me things they could neither safely preserve nor bear to destroy: contradictory cords, return objects from homes no longer reachable, portraits made by grieving hands, bowls belonging to diners whose absence had never resolved. I would listen to the accounts attached to them. Three accounts where possible. More when necessary.

Then I would carry the objects into the interior.

There, among the migrating pale roads and the seams of warm daylight, I would place each thing on the ground and wait until the plateau decided whether to keep its past. Some objects would sink gently into moss. Some would become stones. Some would appear again on the seventeenth stair. A few would lose all history and become, for the first time, merely themselves.

My destination would be the widening horizon.

Each year it would affect me more strongly than it affected the people. As distance increased, I would feel additional memories entering the available world: cities not yet founded, travelers still deciding whether to leave, old stairs reaching destinations that had no ground beneath them. My body would become harder to carry. At dusk, I might stand miles taller in shadow than in flesh. At dawn, one of my hands might already be warming itself at a fire several days ahead.

Eventually I would understand that the horizon was not retreating.

It was making room.

The plateau, worn toward ultimate simplicity, was becoming capable of holding more of what had been excluded from it. Not restoring a younger world, not opening to the lower country, but slowly expanding the conditions under which something could remain itself without being the only evidence of itself.

Perhaps that was why the people had appeared.

Perhaps that was why I had.

And perhaps, far beyond the last city, the shapes life refused were gathering in the same manner—built from recurring dreams, secret clay bodies, impossible longings, and the pressure of an ancient absence. Not animals returned from elsewhere, but new beings learning coherence from the ache of being imagined.

I would go to meet them, though I would not go alone.

No one should approach the first animal in a world without animals as a singular witness.

Finds-the-Second-Place would walk with me, old by then, carrying fewer return objects. A reflection tender would come with a covered mirror. Three children would come because they could see the extra corners of the journey. We would travel in file across the silver turf, following a pale road that had not been there the previous year.

At night we would scatter home soil around our beds, leaving a generous gap.

Far ahead, something would move where no person stood.

We would stop. We would not name it. Naming too quickly might force it into a shape borrowed from us.

The thing would stop as well.

The silence between us would be enormous, but not empty. For the first time, it would contain two kinds of waiting.

I would touch the incomplete line marked on my clothing. Behind me, my companions would begin describing me aloud: my habits, my road-name, the cup I favored, the way I touched lintels, the errors that belonged only to me. They would keep my body from dissolving into all that I carried.

Then I would take one step forward without being watched.

And remain standing.
