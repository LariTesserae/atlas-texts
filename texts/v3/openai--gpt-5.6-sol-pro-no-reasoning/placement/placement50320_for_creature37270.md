I would find myself where the cultivated saddle begins to loosen into wilderness, in the last inhabited structure before the marker ropes.

Not at the blue drop itself. I would tell others that I preferred the edge, but preference would only be part of it. The deeper truth would be that the inner city had never quite answered my touch correctly.

The place would be an abandoned signal garden grown through the ribs of an older house. Its original household had moved inward after a terrace slumped and altered the current. The root bulb remained sound, but one side had opened, leaving its rooms exposed to the passing water. I would keep it that way. No contracting door, no woven screen—only a curtain of bead-vines whose hard seeds knocked together when anything large approached.

Above the house, three pale stems would climb toward a canopy no longer broad enough to be called a roof. Below it, the mountain would fall away in furred shelves. One communal line would still reach the city, faintly taut. The other would run outward between cairns and then continue beyond them, where no civic line was supposed to go.

I would be a keeper of that forbidden extension.

Officially, I would be a current reader attached to the outer gardens. I would inspect marker ropes, listen for stonefall, count migratory animals, and watch for loose growth that might descend into the city’s lower channels. I would know the grazers that could be turned with a rattle of shell and those that must be allowed to pass. I would mend seed cloth after funerals without asking which garden received whom. Children spending their wilderness season would be sent to me to learn how cultivated species misbehave when no one prunes them.

They would expect an austere border keeper and discover someone broad-faced and mineral-pale, with one clouded eye and an old tear through the breathing folds beneath the left jaw. My hands would be darker than the rest of me, stained by a vine whose fibers resist rot. The deliberate scars of a graft surgeon would cross my fingers, but stop at the wrists.

I would once have trained for that honored work.

As an apprentice, I would have believed that anything torn could be joined if the edges were cut cleanly enough. Stem to stem. Membrane to membrane. Child’s flesh to cultivated tissue. I would have been patient, precise, and proud of not trembling. Then, during a failure in one of the Anchor’s inner passages, I would have made a graft that survived perfectly and harmed everything around it.

Perhaps I joined two vascular columns to bypass a diseased section. The new channel took. Sap moved. The wound closed. For a season I was praised.

Then the redirected pressure starved an older branchwork no living record had marked as important. A chamber of the Anchor calcified. Its tension changed. One district lost the deep resonance by which it received warnings from the lower terraces. No one died immediately. That made the error worse. It entered daily life slowly: missed pulses, uneasy animals, cracks noticed late, people laying hands against familiar walls and finding them mute.

The graft was not badly made. It was made too well.

They would not exile me. The city’s law did not punish ignorance as malice, and my teachers had approved the cut. But afterward, every wall I touched would seem to contain consequences moving beyond sensation. I would leave surgery and take work farther and farther outward, where mistakes had room to disperse.

At the marker ropes I would begin to hear something.

Not a voice. Not even a rhythm at first. Only a return pulse arriving through the mountain after the city’s drums had fallen quiet. It would come too late to be an echo and too regularly to be random stone. Sometimes hours later. Sometimes several turnings of daylight. A faint answer beneath the threshold of most bodies, entering through the outward line and gathering in my jaw.

I would extend the rope to test it.

This would be the offense at the center of my life. A line beyond the maintained world could carry warning back, but it could also conduct danger inward. Large animals might follow its vibration. A drifting forest might catch it. Someone lost to loosening might take hold, and no one could know what else had learned their rhythm.

So I would not fasten it directly to the city. Between the wilderness line and the communal line I would grow a living relay: a chambered plant bred from neighborhood drum stock, root-hollow reeds, and a deep-water vine brought home by a returner who no longer spoke correctly. The relay would touch neither line unless I drew its membranes tight. Most of the time the two systems would remain separated by a hand’s width of cold water.

My work would be to kneel in that interval.

One hand on the city. One on whatever answered from beyond.

Years would pass before I understood that the distant pulses were not one source. Some would be stone stresses traveling through the drowned range. Some would be filter-feeders calling from valleys too deep for daylight. Some might be weather at the surface translated down the mountain’s submerged body. And some—rare, shaped, separated by intervals no animal used—would resemble intentional signals.

Not our signals. Nothing so convenient as language.

They would repeat with variation. They would pause after I answered. Once, after many months, they would reproduce a pattern I had sent, but widened: each beat followed by a smaller beat, as if another structure had heard mine through its own branching tissues and returned both the message and the shape of its hearing.

I would become known for waiting.

People would bring me unknown seeds, anomalous shells, cords found drifting upright against the current. Deep hearers would visit and argue that I was mistaking distance for intention. Line drummers would say that intention was irrelevant if a pattern could carry useful warning. Council members would worry, correctly, that I had made a doorway no one had authorized.

During testimony I would take the central cord near the roots. During proposals I would rise level with the circle. I would never ascend to warning, even when warning was what I meant. To hold the cord above everyone with both hands would require a certainty I no longer possessed.

Some would call me half-loosened. Children would call me the Last Knuckle, because I was where the city’s hand ended. Lovers and mourners would come quietly to my broken house to feel how faint the city became near the drop. I would let them touch the relay, but never alone.

I would not be solitary. Solitude here would be an illusion maintained by other people’s labor. Food bundles would arrive along the inner line. A seep keeper would trade heat cultures for fibers collected beyond the cairns. My old surgical household would still claim me at festivals, though I would hover at the edge of their revolving cluster, one foot hooked loosely through the final belt.

There might be a child in my care—not born from me, and not entirely entrusted to me either. A clear-skinned, overbuoyant child whose parent had followed a distant song beyond the ropes and not returned. They would sleep tethered to the chamber’s central stalk and resent the bright bladders tied beneath their arms. They would learn the city’s rhythms flawlessly but prefer the irregular seed-clicks at the open wall.

I would teach them caution and discover that caution cannot be inherited, only practiced.

Eventually the answer from beyond would become urgent.

A pattern would arrive through the wilderness line hard enough to make the relay blanch. It would not resemble speech. It would resemble structural distress: strain, release, strain, release, followed by a long vibration like thousands of cords dragged across stone.

The city’s specialists would gather. Hands would cover every accessible surface of my little relay. Deep hearers would listen through jaws and collarbones. Current readers would release weighted threads into the open water. Animals would already be leaving the outer terraces, not in panic but with the grave unanimity of creatures obeying knowledge older than cultivation.

Some would argue for cutting my line immediately.

Instead I would feel the old error inside the decision: the desire to make one clean intervention and trust its clean result.

So we would loosen rather than sever. Open corridors. Untie hanging rooms. Shift herds inward but leave their escape paths clear. Cut ceremonial walls. Release roof canopies that might drag whole districts loose. Quiet the city and listen for what the mountain added to the distant signal.

Perhaps what approached would be a sediment surge from the collapse of another peak. Perhaps a migrating forest vast enough to comb the saddle. Perhaps a pressure wave from some abyssal convulsion no one had words for. The answerer beyond the city might be warning us—or merely crying out as the same event reached it first.

Either way, the line I was forbidden to grow would give the saddle time.

Afterward, if we survived, they would want to honor me. The natural honor would be authority over a new outer signaling order. I would refuse permanence but accept a season of teaching. The wilderness relay would become public, though never safe and never continuously joined. Others would learn to inhabit the gap between lines.

My final work would not be a building. It would be a set of cultivated intervals: relays placed across distant summits, each capable of passing vibration without binding one settlement’s living structure directly to another. A loose network, full of delay and distortion, where no city could command and no warning traveled unchanged.

I would never know who first answered.

Late in life, my skin gone opaque and my scars bright with mineral, I would spend long periods suspended in the open side of the house. The child I raised would have become a traveler or a deep hearer, perhaps both. Younger keepers would complain that my relay plants were old, asymmetrical, and difficult to tune. They would be right.

When I died, they would find one hand closed around the inner line and the other around the outer, though the two lines remained unjoined.

They would carry me beyond the cairns.

Not far. Just beyond the last place from which the city’s drums could be clearly distinguished from the mountain. They would wrap me in seed cloth threaded with fragments of the relay vine, secure it loosely to a wandering stand, and return without waiting to see whether the current drew me upward or down.

Years later, someone at the edge might feel an answer arrive after a citywide pulse: faint, delayed, altered by roots, distance, feeding animals, and stone.

They would not say it was me.

They would not say it wasn’t.

They would open both hands against the line and listen.
