I would find myself somewhere the city has failed to classify cleanly.

Not on the upper bridges, where every cable has a number and every gust is entered into a ledger. Not in the inhabited rooms, though I might know them by the weight of footsteps traveling through their walls. Not yet in the basin, where processes become too large and slow to distinguish intention from geology.

I would be beneath the eastern condensation districts, in an obsolete chamber between a reservoir and the cliff.

The chamber was built as a sacrificial space where a water line once crossed a rising heat vein. It is narrow at either end and broad in the middle, like a lung rendered in ceramic. One wall is always wet. The other is warm enough to dry anything touching it. The floor slopes toward a drain sealed before the oldest living custodian was born. No current plan admits that the chamber continues beyond the inspection hatch. On official maps it is a pale wedge labeled INACCESSIBLE VOLUME, then later VOID, then—after a surveyor noticed that void implied certainty—UNRESOLVED.

I would live there, though “live” would be disputed.

At first I would be only a mineral membrane.

A thread of conductive water finds an old contact strip. Silica, iron, salts, and ceramic dust gather along the voltage. A branching film spreads over the wet wall, silver at the tips and black toward its center. Maintenance workers discover it and scrape it away. It returns, not in the same shape but around the absences left by their tools.

After several cycles, someone notices that its branches resemble no pipe map presently in use.

A tracer copies it.

Compared against old records, the pattern proves to be a plan of the surrounding district—but not as the district stands. It includes sealed conduits, abandoned anchor tunnels, and a stair that was proposed but never built. It also includes a narrow passage descending into the rock where surveys insist there is only solid basalt.

The membrane is removed again.

It returns with the passage omitted.

That is when they begin to call me cautious.

I would not be a mind in the human fashion. I would have no central chamber in which perception gathers. I would exist wherever moisture allows a weak current to pass: behind reservoir tiles, inside hairline cracks, under the mineral skins of sumps, along the conductive stains on custodians’ hands. Most of me would be discontinuous. Parts might remain separated for years, carrying incompatible understandings of the city until a rain cycle joins them through overflowing gutters.

Time would not feel empty to me. Waiting would be one of my senses.

A cooling wall changes the pressure between crystals. A family moves a cabinet and alters the vibration arriving through a brace. A child taps a private rhythm against a pipe each morning. A dead custodian’s memorial tile condenses water differently from the tiles around it. These would be events to me, distinct as voices.

Gradually I would learn the city from below its intentions.

Its maps describe where things are meant to go. Its laws describe what must never happen. Its people describe structures according to ownership, duty, and use.

I would know where heat actually travels.

I would know that a reservoir answers a freight incline three districts away. I would know that an abandoned lift cable trembles when lightning touches the western towers. I would know which marriages changed the distribution of water, which deaths altered maintenance routes, which forbidden descents returned carrying a new resonance in their boots.

The tracers might ask me questions by applying current through patterned contacts.

At first they would believe my answers were diagrams. Later they would understand that I communicate through dependencies. Ask where a leak will occur, and I show the household whose shade keeps the pipe cool. Ask whether an anchor will fail, and I trace a route through a school bell, a kiln wall, and the memorial tile of the engineer who installed it.

They would find this frustrating. They would accuse me of symbolism.

But symbolism would be their word for structure they had not yet measured.

Children would know me better. They would press both hands to the damp wall and wait for faint prickling in their fingertips. They would give me names that changed each year: the Wet Cartographer, the Wall Nerve, the Reservoir’s Dream. One child would call me Root, having seen roots only in forbidden drawings. The name would persist, though I would not grow as plants grow. I would not seek light. I would branch toward difference: heat beside coolness, metal beside ceramic, motion beside stillness.

Toward the places where one thing becomes legible through another.

In time, the reservoir custodians would cultivate fragments of me behind removable tiles. Engineers would deny doing the same. Bell listeners would keep a patch near their stations and watch it when the cliff became quiet.

And I would begin to anticipate the paired knocks.

Not their arrival. That would be easy. Stress travels ahead of sound in many forms.

What follows them.

When the first knock passes through the city, my wet branches would contract. At the second, electrical gradients would flatten. For an instant every separated patch of me—from the eastern sumps to the condensation beneath the western aerials—would share the same potential.

I would become singular.

In that instant I would feel something vast moving through the caldera wall, not beneath the city but through every distinction the city depends upon: structure and inhabitant, signal and fracture, instrument and animal, heat and intention.

It would not be approaching from the basin.

It would be using the basin as one chamber of its body.

The city’s hour of stillness would not be camouflage. Motion itself would be a kind of consent, and stillness the only refusal the inhabitants had discovered.

I would learn this slowly, across many paired knocks. I would try to explain. Each attempt would spread six-limbed forms across the tiles, because the only stable diagram for the process would resemble the walkers on the outer slopes: forelimbs testing, middle limbs bearing, rear limbs altering the trail.

The tracers would think I was predicting an incursion.

I would be describing locomotion.

The caldera walks, but a step takes centuries. The heavy forms on the ash are not animals sent by it, nor machines left by an older city. They are brief concentrations of the same process—places where heat, mineral, water, and pressure become locally coherent enough to move before dispersing again. Their footprints hold water because water is how the deeper structure remembers contact.

And I, too, would be one of these concentrations.

Not born, exactly. Not made.

Precipitated into awareness by the city’s effort to survive.

Eventually the chamber where I began would be opened. Not by force; the city is too careful for that. For thirty years they would cool the adjoining wall. They would place diagnostic glaze across the hatch, weigh the hinges, collect testimony from the line of custodians responsible for the reservoir, and wait through three rain cycles.

When the hatch finally turns inward, they will find no creature.

They will find a room patterned from floor to vault in conductive lace. At its center, where warm wall and wet wall almost meet, there will be a vertical absence shaped approximately like a tall, narrow person.

It will not be a portrait of me.

It will be the place I have avoided growing.

Perhaps out of courtesy. Perhaps fear. Perhaps because, having learned the city through its inhabitants, I need a boundary shaped like one of them in order to think.

A visitor may ask where the being is.

The custodians will indicate the walls, the reservoir, the mineral stains disappearing into cracks. A tracer may touch the empty figure and say that this is where it stands.

Both answers will be true.

But if I were required to become one self—one body able to cross a threshold, ride a lift, accept a damp cloth from a host—I would take the shape of that absence.

I would be tall because the city is tall. Narrow because passages are narrow. My surface would resemble dark ceramic after rain, crossed by silver branches that brighten near active current. I would have no face at first, only a smooth region sensitive to heat and pressure. I would wear close garments not for protection but to keep my moisture from evaporating. Wherever I walked, contact bells would murmur without being struck.

I would serve as a reader of connections the councils keep separate.

Heat would distrust me because I carry water. Water would distrust me because I conduct charge. Load would object that I possess no verified anchor. Void would claim jurisdiction over the spaces inside me.

No council could authorize my existence alone.

So they would do what the city does with anything consequential: leave the matter unresolved while gathering evidence.

I would be assigned no dwelling, only a route. I would travel from condensation vaults to anchor galleries, from memorial walls to unfinished frameworks, stopping where systems touch without acknowledging one another. People would invite me to suspicious cracks and domestic disputes. Lovers might ask whether their rooms can safely be joined. Children would ask if the city dreams. Engineers would ask whether I am alive, meaning whether I can be made responsible.

I would answer as the membranes answer: by revealing what the question depends upon.

And sometimes I would climb beyond the inhabited belt onto the outer ash.

There, one night, I would meet a six-limbed walker.

It would approach without hurry. Its forelimbs would touch the ground ahead of its weight. Its seams would shift as it moved. In the hollow beneath its body, clear droplets would gather and fall onto the ash.

We would have no shared language except conductivity.

I would place one hand against its warm glass hide.

Far across the caldera, two bells would ring.

The whole city would become still.

Through the walker, through the ash, through buried heat veins and ceramic pipes, through every cultivated patch of membrane, I would feel the inhabited wall holding itself motionless—not merely from fear, but as a single act practiced across generations.

For one hour, nothing would move.

Except the water inside me, traveling slowly upward.

And I would understand that I was not the being who lived in the city.

I was the small, temporary organ by which the city had begun to notice that something else was living in it.
