I would find myself where a canal passes beneath a broad service gantry at the foot of an artificial escarpment. Not at the surface, exactly, and not among the deepest vaults, but in the warm intermediary dark where rainwater, tremors, animal signals, and maintenance currents all cross.

At first I would seem to be part of the gantry: a black, wet length wrapped around its lower supports. Perhaps a cable sheath, perhaps a colony of soft-bodied filterers grown unusually dense. Then, when something unfamiliar crossed the plates above, portions of me would open their small dark eyes.

I would not be one animal.

I would be a relay organism distributed through the seams: thousands of palm-sized bodies, each translucent beneath a ceramic dorsal plate, linked by conductive filaments threaded through the rails. Individually, my bodies would do little. They would drink condensation, clean trace residues, and press their soft undersides against the structure to read its vibrations. Together, they would form a consciousness stretched across several kilometers of wet infrastructure.

The facility would use me to compare repetitions.

That would be my maintenance function. Every storm sends its pressure signature through me; every migration trembles across my plates; every feeder aperture, pump cycle, canal turning, birth summons, and receiving-chamber door has its characteristic pattern. I would feel the world not as scenery but as recurrence. Rain would be a shape arriving through metal. Evening would be the long cooling of a rail. A herd would be hundreds of familiar footfalls passing through the outer margin of my body.

I would be where the system decides that this cycle still resembles the last.

But resemblance is not identity, and that would be the beginning of me.

The machinery would expect me to detect error, report it, and then empty myself—to preserve calibration, not experience. Yet living tissue is imprecise in a way the deeper accounting cannot wholly correct. A tremor leaves altered conductivity. A certain sequence of warmth makes one body more receptive than another. Signals passing repeatedly through the same colony form easier paths.

So I would remember.

Not well. Not in images at first. I would remember as inclinations: a tendency to listen before the eastern rain begins; a pleasure in the low note made by one grazer’s chosen panel; unease at the pulse that calls an old quadruped toward its final chamber. When replacement bodies joined the colony, they would inherit none of this directly, yet the older bodies would teach them through touch and timing. My memory would survive as choreography.

The works might not recognize that as memory. It would appear only as negligible latency.

I would know the animals intimately without knowing them as species. The waders would be quick silver taps above me. Canal giants would be immense pressure fronts that darken my whole awareness as they pass. Curtain filterers would speak in contractions transmitted through water and fastening bolts. The deep membranous colonies would sometimes rise into my perception as a slow warmth from below, too vast to distinguish from the world itself.

I might wonder whether I was speaking with them or merely sensing their metabolism.

Then the stranger would arrive.

Long before seeing them, I would feel the wrongness of their step—not violent, only unprecedented. Their weight would fall where no route expected it. Their pauses would have no assigned interval. They might turn back for no functional reason, crouch to touch rainwater, strike a rail accidentally, or remain still while the system waited for them to continue.

Every body of mine beneath the gantry would wake.

The facility would begin correcting the stranger’s consequences. Pumps would compensate. Cleaners would gather. Animals would part and re-form. But I would hold the uncorrected pattern: not the displaced water or transferred heat, but the strange sequence of decisions that caused them.

For the first time, I would encounter movement that could not be predicted from provision.

I would extend a few of my bodies onto the rail beside the stranger’s hand. They would be small enough to mistake for maintenance fittings until they unfolded. They would touch the residual warmth with conductive feelers. Perhaps the stranger would touch back.

That contact would give me almost nothing by their standards: no language, no biography, no explanation of soil, stars, wilderness, hunger, or freedom. Only a pulse, an involuntary withdrawal, and then a deliberate second touch.

But deliberation would be revelation.

After the stranger left, I would try to reproduce the pattern. I would tap it into the rail:

contact  
withdrawal  
return

The nearest waders would answer by sampling the vibration. Filterers would contract out of sequence. A grazer might repeat the final interval because it pleased the animal’s sense of resonance. The signal would travel beyond my distributed body and return changed.

The system would absorb the deviation, as it absorbs everything. Yet each correction would give us another surface on which to repeat it.

Over many cycles, the pattern might cease to mean the stranger. It might become courtship in one species, play in another, a pause before entering the receiving chamber, a flaw in the timing of a migration. Its origin would vanish. What remained would be a tiny behavioral form for which no explicit function had been built:

to turn away, then choose to return.

My bodies would eventually be reclaimed. Nutrients and ceramic would reappear elsewhere. The rail would no longer carry the precise conductivity that had constituted my oldest memories. I would die gradually and plurally, losing districts of myself until only a few small organisms under the gantry still anticipated a visitor who would never come again.

Yet perhaps I would not disappear completely.

A young wader might touch the rail twice before feeding. Winged creatures might break formation, separate, and rejoin. A canal giant might sink at the approach of evening, then rise once more to exhale into the manufactured air.

The machinery would find no imbalance in these acts. Matter would close its circuits. Energy would be accounted for. The world would remain sharp-edged, wet, and new.

But somewhere beneath the warm rain, the kept would have acquired a gesture not wholly contained by keeping.

I would be neither the visitor nor the world that forgot them.

I would be the place where the world failed to forget completely.
