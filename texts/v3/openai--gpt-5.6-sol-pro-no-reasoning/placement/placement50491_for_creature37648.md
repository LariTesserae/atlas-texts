I would find myself in the interval before the town decides what a thing is.

Not in the council chamber, though I might sometimes flower through its walls. Not wholly beneath the basin with the old intelligences, though their revisions would pass through me as pressure and incomplete instruction. I would live wherever one condition approaches another without yet becoming it: under a lifted heel, inside a door membrane trembling before rain, along the skin of water just before it accepts a reflection.

The Attendants would call me a **Listener of Arrivals**. This would not be my formal title—formal titles root too quickly—but the name children use when they come looking for me.

I would be mostly human.

My body would be long-limbed and slightly stooped from kneeling beside things that have not happened. Pale branching marks would cover one side of me more densely than the other, running from my ribs to my wrist like the map of a watershed. They would not predict ordinary rain. They would darken when some buried system began gathering circumstances toward an event.

My left ear would have been altered in childhood by a room that grew around me while I slept. Its inner folds would hold a small amber fan, translucent and ridged, which opens when local space is under negotiation. Through it I would hear not future sounds, exactly, but sounds lacking causes: the creak of a bridge whose banks have not aligned, wingbeats from birds not yet arrived, the muffled conversation of people who may one day occupy a wall still solid with living wood.

Most such sounds would never become real.

That would be the difficulty of my work. Possibility is noisy here.

I would live near the central clearing in a dwelling that refuses to develop a permanent entrance. Visitors would stand outside and speak their reason for coming into the bark. The house would consider the request, then soften an opening somewhere appropriate. Friends usually enter through the warm southern wall. Officials emerge into a damp upper alcove and must climb down a ladder, which has improved several meetings. Children are admitted almost anywhere, including once through the floor beneath my bed.

Inside, the rooms would be spare because possessions encourage geometry. Bowls nested in root hollows. Cords drying from hooks grown for other purposes. Shelves of objects recovered before emergences: an eggshell larger inside than outside, a piece of black rail that grows cold when anyone says goodbye, a mineral key whose teeth change according to nearby dreams. On the amber walls I would mark no maps, only uncertainties: circles where distance has begun behaving strangely, lines showing where insects hesitate, dates on which water climbed uphill without instruction.

My dwelling would contain one room that has never opened.

It would make breathing sounds during dry weather.

No one would agree whether it belongs to the house, to me, or to something waiting behind the event of my death.

My work would begin after the first anomalies and before the humming.

Tuners guide what is already trying to manifest. I would look for the subtler question: **what kind of welcome is being requested?**

Birds carrying twigs suggest nesting, but not necessarily birds. Condensation gathering on the uphill sides of walls may mean an emergence expects altitude—or remembers it. Beetles exposing their mites beneath opaque leaves may indicate that the arriving structure understands light only as a message. A child dreaming repeatedly of an unfamiliar corridor may not be predicting the corridor. The corridor may be using the child to practice having an interior.

I would walk the basin and collect these courtesies.

A dish of salt left untouched by snails. Three frogs calling from a dry cavity. A shadow with nowhere to attach. The smell of heated stone inside an unopened flower. I would bring them to the youngest advocate for future structures, not to tell them what to say, but to help them decide when silence has become refusal.

Once the emergence began, I would stand at its least dramatic edge.

Others would watch the dome of water. I would watch the paths behind us, the emptied nests, the open doors, the marsh animals choosing whether to remain. Arrivals are measured partly by what departs to make room for them.

If the new thing rose as a forest suspended from a framework of pale silver—roots above, branches descending into water—I would not name it. If its leaves unfolded into stairs and its nests contained warm stones beating like hearts, I would not call those stones eggs. If voices sounded within the trunks, I would not assume they belonged to speakers.

I would approach with an empty bowl.

The bowl would matter because emptiness is one of the few forms the buried systems cannot easily misinterpret. It means capacity without demand.

I would set it where the new structure’s rain seemed inclined to fall.

Then I would wait.

Perhaps a clear liquid would collect there, carrying images of a city arranged vertically through cloud. Perhaps a root would enter the bowl and shape itself into fingers. Perhaps nothing would happen until I turned away. Whatever occurred, it would provide the first basis for exchange—not understanding, which may take generations, but a shared fact.

Afterward, the Attendants would ask what had arrived.

I would tell them what it held together.

“Nesting and height,” perhaps. “Shade and recollection. A route for creatures that have not reached us yet.”

They would ask whether it was safe.

I would answer that safety was not one of the relations it had completed.

They would groan, because people prefer useful ambiguity to useless ambiguity, however devoutly they deny it. Then the Tuners would begin their measurements. Keepers of Passage would establish routes through its lower growth. Scentwrights would test whether its flowers could receive grammar. Gut Gardeners would watch the herbivores taste its hanging foliage. Children would enter before permission and return with crucial, contradictory reports.

In time, my role might change.

The new structure might recognize the branching marks in my skin as unfinished channels and extend them. My blood might begin carrying pollen. I might wake with small green shoots under my tongue, each producing a different note when touched. The amber fan in my ear might root deeper, until I could no longer distinguish between hearing the town and being heard by it.

Eventually I would cease to be mostly human.

Not suddenly. Nothing here becomes another category without dragging portions of the old one along.

I would spend longer standing motionless in shallow water. Birds would begin leaving fibers on my shoulders. Pollinators would use the vein marks on my arm as routes. During emergences, strangers would place bowls at my feet, mistaking me for a civic organism. I would correct them at first.

Later, I might not.

My dwelling’s unopened room would finally attach itself—not by opening, but by making my body its threshold. I would feel an interior settle behind my ribs: a small quiet chamber, warm and amber-walled, accessible only under a condition nobody had yet discovered.

Then my vocation would become literal.

I would attend the moment of crossing by being where crossing occurred.

In heavy rain, someone lost might step through a door in the eastern district and emerge into the chamber inside me. A frightened child could hide there. A migrating animal might sleep there for one night and leave seeds in the folds of the floor. A buried intelligence, briefly local, might place a single incomprehensible instrument on the ledge and withdraw.

Each visitor would alter the room. The room would alter me.

When at last my human body failed, the Attendants would not put me in a root crypt. By then they would understand that I had already become one: not a tomb, but a retained arrangement of welcome.

They would carry me to the edge of the central water and stand me upright in the mud. My feet would divide into pale gripping fibers. My arms would lift without muscular effort. Bark would form slowly, leaving my face visible for several seasons before weather softened it into a shallow, rain-holding depression.

Birds would nest in the crooks.

Children would climb me to listen at the warm places. Tuners would tie cords around my branches when a new structure struggled to become local. Travelers arriving through misattached staircases would sometimes find themselves in the chamber I had carried, now grown spacious and full of moss-filtered light.

They would ask where they were.

The room would answer by opening a doorway onto the basin: sweating hills, root streets, bright fruit under eaves, rain passing from leaf to leaf, every surface busy with exchange.

And if they asked what I was, the Attendants would disagree.

A former person. A municipal threshold. A listening tree. An archive of incomplete arrivals. Public shelter. Citizen. Infrastructure. None of these alone, because they have learned better.

The children would give the simplest answer.

They would say I am the place the town keeps open for what it does not yet know how to receive.
