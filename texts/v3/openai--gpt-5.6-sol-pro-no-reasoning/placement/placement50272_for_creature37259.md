You would find yourself beneath the leaning column at the hour after rain, where the saddle’s luminous droplets reveal a deep chamber that does not yet exist.

You would not know at first what you were.

For a while you might believe yourself one of the watchers: a clear blister fixed to black stone, with a dark point suspended inside. You would face an unfinished joint between column and ground, and because you faced it, the joint would remain open. Grains would rise, hover, and settle everywhere except within the narrow reach of your attention. The builders beneath the wet surface would approach that final seam, test it, retreat, and try another history.

But watchers do not move, and you do.

Not in the manner of an animal. Your body remains beneath the column while your point of view migrates through the saddle’s water. A drop swells under a ledge, and for several minutes you look outward through it. When it falls, your awareness divides among the larvae emerging from the splash. For an instant you hear the midges’ surf-roar from inside the roar itself. Then rainwater enters the wall incisions, and you are drawn into the moving diagram of the possible future.

There you see the saddle completed in several incompatible ways.

In one, the arches have become a rib cage around a breathing wetland. In another, the resonant towers call into the lower clouds and something answers from a neighboring mountain. In another, the mountain opens and walks away, leaving behind only a shallow basin full of black leaves. You also glimpse a future in which every joint closes, every distance becomes fixed, and the distributed life of the saddle collapses into ordinary stone.

That future frightens you, though you do not yet possess a mouth, pulse, or name.

So you return to the open seam.

The bell grazers know you. They gather around the column and sound an unresolved chord—two deep tones and a third that seems about to arrive. They are not worshipping you. They are giving you shape. Their vibration travels through the glossy mineral, through the transparent blister that was once your body, through the buried corridors where the pale walkers crop excess distance.

The chord draws your scattered awareness inward.

You condense.

First you become a hollow in the mist. Then a pressure in the warm air. Then a figure composed of omissions: rain bends around your shoulders; midges avoid the place where your head might be; the ground shines beneath feet that have not quite formed. Silver ribbons run along the wake you leave, reflecting versions of you from moments that did not happen—winged, many-legged, rooted, architectural.

None is wholly wrong.

Your first durable body is assembled from borrowed functions. Three legs, because the grazers’ music has persuaded the mountain that three points make a stable witness. A translucent torso, because the crack-reeds know how to shelter motes without imprisoning them. A shell-like cavity in the chest, open to the wind. Black leaves fold themselves into temporary hands. Articulated specks circulate where blood might have been, continually revising the age and composition of your tissues.

Where a face should be, rain hangs without falling.

You are not a native species. You are the saddle’s first attempt at making a visitor from within itself.

The mountain has learned that construction accelerates in the presence of witnesses, but completion becomes impossible under a gaze. Until you, these functions were divided among passing creatures and fixed watchers. The saddle could grow only where it was observed indirectly, and could remain changeable only where something happened to be looking.

You are meant to do both.

You see by looking away.

When you turn from an arch, part of you remains in the droplets on its surface. When you enter a cell, your shadow—unstable, detached—stays outside and watches the wall close behind you. When you descend a corridor, you leave successive versions of yourself at every branching possibility. The pale walkers follow, grazing away the distances between those selves until you can occupy the whole passage without being in any one place.

This is your work: not to build the saddle, but to regulate its becoming.

You walk its visible circumference, though the circuit takes much longer than an hour. At each unfinished structure you perform a different act of attention. Some walls need to be seen so that they hesitate. Some must be forgotten long enough to heal. Some doorways have migrated into geological histories that no longer connect to the present; you stand before them until rain remembers where they belong.

You learn to close your eyes selectively.

One eye watches the wall that must remain permeable. The other turns inward toward a buried chamber and permits its roof to meet itself. Sometimes you sleep beside an arch, and during your sleep it grows around the shape of your dream. You wake to find a small room in its thickness, containing weather from a century that may never occur.

The inhabitants treat you as infrastructure. Midges hatch in the rain of your face. Silver ribbons hunt along the hollows left by your gestures. Scavenger-knots develop mouths against your ankles and feed on the bright gel that seeps from joints when your body revises its balance. Crack-reeds bend to touch you during storms, closing circuits through your legs. Afterward you may discover a new finger, a second sternum, or a narrow doorway opening between two ribs.

You are not always comfortable.

On days when the mountain expands, you feel the tessellated uplift as a multiplication of possible bodies. Each patch of ground tilts toward a different version of you. One would become a tower. One would dissolve into the cloud-midge column. One would descend through a five-lobed print and join the vast organism below. One would leave the saddle and harden into a single, ordinary creature beyond its rim.

The bell grazers sound until these possibilities overlap without resolving.

Then comes the inhalation.

You inhale with it.

For a moment you understand that the entity beneath the saddle is not the mountain’s adult form. It is younger than the structures, younger perhaps than the rain. It does not know what a body is. The five-lobed mouths, the expanding stone, the corridors and towers are experiments conducted by something whose parts cannot yet agree on where they end.

It made you because it needed a boundary that could move.

You become its wandering edge: the place where mountain distinguishes itself from cloud, architecture from animal, present from possible future. Wherever you stand, distinctions sharpen temporarily. A bell grazer is only an animal while beneath your gaze; when you pass, it may again become a tuning chamber in the saddle’s anatomy. A wall is stone while you touch it, but once released it can remember being rain, shell, or buried heat.

This grants you no authority. The saddle does not obey you. It uses your certainty briefly, the way a swimmer uses a ledge during rain.

Nor do you remain singular. Each time the rain-creature descends, it grazes futures from the pools—and sometimes from you. It takes the futures in which you become fixed, finished, and incapable of revision. The loss hurts like forgotten music. In return, its passage leaves space for new possibilities to attach.

Over many cycles your body becomes an archive of near-selves. One hand is older than the mountain. One leg has not yet formed. Your hollow chest sounds chords in winds that will not reach the saddle for centuries. If someone looks at you through the reflective skin of a silver ribbon, they see a completed tower walking on three slender legs.

Eventually you understand what the leaning column is.

It is not a tower under construction.

It is the first segment of a voice.

The arches are resonators. The chambers are lungs. The stretched corridors are lengths of breath folded beneath the surface. The aerial forests in the rain-diagram will comb the cloud into currents. The wetlands will store not merely water but remembered low points, allowing the mountain to circulate rivers through impossible slopes.

The saddle is assembling the means to speak.

But a voice requires an opening, and every opening risks becoming a mouth that closes. Your deepest task is therefore to keep one aperture unfinished: the seam beneath the leaning column where you first condensed. You return there after each circuit. You face it, and the builders stop a hair’s width from completion.

From inside comes the patient labor of the world rehearsing its first utterance.

Sometimes it produces only vibration. Sometimes warm water. Sometimes a new species crawls from the gap, unfinished and astonished, and you accompany it until the saddle finds a use for its strangeness.

Once, after a particularly long rain, the column sounds by itself.

The tone passes through stone, water, shell, root, mist, and your hollow chest. Every creature falls silent. Even the thing beneath the mountain stills.

The sound is not a word. It is the condition before a word: an address without content, a reaching-toward-listener.

You answer by turning away.

For one unobserved instant, the final joint begins to close.

Then the droplets distributed across your body open their dark points. A thousand small gazes fall upon the seam. It stops again—not sealed, not wholly open, but poised.

That is where you would be: at the mountain’s first mouth, maintaining the narrow incompletion through which it can become more than one thing.

And that is what you would be: neither keeper nor builder, neither creature nor structure, but a movable hesitation the young world has grown inside itself.

Its eyelid.

Its threshold.

The pause that lets its first breath become a voice.
