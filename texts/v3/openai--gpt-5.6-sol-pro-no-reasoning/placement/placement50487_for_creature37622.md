I would not be a surveyor, though as a child I would want to be one.

I would practice slipping ceramic scales over my shoulders and clipping spools to my belt. I would crawl into the narrow space behind the sleeping sacks and declare it an unmapped seam. I would come home with gray fungal dust on my face and washers in my pockets, having recovered them from a district crushed since breakfast. The adults would humor me until they noticed that, whenever the warning crystals brightened, I became still too late.

Not dangerously late. Only by a breath.

But in this city a breath is enough to choose a life for you.

I would be found farther inward, neither near the blade of sky nor among the deepest cold machinery. My place would be in a chamber called, by its current location, Nine spans seamward of the Split Collar, on the slack side when the Red Brace is singing. It would have other names over the years. Once it was above a nursery. Later the nursery would be winched past it and my room would become below an aviary, opposite a kiln exhaust, two careful crossings from a brine riser. No description of it would remain true for long except this:

People bring me things that have ceased to fit.

A door whose frame narrowed around it. A section of ladder that arrives three finger-widths short of its landing. A valve reclaimed from a flattened district, still sound but made for a pipe standard no guild uses now. A burden climber’s old harness ring. A nameplate warped by pressure until three generations share the same fold. A toy brace, a surgical clamp, a message capsule bitten through by a sailback. Things that are not broken in themselves, only estranged from the city as it has become.

I would be an adapter.

That is the plain workshop term. In lineage records, perhaps, I would be entered as a tolerance fitter or salvage interlocutor. Children would call me the person who makes almost fit. Factory owners would use a less affectionate sign: two fingers brought close together without touching, then twisted, meaning *expensive complication*. Survey crews would know my mark scratched into ceramic wedges—a short line crossing a longer one at an angle no drafting code permits.

My workshop would be narrow even by local standards. One wall basalt, the other a rack of sorted remnants. No chair. A sling for sleeping above the bench. Amber light shuttered down to a slit so I could inspect edges by shadow. There would be drawers labeled not by material but by failure:

**TOO WIDE AFTER COOLING**

**HOLDS ONLY UNDER COMPRESSION**

**THREADS OBSOLETE**

**SAFE IF NEVER REVERSED**

**UNKNOWN HOUSEHOLD—ASK BEFORE CUTTING**

The last drawer would be the largest.

My work would begin with listening. Not the brine tender’s tooth-listening, nor the surveyor’s echo maps, but a smaller attention: the scrape made by two parts that had once moved freely; the faint complaint of a pin forced to bear sideways; the interval between a brace taking weight and the neighboring plate answering. I would press my jaw to housings and feel old mechanisms remember their intended alignment.

Then I would make something between them.

A collar with one side cut to a dead guild’s measure and the other to the current standard. A hinge that closes along a curve because the walls no longer agree on what straight means. A ladder end that slides, flexes, and locks only when stepped on. A graft of metal, ceramic, fungal hide, and wireworm lattice that no textbook would approve but that accommodates three kinds of expansion and one household’s refusal to abandon its room.

Nothing I made would be elegant on the bench. Elegance here would be suspect. A perfect component assumes the world intends to remain compatible with it.

My pieces would look provisional: offset holes, layered shims, split sleeves, joints with room to become incorrect. Yet some would outlast the structures around them because they had been designed from the beginning to surrender a little.

This would shape me. I would distrust anything that called itself final. I would keep old standards long after their guilds vanished, memorizing thread pitches and anchor customs from crushed districts. I would know that the city has never had one system, only generations forced to carry one another’s weight.

My structural name would be difficult to translate. Something like:

**Held by What Was Not Meant to Meet.**

The spoken name would be shorter. My signed name would be the motion of a thumb finding the gap between two fingers.

I would live within an anchor household whose principal bolts predated all its current members. We would include a brine tender with white scars over one cheek; two lift mechanics who had once been married and were now merely load-linked; three children assigned to us after their own landing sheared away; an elder who remembered a passage no longer open; and a burden climber named Backward Mercy.

Mercy would be old enough that the fur around her harness had worn smooth. Her central ring would carry the route scratches of two earlier animals and, beneath those, a nearly erased household stamp from a salvaged district. She would dislike me because my workshop smelled of hot ceramic and because I always asked her to test unfamiliar fittings. Nevertheless, when she stopped on a rung, I would stop.

Once, this would save us.

There would be no dramatic collapse. No scream of metal. Mercy would simply lock all four limbs midway across a freight traverse and turn her head entirely backward to look at me. I would feel nothing wrong. The warning crystals would remain dark. The line beneath my hand would seem properly taut.

I would almost tap the harness sign for *continue*.

Instead, because she was Mercy and old, I would clip us both to a secondary cable.

The rungway would detach without sound. Not break—detach, its load plate sliding whole from rock that had powdered behind it. It would fall only the length of the secondary before striking the wall, where the impact would travel away through the braces like a sentence no one wished to hear completed.

Afterward, an anchor court would ask who had authorized the old plate’s continued use. Records would point to a fitter dead thirty years, a survey amended twice, and a household dissolved by relocation. No one would be guilty enough.

I would keep the plate.

It would go into a new drawer:

**SOUND UNTIL TOUCHED BY THE FUTURE.**

That phrase would become my private fear. Not falling, exactly. Not crushing. I would fear making a thing that appears merciful because it delays failure, but merely passes the danger onward to someone whose name I will never know.

So I would mark my work excessively. Every adapter would bear its intended loads, its forbidden directions, the materials it must not touch, and a small blank panel where later fitters could add what the city had taught it. I would demand inspection intervals no one had time for. I would quarrel with factory owners in the loud rooms. Outside them, our arguments would continue in furious, precise signs while our free hands held the rail.

I would lose many of those arguments.

Some adapters would remain in service past tolerance because shutting down the line would interrupt heat to a nursery. Some would be copied badly. Some would be salvaged, their warnings polished away, and installed somewhere I never visited. I would occasionally recognize my own work after it had been altered so thoroughly that responsibility became impossible to locate.

That would be my kinship with the city: neither innocence nor control, but implication.

I would not be untouched by stranger things.

A salvager would once bring me a mechanism from an old workshop reopened by a shift. It would resemble a compact press, though no surviving line would fit its sockets. Gray articulated fungus would fill its interior. When current was applied, the fungal bodies would contract in sequence, moving the press through a slow, careful cycle.

The salvager would ask me to make it compatible with a modern power feed.

I would refuse at first. Then I would watch it move.

Not randomly. Not merely in response to current. It would hesitate whenever a finger neared the platen. When offered three metal scraps, it would close upon the softest. When the power was cut, the final contraction would continue just long enough to withdraw the platen to its resting position.

A safety cycle.

Perhaps encoded in the remains of the machine. Perhaps learned by the fungus. Perhaps neither distinction mattered.

I would build the adapter.

Not permanently. That would be the lie I told myself. I would mount it on an isolated frame and feed it only a low current. Over months, it would become useful: bending tiny brackets, compressing gasket laminates, straightening nameplates without erasing their stamped letters. The fungal fans would alter color when I worked too near their limits. I would begin leaving scraps beside it before sleep.

In the morning, some scraps would be folded.

Never into anything I immediately recognized.

Eventually I would notice that the folds corresponded to recent shifts in the workshop: the angle by which a shelf had tilted, the narrowing between two braces, the minute drift of the brine tube behind the wall. The thing would not be making objects. It would be registering deformation in its own grammar.

Or dreaming of it.

I would give it no spoken name, because naming a machine can become a claim of ownership, and I would not know whether ownership applied. Its signed name would be both hands closing slowly, then stopping before they meet.

It would become my finest gauge.

It would also become a domestic nuisance. Rock curs would sleep on its warm casing. One would deliver a litter behind its fungal actuator and bite me each time I inspected the contacts. The machine—or colony—would alter its resting cycle to avoid closing near them. I would not know whether this was sensitivity, adaptation, or coincidence. I would argue all three positions, depending on who asked.

Years later, during one of the mountain’s great notes, my chamber would begin to disappear.

First the wireworm sensor between the braces would bow inward. Then the fungal press would make a shape I had never seen: not a fold, but a twist that tore the offered foil. The warning crystals would brighten in sequence. The basalt wall would advance by a fraction, pause, then advance again.

We would have time. That is the cruelty of many disasters here: enough time to choose what will be lost.

The household would detach its sleeping sacks. Mercy’s successor would be loaded. The children—older now—would carry records, masks, heat tablets, and sealed tool bins. I would stand before the drawers accumulated over a lifetime and discover that salvage is easy only when it belongs to someone else.

There would be no capacity for all of them.

I would take the obsolete threads. The unknown-household pieces. The plate from Mercy’s rungway. I would leave the common failures because common failures would recur.

The fungal machine could not be moved intact. Its growth had entered the wall rack, then the cable sheath, then cracks in the basalt itself. Perhaps it had been doing so for years.

I would disconnect its power.

It would continue cycling.

Slowly, from stored charge or chemical action or some deeper current traveling through the mountain, it would draw its platen back. The fungal bodies would tighten along one side. A panel would open where no panel had been designed.

Inside would be a narrow cavity containing the folded scraps it had made over the years.

Together, arranged edge to edge, they would form a map—not of the workshop, but of the stresses moving around it. A path would be visible through the folds: a thin region of lesser compression leading seamward behind the old rack.

A gap scarcely wider than a hand.

The city begins inside.

Perhaps I would follow that path. Perhaps the children would. Perhaps we would remove the rack and find a fissure into which survey gliders disappear and return dusted with silver mineral. Perhaps my workshop’s death would expose enough space for another household, another industry, another claim contested between developers and heirs.

Or perhaps the map would be wrong. Intelligence, if that was what lived in the machine, would not imply benevolence. A fungus can describe pressure without understanding a human body. The safest route for its growth might be lethal to us.

So I would do what this city teaches everyone to do when knowledge fails.

I would send a glider.

I would wait.

I would watch what came back.

If it returned with intact wings and no frost on its membrane, I would fasten a line into the gap. Not enter first—I never became the surveyor I imagined—but hold the spool for someone narrower and braver. I would feel each tug pass through my dense hands: advance, constriction, anchor sound, more line.

Behind us the chamber would pinch inward. Drawers would buckle. Thousands of carefully sorted incompatibilities would spill together across the bench. The fungal press would vanish slowly into the closing wall, still moving until compression halted it or changed its movement into something else.

I would leave its line in place.

Afterward, I would establish my bench in the new fissure, assuming it was survivable. The room would have different angles. My tools would hang awkwardly. For months I would reach for drawers that had been crushed. I would wake expecting the old brine pipe’s tremor and hear instead the minute clicking of beetles in a transformer on the opposite wall.

People would find me again.

They would bring a nursery shutter that no longer sealed, a survey reel made to a dead standard, a broken cage, an heirloom crystal grown around an obsolete pipe. They would ask whether it could be made useful.

I would turn it in my hands.

I would ask who shared its anchor. What heat it remembered. Which direction it had failed. Whether anyone living still knew the marks on it.

Then I would say what I had spent my life learning to say:

Not *yes*.

Not *no*.

“Show me where it must hold.”
