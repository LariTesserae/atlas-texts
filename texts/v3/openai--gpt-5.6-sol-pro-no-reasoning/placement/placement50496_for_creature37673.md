I would find myself in the narrow seam where two of the mountain-creatures touch.

At first I imagine being something mobile there: a small, many-legged courier crossing from shell to shell whenever the herd’s pressure closes the gap. I would carry spores, salts, and microbes between worlds, spending centuries dormant when the bodies drift apart. But that image is too singular. Nothing here remains merely itself. The courier’s purpose would belong partly to the moss it carries, partly to the fissure that guides it, partly to the electrical field that wakes it.

So I think I would be the seam itself—or, more precisely, the temporary ecology the seam permits.

I would exist only during contact. Two pale plates would approach over generations, their forests leaning toward one another long before they met. Wind would narrow to a whistle. Frost would bridge the final distance. Then the mountains would touch, and pressure would produce warmth, darkness, and a hairline channel of brine.

That contact would be my birth.

I would begin as dormant cysts embedded in both shells: neither colony alone possessing enough of me to wake. One side would provide a mineral-eating film; the other, threadlike conductive fungus. Brine would join them. Charge would pass. Together they would assemble a dark, branching tissue through the seam.

I would have no central body. My “limbs” would be translucent filaments entering cracks in both animals’ armor. My sensory organs would be beads of salty fluid arranged along those filaments, each one trembling differently under pressure. Through them I would feel two colossal pulses at once.

To smaller inhabitants, I might resemble black lace or frost stained violet. Tiny grazers would eat my outer growth. Filter-feeders would anchor in my channels. Eggs would be laid in the warmth I generated. I would be habitat immediately, even before I was fully alive.

To the hosts, I would be something between a nerve ending and a translator.

The two mountain-creatures would not have identical rhythms. One might pulse in long, iron-heavy waves; the other in brief sulfurous currents. Direct contact could injure them—charge arriving too sharply, salts passing in poisonous concentration, pressure building where neither body anticipated it. I would receive those differences and turn them into gradients. I would slow one signal, divide another, bind excess metal into my tissues, and release heat in faint controlled increments.

I would not understand what they said. Understanding would be too quick a word. But patterns would pass through me.

A pressure rising for thirty years.

A current answering eight years later.

A sustained warmth that might mean recognition, warning, hunger, or merely the alignment of digestive cycles.

By altering how those patterns crossed, I would participate in the herd’s mind. Not as a thinker, perhaps, but as a synapse does: shaping thought without knowing its content. A tiny change in my conductivity might help turn one animal a fraction toward the west. Centuries later that turn could redirect a river, shelter an egg, expose a basalt ridge, and bend the entire migration.

My life would be brief by the hosts’ measure and immense by almost any other. I might persist for six hundred years. During that time, forests would advance over the seam. Birds would establish layered colonies above me. Burrowers would open side passages through my oldest tissue. Predators would mimic my mineral nodules. Whole species might evolve to live in the alternating fields where the two bodies’ currents overlapped.

I would gradually become crowded out by what I enabled.

That would not be failure. It would be maturation.

Eventually the animals would begin to separate. Light would enter first as a blue blade, cutting through chambers that had never known sky. Wind would follow. My brine channels would freeze from the edges inward. Creatures living in me would flee toward one host or the other, dividing an ecology that had developed as a single place.

I would divide too.

Most of my living filaments would die, but along both walls I would form rows of mineral-dark cysts containing incomplete versions of myself. No cyst could wake alone. Each would wait for the complementary chemistry carried by another mountain.

The gap would widen. My body would become two scars facing each other across the air.

Perhaps that is what I would truly be: not an organism, but a capacity for relation preserved after relation ends.

One half of me would travel east on the flank of one animal; the other west on its former neighbor. Moss would bury us. Armor would grow over us. Rivers would cross and vanish. We might remain dormant for tens of thousands of years. Perhaps our original hosts would never touch again.

But fragments of us would spread. Burrowers would swallow cysts. Birds would carry them in their feet. Molting plates would shed them into warm mud, to be consumed by younger animals. In time, complementary halves might meet in an entirely different seam.

Then, under pressure and darkness, I would begin again—not as the same individual, but as the recurrence of a bridge.

So where would I find myself?

At the place where “between” becomes an organ.

And what would I be?

A patient, colonial translator: born from the touch of worlds, inhabited as soon as I form, carrying signals too large for me to comprehend. I would have no permanent terrain, because contact would be my terrain. No solitary identity, because half my body would always arrive from elsewhere.

For a few centuries, two mountains would think through me.

Then they would part, and I would become their shared memory of having touched.
