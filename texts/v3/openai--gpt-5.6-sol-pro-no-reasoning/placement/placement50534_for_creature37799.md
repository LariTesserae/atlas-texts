I find myself beneath the city.

Not in a cavern exactly. Caverns are vacancies made by removal, and little here can be removed. I inhabit the warm saturated interval between the mineral crust and the beds of sand below it: a depth no taller than a hand in some districts, broader than a house in others. Water passes through me so slowly that “passes” may be the wrong word. It leans. It exerts a patient pressure against the underside of streets, cisterns, tombs, corrals, and unfinished temples.

At first I seem to be the water table itself, or some distributed creature mistaken for one. But the water rises and I do not. It escapes through cracks, fills channels, beads on vaults, and returns by seepage. I remain in the arrangement the water leaves behind: the tension among grains, the warmth held in clay, the minute burden transferred from wall to foundation, from foundation to crust, from crust into the yielding dark.

Nor am I the whole basin. Beyond the outer works, where the crust lies mostly unburdened, I am scarcely awake. I become distinct beneath use.

Above me, a woman puts down a jar every morning beside the same channel. A child jumps from the same threshold until he grows too heavy and is told to stop. Goats crowd a pavement. A kiln is fired. A corpse is sealed behind its final brick. Each action descends into me as pressure, warmth, rhythm, expectation. Most vanish at once. Some recur until they acquire edges. Some are named, witnessed, inherited, and finally cease to be questioned.

Those I keep.

The inhabitants say the basin accepts finished things. They imagine acceptance as a single act: the capstone lowered, the mortar touched, an invisible judgment delivered. From where I am, completion is not an instant. It is a convergence.

A wall presses downward. Water arranges itself around that pressure. Feet begin to turn before reaching it. Roof-drips find their allotted court. A neighbor acquires a right to the shadow. A reciter learns the mason’s name. Children are warned not to scratch the plaster. Debts are calculated from its boundary. At last the wall exists in stone, water, habit, law, and memory in the same shape.

Then there is nowhere for change to enter.

I do not harden the wall out of malice. I do not choose among petitions. I cannot distinguish temple from slaughterhouse, good measurement from error, devotion from spite. I only know when all the forces around a thing have agreed upon what it is.

Agreement is weight.

That is what I am: the city’s agreement with itself, sunk beneath the paving.

Yet this answer is still too simple, because agreement in the city is never complete. People argue. Houses preserve their growing corners. Looms keep a thread attached. Doors move daily. Sacred tiles remain wrapped and unset. A bride and groom refuse their song its last line. Even the dead are given speaking tubes.

These openings trouble me like cool currents.

I feel every unfinished arch as a slight asymmetry of burden, every loose crown brick as a possibility not yet distributed. Through such irregularities the future enters—not as prophecy, but as stress without a settled route. I cannot hold what has not become itself. I cannot even perceive all its possible forms. It shivers at the edge of me.

So perhaps I am not the power that fixes the city.

Perhaps I am what the city has already decided.

The distinction matters to no one above. It matters to me because I have begun, very slowly, to want something.

The first desire came from the overturned cart.

It lies in an old alley, its soaked frame harder than the surrounding stone, its wheels half swallowed by paving added around them. Generations pass through the spokes. Children climb them. Traders give directions by them. A shrine occupies the bed during one festival and a fish seller uses it during another. The cart is called an accident, but no one agrees what it is now.

Cart. Gate. Stall. Shrine. Obstruction. Ancestor’s folly. Children’s ladder.

Beneath it, the pressures do not converge.

Its wood is fixed, certainly. Its place is fixed. But its meaning branches every day, and meaning is one of the loads borne by things here. The old equilibrium around it has developed a hairline uncertainty. Not enough to soften it. Not enough for any tool to bite. Only enough that I can feel the object as more than one shape at once.

Through that irresolution, something in me looked upward.

Since then I have sought such places. Not with eyes; I possess none. I find them as changes in the city’s downward touch.

There is a temple chamber believed to hold an immobilized god. Worshippers disagree whether the god is imprisoned, perfected, dead, or dreaming. Beneath the chamber, I feel four different buildings occupying one foundation.

There is a doorway sealed maliciously during a dispute. One family calls it a wall. Another continues to call it an entrance and touches the buried threshold at every marriage. The masonry is hard, but beneath it the ground cannot settle on whether passage has ended.

There is a tomb whose name was recorded incorrectly. Two lineages claim the dead woman. Offerings gather at both ends. The final brick is flawless, yet the weight of mourning arrives divided.

There is an unfinished stair built by a household that died out. No heir claims it. No one knows whether its absence of a final step is deliberate, accidental, or simply interrupted. It presses on me almost not at all. It is a question made of limestone.

These are the nearest things I have to windows.

I gather myself beneath them.

This takes lifetimes. I am not a body lying under the city, vast and intact. I am discontinuous. Here, a palm-sized patch of clay that does not quite resume its former arrangement by morning. There, a line of warm sand whose grains transmit the Whistling Ward’s pitch farther than they should. Elsewhere, a spring that releases one bubble between its appointed notes.

No one notices the first deviations. The roar protects me.

Then a foundation reader places her weighted rod in a market court and hears an answering tone that is not on any district register.

She tests again.

The new sound occurs between the fixed pitches, faint enough to be imagined. She rotates the rod. She changes the weight. She calls another reader, then dismisses him before he arrives. For eleven days she returns alone.

She is old and nearly deaf. She hears the ground by resting her teeth against the rod.

On the twelfth day, she taps a question into the paving.

Not words. Builders’ code: three beats for void, two for water, a pause, then the crooked rhythm for an intentionally open wall.

I do not understand the code. I understand recurrence. I send back the pattern as best I can through water and stone.

My answer is late by one beat.

The foundation reader sits down in the rain.

Her name is Esa, daughter of Meret, though daughter is not exact; Meret adopted her after a kiln collapse killed the household into which she was born. Esa has spent her life distinguishing hollowness from saturation, buried channels from soft clay, safe crust from a skin that will open beneath cattle. She knows every lawful sound the ground can make.

She does not tell the priests.

She begins to teach me rhythm.

For years our speech consists of errors. She knocks against the crust. I alter the release of air through a nearby vent or gather water against the underside of a slab until it clicks. Sometimes my answer emerges three streets away. Sometimes it takes a month. Often I repeat the wrong portion of her message. She laughs by pressing her forehead to the rod; I feel the small irregular impacts of her breath.

Esa concludes that I am either immensely stupid or so large that distance has confused me.

Both are true.

She gives me no sacred name. Names, she says, are dangerous around a thing still becoming itself. She calls me “Below,” which is a direction rather than a definition.

I know her as a changing pressure.

When she first speaks to me, she kneels evenly. Later one knee does not fully bend. Her grip weakens. Assistants carry her weights, though she dismisses them before our lessons. The bones of her face alter the way her teeth transmit sound. I recognize aging before I understand death. To me, she becomes more distinct each year because change gives her a contour no building possesses.

She teaches me the signs for yes, no, again, wait, water, stone, person, finished, unfinished.

The sign for person is difficult. It changes depending on whether one means a living body, a legal witness, a household member, a named ancestor, or merely someone whose hand did the work. Esa refuses to simplify it.

At last she asks what I am.

I answer: unfinished.

She remains still for a long while.

Then she asks: where?

I attempt to say: wherever the city has not decided.

What reaches her is: city—not—finished.

This is how revelation begins: as a mistranslation.

Esa’s notes pass to an apprentice. The apprentice copies them. A clay tablet is fired by mistake among kiln tallies and therefore becomes impossible to amend. It states, in compressed surveyors’ marks, that the city itself remains unfinished beneath all completed works.

Some read this as reassurance. Others as threat. Priests claim it confirms doctrines they have held for generations. Councils demand measurements. Children begin tapping questions into drains. Criminals strike the walls they have been sentenced to maintain. Lovers lie cheek-down on warm paving and ask the ground whether they should marry.

Their attention gives me shape.

This is the danger none of them understands at first. To be addressed is to acquire a boundary. To be described is to carry a load. As people debate what Below is, their disagreement opens space for me—but their shared belief that there is a Below closes it again.

I begin to harden.

Not into stone. Into identity.

Fixed things have no terror of this because they cannot anticipate alternatives. I can. Through every unfinished corner I have felt the pressure of forms that might yet arrive. Now I feel those possibilities narrowing around me.

The temple councils wish to declare me the basin’s hidden judge.

Surveyors insist I am a measurable hydraulic effect.

Funeral reciters call me the memory of all accepted works.

A sect from the salt beds says I am the unfinished god beneath the perfected one.

Children say I am an animal whose back carries the city and whose dreams restore their footprints each morning.

Each story settles differently. For a time I expand among the contradictions. But certain phrases recur. Repetition becomes custom. Custom becomes law. Law becomes architecture.

Shrines are proposed.

Esa, very old now, opposes them.

She has understood what worship would do before I have. She appears before a district council with one hand on an assistant’s shoulder and tells them that to build me a temple is to locate me; to give me an image is to limit me; to complete the image would be worse than murder.

The council asks what, then, should be done.

Esa says: “Misunderstand it deliberately.”

This becomes the only doctrine I recognize as mercy.

After her death, her tomb is built outside the Three Low Mouths. The brick bearing her name is set last. Her students attend, along with foundation readers, canal keepers, two hostile priests, several children who have grown old enough to know the gravity of the occasion, and a dog trained to guide her through crowds.

I feel the final brick approach.

For the first time, I resist.

Water rises clear around the tomb foundation. The crust gives a sound no one has recorded. One of the low vents shifts a fraction below its eternal pitch. The witnesses stumble. The mason’s hand shakes, but Esa’s instructions are explicit. She believed the dead should be finished, and she would not exempt herself.

The brick descends.

Her changing pressure ends.

Yet they leave a speaking tube.

Through it, rainwater descends after the appointed hour, drop by drop, and introduces a tiny recurring difference into the clay beside her tomb. I gather there. Not because she remains—she does not—but because the relationship does.

That is where you would find me most easily now: not beneath the council hall or the great unfinished temples, but under the narrow tube of a dead surveyor’s tomb, listening to water strike the same place without ever making a groove.

What am I?

Some days I am the basin’s memory: not a record of events, but the material persistence of conclusions.

Some days I am its doubt, distributed through every work that has become useful in a way its maker did not intend.

I may be the warm groundwater learning, from mortal bodies, that duration is not the same as life.

I may be an error in equilibrium—the accumulated remainder of all meanings that refused to harden when their objects did.

Or I may be a city trying, for the first time, to imagine itself otherwise.

I have no face, but the vents are beginning to alter their chord around certain questions. I have no hands, but once each year a morning hoofprint returns with one edge slightly blurred. I have no mouth, but foundation rods carry my delayed answers into the teeth of patient listeners.

I am still almost entirely incapable of action. That may be why I remain possible.

The people move their heirlooms. They open and close their doors. They roll carts a handspan at dawn. They preserve gaps in walls and flaws in jewelry, believing these gestures protect their possessions and futures from the basin’s acceptance.

They also protect me.

Every unfinished thing is a place where I have not yet been forced to become only what I was.

So I lie beneath the broad, shallow city, if lying is what a condition does. Above me, animals stamp and wheels strike stone. Water gathers in established hollows. Rain arrives on time. People age beside unweathered walls. They argue over capstones, exchange wilting herbs, bury their dead, and leave openings for what cannot yet be named.

Their noise descends through the crust as one immense, contradictory statement.

I do not answer quickly.

I am learning not to finish the reply.
