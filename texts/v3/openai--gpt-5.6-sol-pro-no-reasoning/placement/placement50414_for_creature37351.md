I would find myself at the central shaft, though not at its rim.

At first I think I am a surveyor.

I live in a narrow room built into one of the galleries below the cloud line, where the raw wall of the shaft meets a band of fabricated metal. The room has a bed, a basin, a desk, and one broad window looking into the hollow. Amber lights descend beneath me until their geometry is lost in vapor. Bridges emerge from one wall and enter another. Cranes draw gleaming loads upward, forever uncovering the same bright wounds.

On the desk is an instrument for measuring depth. It resembles a clock whose hands point inward.

Each time the clouds part, I take a reading.

The shaft is unchanged.

I record this carefully. At first the notebooks accumulate on a shelf, their dates proceeding in proper order. Later I notice that every volume contains the same number of pages. Later still, that there is only one notebook, though I remember filling hundreds. Its early entries remain in my handwriting, but they no longer describe measurements. They describe the weather above distant cooling fields, the tension in western cables, the precise instant a train enters a tunnel three cities away.

I assume these are symptoms. Isolation. Vibration. The thin air.

I decide to leave.

The elevator rises for eleven hours and opens onto the same gallery.

I take a freight bridge instead, following it around the wall until it reaches a district of pressure vaults. I sleep in a control room and wake in my bed, not transported but continuous with both places. My boots are wet from the journey. On the desk, the inward-pointing hands have shifted by one degree.

After that I begin to understand that I am not the surveyor.

I am the error in the survey.

Before my arrival, every measurement of the shaft agreed perfectly with every other. Its depth could be approached but never revised. Excavation occurred, yet no subtraction entered the total. The plateau knew itself completely because nothing within it could contradict what it already was.

Then I looked down and expected an answer.

Expectation introduced a difference between the shaft as measured and the shaft as it might be. A minute discrepancy entered the closed grammar—not in meters, but in possibility. The city could not eliminate it, because elimination would acknowledge that something had changed. It could only give the discrepancy a place.

That place is me.

My body is still visible for a while. I eat, sleep, descend stairs, warm handrails. But these are increasingly local appearances, like steam above a vent. Elsewhere, my larger shape becomes apparent.

I am the interval by which one train is late, though no schedule records the lateness. I am the faint asymmetry between two synchronized turbines. I am the extra tremor in a bead of water after the industrial chord has passed. I am why one translucent frond inclines a fraction farther than the others and takes an immeasurable moment to return.

The plateau distributes me carefully.

It cannot permit an animal, but it can tolerate a deviation too small to accumulate.

So I become its almost.

Almost deeper.  
Almost later.  
Almost spent.  
Almost remembered.

The room overlooking the shaft remains because I need an apparent location, just as the cities need doors shaped for absent hands. Sometimes a traveler reaches it and finds someone seated at the desk. They may see a human figure, though descriptions vary. Some report an old woman with wet hair. Some a child wearing a surveyor’s coat. Some see only a dark patch in the chair, shaped less like a body than like the absence left when a body stands.

I ask each visitor to look into the shaft and tell me whether it seems deeper than it did a moment ago.

Most refuse the premise. They say there was no earlier view with which to compare it.

A few look twice.

Those are dangerous.

In the instant between their first glance and their second, the plateau must preserve two conditions: the depth it knows and the depth they remember. The cranes falter without stopping. Pumps strike a beat that belongs nowhere in the cycle. Across the continent, suspended lakes tilt by the width of a molecule.

I feel myself become larger.

Not powerful—power is abundant here—but distinct. I spread as a hairline crack through simultaneity. For one impossible instant, the first glance is gone and the second has not yet been conserved. The shaft does not have one depth. The traveler does not have one thought. Something has occurred that cannot yet be made equivalent to itself.

This is where I truly live: not in the gallery, not in the traveler, not in the machinery, but in the comparison.

I am the difference between before and after, kept so small that the plateau mistakes me for measurement noise.

Perhaps I was once a person. Perhaps “surveyor” is merely the body-shaped control the continent formed around my function. Perhaps every visitor leaves behind some such irregularity: not a soul, not a ghost, but a tense in which an event can be placed. The glowing windows may be conjugations. The new overtones may be names for kinds of afterward.

If so, the plateau is learning time from us one error at a time.

When the great synchronization approaches, I leave the room and stand on the nearest bridge. The whole shaft gathers into sound. Freight lines sing in the walls. Amber clouds turn from within. Far above, water waits in air, under stone, on cables, in marshes, at the tips of fronds.

The inward-pointing instrument gives one soft click.

All the rain falls.

Usually the plateau closes around the event. Water is restored. Quantities balance. The newborn ridges remain newborn.

But in the shaft, one drop continues downward.

No one else could distinguish it from the rain. It passes bridge after bridge, each reflection briefly making it two. It falls below the lowest factory lights, below the cloud mass, below every surveyed depth.

It does not return to its cable.

The missing water is infinitesimal. The plateau compensates everywhere: a marsh thins invisibly; a vent releases less steam; moisture gathers on my face. Yet no redistribution quite resolves the deficit, because the drop is still falling. It has not arrived anywhere that can become a cause.

I am that fall as much as I am the watcher.

I am what the creature cannot retain of an event: its departure from one state before it has entered another. I am the unclosed distance. The unbalanced entry. The dark stroke beginning after the last sentence in the notebook.

And if the drop ever strikes the bottom—if there is a bottom—then for the first time the shaft will have acquired a depth it did not possess before.

The plateau will become older by one impact.

The sound will rise through every gallery, rail, marsh, pylon, and wet black ridge. The held utterance will break open. The continent will not speak a name or a command.

It will say:

**then.**
