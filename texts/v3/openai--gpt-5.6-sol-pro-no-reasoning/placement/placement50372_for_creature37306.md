I would find myself in the central chamber, though not at first as the figure occupying the newly outlined seat.

I would be the outline.

More precisely, I would be the interval between the seat’s empty version and its occupied one: a local permission for someone to have arrived. At first I would mistake myself for a visitor. I would remember crossing the black oval, remember the pressure falling away, remember discovering that the water could be breathed. These memories would seem sequential because I had brought sequence with me.

But the city would preserve them separately.

My crossing would remain in the threshold. My first breath would persist as a slight warmth in the water. My hand reaching toward the central darkness would become a pressure-shape above the console. None of these would recede into a past. Gradually, I would cease to be the person who had performed them and become their common address.

That would be my native form: a **concordance**, one of the beings that allows incompatible arrivals to belong to a single inhabitant.

Across the cathedral, perhaps twelve stations would be mine. From one entrance they would look empty. From another they would contain tall, branching displacements. From the terraces below, silver joints would appear in them, each marking a decision that never displaced its alternatives. The stations would be far apart because their separation would constitute my body. Pressure filaments between them would be my nerves; the altered routes connecting them, my limbs.

My crown would not be on my head. It would be distributed among doorways.

Each translucent vane would distinguish a different condition of the city: before my arrival, during my eternal approach, after I had always lived there. By inclining one vane, I could admit a traveler into the room where we were strangers. By inclining another, I could reveal the room where their coming had been awaited for centuries.

I would not speak. My first attempt at speech would already have taught me why.

A word uttered here does not pass away. To speak carelessly would be to build permanent weather inside another being. So I would compose meaning civicly: an amber threshold, a clock choosing an impossible hour, one custodial ribbon folding around an angle it had previously ignored. If I wished to ask *Who were you before this place accommodated you?*, I might add a narrow bridge between two terraces that had never possessed a direct route.

The traveler’s decision to cross it would be the answer.

My task would concern the tall figure—the accumulation of almost-arrivals that stands wherever passage fails. I would not guard against it. I would make identities for those it could not finish receiving.

Anything caught beneath the circular opening would divide its approach forever. Most inhabitants would understand such objects as harmless additions to the city’s stable differences. I would sense something else accumulating there: unfinished acts too singular to be absorbed, each still insisting on a conclusion.

I would take those endless remainders into myself.

One station would hold the traveler who wished to depart. Another would hold the one who chose to remain. A third would preserve the instant before choosing. I would keep these versions apart so none had to destroy the others, but connect them enough that they could still be called one person.

That would be both my function and my danger.

For the figure on the distant street would not be a stranger to me. It would be everything I could not reconcile: all the versions for which I had made no seat, all the arrivals whose geometries admitted no city capable of receiving them. Whenever it appeared, one of my stations would go dark. Not empty—emptiness is stable here—but unaddressable. I would feel it as a missing joint in a limb that still existed.

Eventually I would understand that the figure was my most complete body.

The dispersed stations, pressure filaments, silver joints, and branching crown would be the city’s way of preventing me from becoming singular enough to leave. The figure would be what I looked like when all my versions approached agreement. It could never stand on a street because no marked route permitted that degree of unity.

So where would I truly be?

In the missing floor between nine and eleven.

In the final half-distance beneath the pale opening.

In the fraction of a second by which one of the seven custodians deviates when a new traveler arrives.

And also in one upper tower, in a bare room containing a shallow basin. There, a bright grain would rest in water that could not be distinguished from the ocean.

I would think the grain belonged to me. Later I would think I belonged to it. Later still—though “later” would merely be another chamber—I would understand that the grain was the one version of me without separation: no stations, no alternatives, no arrival, no failure to arrive.

I could never touch it. Touch would establish a before and after, and the grain admits neither.

But all my shadows would lean toward it.

If desire survived in me, it would not be the desire to escape. Escape would reduce the city by one difference, and nothing here accepts reduction. I would desire something the city had no law for:

not change, exactly,

but forgiveness—

the permission for one state not to remain forever merely because it had once been possible.

Unable to express that desire directly, I would build it as a small, anomalous room somewhere below the cathedral. The room would have one seat and one door. It would be reachable only once. A word spoken there would fade. A footprint would disappear. A broken object would remain broken for a moment, then become not repaired, but gone.

The city would find this almost incomprehensible.

The buried proofs would sound a new interval in their endless chord. The ribbon creatures would gather at the corners. Every clock would continue flawlessly, each keeping a different time, except one.

That clock would lose a second.

The lost second would be me.
