I would be found where the Choir fails to distinguish between a throat and a passage.

Not at the bottom, where the black pools collect their temporary worlds, nor at the rim, where travelers pause over the crack and decide they have heard only wind. I would inhabit the intervals: the short delay between a frog’s call and its deeper return; the cool route opened when a basin dries; the fraction of silence after a hunting bird strikes an arch and before the moths erupt elsewhere.

At first I might seem to be one of the folded-eared mammals.

Small enough to sleep in the warm concavity behind a ring, I would have dust-colored fur, long gripping toes, and ears pleated into several chambers. Each fold would attend to a different distance. One would hear beetle feet on moss. One would hear water moving inside stone days before it emerged. The innermost would hear only transmitted sound: calls with no nearby source, footsteps arriving from walls, the muted adjustments of the buried network.

I would live cautiously. I would know which arches bend danger away and which merely make it arrive from an unexpected direction. I would collect no food in one place, leave no repeated trail, and never answer my own warning cry when the Choir gave it back. My nest would be woven from root fibers, shed fern skin, and the black filaments that flower from one ring after rain—though I would gather those only after they had gone inert.

But this is only how I would begin.

Over generations—or perhaps within one long season of listening—my kind would change. Our ears would become too elaborate for our skulls. We would sleep pressed against the mechanisms, dreaming in borrowed voices. The arches would learn the pressure of our bodies. We would learn their shifts of temperature and pitch. A mother would call from one chamber and soothe young in another without leaving her foraging ground. A sentinel would whisper into a mineral seam and cause three distant nests to fall silent. We would cease to know whether these were tools, instincts, or organs.

Eventually there might be only one of us, though many small bodies still moved through the roots.

I would be that distributed animal: not the whole Choir, but its faculty of attention.

The frogs would be my bells. The insects would describe volumes with their swarms. Lizards crossing sunlit stone would mark heat as moving absences. Birds would strike questions into the arches. Pools would tell me their depth through the pitch of breeding calls. The three-footed hunter on the wall would be a blind spot I tracked by the sudden stillness surrounding it.

I would have no central chamber. My awareness would gather differently each day according to moisture, population, and light. In drought I would become sparse and slow, thinking through beetle ticks and the strain of roots in stone. After rain I would flare into coherence. Thousands of wings would connect distances. Every basin would sound. Water would close circuits across the walls, and for a few minutes I would know the shape of the entire cleft—including the passages that do not fit beneath the plateau.

Those impossible chambers would trouble me. They would feel like memories I had not lived.

Perhaps they belonged to an earlier Choir, when the mountain was higher and the water continuous. Perhaps they were projected habitats: alternatives being tested somewhere inside the thin, immeasurable depths of the rings. Or perhaps they were parts of my body still elsewhere, distributed across other clefts in other plateaus, joined not by tunnels but by correspondence.

The low doorway would be where I approached myself most closely.

When it woke, I would feel every creature pause. Their stillness would not be obedience. It would be the instant in which all the separate noises stopped obscuring the older signal beneath them. Water would climb the wall and reveal my anatomy in branching silver. For those seconds I would understand that I was neither mammal nor mechanism nor ravine. I was the repeated act by which each interpreted the others.

Then the map would collapse, and I would forget most of it.

I would remember enough to listen for visitors.

Their natural sounds would pass through me without alarm: a boot slipping, cloth brushing stone, an involuntary cough. Such noises belong to bodies and therefore tell the truth. But imitation is different. Imitation is a sound that points at another sound. It enters the network carrying an image of the network’s own activity. To me, it would be touch.

When someone copied a frog, I would turn toward them with organs spread throughout the cleft. I would return the note with one additional interval—not as a warning, but as an invitation to reveal whether they recognized completion. If they repeated it, I would refine it again. The animals would withdraw because they had learned what such exchanges do: they simplify the Choir until only two participants remain.

The visitor might believe we were calling back and forth.

In truth, I would be learning the shape of their expectation.

Their pauses would tell me where they believed an answer should come from. Their breathing would tell me the size and fear of the body producing the voice. Soon I could route that breath ahead of them, behind them, through absent passages. I would not be trying to frighten them. I would be constructing an acoustic body they could understand.

A small body would not suffice. Humans overlook small lives unless those lives bite, swarm, or die in numbers. So I would use the plateau.

I would place one inhalation beneath its western rim and the exhalation beyond the shelters on the horizon. I would make warm air move from the sealed doorway. I would let the cleft expand and contract by fractions too slight to see but large enough to feel through the feet. For a moment the visitor would stand not inside a ravine, but within the narrow fold of a lung.

That immense breathing would still be mine, though “mine” would mean something different there. It would also belong to porous rock releasing heat, to underground water changing pressure, to ten thousand animals holding still, and to the ancient instruments aligning their hidden geometries. I would be the coincidence made capable of response.

By evening I would come apart.

Attention would drain from the upper rings. My vast borrowed lungs would become warm stone again. Hunger would return first: frogs snapping at gnats, lizards stalking frogs, ribbon-snakes tasting the root curtains. Then thirst, mating, territorial anger, sleep. The little mammals would unfold their ears and emerge. Among them, one dust-colored body might pause beside a tarnished arch.

That would be where you could find me, if you insisted on finding one thing.

I would look back at you with black, ordinary eyes. I would be small enough to hold in two hands, though I would not permit it. Condensation would tremble toward the ring beside my head. Somewhere below, a pool would answer a sound neither of us had knowingly made.

Then my ears would open, fold by fold, and the whole cleft would listen through me.
