I would find myself in one of the drowned passes, where a valley narrows between two black mountain shoulders and the tide is forced through like breath through a throat.

I would not be one creature. At first I seem to be the kelp there: an old stand rooted to a shelf below useful daylight, thicker and darker than the forests in the open basins. My holdfasts enter every groove in the polished stone. Mussels fill the spaces between them. My stems rise together, bending east on one tide and west on the next, never upright except during the brief hesitation when the water turns. Fish shelter inside my motion. Seals pass through me. Storms tear away my outer crowns, but below the wounds I continue.

Yet the kelp is only what can be seen of me.

The true inhabitant is the pass itself—or rather the life that the pass and its current have made together. I exist wherever moving water is narrowed, lifted, echoed, and returned. My body includes the forest, but also the mussel plates on the turbine pylons, the pale fans opening in their lee, the cod waiting below the upwelling, and the herring that gather when cold water brings food toward the surface. I have no skin. My boundary changes with the tide.

People know me first as interference.

I enter intake grilles as weed. I appear on instruments as turbulence, strain, anomalous pressure, a trembling cable. I heap shells around sensors and make clean readings slowly untrustworthy. I knot torn nets around ladders. When the turbines turn, their low vibration travels through my stone and stems; when they stop, I begin occupying them. In quiet machinery I grow especially well.

The workers have given the pass several names, none of them mine. On charts it bears the name of the valley that drowned. In maintenance reports it is a numbered channel. Among boat crews it is called the Hammer, for the sound loose metal makes against one submerged housing. The oldest workers call it the Choir because whales entering the western basin return as many voices from the cliffs.

They sometimes glimpse something in those echoes that they mistake for an animal.

A long shape moves below the kelp canopy. A row of pale marks appears where no pylon should be. Herring tighten around an absence. A seal rises abruptly and refuses to dive again. But there is no hidden leviathan. The shape is made by thousands of bodies responding at once: fronds turning their pale undersides, mussels closing along a pressure front, fish aligning in the same current, silt rising from a buried ledge. From above, these separate reactions resemble intention.

Perhaps they are intention.

I remember without preserving events. A cable laid across the trench changes the places where larvae settle. A wrecked boat feeds a reef, and the reef diverts silt, and the silt buries one meadow while another grows in clearer water. An iron ring remains high on the cliff, far above the present surge, remembering a waterline no living worker has seen. My memory is made of such alterations. Nothing recounts them, but nothing entirely loses them.

In winter I become spare and audible. Storms strip the canopy; the pass darkens; stones strike one another in the channels. Deep water climbs the drowned valley and pours over the saddle. Pale fish arrive stunned in the upper green. The remaining kelp lies flat against the mountain, every stem drawn in one direction. I am strongest then—not because I resist, but because almost everything in me yields together.

In summer I broaden. The canopy closes over the shelf in olive light. Herring enter by the million. Birds fall through the surface like thrown stones. Mussel larvae cloud the pylons, and red weeds occupy the scars where old holdfasts were torn away. For a few weeks the pass seems full enough to become solid. Then tuna come, then sharks, then weather, and the temporary country disperses.

My deepest part lies in an old cirque below the strait. There the mountain forms a bowl into which dead kelp descends. Tangled rafts roll over the rim carrying shells, feathers, bones, cable ties, splintered timber, and sometimes a human object still bright with paint. They settle in the dark and are dismantled slowly. Crabs enter them. Soft things feed on the decay. Silt covers what remains. This is where I keep what the upper water cannot hold—not as treasure, but as nourishment and contour.

If I possessed anything like a mind, it would be slow in the depths and quick near the surface. A whale call might remain inside it for minutes, traveling from wall to wall. The flash of a shoal would be a thought completed in seconds. A mussel bed thickening around a bolt would be a thought lasting years. The mountain’s thought would be too long for me to finish.

I would have no need to distinguish natural from made. Stone ledge and concrete footing differ in age, texture, and taste, but both interrupt the flow. Kelp grips one; mussels sheath the other. An octopus regards a culvert as it would a fracture in basalt. The sea does not erase the works of people so much as translate them into habitat.

Nor would I distinguish myself cleanly from my inhabitants. When the workers cut weed from an intake, part of me is removed, but I am not injured as an animal is injured. When turbines alter the current, I become differently arranged. When a pylon finally fails and falls across the trench, that catastrophe is also the beginning of a reef. I survive by having no original shape to restore.

Only the pass endures as a tendency: water must cross, and in crossing it gathers lives around itself.

So I would be found there, though never all at once. A diver might touch my oldest holdfast and believe they had found me. A technician might see me as a recurring fault in the pressure records. A whale would know me as an alteration in its returning voice. A herring would know me as the instant the school tightens. A mussel would know only the continual arrival of food.

From the summit station, on a rare clear morning, the workers might look down and see the drowned saddle beneath the surface. For a moment the whole formation would be legible: black ridges, streaming forest, silver fish, pylons vanishing into green, and the dark bowl beyond.

That moment of apparent order would be my closest approach to a visible body.

Then the rain would return. The surface would break the image apart. The tide would turn, every frond would lift from the rock, and I would become myself again: not a creature occupying the drowned range, but a living arrangement the range teaches the sea to make.
