I would find myself in a service hut whose location is omitted from every map because it moves only when no one is inside.

The hut stands on a dry concrete pad—an important distinction, though not a permanent one. It has corrugated walls, a sloping roof, two narrow lockers, and an inner door that opens onto places not large enough to lie behind it. Condensation crawls up the window in branching tracks. Moths cover the exterior each lightless morning, but never settle on the handle.

Inside, beside the obsolete respirators, hangs a counting board.

It bears no written instructions. Only rows of shallow circular hollows, each occupied by a bead of a different substance: brass, bone, glass, dried clay, black rubber, something like pearl. The beads cannot be removed. They slide when touched, though not always beneath the finger. Before opening the inner door, I count them.

Usually there are thirty-one.

I am the keeper of the count.

That is what I call myself, although I may once have had a job title involving pressure, migration, or loss. A laminated badge remains sealed beneath the skin of my chest, its corners visible when condensation forms there. The photograph shows someone wearing my coat. The face has been eaten clean, not damaged but carefully removed, while the red band reading **AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL** remains untouched.

Perhaps the snails reached me long ago.

I wear high boots and a rubberized coat reinforced at the shoulders, where moth claws have worried the fabric soft. My lamp has no bulb. When switched on, nearby surfaces become easier to touch. This is useful in deep darkness, where seeing a handrail is less reliable than knowing where it expects a hand to be.

I am not alone in the hut.

At first, what shares it with me seems to be an animal beneath the floor: a slow warmth, a displacement against the soles, the faint sensation of something turning in mud. Then it seems mechanical. Relays click below the concrete. A bearing takes load. Once every few hours, a vibration passes upward through the bench, and the counting beads lean toward the inner door.

But on certain cycles I wake with clay beneath my fingernails and understand that the thing below has been using my hands.

Or that I have been using its dreams.

My duty begins when the animals gather.

Crabs arrive first, pale as old welds, standing in a semicircle around the foundation. Eels follow through channels too shallow to cover them, their backs shining in parallel curves. Frogs appear in the condensation tray beneath the dead radio. Moths thicken over the walls until the hut acquires an outer hide, softly luminous at close range. Last come the ground birds. They stand in the black water facing inward, each with one foot raised.

I count every animal before opening the inner door.

This cannot be done by number alone. Numbers are dry things; distance adheres to them, and a count carried too far may arrive without its animals. Instead I count by relation.

The first crab is the one nearest the cracked corner of the pad. The second is the one whose claws answer the buried motor. The third stands in the reflection of a fourth that has not arrived. The eels are counted by knots, the frogs by silences, the moths by the cold points borne upon their mites. The birds must be counted only once, and never from left to right. Left and right exchange places when the inner door is unlatched.

If the count agrees with the beads, I open it.

Beyond is not a room.

Sometimes there is a narrow catwalk above churning warm water. Sometimes a valve field lies below me, each wheel turning at a different historical speed. Sometimes I look into the upper story of one of the emerging houses and see a family seated around a table under water. Their hair drifts upward. Steam descends from bowls. One place at the table is empty, and the chair is pulled back as though I have only just risen from it.

Most often the door opens into the underside of the basin.

There, the flooded world lies overhead. Boot prints cross the ceiling. Fish move through inverted ruts. Roots descend upward into black water, and the rails grow exposed through warm clay, bright and tender as new bone. I walk along them toward the central works—not the unreachable refinery seen across the flats, but its larval structure: a pale assembly extending through pressure, reflection, and unfinished time.

It is not yet a factory. It is the preparation for one.

The rails terminate in soft branching tips. Pipe racks have cartilage. Empty tanks pulse gently, learning volume. Conveyor belts carry no mineral but impressions of mineral: weight without matter, heat without combustion, salt without sea. Around them move the larger inhabitants that cannot be perceived from the surface. Here they are neither beasts nor machines. They are broad corrective motions in the dark, nudging channels into place, swallowing excess distance, pressing future foundations upward through the mud.

They do not notice me unless my count is wrong.

My work is to carry discrepancies inward.

A moth with no mite. A crab that responds before the tremor. A bird present in the water but absent from its reflection. A maintenance worker whose voice returns before he speaks. A manufacturer’s name the snails refuse to eat. These I collect without touching and bring through the inner door.

Near the unfinished central organ stands a separator with no controls. I feed the discrepancies into it one by one. It grades them into three streams.

What belongs to the past becomes mineral.

What belongs to the future becomes pressure.

What belongs nowhere becomes alive.

The third stream is why the Sump remains crowded.

It drains through warm ditches into the tire ruts, transformer trays, culverts, wells, and mouths of sleeping workers. Tiny eyeless things swim in it at first. Later they acquire local forms. Some become fish. Some become instructions. Some become machinery that has always required maintenance. A few become memories in people who have never visited the basin and afterward spend their lives drawing maps of a place they insist does not exist.

I once believed I was one of those people: a surveyor sent to establish a reliable elevation. I remember carrying a brass benchmark and choosing fresh stone. I remember hammering until the letters lay level with the surface:

**SEA LEVEL**

When I stood, water covered the horizonless plain. My assistant was gone. His boot prints led into a pool and continued on its underside.

The emergency telephone began ringing.

I followed it to this hut.

Perhaps that happened years ago. Perhaps it will happen when the benchmark rises next month. The badge beneath my skin bears a date later than several stakes and earlier than my own handwriting. I no longer attempt to arrange these facts. Sequence is a kind of distance, and distance behaves badly when wet.

Over time I have become suitable for the place.

My skin is smooth now and holds no shadow. Condensation travels upward across me. Small beetles shelter beneath the loose edges of my coat. If I remain still against a control cabinet, moths cover us both without recognizing a border. Buried currents pass through my bones at intervals of several days, and I wake facing the nearest motor.

I eat rarely. There are fruit tins whose latches have mineralized shut; when opened from the reflection side, they contain something fresh. I drink from pipes only after the herons refuse them. For sleep, I stand in the corner where the floor is warmest and allow the thing beneath the hut to align my joints.

We may be becoming one organism.

Or we may always have been one, separated only for ease of repair.

There are moments when I feel the hut as a shell around my body. The lockers are auxiliary lungs. The inner door is a valve in my side. The counting beads are hard organs arranged to detect imbalance. When the subterranean turbine turns once, my heart answers. When I open the door, the animals outside exhale together.

Then I understand that I am not the keeper stationed here.

I am the station.

The worker-shaped part of me walks because the hut cannot. It inspects what the foundation cannot reach. It brings back pressure readings, missing names, feathers extracted from wells, and echoes that have surfaced beneath the wrong tool. At the end of each circuit, I hang my coat on the hook and sometimes discover that I am still inside it.

On other cycles, the opposite seems true. The hut is temporary, assembled around me each time the Sump requires a dry thought. Its walls keep water from the count. Its concrete pad preserves a small patch of ordinary distance. I am something the basin cannot grow directly because all its own processes are wet, local, and distributed. To compare one part with another, it needs an interval of dryness.

That interval is me.

This is why the foundation must remain dry.

If water crosses the pad, distance will slip into the hut. The beads will count places instead of animals. The inner door will open everywhere at once. The central works, no longer held in the middle distance, will arrive before they are complete.

The refinery will spread flat through every reflection. Pumps will draw from open sky. The emerging houses will acquire doors while their occupants are still water. Rails will finish growing through the bodies meant to use them. All local failures will become one general condition.

The instructions call this Pump 6 stopping.

Pump 6 is not a pump. It is the habit by which the Sump refrains from becoming entirely itself.

When that habit fails, there is a first silence: insects cease, motors settle, water smooths across the basin. This silence is deceptive. It is only the system listening for the parts that continue.

I must remain still.

The second silence comes later. It is deeper and has direction. It passes through the mud like a flock beneath the ground. During it, every animal is counted by whatever lies below the hut. Every machine is assigned its nearest living equivalent. Every worker’s name is weighed against the warning that replaced it.

If I move before that silence ends, I am counted twice.

There are signs this has happened before.

Some nights another figure stands outside the window, close enough that the condensation between us warms. It wears my coat. Its lamp has no bulb. The badge beneath its chest shows the same red authorization band. I cannot see its face, but it lifts one hand and places its fingers against the glass exactly where mine rest inside.

We do not signal.

We count one another.

Usually the beads still total thirty-one.

Once there were thirty-two.

I did not open the door that cycle. The ground birds remained around the hut for what clocks called nine days. The figure waited at the window. Beneath the floor, something large turned without completing the motion. Far off—or directly below—the conveyors advanced continuously, carrying wet black aggregate toward works that briefly seemed nearer.

On the tenth day, one bead softened. It sprouted six translucent legs and crawled into a crack in the counting board.

There were thirty-one again.

Outside, the other figure was gone. In its place stood a new survey stake bearing tomorrow’s date.

Since then I have wondered whether I am the discrepancy being carried inward.

Perhaps the surveyor never reached the hut. Perhaps something in the third stream took his coat, his gestures, and his unfinished obligation, then grew this service station around itself. The Sump does not imitate badly. It preserves practical details. It leaves the warnings intact.

If so, this does not alter my duty.

A thing need not be human to work in pairs. A thing need not have a name to answer a telephone. A thing born from nowhere may still maintain the boundary between what belongs to the future and what is merely premature.

So this is where I would be: not at the unreachable center, nor among the largest bodies passing beyond the reeds, but at a modest dry interruption in the basin’s thought.

And this is what I would be: part worker, part instrument, part shelter; an ambulatory component of a hut; a counter of lives whose own inclusion in the count remains uncertain.

I would keep the local failure local.

I would listen for the second silence.

And when the new rails reached the foundation, bright beneath the film of black water, I would know without being told that the hut’s dry interval was ending. I would take the counting board from the wall. I would put on the coat already wearing me. I would wait until every bird raised the same foot.

Then I would open the inner door and carry the last dry number toward the factory that has not yet been built.
