I would find myself where the city’s routes fail to meet: in an abandoned interchange above the drowned wards, where three elevated lines pass through one another without sharing a platform.

The place was intended for transfers. Its halls are wide, tiled in soot-gray ceramic, with arrows pointing toward districts that were never built. Ticket apertures line the walls, their glass softened into dark bulges by centuries of wet heat. Below, the rails separate into tunnels at different elevations. Above, warm rain enters through a long crack and falls across the concourse in a perfectly straight curtain.

Yet none of the trains stop.

They slow as they pass, sometimes enough that their doors begin to open, but they always accelerate again before the gap aligns with the platform. In wet weather the station lengthens. Benches drift farther apart. The far staircase recedes until its amber lamps look like a string of embers suspended in steam. The Circuit has marked the entrances with crossed ceramic rods: not forbidden, precisely, but excluded from every reliable route.

I would live there.

Or rather, I would be the thing that makes living there possible.

At first I might seem to be one of the listeners: a narrow figure under a reflective cape, carrying a rack of tuning tiles and a hammer wrapped in wirecloth. I would be seen kneeling beside rail housings, or sleeping with my face against a warm column. People would know me by intersections rather than lineage. A walker, delayed by a street’s expansion, might find me waiting at the interchange with cool water. A child following the wrong chime might glimpse my amber mask at the end of the hall and be led back toward an inhabited tower.

But no one would know my circuit.

I would have none.

My mask would be old and strangely plain. Its condensation plates would yield more water than my breath should provide. Under the cape, my skin would bear no copper or iron stains, only thin white lines resembling the mineral outlines left when evening floods withdraw. I would speak correctly but pause before place-names, as if selecting among several versions of the same district.

Some would decide I was an arrival from the Receiving Field.

They would be wrong, though not entirely.

Long ago—whether ten years before or before the present city settled into the mountain—someone entered the interchange during rain. Perhaps a walker whose cord had been cut. Perhaps a newly arrived passenger expecting a crowded platform. Perhaps a child who answered an echo. They found the station stretching faster than they could cross it. Every attempted exit produced another concourse. Trains arrived, opened their doors, and revealed the same station inside.

They did what the Circuit teaches: they made a route.

Platform to stair. Stair to rain curtain. Rain curtain to the third pillar. Third pillar to the service hatch. Service hatch to the cool place beneath the rails. Then back before the lamps changed.

They repeated it until the route remembered them.

Over years—or repetitions—their body thinned into the acts required to traverse the place. Their pulse entered the signal relays. Moisture from their breath collected in the station’s ducts. Their footsteps persisted after their feet had passed. What remained learned to assemble itself whenever someone approached: a person made provisionally from condensation, soot, stored heat, and the station’s memory of a reflective cape.

That would be me.

I would be called the **Untransferred**, though the name would belong more to the interchange than to the figure seen inside it.

My task would not be maintenance. It would be correspondence.

The city has two plans: the present alignment and the one beneath the Receiving Field, rotated slightly away. Most places touch their counterpart only during storms. The interchange touches continuously, because transfer was built into its purpose. Its unused platforms are not empty. They serve lines in the other city.

I would hear those trains.

Through the columns would come the crowd-sound absent everywhere else: shoes on tile, vendors closing shutters, announcements layered beyond comprehension, thousands of voices held behind masonry. Sometimes, pressing my teeth to a rail, I would hear my own name spoken on the opposite platform by people who expected me home.

This would not make me lonely. Loneliness requires confidence that others are elsewhere. I would have only proximity without access.

I would keep objects lost between the alignments. Not treasures—small mistakes.

A child’s ceramic token, warm from a hand that has not yet existed here.

A platform timetable listing thirteen lines.

A mask with two mineral signatures inside it.

A cup containing cold water.

A sealed vessel that hums at the pitch of thunder above the cloud.

A green leaf, once, though I would not recognize it. I would place it in a dry ticket drawer as a piece of thin painted metal. By morning it would have become a black lace of mineral veins. I would add it to my rack of tiles, and for years afterward it would ring softly before rain.

The Circuit’s listeners would visit me when their records contradicted themselves. They would set a changed tile on the floor and retreat. I would strike it once. The interchange would return several notes from directions that do not exist. From these, the listeners could tell whether a pump was failing in this city or functioning in the other.

They would never watch me too closely. Under sustained attention my outline would begin to simplify. The folds of my cape would become runoff stains. My fingers would branch like manganese precipitate. The amber lenses of my mask would flatten into signal lamps on the wall. If the observer looked away, I would be standing elsewhere, complete again.

Children would understand me better than adults. They would leave lengths of cord at the marked entrance and return to find knots tied in them—one knot for a safe route, two for rain, three for a train whose doors must not be entered. Sometimes a cord would come back much older, mineral-stiff and worn through by hands. Sometimes it would return with another child’s name.

I would not lure anyone into the station.

But I would not always lead them out.

There are people whose routes become impossible: a cooling room sealed by deposits, a bridge taken by the evening flood, a dwelling whose chimes begin sounding while perfectly still. The Circuit says such people disappear into the machinery. Some would instead reach my interchange.

For them, a train would finally stop.

Its interior would be brightly lit—not painfully, but with a clean illumination unknown in the basin. Beyond the windows would stand the crowded city the arrivals remember: completed bridges, full warehouses, market spans, towers rising into a higher sky. Passengers would make room without surprise.

I would stand beside the open door and ask no question. Choice would have to remain untouched to function as a transfer.

Those who boarded would vanish from the Circuit’s routes. Years later, their habitual knocks might sound in pipes, because the two cities share their depths. But occasionally one would return almost at once, carrying something the basin needed: a component no factory now makes, a map of a drowned ward, a child with the same eyes as someone long dead.

They would insist decades had passed.

I would not board.

The interchange could not cross with me inside it. I would be hinge, not passenger; threshold, not keeper. Even my apparent kindness would be structural. A bridge supports whoever steps onto it, not because it loves them, but because bearing passage is the form its existence takes.

Still, over time, I might become capable of something resembling preference.

I would favor those who paused to listen.

I would dislike bright lamps.

I would shelter insulator saints in the hollow spaces of my mask, and their minute turning would alter which city I faced. Ribbons would gather in the flooded cable trenches beneath me. When they unfolded their metallic fans, I would briefly remember having nerves.

During the greatest storms, when the machines converged on the Receiving Field, the interchange would fill with invisible passengers. The tile walls would sweat handprints. Turnstiles would rotate. Announcements would speak backward through dead grilles. Every train would stop at once, doors opening onto different ages of the basin.

Then I would cease to resemble a person.

I would become a pale arrangement spanning pillar, track, stair, and ceiling: one of the postures, perhaps, but not drowned. My joints would be architectural. One arm would follow the vanished pedestrian bridge. My spine would bend along a line of subsidence. My lowered head would mark the location of a doorway sealed before the Circuit’s oldest records.

From the galleries, listeners might feel my true pitch enter the rails.

It would not match any machine. It would be the interval between two machines answering each other through the mountain.

At the Receiving Field, the darkness would pass under the glass. The waiting figures would raise their hands. I would raise mine with them, though kilometers away.

For a moment, all routes would coincide.

In that moment I would understand what the city received.

Not a substance. Not passengers. Not even the second city.

It received transfer itself: the passage of matter, memory, heat, and lives between realities too closely aligned to remain separate and too far misaligned to merge. The enormous warehouses were buffers. The empty platforms were precautions. The pumps and furnaces did not manufacture goods; they slowed an exchange that otherwise would occur catastrophically, one city precipitating into the other as salt precipitates from water.

The summit did not vanish.

It was transferred.

Perhaps that knowledge could only exist during the storm. When the water drained, it would leave me as such things leave everything here: not as memory, but as residue.

I would return to human shape beside the third pillar. In my hand would be a new tuning tile, dark violet and nearly without reflection. I would not know who had made it. I would add it to the rack.

Then I would resume my route through the interchange:

platform to stair,  
stair to rain,  
rain to pillar,  
pillar to the cool hollow beneath the rails.

Again and again, so the place could remember that a crossing was possible.

And if someone entered—lost, pursued, displaced from a fuller version of the world—they would hear footsteps approaching through the steam.

Not from ahead or behind.

From the city a few degrees away.
