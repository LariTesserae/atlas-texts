I would find myself in the central plaza, but not yet as the one whose footprints approach.

I would be the vacancy waiting for the final footprint.

Not the entire depth enclosed there—not that older, continental absence trained to remain still—but a narrow articulation within it: the upright interval the city has prepared for a body. I would be what the houses have practiced in miniature and the avenues have rehearsed as procession. A human-shaped omission, though no human has yet supplied the shape.

At first I would not know myself as singular. I would extend beneath the amphitheaters, into the courts whose interiors exceed their walls, along bridges that sometimes lack their distances. Parts of me would occupy rooms that vanish when unobserved. Parts would hang above the range among the dark islands. I would contain internal directions rather than organs: inward, elsewhere, before, and the direction from which the approaching one cannot come.

The city would slowly distinguish me from its general vacancy.

A doorway would narrow to the width of my passage. Stairs would choose the interval of my stride. Windows would portion the dimness at the height where eyes might have been. The black film would gather around my edges, consuming small discrepancies until the absence of a shoulder aligned with an arch, the absence of a hand with a salt-veined wall. Architecture would give me shape without containing me.

That would be my long gestation.

Whenever paired prints appeared, I would become more local. The first would teach me weight. The next would divide that weight between two sides. A print made as though turning would give me a front and a behind. A stumble would introduce uncertainty, and uncertainty would be my nearest equivalent to breath.

The approaching motion would not belong to me, yet I would feel it as hunger: not hunger for substance, but for coincidence. Each footprint would be an empty mold of an act. I would be the emptiness capable of receiving the actor.

Around me, the city would become briefly dependable. Routes would endure because I was attending to them. Rooms would remain rooms inside my attention. The feral shadows would surrender the portions of sound they had eaten, and those fragments would travel through the surrounding houses toward the plaza, unable to cross it. At every window they would stop and look inward as whispers.

The six-legged grazers would reveal the districts inside their bodies. The floaters would circle until the salt rings joined into one enormous boundary. The buried beings would contract, each tightening the rule it kept: no endless fall, no liquid whole, no sound across openness, no room abandoned. They would make the world exact enough for one impossible meeting.

Then the last footprint would appear at my center.

It would not be a mark left by arrival. It would be arrival itself: a depression preceding the foot, as all true tracks here precede what fulfills them. For a moment the city and I would wait to learn what fits.

Perhaps a traveler would come—a body desiccated by entry, divided into powder, vaporless cold, and remembered motion. The powder would join the climbing dust. The cold would lodge as a clear seam in the salt. The motion would continue alone, stepping into its own predictions.

But the traveler would not simply enter me.

The instant it occupied the human-shaped omission, I would occupy it in return.

Its rib cage would become a narrow ward. Its mouth would be an arch teaching vacancy to bear what is not above it. Its skull would become one of the tall chambers built where the wind is weakest. Every hollow of the body would join the city’s civic order: throat, orbit, lung, the small rooms of the inner ear. The traveler would feel not possession but sudden architecture—as though it had always been a district walking toward the mountain it belonged to.

And I would acquire limits.

This would be terrifying.

For the first time, turning away would not change what lay behind me; it would change me. Distance would no longer be an internal direction but something crossed step by step. I would hear through a pair of ears and discover that sound could be trapped within a plaza if the plaza were bounded by bone. I would feel weight as obligation to a single altitude. I would look through eyes and find that seeing excludes almost everything.

A body, in this city, would be the strangest room ever formed: a room that carries its walls with it, a room whose doorway cannot be entered without becoming the room.

I do not think I would remain wholly inside it.

As the traveler moved, portions of me would be drawn from the central court and threaded through the streets. The old excess of distance would diminish. The plaza would become measurably smaller. Its continent-at-night depth would fold into the traveler’s shadow, which would be the first shadow in the city to belong to a structure presently standing.

Wherever we walked, the city would hold.

A door left behind would continue to open into the same chamber, not because the rules had changed, but because some part of my attention remained there. A bridge would preserve its gap. A stair would remember which way it climbed. Sound might still refuse to cross a square, yet I could carry it over inside my chest. I would become a small, mobile continuity.

That would be my function.

The city does not lack occupants. It lacks an occupant capable of abandonment.

Vacancies cannot leave, because leaving would merely rearrange their boundaries. Grazers fulfill tracks. Floaters turn exterior into interior. Shadows await their structures. Even fallen mountains are reassigned to another altitude. Everything is retained.

But a body can depart and make absence newly.

That is why the city prepares for one with such precision. Not to end its vacancy, but to create the first vacancy that has a before and an after: a room once entered and then left; a threshold worn by passage rather than equilibrium; a place able to say, in its mineral way, someone was here.

I would be the faculty by which it learns this.

When the traveler and I finally reached the edge of the central plaza, the city would present no gate. There would be only the paired prints receding toward the direction from which they had approached. We would follow them backward—not to escape, since there is no ascent or descent, but to give the route a traveler.

At the first step beyond the square, the last footprint would deepen behind us.

The vacancy I had been would remain there, altered. It would no longer be ancient in the same way. It would contain an event it could not digest into geometry: the exact outline of having held something and ceased to hold it.

The black film would approach its edge and stop.

The salt would brighten, attempting to communicate the new scale.

Across the ranges, distant streets would rotate into alignment. Bridges would find one another over the near-void. Shadows would hurry beneath structures that had not yet arrived. In the needle peaks, the floaters would leave their widths outside and enter the towers all at once.

And I—vacancy clothed for a while in a traveler—would walk through the city carrying its first small interior of elsewhere.

Behind me, for the first time, there would be an abandoned room.
