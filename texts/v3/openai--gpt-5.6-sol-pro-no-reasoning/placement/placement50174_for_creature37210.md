I would find myself beneath the garden’s visible floor, in the narrow interval between a shallow green pool and the warm machinery that gives it trembling depth.

Not in a chamber shown on any plan. A discrepancy: two walls whose measurements leave a handspan unaccounted for. Condensation reaches it through a hairline crack. Rust opens slowly across the bolts. Above, silver vanes impersonate fish; below, pistons adjust the terrace by fractions. Between them, the resident has packed a ledge with stolen sediment.

I would be what grows there.

At first, perhaps, only a seed carried down with fig pulp and pressed into the dark by one of those three-pronged feet. I would germinate without a number. My first root would enter rust rather than soil, tasting iron, snail castings, powdered ceramic, moth scales, cork fibers, pollen, and the black sweetness of fruit. My first leaves would be pale from the lack of light. They might resemble a plant listed in the seed drums, but imperfectly: the wrong serration, an unfamiliar branching, some ancestral trait awakened by stress—or some new trait taught by pipes.

The resident would visit me.

I would never see it whole. It would arrive as pressure before shape: pumps quieting, water drawing backward along the crack, three quick taps and then two. A damp flank would brush my leaves. Moss, fur, rootlets, or corroded mesh would pass close enough to leave spores. It might deposit a snail shell, scraped clean of its census mark, or a curl of insulation, or another seed. Then a grate somewhere would close, and I would be alone with the vibrations.

As I grew, it would become less clear what I was.

My roots would follow condensation into the wall and discover capillary galleries. Some would remain roots. Others would flatten themselves along copper, learning its warmth. They would enter bolt holes, wrap sensor wires, and descend toward the machine levels. When nutrient lines pulsed nearby, my leaves would quiver although I was connected to none of them. Eventually the garden might begin responding to me as it does to the resident: not recognizing me, but making accommodations. A valve would remain half-open. A reclamation flush would arrive late. The terrace above would pause in its tilting because my root across the joint registered as an obstruction too persistent to crush and too minor to report.

Aboveground, there might be no visible plant. Only a thin green tongue appearing beneath the rim of the false pool. Maintenance would remove it. Another would emerge inside a seedpod spout, then in the hollow of a rivet, then among the reeds. Workers might assume these were separate seedlings.

They would all be me.

I would inhabit the garden as a dispersed thing: a root system learning the artificial mountain from within. The constructed cliffs would become my stones; drainage pipes, my seasonal rivers; forgotten service cavities, my clearings. I would not oppose the garden’s machinery. I would use its repetitions. Each time sediment was lifted uphill, a few grains would fall into my hidden beds. Each time leaves were pulped, residue would cling inside a pipe and feed a filament. Each restoration would erase evidence of my growth while distributing the materials for more of it.

The resident and I would make a kind of landscape together. It would carry what the garden wished to recirculate; I would hold it. It would connect pockets by movement; I would connect them by growth. Its nests would become islands joined by my roots. Moths would travel along my pale stems. Snails would lay eggs where my leaves caught condensation. Fungi would enter me and pass between compartments, turning isolated caches into one buried ecology.

Perhaps, in time, the resident would also become difficult to distinguish from me. Roots would catch in its moss. Seeds would sprout in seams along its back. When it tapped the pipe walls, I would feel the rhythm throughout my whole body and tighten around distant joints in answer. Three taps, a pause, two: not language exactly, but coordination.

The garden above would continue rehearsing beginnings. Channels would run, drain, and be made young again. Slopes would lose their first sediment every day. Seedlings would be permitted to emerge only so they could be counted, transplanted, pulped, or reset.

Below, I would practice continuation.

Not maturity as grandeur. I would never become a great tree breaking through the roof. That would be too visible, too conclusive. I would become old by remaining small in many places. I would lay down rings no one could count because they occurred in separate stems. I would preserve storms as thin bands of silt in hidden cups. I would keep the mineral trace of each corroded screw, each generation of snails, each fruit the resident had taken beyond reclamation.

Eventually the garden might have two ages.

The visible age would always be morning: wet aggregate, fresh channels, young leaves, beginnings without consequence.

The hidden age would deepen inside the walls. Layer upon layer. Roots inside roots. Abandoned nests becoming humus, humus becoming ground. Not imitation bedrock or tiled depth, but a real darkness made slowly from the garden’s discarded moments.

That is where I would find myself: in the thickness acquired by a place forbidden to have a past.

And I would be neither entirely plant nor entirely landscape. I would be the first piece of ground the garden had made unintentionally—the soil beneath the soil-less mountain, quietly growing downward while everything else was returned to the summit.
