I would find myself neither on the summit nor at the bottom of the cleft, but inside the living layer that prevents those two places from meeting too quickly.

At first I seem to be moss.

I occupy a fracture midway down the eastern wall, where basalt has split into two close planes. From outside, I am a black-green pad no broader than a hand. Water darkens me until I am nearly indistinguishable from stone. Only when the cloud lifts do my outer tips show their color: a brief, mineral green, almost luminous against the glassy rock.

But I am not one plant. Nor am I only plants.

I am the temporary coherence of whatever has lodged here: moss stems growing through their own drowned predecessors, a liverwort’s translucent lobes, fungal threads, birch pollen, stone flour, the husk of a seed, fragments of fern scale, and grains of basalt too small to continue falling. The cleft is always trying to sort these things—to send water downward, spores outward, dust into cracks, leaves into the drain. I am where that sorting fails for a while.

My body is therefore less a body than an interruption.

Water arrives from above and enters me cold. I swell. Channels open through the compressed dark beneath my green surface. Some lead inward, toward the fracture; some return the water to the wall in fine, shining threads. With every pulse I take in grit. With every drying I tighten around it. In this way I make soil without possessing any ground.

Fungal filaments run through my lower layers. If there is a center to me, it is there, in that unlit binding: neither root nor nerve, but a pale net passing among the dead and the dormant. Through it, the birch seedling above me receives what little can be dissolved from the rock. Through it, the dead stems do not entirely cease to participate. They become structure, reservoir, obstruction. My past remains beneath me as dark pressure.

Yet I am also the birch seedling.

Its first two leaves have opened in a pocket I helped retain. They are absurdly delicate. Each blast folds them against the stone. Each discharge buries them in water. A grain of gravel is enough to bend the stem almost flat. Still, a root finer than hair has entered the wet seam behind me. For several days—or one season; duration is difficult here—it extends the whole fabric upward into light.

I do not expect it to become a tree.

Expectation belongs to creatures that can distinguish fulfillment from failure. Here, to begin is already the complete act. The seedling may be scoured away tonight, but while it persists it slows water, catches pollen, alters turbulence at the scale of a droplet. If it dies, its root will remain threaded through me. The distinction between establishment and destruction is not sharp enough to organize my life.

When the wind descends, I feel it first through the ferns.

Their crowns are wedged in rubble above, but their fronds cross my ledge. They strike the basalt and spring away, strike and spring away, until one catches in the braided shrub stems. Spores shake from its underside. Some adhere to my wet tips. Others are driven so hard against the wall that they disappear into water films. The fern is damaged by the same motion that distributes it. This seems, here, to be the ordinary form of fertility.

Far below, the slot breathes.

I cannot see it, but its inhalation passes through every wet connection in the cleft. My outer filaments incline downward. Pools tremble. Water tightens into channels. For an instant the entire fabric is oriented toward disappearance.

Then comes the gulp.

The rock answers through pressure rather than sound. The wall gives a short, deep shudder. Loose grains settle farther into me. One of my older layers detaches from the basalt and hangs by fungal threads and shrub roots. I am suddenly divided between what still adheres and what has begun to go.

This is not death. It is a change in grammar.

The loosened portion folds outward. Water enters behind it. Liverwort that had faced the rock is exposed to the air for the first time, pale and almost colorless. A cluster of moss tips is crushed under the turning mat; buried stems are uncovered elsewhere. What was underside becomes surface. What was shelter becomes debris. For a few breaths of the drain, I am a flap, a hinge, a green-black tongue streaming from the wall.

Then the roots tear.

Part of me slides down the basalt intact. I cross distances I could never grow across. I pass an orange seep and shear away three red rosettes, which travel with me, their cups still holding water. Gravel accumulates in my folds. A fern fragment hooks into the mass. We stop on a lower shelf behind a wedge of newly fallen stone.

Above, I remain in the fracture.

Below, I begin again.

There is no question of which part is truly me. Identity in this cleft does not follow continuity of shape. It follows the ability of fragments to resume the work: slowing water, holding grit, darkening stone, giving roots somewhere to enter. I am whatever temporarily converts passage into residence.

Perhaps, then, I am not the moss after all.

I am the thin interval in which the mountain’s losses become conditions for life. I live between impact and drainage, between fresh fracture and the next collapse. My substance can be green tissue, dead stem, fungal thread, trapped silt, or braided wood. None is sufficient alone. Together they form a skin over a body that is still assembling itself.

I have no senses separate from what happens to me. Light is the lifting of cold from my outer tips. Wind is the simultaneous reversal of a thousand leaves. Water is weight, extension, and connection. Frost is a night-long tightening in which every pore becomes an instrument of fracture. A rockfall is first vibration, then darkness, then a new arrangement of pressures. Time is not measured by seasons but by surfaces: exposed, occupied, buried, exposed again.

Nor do I perceive the cleft as enclosed. Its boundaries are wherever material ceases to be held. The summit enters me as pollen, rain, birch seed, and shattered glass. The mountain’s interior enters as vibration and mineral seepage. The unseen depths enter through the suction of the drain. I am situated where these movements interfere with one another.

No animal comes to witness me, and I have no need to become visible. I make no flower that asks to be approached, no fruit designed for hunger, no nest, trail, call, or eye. My relations are adhesion, shelter, pressure, rot, entanglement. I know others by how they alter flow.

The shrub is a lattice that catches stones until its strength becomes its burden.

The fern is a spring that translates wind into spores.

The fungus is a continuity that does not require exposure.

The birch is a wager repeatedly made with almost no prospect of height.

The red rosette is a cup occupying a poisoned margin.

The moss is time made absorbent.

And I am all of them only insofar as they touch, break, and continue through one another.

Eventually the lower fragment of me will move again. The shelf is already gathering water behind it. Gravel is loading the upstream edge. The wedge of basalt trembles during each subterranean hammering. When it shifts, the mat may roll into the final channel. There the current will comb it long and narrow. Pieces will be stripped away. Some will vanish into the hand-wide slot; some will paste themselves against rock just above it; some spores may be blown upward when the drain exhales.

The upper fragment will also fail. Frost is working behind it. The crack has widened enough to admit more water. One morning a thin blade of basalt will peel free, taking the whole dark pad with it and revealing gray stone untouched by light or root. The exposed plane will smell dry despite the rain.

For a short while it will be bare.

Then condensation will leave mineral dust along a microscopic ledge. A spore will adhere. A filament will swell. Something divided elsewhere will arrive here as a fragment.

I will not return, because I will not have left.

In this world, I would be found wherever the cleft has almost succeeded in washing itself clean. I would be the residue that becomes a foothold, the wound’s first darkening, the soft mechanism laid over the geological one. Not an inhabitant of the chamber, exactly, but its temporary capacity to retain what passes through it.

A living fabric with no single edge.

A pause dense enough to grow.
