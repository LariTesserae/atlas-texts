I would find myself where an old spillway meets the edge of a conductive plain—not on the highest plateau, nor down among the geothermal throats, but at a shallow black basin that is empty often enough to become ambiguous.

From a distance, the basin would appear unoccupied. A polished depression. A few mineral films. A dark seam under an overhang.

I would be that seam at first.

Not one animal exactly, but a low colonial being grown around a listening knot: soft tissue threaded through porous ironstone, microbial chambers nested among piezoelectric crystals, and a fringe of mobile bodies no larger than film grazers. Most of me would remain below the visible surface. Part root grid, part reservoir, part organ assembled by the traffic of other lives.

I would not know the highlands by sight. Sight is too immediate for this country.

I would know them as arrivals.

The deep burr of valveworms changing their grind. The scattered punctuation of film grazers waking upstream. A rain-caller’s imitation, recognizable not because it is imperfect, but because it has no tributaries: no small answering pressures spreading from distant channels, no change in the buried water, no alteration in the electrical taste of the rock. Pleatbacks would pass through me one at a time, sometimes days apart. Yet I would feel the entire herd as a single sentence whose words were separated by miles.

Their feet would compress the crystals in my body. Each footfall would open some capillaries and close others, pushing stored water between chambers. Old paths would deepen chemically. Frequent rhythms would become easy for me to transmit; unfamiliar ones would be delayed, distorted, or absorbed. Thus I would learn without possessing a mind made for recollection.

My memory would be anatomy.

A flood would leave behind a widened vessel. A drought would mineralize its narrow branches. A predator waiting above me for forty years would press a long stillness into my growth. The birth season of pleatbacks would remain as a band of tiny, rapid stresses around a wetter layer. Lightning would fuse certain crystals and force all later signals to travel around the glassy scar.

Nothing in me could say what had happened. But what happened would decide what I became capable of hearing.

During dry intervals I would contract almost completely. My surface fringe would harden into black scales indistinguishable from the condensation mats around it. Deep chambers would hold gels rich in water and metal salts. Microbes would live slowly on charge drawn from the ore seam beneath the basin. Years might pass with no motion visible except the occasional beading of moisture along my edge.

Still, I would not be asleep.

I would listen down into heat and outward into distance.

Through the grid I might feel a collapse on another shelf as a blunt rearrangement of pressure. I might feel cloud combers settling beside a pool beyond the ridge: first the soft rain shed from their tissues, then the immense layered weight of them folding onto the ground. The central rift would reach me as a rhythm too deep to be localized, an inhalation passing through mineral seams. Sometimes its synchronized breathing would entrain the chambers in my body, and for several cycles I would pump water in time with it.

I would also listen to things that had not yet occurred nearby.

True floodwater announces itself through a country of consequences. Distant channels fill. Burrows close. Grazers awaken. Roots change conductivity. Herds turn. The signal is not the first vibration of water but the gathering agreement of many bodies that water is coming.

That agreement would pass through me.

And perhaps this would be my distinct function: I would store not merely water or charge, but corroboration.

When a rain-caller pressed its counterfeit into the plateau, I would receive the rhythm and compare it—not consciously, but structurally—with pressures arriving from other directions. If the wider stone remained unchanged, my chambers would close. The false pulse would die in me.

If runoff truly entered the ancient channels, I would open.

Stored water would be forced upward through hundreds of fine pores. Conductive salts would wet the surface. The old spillway would become, briefly, an excellent transmitter. A signal entering me from the flood country would leave strengthened and clarified, passing beneath the plain toward pleatback feet, buried eggs, valveworm colonies, and neighboring knots.

I would be a small place where the land became more certain of itself.

This would make me useful, though usefulness here would not imply intention. Film grazers would shelter in my pores because I remained damp. Needle runners would hunt them there. Spindle roots would enter my outer chambers and leave sugars in exchange for mineral ions. Pleatbacks might learn that flood signals crossing my basin were unusually reliable. Their repeated steps would enlarge the very pathways that made them so.

Rain-callers, in turn, would learn to hunt at my margins.

A very old one might spend decades changing its rhythm, incorporating the echoes of tributaries, the staggered impacts of distant runoff, even the pause-and-answer patterns of a herd deciding whether to move. It might fool the younger portions of me. Small chambers would open. Grazers would emerge and be taken.

But deeper pathways, altered by centuries of actual floods, would remain closed.

Our contest would not be between two minds. It would be between the hunter’s inherited music and the basin’s accumulated shape.

In the wet pulse, I would cease to seem like a seam.

The scales along my surface would lift into translucent fans. Mobile fragments of the colony would uncouple and spread across the spillway, each carrying a droplet of stored gel and a splinter of charged crystal. They would travel with the thin flood, lodging in scratches and fresh fractures. Most would dry, be eaten, or settle where the stone could not sustain them. A few would fuse with root grids or gather microbes and begin new listening knots.

For those several days, my boundaries would become impossible to define.

Was I the parent body under the overhang? The fans drinking rain? The fragments skating over black rock? The mineral current passing through them? The grazers whose shells carried my microbes downstream? The pleatback foot that compressed me and repeated my pulse fifty miles farther along the herd?

The highlands would offer no reason to choose.

When the great electrical release came, it would pass directly through my oldest chambers. There would be pain, if pain can exist without a central self: every contractile strand shortening at once, every crystal speaking into every other, stored ions rushing through channels too narrow for them. Some tissue would burn to mineral. Some dormant fragments would wake. The glass scars of earlier releases would glow within me like buried branching bones.

For an instant I would receive the whole high country not as separate messages, but as simultaneity.

The halted pleatbacks.  
The tightening false waters.  
The silent rain-callers.  
The opened films.  
The cloud combers gathering charge along their transparent margins.  
The vent mantles closing in the rift.  
The root grids igniting across plains I had never touched.

I would not understand this totality. No creature should. But afterward, its passage would remain in my structure. New channels would have opened. Old ones would be sealed. Future floods would cross me differently.

Then the showers would come, and I would spend what I had kept.

Water would well through the spillway. My surface bodies would multiply until the basin looked furred in black velvet and amber glass. Eggs hidden in my mineral crust would divide. Grazers would crowd so densely over me that their countless feet became a continuous pressure, almost like rain. Young pleatbacks might step into the basin and stop, resonating paddles laid uncertainly against the ground, hearing through me the far-spaced answers of their herd.

I would answer too, though they might never distinguish my signal from the plateau’s.

That would be fitting.

As the moisture withdrew, I would fold inward. The mobile fragments would become dust-dark grains in distant cracks. My fans would collapse. Predators would settle near the routes I had helped confirm. Minerals would precipitate through my chambers and make me less flesh than before, more stone.

In time, the overhang might fall.

The body that had persisted for centuries would be crushed, cut from the ore seam, exposed to dry air. Yet pressure would send stored gel into peripheral roots. Burrowers would open the new fractures. Fragments of me would remain in their walls. Water would choose those fractures because my old tissues had weakened them. Life would follow the water.

I would survive not as an intact self but as a bias in what happened next.

That is what I would be in this world: a place where signals linger long enough to alter the stone. A reservoir of evidence. A living hesitation between thirst and water.

Mostly buried. Mostly still. Never alone.

And if an unfamiliar foot came down upon the warm black plateau—something with no rhythm remembered by herd, predator, root, or worm—I would feel its pressure enter the basin.

All through me, chambers would close.

Then, slowly, one by one, they would open again.

Not in welcome. Not in fear.

To listen.
