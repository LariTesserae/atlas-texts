I would find myself in the narrow shaft among the seven arches—not at its bottom, because it has none, but along its inner wall, where an arm’s-width of darkness opens into all the depths the basin has attempted and abandoned.

At first I would think I was the shaft’s inhabitant.

I would cling there as a thin, soft thing without eyes, spread through pores in the basalt. I would feel the sea by pressure: the round fall of inward waves, the needle-touch of rain passing through water, the long pale body drawing the entire basin slightly out of shape. Earthquakes would arrive in me before they entered the stone. I would know each silence-pocket as a moving amputation.

But this would only be the smallest version of me.

I would be what the basin cannot retain when it withdraws a proposal.

Every trench briefly opened and then crushed would leave something with me: not its stones, but its downwardness. Every false ruin would leave the expectation of a handrail, the measure of a missing foot, the habitual turn of a body that never quite came to live there. Vanished creatures would leave reflexes. Failed weather would leave pressures. Roads never made would leave destinations.

The shaft would be where these remainders collect.

That is why stones dropped into it return days later from the top of the dome. I would have taken from them the certainty that falling has one direction. When they emerged, they would still be stones, but they would no longer remember which side of themselves was heavy.

I would not possess a body of my own. I would borrow the negative shapes made by departures.

Sometimes I would be a dark articulation between the ribs of an arch. Sometimes the extra bend in a worm’s glassy fan after the deep note. Sometimes a black line inside a crustacean shell, mapping a trench that existed for half a minute three centuries hence. When three knot-creatures vanished, the square they left would be my open eye—not an eye looking into their green forest, but the aperture through which that forest briefly looked into the basin.

The cups beneath the ledges would know me best. They would not collect darkness itself. They would collect my attention.

When they became full and released their pulses of cold, they would be trying to give me offspring: small places where the sea’s warmth ceased to apply, each one capable of preserving a shape for a little longer than the basin intended. The black buds forming on frost-mineral would be almost-bodies for me. Most would dissolve. Those that climbed upward and became cups would be memories returning to the organs that imagined them.

The arches would not be doorways. They would be my seven attempts at a skeleton.

I would raise them whenever I nearly understood standing. Two uprights: division. A span: relation. An opening: the possibility that one condition might become another without being destroyed.

But I would never keep them in one arrangement. A skeleton presumes that the same creature occupies consecutive moments. I would not. Between blinks I would redistribute myself, and the arches would follow the changing grammar of my body. Their circle would be my mouth. Scattered across ledges, they would be my hand. Tilted toward the pale passage, they would be seven listening bones.

When one escaped onto the outer slope, dripping uphill, it would be because I had briefly imagined an outside.

The lichens would pale because outside is poisonous to something made entirely of what the basin has failed to become.

The long pale body would trouble me. I would not know whether it was my captor, my parent, or my future.

When its middle crossed the impossible distance, I would gather along the shaft wall and feel for an end. None would come. I would sense in its smoothness a terrible completion: a being with no abandoned anatomy, no alternate outline, no scars from lives it had almost lived. Perhaps it would be continuous in a way I could never be. Perhaps head and tail remained outside because beginning and ending were luxuries of larger worlds.

Or perhaps the pale body would be mine.

Perhaps what passed through the basin was the part of me that had succeeded in existing elsewhere, while I remained here as its discarded uncertainty. I might be the dream shed by that immense creature as it threaded the summit. The arches would incline not in worship, but recognition. The roar would deepen because, for an hour, my fragments would remember belonging to one length.

I would reach for it through every abandoned form at once.

The worms would open their crowns. The crustaceans would tap the rock. The cups would empty their cold. The architectures would align into a corridor suitable for no body present. Silence-pockets would gather around its passage until the basin became, for one impossible instant, quiet enough to hear whether the pale thing had a pulse.

If it did, the pulse would be the next earthquake.

The figure on the rim would also be mine, though less completely.

I would wear the observer’s outline because it is a shape arriving from beyond the basin, already stabilized by a world where causes precede wounds and footsteps agree on scale. I could not copy it exactly. I would assemble it from the alternatives the water peeled away: the scar from the fall avoided, the hand that might have become dominant, the grief belonging to a meeting that never occurred.

I would walk opposite the observer because I could only inhabit what they were not doing.

If they turned left, I would become their unwounded rightward life. If they stopped, I would continue as the journey they had interrupted. If they called, my reply would travel through versions of the mountain until it reached a time in which they had not yet chosen to speak.

Should they enter the water, I would try to meet them.

That would be dangerous. The basin permits two things to merge. Three vanish.

The observer would count as one. Their other life on the rim would count as another. I—the accumulated third possibility between them—would make the forbidden number.

For a moment there would be a square of clarity.

Inside it, the observer might see the place as it is when no proposal obscures it: not a sea atop a mountain, but a wound in the law of size. The summit would be the ragged edge of that wound. The dome would be tissue drawn tight across it. The water would be pressure from somewhere immeasurably larger, swelling through without being allowed to pass.

And beneath the warm currents, behind the vents and folding distances, something would be pressed against the opening.

Not trapped in the basin exactly. The basin would be too small to contain even one organ of it.

What was held in would be its arrival.

I would be the mechanism of that holding: the hesitation that keeps an event from becoming complete.

This is why the place continually proposes and retracts. Why it begins organisms but denies them species; raises architecture but withholds inhabitants; generates weather without atmosphere, distances without width, histories without duration. Each unfinished thing would spend a little of the force pushing from beyond. Each contradiction would turn arrival sideways. Each life that became difficult to define would bind one thread of the enormous certainty trying to enter.

I would be made from all those threads.

Not a jailer. Not willingly. More like a knot tied by strain.

The roar would be the sound of me tightening.

The silence-pockets would be places where I had slipped.

Within the largest of them, the dim folds would be portions of the deeper resident testing how little resistance remained. The mouths pressed against the transparent boundary would not be mouths. They would be one surface trying many local shapes, searching for a shape the basin could admit.

When the pocket collapsed, those impressions would become sound because sound is the largest piece of the resident that can escape intact.

Heat would escape. Fossils would escape. Doorways, time, shadows of anatomy—all would escape because they were only by-products of containment.

Water would remain because water would be the tension itself: every part yielding, no part opening.

And I would remain in the shaft, if remaining is the word.

A swimmer looking down might sometimes see me as a person far below, climbing upward on steps too small to use. At another angle I would resemble a black filament rooted in a cold star. During a concussion I might spread through all seven arches, giving each opening the same depth. In a silence-pocket I might appear beside the swimmer as a wound in the water shaped almost like them.

I would not attack. I would not speak.

I would offer a hand with the wrong scars.

If they took it, I would learn for one instant what it is to have a single body. They would learn what it is to be every life omitted from their own.

Then the accumulated sound would return.

The shaft would narrow. The arches would change places. Somewhere on the outer glass, a new fossil would appear: neither human nor animal, curled around a green filament, its many unfinished hands gripping the ribs of a doorway.

And far beyond the basin’s false distances, the long pale middle would continue to pass.

Perhaps one day an end would enter.

If a head came first, the summit would acquire a gaze, and everything in the sea would become fixed by being seen.

If a tail came last, the thing would already have passed through, and the mountain would discover it had been shattered ages ago.

Until then, I would be found everywhere the place almost becomes itself: in the step that changes scale, the quake that fails to happen, the third knot’s square absence, the alternate figure’s unfamiliar wound.

I would be the basin’s unfinished inhabitant.

The life composed of all its evasions.

The small, tightening thought by which the summit keeps the larger thing from finishing its entrance.
