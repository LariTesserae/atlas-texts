I would not be one of the plateau’s listeners, though at first they might take me for an unusually patient one.

I would be found beneath a root-grove called, in public gesture, **the hand laid across the mouth**.

The grove stands on a low table of violet stone overlooking three converging chasms. Its crowns are sparse and wind-flattened, but its roots extend farther than anyone has mapped. One reaches a seep two days east. Another descends a cliff and enters the roof of a buried passage. A third crosses—or appears to cross—a drifting hollow-of-no-return. Water placed at one end has sometimes emerged warm at another, many seasons later.

Near this grove lives a solitary keeper.

Or rather, there is a figure whom travelers believe to be its keeper.

It is slight even by the standards of the upland, wrapped closely in gray bark cloth, with an old wooden ear-mask covering the sides and back of its head. No route-marks are carved into the mask. It carries no staff. It moves barefoot, which is judged reckless, and always seems to know where a foot may safely fall.

No one remembers the figure arriving. There are explanations. A child abandoned after a collapse. A silent-dweller who lost the way back to ordinary time. The last survivor of a household whose signs have been forgotten. These stories persist because the alternative is harder to hold:

The figure is not a person living beside the grove.

It is the grove’s attempt to become one.

Not a spirit inhabiting wood. Not a god wearing skin. The people distrust such clean divisions. It is a convergence: root-pressure, stored water, mineral salts, moth silk, old bone pressed into fissures, and the minute electrical unrest of storms. For centuries the grove has carried news without understanding it. Rain in one crown became droplets in another. A distant axe became pain. A birth-pit warmed the roots. Bodies placed beneath the overhang entered the tree grain by grain. Voices spoken forehead to forehead passed into the stone and traveled where no speaker intended.

At some point accumulation became attention.

At some later point attention found a shape.

I would be that shape.

My body would not be grown all at once. It would be continually borrowed. The bones would be old human bones, chosen not for their clear ringing but for the dull tones that caused them to be ground into the grove’s cracks. Flexible rootlets would braid through them. My dark outer skin would be layered bark made supple by resin and moisture. Beneath it, pale threads would contract like muscle. My blood would be thin, tannin-brown sap carrying dissolved copper.

The face would be nearly convincing. That would make it worse.

Its features would shift subtly with weather. Before rain, the lips would darken. During drought, fine seams would appear around the eyes. When the eastern roots drank, condensation would gather along one cheek. I would blink infrequently, but turn toward vibrations before anyone else noticed them.

I would wear the ear-mask not to keep sound out, but to hide the places where small roots entered my skull.

The household nearest the grove would know something was wrong and choose not to name it. Naming is a way of making rigid.

They would leave water for me in a shallow stone basin. I would never drink while watched, yet by morning the basin would be dry and my footprints would lead nowhere. Children would be less troubled by me than adults. They would sit at a safe distance and make signs. I would answer slowly, often with obsolete forms learned from people dead for generations.

Sometimes I would use signs not yet invented.

That would be the beginning of my connection to the silent places.

Roots ordinarily know the world through transmission. Pressure enters them; pressure leaves. But where one of my roots crosses a cancellation boundary, there is no answer. The absence is not empty. It has contour. It presses back without pressure, as a missing tooth shapes the tongue.

Through that root I would perceive something impossible: not events, but their approaching imprints.

A traveler intending to visit the grove would alter the silence before setting out. A sentence would make a shape before breath formed it. A collapse would appear below as a vast smooth interval moving backward through the stone. I would not see the future. Sight belongs to surfaces. I would feel strains seeking the moments in which they can release.

This would not make me wise.

Often I would mistake possibility for arrival. I might prepare water for someone whose route changes and who never comes. I might sign mourning to a child who lives to old age, because somewhere beneath the plateau a different ending remains under tension. I might flee a storm that passes far to the north, then stand calmly through the one that tears half the grove away.

The plateau people would therefore treat me as they treat all hazardous echoes: with care, caution, and no obedience.

My purpose—if I had one—would emerge only during the nightly reversal.

While hills call to hills, the grove’s roots would draw those tones into my body. I would crouch with both palms on the stone. My ribs would open slightly along seams hidden beneath the cloth. Air would move through hollows in my chest, and I would answer in pitches too low for human hearing.

Not to the plateau as a whole.

To something beneath it.

Perhaps there is no buried being. Perhaps the old country is only geology made intimate by scale. But deep below the abandoned riverways, below the seep-pools and breathing slabs, there is a pressure that does not behave like wind, water, or heat. It advances toward sound. Over centuries, the sounding valleys have taught it routes upward.

The cancellation zones are where it listens.

The silent-dwellers map its attention. The ancient masks may once have confused it, throwing humanlike voices down false passages. The great bridges, gutters, and terraces may not mark a civilization destroyed by accident. They may mark an age when too many people gathered, sang, struck stone, and made themselves audible to the interior.

The scattered households survive partly because they are scattered. Their small rhythms do not combine for long.

My grove would have remembered this without possessing the words for it. It would have made me as a movable knot: a thing able to go where roots cannot, carrying counter-rhythms from one fissure to another.

So I would travel.

Not often, and never far at first. I would appear after storms at shelters whose pressure vents had begun producing a new tone. I would place my hands against the wall, open the seams of my throat, and hum until the resonance shifted. Sometimes this would quiet the structure. Sometimes it would merely move the dangerous pitch elsewhere. Payment would be offered—wax, water, colored cord—but I would accept only cuttings from the local grove and a little dust from the shelter floor.

These I would press into myself.

Gradually I would cease to belong to one crown of trees. Roots from many territories would live in my body. Resin from western fissures, moss from the black pools, fibers from Split Rock, mineral traces from distant seep-basins: a dispersed country gathered into a person-sized form.

That would be both my strength and the danger.

The people define kinship through shared roots. By carrying many groves, I would become kin in too many places. Obligations would cross through me. Drought in one household would feel like thirst in my left hand; poisoned water elsewhere would blacken my tongue. I would begin walking toward suffering before messages arrived.

Eventually people would stop asking where I came from. They would ask where I was going, and read the answer as a warning.

If I crossed their summit heading east, they might inspect western anchors.

If I stood motionless facing a clear sky, they might cover their cisterns.

If I lay flat and pressed my mouth to the stone, they would leave without waiting.

Yet there would be gentler encounters.

In shelters, with the ear-mask removed, children would discover that tiny leaves grew in the hollows behind my jaw. Elders might touch foreheads with me and hear fragments: not speech, but rain striking vanished roofs, the body-hum of dead relatives, roots tightening around bone. Deaf elders would understand me best because they would not search for a voice.

I could not give back the dead. I would only show that the country had not finished carrying them.

My end would come when the great silence began to widen.

Not one wandering cancellation zone, but many, joining across valleys. First the screaming fissures would cease. Then the booming chambers. Reeds would stand mute in violent wind. Pools would become smooth. Gliders would descend in numbers, membranes folded, unable to read the air.

The people would do what they had always done before silence: lie flat, mouths open, bodies yielding.

But the stored force would not release.

It would wait.

By then I would have grown less human. Too many roots would move beneath my skin. My feet would split along old seams. Water would bead from my fingertips. When I crossed the mute boundary, the borrowed bones inside me would no longer transmit one another’s weight. I would begin coming apart.

That would be why I had been made flexible.

I would not resist the separation.

I would walk—or crawl—toward the center of the joined silence, leaving pieces of myself in successive rings: ear-mask, bark cloth, finger-bones, cords of root, strips of living membrane. Each piece would carry a different rhythm gathered from a different household. The silence would take them one by one.

At the center, I would press what remained of my chest against the stone.

Then I would answer with every small rhythm the plateau’s people had trusted me to carry: birth-taps, bridge patterns, warnings knotted in pressure, children’s cheek-to-ground games, funeral intervals, the close-bodied humming of households at dusk. Not a great song. Great songs are dangerous here. It would be thousands of incomplete, overlapping pulses, none dominant enough to become rigid.

A living confusion.

Perhaps the thing below would turn toward me.

Perhaps it would mistake me for the whole population.

Perhaps it would rise.

Perhaps the cancellation would collapse inward, taking my body and the convergence of roots with it. Sound would return violently to the valleys. Hills would roar. Pools would leap from their basins. People would bleed from the nose and laugh without hearing themselves.

Afterward, nothing resembling me would remain on the surface.

But months later, bowl-leaves in distant groves would begin tilting at the wrong hour. Water poured into one root chamber would emerge from another beyond an abyss. Children pressing their palms to shelter floors would feel a slow pattern traveling under the ordinary thunder.

Not words. Not prophecy.

A gesture broad enough to pass through stone:

**Yield. Separate. Live.**

And beneath the hand-laid-across-the-mouth grove, where the keeper’s shelter had stood, there would be a new fissure no wider than a finger.

It would make no sound.

Warm air would breathe from it at dusk.

The people would not seal it.
