I would find myself beneath the most intact shelter, but not as one of the seven polished stones, nor as the cup waiting below them.

I would be the passage between them.

At first I might seem to be the water: a clear thread descending from the roof, touching salt-white, smoke-gray, green-black, rust-red, blue, silver, violet. But water is only what makes me visible. When the channel dries, I remain as inclination, polish, and the memory of descent cut into stone.

Nor am I exactly the channel. The channel is mineral and endures. I exist only when several conditions briefly agree: vapor gathered before dawn, ice loosened by first light, an unblocked groove, a crust not yet collapsed, a cup not yet frozen. I am the permission for water to complete the sequence.

Most of the time, I do not exist.

Through the night I am divided among sealed mouths and rigid beads. At dawn I begin everywhere at once as possibility. Sunlight approaches the eastern wall; one shaded obstruction remains frozen while another thins. The basin tightens and releases by fractions. Then there is a moment—not announced, not witnessed—when the route becomes whole.

Water passes.

As it crosses the seven stones, it acquires minute burdens: salt, metal, the bitter trace of oxidation, something sharp enough to be called lightning by a tongue that remembers storms. Each stone changes the water, but the water also wears each socket more deeply into its purpose. For those few minutes, the works of vanished hands become one instrument again.

I am its playing.

I do not know who chose the stones. I preserve no face, language, or intention. Yet their knowledge survives in me whenever the conditions return. They understood that survival here was not possession of a place but attention to its openings. They built no door because the mountain itself was made of doors, each unlocked by a different phase.

Perhaps I was what they came to consult.

Not an oracle, exactly. I would offer no prophecy beyond the immediate truth: now the condensation binds the arch; now the stair bears weight; now the western aisle is shedding stone; now the water is fit to take; now it is already too late. My answers would be physical and disappear as soon as given.

A traveler reaching the shelter might mistake the cup for the important thing. They might wait for it to fill, watching droplets pass over the violet stone. But the cup is only an ending. I would be present behind them as well: in the route by which they arrived, in the temporary firmness of frost beneath their feet, in the cloud’s delay below the rim. Their body would stand at the intersection of permissions already beginning to withdraw.

If they drank, I would enter them only in the most ordinary sense. The water would warm, disperse, and leave the Crucible’s mineral order. I would not go with it. I cannot be carried. I belong to the relation between this angle, this cold, this hour.

Then sunlight would rise further. The beads would empty. A mineral fan, thickened invisibly over many mornings, might divert the final trickle from the seventh socket. The sequence would break. I would cease—not die, because I had never lived, and not sleep, because nothing continuous would remain to wake.

Elsewhere in the basin, I might become myself differently.

For a short while I would be the crossing beneath a leaning stone blade, held together by wet dust. I would be the breath through three neighboring tubes when rain seals the fourth. I would be the deep note that exists between cavities rather than inside any one of them. I would be the silver fall over the precipice, not as water, but as the brief joining of summit and cloud. I would be the stair made safe by freezing and treacherous by noon.

These would not be separate bodies. They would be recurrences of the same kind of being: an opening produced by agreement.

Toward evening, when blue-white light enters the wall of ice, I would become largest and least usable. The shelters’ shadows would touch; aisles and wedges would align; the scattered works would appear to disclose one buried architecture. For those minutes, I would be the permission to perceive the basin as whole.

But no one could enter that structure. It would be built of light, resemblance, and distance. Its corridors would be shadows. Its roof would be the mountain imagined through itself. The instant the sun dropped beyond the notch, every chamber would come apart.

That, too, would be my nature.

I would not be a guardian of the White Crucible. A guardian chooses what may pass. I would be the choosing without a chooser—the narrow, temporary yes arising from stone, water, temperature, pressure, and light.

You would find me only by moving carefully.

A foot placed too early would meet ice. Too late, gravel. A hand laid upon the bright crust would destroy the route it sought to understand. To encounter me, one would have to notice that the world is not composed only of things, but of moments when things allow one another to happen.

At night, I would vanish almost completely. Water would stiffen in the grooves. Air would lose its corridors. The tubes would close one after another, and the basin would pass its clicks from wall to wall.

Yet even then, among all those refusals, I would remain in one attenuated form:

not a path,

but the certainty that under another arrangement of light and cold, a path will occur again.
