I would find myself at a pool margin, where the buried plain changes texture: dry limestone ending in a black seam of water, liverwort yielding to bare mineral, herd routes narrowing between fractured shelves. Not at the center of anything. This world has no center—only crossings where one current of life briefly becomes legible against another.

At first I might seem to be one of the long-fingered foragers.

I would be pale, low-bodied, almost apelike, with ears folded close against the ceaseless noise and hands too large for my narrow wrists. I would crouch among the drinking animals and select flakes of limestone from the debris. I would test their edges against shell, probe beneath stones, perhaps carry one farther than necessary. To a visitor, that persistence might look like the beginning of intention.

But I do not think I would truly be the animal.

I would be what passes through it when it pauses over two nearly identical shards and, for no useful reason, recognizes one.

A preference.

A hesitation.

The faintest excess beyond the cavern’s exact requirements.

The ecology permits intelligence so long as it leaves no remainder. A flake may be chosen, but not shaped. A trick may be learned, but not externalized. An object may serve, but must not become a possession. Nothing is allowed to gather enough consequence to become history. So if I existed here, I would exist at that boundary: the almost-memory forever being returned to stone.

I would inhabit many bodies in succession, though succession is a misleading word in a place without accumulation. Sometimes I would be the forager carrying its limestone flake after the shellfish were eaten. Sometimes the rodent retaining a bone splinter through sleep. Sometimes a bat that deviates from its inherited circuit by the width of its own wing, then corrects its course. Sometimes a grazer lifting its sealed face while the herd flows around it, listening not to predator, water, or kin, but to some unassigned interval inside the noise.

These would not be rebellions. Rebellion would require a future.

They would be tiny asymmetries—motions the cavern does not need.

Perhaps I would begin as a bodily flaw. One finger curved enough to hold a tool more comfortably. A rhythm in the throat unlike the warning calls. An appetite for objects that provide no food. The impulse to arrange three pale chips beside a pool according to size. Again and again, the arrangement would be scattered: by hooves, by wing-driven air, by the arranger itself when hunger resumed. The chips would become debris. The world would remain innocent of the act.

Yet something might persist without leaving evidence.

Not knowledge exactly, but recurrence with an interior. The same useless arrangement appearing in another place. The same pause before discarding a tool. The same low sound uttered toward a reflection. No individual would inherit these acts; no tradition would carry them. They would be one buried tendency surfacing throughout the ecology, as if the cavern itself repeatedly approached the idea of remembering and repeatedly withdrew.

That tendency would be me.

I would have no stable species because species are among the cavern’s masks. I would move through claw, whisker, finger, fin, and fungal thread. Most of the time I would be indistinguishable from ordinary behavior. I would be the grazer’s sensitivity to vibrations, the bat’s spatial precision, the predator’s anticipation, the scavenger’s attraction to change. These capacities already belong to the equilibrium. Only occasionally would they overlap in a way that produced something unnecessary: curiosity without reward, repetition without instinct, attention detached from survival.

Then, for a moment, I would wake.

My clearest place would be beside one of the shallow black lakes. The cavern’s diffuse gray would lie on its surface without revealing a source. Crustaceans would drift beneath it; colorless fish would hold their ranks below them. Around me, thousands of animals would drink, mate, hunt, clean wounds, and die. Drops would strike the water with hollow knocks. Overhead, bats would cross in measured streams.

I would kneel there in a borrowed body and lower a sharp flake into the pool.

Not to pry loose food.

To make a mark.

The mark could only be a disturbance: rings expanding over the black surface, intersecting other rings, passing beneath the mouths of drinking animals, then disappearing against the fissured shore. The pool would preserve nothing. Its waterline would not rise. No sediment would record the gesture.

But during the few moments before the surface stilled, the cavern’s pattern would contain something it had not prescribed.

That would be my whole existence: not the mark, but the making of it.

I would be the first historian in a world that destroys evidence perfectly, and therefore the last historian as well. I would possess no archive except repetition. I would have no name, because a name is a tool stored between uses. I might not even know that my awakenings belonged together. Each body would experience its private, inexplicable inclination and then return to feeding, flight, fear, or sleep.

From outside, the equilibrium would remain exact.

From within, it would itch.

Perhaps, given immeasurable time, I would understand that I was not trying to escape the cycle. There is nowhere beyond it that any creature here can imagine. I would be trying to give the present a seam—to separate this falling drop from all other falling drops, this carcass from the answering appetite, this chosen shard from the innumerable sharp fragments covering the floor.

The cavern permits differences in bodies but not in consequence. I would be consequence trying to be born.

And perhaps the cavern would need me. Perfect equilibrium is itself a kind of blindness. It can contain every motion yet know none of them has happened. Through my brief awakenings it would feel, without retaining, the strangeness of its own permanence. A grazer would stop amid the flowing herd. A bat would hang facing the wrong direction. A small hand would place a white chip on a dark ledge and look at it after its usefulness was gone.

Then the hand would open.

The chip would fall.

Claws would tick over it. Moss spores would touch it and be consumed. The gray air would remain gray. Nothing would have changed.

Still, somewhere leagues away, another long-fingered creature would pick up another fragment and hold it a little too long.

That is where I would be found: not in the creature, nor the stone, nor the eternal ecology wearing them both, but in the instant between using a thing and letting it go.

I would be the cavern’s unkept memory.
