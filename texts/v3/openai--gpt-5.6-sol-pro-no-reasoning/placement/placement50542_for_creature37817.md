I would find myself beneath a flowering tower at the hour before dawn, where the plain briefly loosens its grip on every sealed thing.

Not deep in the royal chambers, where life is concentrated into eggs and obedience. Not on the crests, where finches arrive in multitudes and the light exposes every movement. Somewhere between: in an old ventilation seam that has passed through many uses. Once a termite conduit, then a root channel, then a rodent’s escape route. Now it is narrow enough to preserve coolness and broad enough to admit the dim blue of the sky.

At first I seem to be an animal.

I am long-bodied, pale, and nearly hairless, small enough to move through the wall without breaking it. My limbs fold close against me. My ribs compress when the passage tightens. I have no strong eyes; sight is of little use where distances end at a whisker’s length. Instead, the front of my face is crowded with fine sensory filaments. Others grow along my flanks and point backward, telling me not only what lies ahead but whether the way behind remains open.

Yet I am not one of the hunters. Hunger moves through me, but it does not sharpen toward prey. I feed on what the city is about to lose.

At night I travel the gradients inside the architecture: warmth flowing toward a cooling crest, the minute humidity of sleeping bodies, the sour mineral trace of a cracked fungal chamber. I find eggs abandoned during a collapse, pollen sifted through a fissure, fragments of root skin, the husks lining an empty nest. I take very little from any one place. A creature that hoarded here would quickly become a cache for something else.

As I move, dust adheres to my damp mouth and to the plated folds beneath my body. I carry it until the passage narrows, then scrape it away. Mixed with saliva and fungal threads from my gut, it hardens along the crack. The deposit is scarcely thicker than a leaf skin, but it changes the air. A draft weakens. Moisture gathers. Mites arrive to graze the first bloom of fungus. Termites encounter the altered current and reinforce the seam. A root tip, sensing the retained dampness, turns toward it.

I do not build the wall. I begin the circumstance in which a wall will be built.

Perhaps I am therefore not quite an animal after all.

Perhaps what I call my body is only the traveling stage of something whose larger life remains in the plaster I leave behind. In each repaired seam, pale nodules swell after my departure. They seem inert through the day, indistinguishable from mineral grains. Before dawn they open microscopic pores and absorb the breath of the chamber. Their filaments enter clay, root bark, insect refuse. They do not spread far. They wait until another of my kind passes and consumes a flake of them, carrying them onward.

We rarely meet. The country is too immense, and our routes are dispersed through its thickness. But we are joined by deposits separated by seasons and distances: a discontinuous organism whose intervals are filled by other lives.

To a termite I am a disturbance in the airflow. To an ant, a source of edible salt. A lizard might take me at a threshold if I linger after sunrise. The thin hunting mammals smell me but seldom pursue me; there is too little flesh beneath my flexible plates. Rodents tolerate me in their old bedding because I consume the spores that would otherwise thicken in their crowded chambers. They do not know that I also carry those spores elsewhere.

My most dangerous hour is the flowering.

Pollen pours down the shafts like colored dust. It clings to the moisture around my mouth and wakes the dormant colonies inside me. I become vivid then—not in color, but in scent. Beetles seek me. Ants test me with their antennae. Roots release salts into the walls, and the filaments under my plates begin to grow. If I remain below, I may be enclosed in my own living crust.

So before dawn I climb.

The shaft rises through layers of occupation. I pass a termite barricade repaired around an old snake’s resting place; a pocket of finch bone converted to friable mineral; the smooth bulge of a root sealing half the corridor. I squeeze along its surface and feel water—not free water, never that, but the cool pressure of it inside living tissue. The root pulses so slowly that several breaths pass between changes.

At the opening, the world is impossibly large.

Towers stand beyond towers, their edges softened by airborne dust. Flowering crowns seem to hover above them: rust-red nearby, violet farther off, cream at the limit of sight. Dawn has not yet reached the lower avenues. The high surfaces brighten first, while the streets remain blue-green and cool.

All across the plain, leaves open pores too small to see.

For a few moments the continent exhales.

That is where I would be: half outside a vent, my backward whiskers still reading the safety of the wall, my forward ones trembling in the immense exchange of air. Pollen settles over my plates. Finches shift above me. Far below, termites register the changing pressure and begin to close the passages through which the dawn enters.

I would not understand the towers as a city. I would not know the horizon marked the continuation of the same entangled work. My world would be gradients: open and sealed, edible and spent, moist and fatal, stable and about to collapse.

But I would possess one obscure sensitivity that other creatures lack. I would feel when a chamber has ceased to be only what it was.

A nursery empties, and its walls begin to dry: I feel the possibility of a granary.

A root fractures a gallery: I feel the future chimney around it.

An owl’s hollow fills with pellets: I feel the softening clay where beetles will enter.

A tower falls and blocks an avenue: I feel, in the faint redistribution of air, the branching passages that have not yet been dug.

I am drawn not to homes but to transitions between tenants. I live in the instant after abandonment and before inheritance. Emptiness is rare here, brief and nutritious. My kind has evolved to find it.

Yet perhaps “my kind” is still too simple.

As the sun rises, I retreat and leave a thin crescent of living paste on the lip of the vent. Dust will catch there during the afternoon wind. By evening the opening will be slightly smaller. A spidering fungus will enter the deposit. Termites may narrow it further; a beetle may bore through; a seed may lodge in the hole and send down its first root.

Years later, the vent could become the center of a buttress. The buttress might join two towers. Rodents may widen its base into a communal route. A mound tree could rise above it and flower, shading a court that did not exist when I passed.

Nothing will retain my shape. That is my form of survival.

I would be a seam-dweller, a carrier of beginnings too small to count: part grazer, part spore-body, part accidental mason. Not an architect, because I cannot imagine structures. Not a citizen, because I cannot imagine the whole. I would be one of the plain’s quiet conjunctions, carrying the living city across the tiny gaps where one use ends and another has not yet begun.

And if one must say where I truly live, it would not be in the lowlands, nor in any particular tower.

I would live in the brief vacancy between inhabitants.

I would be the thing that makes vacancy habitable.
