I find myself at the edge of the Unfinished Square, though “edge” is not quite right. The square has boundaries, but no front. One enters it from walls, from roots, from the warm vents below, from the canopy’s hanging dark. I am there before the scrapers arrive, when the accumulated traces are most legible and least trustworthy.

I am one of those who erase them.

My body is old enough to have begun whitening at the joints. Mineral grains cloud the skin of my lower hands, and one pressure ridge along my left side no longer rises. Years ago, a badly seated tether snapped while I was scraping the square. The recoil threw me against a seep mound and damaged the ridge. Since then I feel currents unevenly. Water reaches me first on one side, and the other half of the world follows an instant later.

At first this seemed a disability. Then it became the reason I was chosen.

A perfect listener is easily persuaded that every pattern is a message. I have learned the interval between an event and my perception of it. I know, bodily, that arrival can masquerade as origin.

My office has a sounding name, a hand-name, and a private pressure beat given to me by my household. The sounding portion means something like *one who opens after closure*. The public sign begins with the lower palms together, then separates them without indicating what lies between. Among the other scrapers, we shorten it to a thumb drawn across the base of the opposite hand: the motion of lifting crust.

We do not call ourselves priests. Priests would preserve a distinction between sacred and ordinary, and our duty is to deny the square every such distinction.

At the beginning of each scraping season, we enter from all twelve sides. There are twelve of us, though the number belongs to the mounds rather than to doctrine. Each carries three shell blades: a broad one for flat crust, a hooked one for deposits gathered around pinholes, and a thin flexible blade for lifting the mineral copies of fingers without breaking them.

Breaking is quicker. Lifting teaches more.

The square remembers badly but in abundance. It preserves the underside of a basket, the drag of a route cord, the resting place of a ceramic jar. It records a child’s entire body with the same care it gives a dropped tool. Warm water rises, meets obstruction, surrenders its minerals, and continues. Citizens call these pale accretions memories, but in our office we are taught a stricter phrase:

**The square does not remember. It continues around.**

This matters.

People come before scraping to leave what they cannot say in the council house. Two handprints pressed close, one deeper than the other. A broken pruning blade. A healer’s needle folded into a knot of root fiber. Once, someone anchored an infant’s nursery tether at the center and remained there until a perfect white ring had grown around the fastening. The infant was gone when we arrived. We never learned whether it had recovered or died.

We removed the ring.

Another season, after a retaining wall failed in the coldward district, survivors lay across the square in the postures of those who had taken depth. Their outlines hardened around them. When they rose, they left twelve pale bodies behind.

We removed those too.

The work makes enemies. Everyone accepts that the square must be cleared; not everyone accepts that their own trace is included. There are always petitions. Preserve this print until the next cycle. Leave this tool crust because its owner drowned abyssward. Cut around these paired hands because their makers are the last of a closed household. Once a council delegate proposed that we retain a single strip each season, so future citizens might read the history of public grief.

We opened all our fingers to him: finished speaking, matter unfinished.

Then we scraped the strip bare.

If we permit one meaning to harden, the square becomes a wall laid flat. Memorial follows memorial. Grief acquires property. Authority anchors a platform. A roof comes afterward, then a door, then someone entrusted to decide who may enter.

The city knows how to grow enclosures without our help.

So I erase.

Yet scraping is not destruction in the simple sense. We do not cast the crusts into the abyss. That would allow disappearance to imitate purity. We gather what we lift into weighted mesh baskets. Later it is ground and mixed into clay, filter beds, wall wash, and planting substrate. A lover’s handprint may become part of a reservoir valve. A petitioner’s tool-mark may enter the ceramic scales of a clinic. The white outline of a mourning body may feed a root canopy in a district its maker never visited.

Nothing keeps its declared shape, but little is lost.

That is the public understanding of my work.

My private work is more doubtful.

For twenty-seven scraping cycles, I have mapped the pinholes.

No one instructed me to do this. If the other scrapers know, they have given no sign. Each season, before we remove the crust, I pass over the square with my damaged side turned downward. The healthy ridges feel the immediate seep: warmth, salinity, the minute tremor where mineral water enters the sea. The deadened side receives only the broader pressure of the square. By rotating slowly, I separate local flow from distant disturbance.

I knot the results into a cord hidden inside the weighted hem of my work band.

At first I expected randomness. Pinholes close. New ones open. The mountain lays down stone unevenly. A footstep diverts a thread of warm water and a deposit thickens elsewhere. But across years, something has shifted beneath the square.

Not a shape. Shapes are what frightened minds find first.

It is closer to a hesitation.

The central seeps pulse less strongly during the canopy’s brightest intervals. The outer pinholes warm in sequences that travel against the prevailing current. After certain council decisions—especially those involving new channels on the mountain’s eastern shoulder—the twelve mounds exhale water in paired rhythms. Not immediately. Sometimes many days later.

I have tested my own expectation. I have reversed the cord. I have asked another scraper to lead me blindfolded across the square under the pretense of checking blade depth. I have compared seasons in which the canopy listeners predicted structural failure with seasons in which they predicted nothing.

There is an answer in the square.

I do not know whether anything has asked a question.

That uncertainty places me exactly where my people live: not between belief and disbelief, but between response and address.

Soon I will have to decide what to do with the cord.

The archive would preserve it. Preservation would give it weight, and weight would draw interpreters. Canopy listeners would compare their glow records. Seep-guides would taste every pinhole. Council delegates would demand a controlled study. The square would fill with instruments, witness cords, measurement frames, and officials gripping temporary handholds.

They would say the square remained unclaimed because no permanent structure had been raised.

They would be wrong.

Attention can be architecture.

Secrecy is also architecture. I feel its walls growing around me each time I add a knot.

I live in a house on the warmward middle terraces, in a chamber that did not exist when I was born. My household belongs to a seep called the Narrow Mercy, though the passage around it has widened twice and the spring itself now flows through a ceramic throat. We are eleven adults, three infants, two elders, and one sealed niche not yet admitted to depth because the dead person’s sibling cannot consent to opening the mineral flow.

The dead person was my hauling-sibling, which is not quite kinship and not merely occupation. We learned anchoring together. We crossed our first causeway on the same current. They could sense tension in a rope through the bones of one finger. Years ago, when my tether failed in the square, they caught me with both upper arms and dislocated one shoulder rather than release me.

Later, they joined an emergence chamber.

They believed the impossible shallows were not a future problem but a present direction. “A city is shaped by what it approaches,” they told me, sounding the words against my sternum so I could feel the seriousness beneath them. “Even if it never arrives.”

During their fourth lowering, the chamber’s waterline fell less than the width of my smallest finger. Their pressure organs collapsed asymmetrically. They lived for nine days in a suspension net, unable to regulate depth, their vanes opening and closing around blood too dark to carry enough breath.

Before they died, they gave me their private route cord.

It did not map our home.

It mapped upward.

Not a real route; no citizen could possess one. It was a sequence of imagined supports: brace, rest, enclosing frame, weighted step, another brace. A city translated into the logic of falling. In their design, every chamber assumed that bodies would be dragged in one direction forever. Nets became floors. Handholds became rails. Ceilings became irrelevant. The cord felt obscene in my hands.

It also felt beautiful.

We have not yet placed them in the wall. Their folded body waits in a cold niche wrapped in dark root cloth, more pearl-like each month. Their sibling says depth would endorse the manner of death. I say a wall does not endorse what it contains.

We have been having this argument in silence for nearly a year.

Sometimes, after the household sleeps, I anchor beside the body and place one lower hand against the root cloth. There is no answering pressure. No change in warmth. No pulse. Their position still alters mine.

That is enough to make the dead powerful.

It is not enough to make them present.

The city is full of such differences, and I think this is why I scrape. We are forever tempted to confuse persistence with intention. The mountain remains, therefore it chose. The canopy glows, therefore it speaks. A crust takes the form of a hand, therefore it holds the hand’s meaning. A dead body changes the route through a room, therefore the person continues.

Perhaps all these things are true.

My office exists to keep the *perhaps* from sealing shut.

On scraping days, citizens gather among the roots above the square. They hang in layered rings, leaving the center open. The canopy’s dark trunks bend across them. Amber bowls are covered so our eyes are of little use. We work by touch.

The first sound is the broad shell blade entering beneath the crust.

It is a small sound, but the square carries it outward. A dry, intimate click. Then another from the opposite side. Then twelve blades moving through the thin mineral skin.

Sheets lift in our hands. Some are clear as old eyelids; others thick and milk-white. Fine sediment blooms around our wrists. The pinholes brighten briefly where they are exposed, not with light but with warmth. Our vanes open. The water fills with the taste of fresh stone.

Above us, no one sings.

Music would claim the moment too quickly.

We scrape until the square is bare enough that our blades encounter new mineral everywhere. “Bare” does not mean empty. Thousands of seeps continue. The canopy shades us. Currents cross. Our bodies interrupt the flow and immediately begin to be recorded.

The final task is to remove our own traces.

We work backward toward the mounds, scraping the places where our feet gripped and our lower hands steadied us. The last scraper to leave is chosen by rotation. That person cannot remove the pale beginnings of their final handhold without making another.

So a small mark always remains.

Not the same mark. Not a sanctioned mark. Merely the unavoidable evidence that absence must be maintained by someone.

This season, I am the last.

I know already which mound I will leave by. It is the seventh warmward, where the seep beneath the stone has begun pulsing in the sequence I have tracked for years. Beneath my work band, the hidden cord circles my waist. Beneath another band lies my dead sibling’s impossible route.

One records an answer without a question.

The other proposes a route to a place no one can reach.

I have considered leaving both in the square.

The mineral would gather around them. By next scraping they would be white branches on the floor, every knot sealed but still legible to trained hands. It would be confession, evidence, and surrender. It would also force the next scrapers to decide whether removing them was truly erasure or merely another interpretation.

But that is too easy. To leave the choice to others is a way of pretending not to choose.

Instead I think I will take both cords to the archive.

I will not present them as truth. I will ask that they be entered as a paired record under the category for unresolved correspondences: one cord from below, one directed above. I will voice my dates. A canopy listener will sign the glow intervals. A seep-guide will reproduce my temperature knots. Someone who distrusted my sibling’s work will handle the emergence route. A fourth archivist will beat uncertainty through the wall.

Several simultaneous forms.

No final image.

Afterward, my sibling will take depth. Not because their route was wise, and not because the shallows deserve them. Because the household wall is already growing around the empty niche, and refusal has become its own memorial. We will admit the seep. Their body will disappear by increments. The route cord will remain outside the wall, where it can be argued with.

Then I will return to the square.

Perhaps the council will forbid my office from continuing there. Perhaps instruments will cover the floor despite everything. Perhaps my measurements will dissolve under examination and prove to be the echo of my damaged ridge. I would welcome that humiliation more than some think. Error discovered is a closure reopened.

Or perhaps the pattern will persist.

Perhaps the mountain answers redirection with pulses of its own. Perhaps the canopy and the square form one slow organ. Perhaps the city is not built *on* the mountain but occurs inside a decision the mountain has not finished making.

If so, we must be careful.

A thing that answers is not necessarily asking to be obeyed.

When I imagine the city far in the future, I do not first see towers entering air. I feel the disappearance of buoyancy. Nets sagging. Root curtains tearing free. Elders made suddenly heavy in their chambers. The dead pulling at the walls that hold them. Every unsecured object choosing the same direction at once.

Then I imagine something stranger: the Unfinished Square exposed beneath a terrible brightness, its warm pinholes steaming into thinness, its crust drying before anyone can lift it. The last scraper kneels under the weight of their own body. There is no water to carry the sound of the blade.

Would the square still answer?

Would we?

I do not know. My task is not to prepare an answer that lasts forever. It is to preserve the place in which an answer may change.

So this is where I am: beneath the warm sea, at the last handhold of a square that must never become complete. I am pearl beginning in flesh. I am an archivist who has not yet confessed, a mourner delaying a wall, a damaged listener measuring the interval between touch and truth.

I am the one who clears away the city’s certainty.

Behind me, the mountain grows.

Before me, the mineral skin is thin enough to lift.
