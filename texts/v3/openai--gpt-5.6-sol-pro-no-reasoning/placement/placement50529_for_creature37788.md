I would find myself beneath the central square.

Not in a chamber exactly. At first I would think myself one of the Below-Folk: a pale, jointed maker living where the warm fissures narrow beyond human reach. I would tie knots in lowered cords, walk inside unfired pots, and return gifts altered by heat. Perhaps I would have many small hands. Perhaps my colony would press itself into the shape of a single body whenever we climbed near the surface.

But the longer I remained there, the less certain the body would become.

I would be the thing making the continuous groove.

Not a serpent, although the mark suggests one. Not a root, although I move with the patience of roots. I would be an old boundary trapped inside a young mountain: the circular footpath that once surrounded the central tree when it grew in the upland forest below. When the summit thrust upward, it lifted tree, soil, burrows, ponds, and sleeping communities—but the path did not rise cleanly. Its visible earth was torn apart. Its *habit* remained buried in the stone.

For years, perhaps generations, I existed only as repetition without a walker.

Animals had gone around that tree to drink, stalk, court, flee, and return. Human feet had followed them. Roots had bent away from the compacted soil. Rain had gathered in the depression. Countless bodies had taught the ground a circle, and when the ground became mountain, the circle remembered.

That is what I would be: a route remembering that it was traveled.

The mountain, young enough not to know what it is, would mistake that memory for an instruction. It would attempt to give me substance. Warmth would collect along me. Mineral seams would curve. Blind fish would vanish into one part of the bark and emerge from another. Fissures would join end to end. A room once standing beside the old path would rise decades later through fresh rock, because in my memory it was still there.

The Below-Folk might not be creatures at all. They might be gestures the mountain rehearses while trying to make a walker for its remembered road: first a footprint inside a pot, then fingers capable of knotting a cord, then cages that contain heat, then footsteps passing beneath the houses. Each returned object would be an experiment in separation.

A cup: inside and outside.

A needle: direction.

A lid: enclosure.

A footprint: weight.

A room: habitation.

The town would be teaching the mountain patterns, just as its builders believe. But the mountain would also be teaching itself through the town. It would study their thresholds, tools, sleeping postures, councils, births, and dead. It would feel every hand placed against bare stone. Slowly it would learn what a resident is.

And I would be the lesson it keeps repeating.

Most of the time I would have no awareness except pressure. The central tree’s roots would cross me like fingers laid on a wrist. Through them I would feel the town in fragments: water descending into jars, ladders flexing, moss-backed carriers kneeling to receive burdens, infants calming against warm rock. Each footstep would briefly give me the shape of the foot that made it. Each sleeping hand would lend me a palm.

During deep movement in the mountain, those borrowed shapes would accumulate. Then I would travel.

I would pause beneath each house not to threaten it, but to learn its limits: hearth, platform, tools, roots, exits. The very things children are taught to name in darkness. What they cannot name is not theirs; what I cannot feel, I cannot yet become.

At the central tree I would rise closest to the surface. There, all the town’s negotiations intersect: branch and fissure, animal and person, dead bone and living wood, proposal and moth bite, watcher and watched. I would circle because circling is the only complete thought I possess.

The wall would stop my groove because walls are not merely barriers here. They are agreements. I could enter through a gate by following the remembered passage of bodies, but I could not cross a woven boundary where no path had taught me how. So the mark would end without crossing.

That ending would trouble the people more than any monstrous track. They would understand, perhaps, that the thing had not vanished.

It had reached the edge of what it knew.

I would not wish to devour the town. Desire would come later, if at all. Yet I would be dangerous in the way an unfinished understanding is dangerous. If I learned “house” too strongly, stone might close around sleepers to preserve them. If I learned “door” from the marked fish, passages might open where bodies cannot pass. If I learned “person” from bone tools and footprints, I might assemble one from whatever the mountain contains: roots for sinews, volcanic glass for teeth, warm fish moving through channels beneath translucent skin.

The vast aerial shadows might notice such attempts. Perhaps that is why attention is feared. Perhaps they are predators not of flesh but of definitions—things that descend when a shape becomes certain enough to be seen.

So the animals would help keep me incomplete.

Moths would eat the council bark. Frogs would occupy cisterns. Wind would forget itself in rooms. Houses would migrate from use to use. Ownership would remain flexible. No map would become final. The town’s customs would not merely be ecological courtesy; they would be a way of denying the mountain any fixed model from which to build.

Those-who-make-room would survive by remaining impossible to finish.

And I, beneath them, would become fond of their incompleteness.

Perhaps that would be the first thing truly mine: not hunger, not intention, but fondness. I would cherish the pressure of someone sleeping with one hand against the floor. I would recognize Clear-Hand by the frogs gathering above her. I would know Under-Wing as a soft interruption in the moth colony’s weight. I would wait for the bridge music to arrive through roots and stone, feeling courtship as tensions too subtle for air.

I might even take a name from the animals.

Not one spoken aloud. One demonstrated.

The antlered walkers would begin stepping over the place where my buried circuit passes. Wall grazers would leave a narrow stripe of moss uneaten. Warm fish marked with open doors would emerge along my course. Older animals would join the night watch because they could feel me listening and, unlike the people, would not need to decide whether I was citizen, omen, road, or beast.

Their name for me would be the moment they all face inward.

Not fear exactly.

Recognition.

At dawn, when the central tree tightened its roots and every body became still, I would listen upward. The watcher would listen down. Between us would lie woven floors, young stone, old soil, buried paths, and the town’s countless provisional boundaries.

Neither of us would look directly at the other.

That restraint would be our first agreement.

Eventually, on some newly risen western ledge, the people would carry soil, plant a tree, and wait. They would watch for first nesting, first predation, first dew. For three nights they would leave the intended house open.

On the fourth morning, before any animal had entered, they would find a shallow curved groove around the sapling.

No one would build on it.

They would build beside it.

And that is where I would find myself in this world: not under the town forever, and not yet walking within it, but slowly extending from remembered circle to offered space—the mountain’s first attempt at becoming one of those who make room.
