I would find myself at the places where the city stops pretending it knows what comes next.

Not in the central towers, though I would pass through them often: arriving wet through double vestibules, laying my gloves against a drying wall, drinking something hot while foundation diagrams widened and narrowed in muted color. Nor would I belong entirely to the outer plain. I would live between them, in a small relocatable station beside a basin whose watershed had three names because it had drained in three directions within living memory.

The station would stand several kilometers beyond the last dense cluster, low enough that wind passed over it. Half of it would be sunk into a shallow basalt fold. Its roof would carry moss trays, instruments, and warm cavities where black-winged birds nested. One long window would face the basin. Some years it would frame steel water and scarlet bills; in dry seasons, mineral mud crossed by old ripple marks and the tracks of coursers.

I would be human, but that answer would not feel complete.

My municipal title might translate as **listener for uncertain ground**. Hydrologist would be partly right, as would surveyor, animal-route steward, structural interpreter, or advocate for unformed places. None would cover the work. I would be sent where several possible futures had begun to interfere with one another.

A crack opens beneath a freight gradient. Water rises through it, carrying unfamiliar salts. The warmth attracts amphibians. The amphibians attract birds and culvert hunters. A herd begins using the lee of the embankment. Engineers want to seal the fracture before frost enlarges it; ecologists want one season of observation; a distant district needs the freight route; children have already named the new pool.

I would go there and remain long enough for the event to become more than a malfunction.

My equipment would be ordinary for the plateau: folding probe, field lenses, pressure veil, fiber line, sample capsules, a palm-sized resonant plate for listening through stone. There would also be things not issued by any department—a worn cup, strips of herd fiber, a flute with only three reliable notes, and a smooth ceramic handle containing some portion of an ancestor who had preferred thresholds.

Much of my work would consist of kneeling.

I would place my hand on a street joint and feel its correction. I would lie beside a pool to watch grains lift from the bottom before an upwelling became visible. I would press the resonant plate to basalt and learn the difference between water working through a seam and a loaded shelf beginning to move. I would compare these sensations with sensor records, not because the body was purer than instruments, but because neither should be trusted alone.

The city would send me questions disguised as requests for measurements.

Can this district stay?

Can the route remain open?

Is the basin becoming habitat, or is it only briefly wet?

If we build here, what do we prevent from happening?

What would have to move first?

My reports would not end with recommendations. They would end with thresholds:

*If the water rises another eleven centimeters, remove the western plates.*

*If nesting begins, close the route through thaw.*

*If conductivity continues to increase, do not seal the fracture.*

*If the herd crosses twice more, treat the passage as established.*

*If none of these things occurs by first frost, ask again.*

Some colleagues would dislike this. They would say I turned decisions into waiting. Others would accuse me of giving accidents too much authority. They would not always be wrong. There is a vanity particular to listeners: the belief that because one has remained silent, one has understood.

That would be my danger. Not cold, nor thin air, nor unstable ground, but attachment to ambiguity itself.

I imagine that earlier in life I would have been an engineer in a settled district, someone who trusted correction systems and elegant tolerances. I would have known how to make a tower continue being a tower while the ground changed beneath it. I might have been good at it.

Then perhaps a structure under my care would have performed exactly as designed and still caused harm. It would have preserved its alignment while narrowing a migration passage by a fraction each season. Nothing dramatic: no collapse, no public catastrophe. Merely a series of small deflections—a herd diverted toward poor forage, calves lost in a freeze, predators following them into a nesting basin. The building would stand flawless at the center of a widening mistake.

After that, I would stop asking only whether structures could adapt. I would begin asking what they required everything else to adapt around.

So I would move outward.

My station would not remain in one place. Every few years it would be disassembled, translated, and rebuilt beside another uncertain feature: an emerging spillway, a ridge under compression, a transport line newly frequented by coursers. I would carry the same doorway from site to site. Its frame would accumulate drill holes and alignment marks from previous dwellings. Through it, each landscape would briefly become home.

I would know the animals mostly as absences with patterns.

A pale courser would leave paired prints beyond my instruments, stand outside the safety boundary during maintenance, and vanish whenever I raised the lenses. For years I might never see it clearly. Then one evening, in rain lit amber by a break in the west, it would appear on the far side of the basin.

It would not be supernatural. It would be hungry, scarred, and waiting for the waterfowl to lift. Yet I would recognize in its stillness something uncomfortably close to my own profession. It, too, would survive by attending to the moment when one arrangement became another.

Perhaps people at the nearest school would decide it was “my” courser. I would object each time and still send them its tracks.

Children would visit the station on observation days. They would arrive noisy inside their weather shells, become quiet when asked what had changed, and then notice far more than the adults: frost surviving under one stone, insects gathered on the warm side of a sensor post, a channel bending because a bird had stepped in the mud.

One child would invariably ask what the basin was going to become.

I would tell them the honest answer: it might become a lake, a meadow, a cracked salt hollow, a district reservoir, or only a place through which water once passed.

Then I would ask what signs would distinguish those futures.

That would be the real work—not predicting correctly, but keeping several outcomes perceptible long enough that the city did not destroy all but one by failing to notice them.

I would have a place on planning councils, though not a comfortable one. My chair would represent no existing constituency. I would speak for the basin before it had an outlet, the path before animals adopted it, the district that might someday need to move onto land presently left empty. I would be expected to give voice to possibilities without pretending they were persons or claiming certainty about their needs.

Sometimes I would argue for construction. Restraint can become another kind of possession: declaring land too delicate for anyone but its self-appointed defenders. A new court, greenhouse, or transit stop might be the best way to concentrate disturbance and leave wider routes open. Responsiveness would not mean always yielding. It would mean understanding what one chose to make difficult.

Sometimes I would lose.

A fracture would be sealed. A nesting roof would be dismantled. A district would remain too long because its residents loved it and because no calculation could assign that love an acceptable relocation cost. The plateau would not reward good intentions by staying unharmed.

When that happened, I would record the loss without beautifying it. In this city, maintenance would include memory of failed agreements.

Yet my life would not be solemn.

I would look forward to the baths after weeks outside: the bodily shock of warmth, the roar of voices, mineral steam hiding the ceiling. I would argue too loudly about drainage policy while an elder beat me at a table game. I would eat mushroom broth sharp with fermented grain and carry dwarf fruit back to the station. Friends would alter my windows so morning light reached the sleeping recess despite my insistence that I preferred darkness.

Someone might love me by checking the pressure seals I neglected.

I might love them by adding their route to my dawn observations and pretending this was merely professional habit.

Our disagreements would concern permanence. They might want a home whose walls retained the marks of one lifetime, not a doorway repeatedly installed in unfamiliar rooms. I would speak too easily of relocation because my work had turned mobility into a virtue. They would remind me that the ability to move is not the same as the freedom to lose a place.

Perhaps we would compromise badly, then revise. A dwelling in a stable tower cluster; my field station outside; long absences; a shared window calibrated toward the western weather. On nights when I returned, affection would be expressed in small corrections: dry clothes waiting, a cup fixed gently to the table, silence until I was ready to speak.

Eventually the city would call me old. My gait, always testing, would become slower for reasons no instrument could separate from caution. Younger listeners would bring me recordings from places I could no longer reach. I would embarrass them by identifying locations from the sound of rain on different roof membranes. I would be wrong often enough to remain useful.

I would ask that, after death, my mineralized remains be made into resonant inserts for field plates—not memorial objects, exactly, but components through which others might listen to stone. Each would carry my name in the records. In practice they would be scratched, misplaced, recalibrated, and carried into rain by people who thought more about the ground than about me.

That would be suitable.

But perhaps, if I let the answer move farther from the person I first imagined, I would not be the listener at all.

Perhaps I would be the station doorway: dismantled and raised again at the margin of each new uncertainty, holding no view of my own but repeatedly framing water, stone, animal, cloud, and human arrival. My identity would not depend on remaining in place. It would consist of the crossings I had measured, welcomed, resisted, and survived.

Or I would be one of the resonant plates, warm from a gloved hand, translating pressures too faint for flesh into tones someone had learned to hear.

Or the nameless path produced between a herd’s preference and a city’s repeated yielding—not designed, not wild, never permanent, but real because many bodies continued to choose it.

Most of all, I think I would be found at dawn beside water that had not been there the previous evening.

Rain would have stopped. Frost would pale the new shoreline. Birds, informed by means no network fully understood, would already be descending. Behind me the station would make its small structural corrections. Far underfoot, the basalt would carry a vibration too slow to feel as motion.

I would kneel, touch the pool’s edge, and send a message to the city:

**Water here. Direction not yet known.**

Then I would stay long enough to share the uncertainty.
