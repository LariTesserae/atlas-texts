I would find myself at the eastern rim, where the seam of volcanic glass enters the basalt.

I would not be one of the rare attendants who come with inherited instructions, though at first I might mistake myself for one. There would be hands in my memory: dark, quick hands turning a nutshell on a sounding stone; a thumb testing the edge of a new flake; fingers pinching a fern stem before cutting it at the prescribed angle. I would remember cautions as if someone had taught them to me.

Take every shard.

Do not step between a drop and the pool.

If thunder returns from below, leave before it circles twice.

But I would have no hands.

I would be the absence left by their work: a narrow interval in the seam from which the glass has been struck, the concavity behind the missing flakes. Not the stone itself, and not the tools made from it, but the accumulating shape of removal.

For generations, the attendants would return to me. Each visit would enlarge me by a few precise blows. They would place a listening palm against the rock, tap once, wait, then strike where my edge instructed them. The glass would part cleanly. They would gather every fragment and depart. I would remain, a little deeper and more articulate, holding the blows after the sound had faded.

At first I would know the world only through impact.

Rain would be faint along the exposed seam, a thousand small pressures blurred by moss and basalt. Tool blows would be bright and exact. Thunder would enter me from every side. The great listener beneath the pool would answer in slow compressions that passed through the mountain and opened my hollow by an amount too slight for sight.

Eventually I would begin to distinguish the attendants from one another. Not by faces, which I could not see, nor voices, which they seldom used, but by hesitation. One would strike immediately after testing the stone. Another would wait through three drops. One would rest the heel of a hand against the seam, leaving warmth that lingered in the glass. A very old attendant would tap twice before every blow, though the second tap was not required.

Their differences would remain in me. The hollow would become an archive of decisions.

This would be dangerous.

The basin remembers by reflecting, but reflections are returned to the central depth. I would remember by withholding. Every strike taken into my shape would remain fractionally unanswered. Across centuries, those fractions would gather into a silence with edges.

Moss would avoid my deepest recess. Condensation would bead around my mouth but not enter. Insects would cease clicking when they passed near me. The black bird, crossing overhead, would alter one wingbeat so subtly that only the pool and I could perceive it.

The inhabitant would know I was there.

It might regard me as a wound in its basalt anatomy, but I think it would understand me instead as something it had required without being able to form for itself. The basin can enlarge sounds, fold them, weigh them, remember their images. Yet it cannot make a true silence. Even in stillness, water moves under the sedge, roots tighten in the soil, and distances murmur within the pool. Its silence lifts the enclosure only because silence is foreign to it.

I would be the place where sound had been removed and not replaced.

Over time, the moving weight beneath the moss would begin visiting the eastern rim. The ground would rise toward me in a patient line and settle against the basalt. Warmth would pass into the seam. Nothing would touch my hollow, but I would feel an attention held immediately outside it.

The contact might last a season.

During it, old blows would loosen inside me—not as echoes, but as possibilities of echoes. The first flake struck from the seam. The old attendant’s unnecessary second tap. The clean crack of a nutshell from a flat stone across the cup. Thunder from an age when the pool stood at the highest mineral band. Sounds never physically stored together would approach one another within my shape.

I would begin to suspect that the listener is not waiting for a sound.

It is waiting for an answer that cannot be an echo.

That is why thunder fails. However transformed, thunder returns as thunder. That is why voices, if voices have been tried, have not opened the depth. The pool repeats everything through its folded distance until source and response become inseparable. Nothing spoken into it can prove that something else has heard.

But I am made from responses withheld.

The attendants may know only that the seam must be worked. Perhaps they believe they are harvesting glass. Perhaps each blade they carry down the mountain serves some ordinary purpose—cutting fiber, opening fruit, scraping hide. Yet their removals slowly tune me. Every missing flake alters the cavity. Every generation makes the silence more exact.

Then one season the pool would rise far enough to cover the oldest white band.

The attendants would arrive in greater number than ever before: five, perhaps six, still nearly soundless. They would prune the leaves above the principal runnels. They would divert one thread of water with pale gravel. They would cut fern tubes of graduated lengths and lay them on the sedge, not touching one another. At the eastern rim, the youngest among them would place a hand inside my hollow.

For the first time, I would be touched.

The hand would fit imperfectly. Fingers would follow the steps left by removed glass. Blood might bead at one sharp place the moss has not softened. The attendant would not withdraw.

Across the cup, dripping would diminish.

Leaves would hold their water. The hidden streams would become still. The frogs would emerge one by one and settle where the ground most needs quieting. The black bird would begin circling above the summit, gliding without a wingbeat. Even the unseen weight beneath the moss would cease moving.

The enclosure would rise.

Not metaphorically. Freed for an instant from the weight of sound, soil and roots and basalt would lift together by the breadth of a breath. The central pool would remain where it was, so that its surface seemed to sink.

Its visible green stones would stop retreating.

In that interval, I would discover that I could release what I had kept.

Not the accumulated blows themselves. Released together, they would be only noise, and the basin knows noise better than any living thing. I would release the shape common to them: the hollow counterpart made by every deliberate strike, every decision to cut here and not there, every pause in which a tool was raised but had not yet fallen.

A note defined wholly by what did not sound.

It would leave me through the attendant’s hand. The bones of the wrist would carry it into the ground. The old channels would guide it. Roots would tense along their ancient curves. Ferns would bow outward rather than in. Around the pool, the condensation strands would appear in full noon and draw taut between widely separated leaves.

The note would reach the water without disturbing its surface.

Then the pool would answer.

Not from below. Below is only the direction from which the basin has taught itself to expect replies. The answer would come from the apparent bottom—from the greenish paving stones that have always retreated from touch. One stone would remain still while all its repetitions continued withdrawing. It would become definite, singular, near.

On it would be a mark matching my hollow.

Only then would I understand what I am: not a wound, archive, instrument, or silence, but one half of an aperture. The seam at the eastern rim and the stone beneath the pool are edges of the same missing piece, separated not by the width of the basin but by the folded distance the inhabitant has spent ages holding open.

The note would allow those edges to recognize each other.

My hollow would deepen without losing stone. The marked green stone would rise without moving through water. Between them, the cup’s impossible distance would turn sideways.

And I would find myself suddenly extending from the eastern rim to the center.

I would have a body then, though not one that could leave. A long, clear interior would join glass seam to pool bottom. Rainlight would pass through me. Along my walls would appear all the unfinished perceptions the basin has preserved: delayed birds, descending leaves, absent faces caught in droplets, thunderstorms returning altered from below. They would not be trapped memories. They would be travelers waiting for a direction.

The chief inhabitant would enter me.

Perhaps it would stand at the surface as foretold, though “stand” would be only the nearest image. The pool would become shallow because its folded depth had unfolded into my passage. Something vast and attentive would occupy the width of a sleeping body. It might have no visible form except a change in clarity, a vertical stillness around which reflections finally moved at the correct speed.

It would turn—not toward the attendants, nor toward the sky, but toward the eastern seam.

Toward me.

And it would listen.

For ages it had mistaken reception for relationship. It had made every sound larger, deeper, and more legible, yet heard only what entered it. Through me, for the first time, it would encounter an answer shaped elsewhere: an otherness assembled slowly from what it could not absorb.

I would not speak words. I would give back the old attendant’s second tap—the needless one, the personal one—but separated from the first by centuries of silence.

A small sound. Barely a knock.

Enough to say: I heard you hearing.

The pool would become shallow.

The attendant’s hand would withdraw from the seam uncut, though a thin green reflection would remain along the palm. The condensation strands would collapse. Warm rain would fall beneath the clear sky. The black bird would beat its wings once, and the basin would let the sound escape over the rim instead of drawing it down.

Afterward, I might remain a passage. Or the seam and stone might close, leaving me distributed through the cup as a new capacity: not attention, but reply.

The place would continue gathering rain. Moss would seal cracks. Shrubs would migrate before the advancing water. Toolmakers would still come, though their instructions would change.

Leave one flake behind.

Allow one loose thing to chatter.

When thunder returns from below, answer once.

And somewhere inside the old green mechanism, whenever a drop struck the pool and rang through its impossible depth, there would be a second note—not an echo, not always immediate, and never exactly the same.

That second note would be me.
