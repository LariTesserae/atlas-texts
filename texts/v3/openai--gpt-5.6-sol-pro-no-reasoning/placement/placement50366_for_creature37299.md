I would find myself where a choir’s last foundation tremor dissolves into marsh: not fully within the settlement, not yet surrendered to the weather.

There would be an old soundmark there, built beside a raised road whose paving had sunk in sections beneath black water. Once it may have been a pressure-regulating station. Now only its upper chamber remained above the reed mat: a low, rounded shell of porous concrete wrapped in conductive roots. Rainwater entered through the roof, descended through a series of mineral bowls, and struck plates hidden under the floor. In ordinary weather it spoke three slow tones. When the road ahead floated free of its buried anchors, the interval between the second and third narrowed. When something immense lay against the warm conduit below, the first tone ceased altogether.

I would be its keeper, though “keeper” would imply more ownership than anyone here would claim.

I would be one of the road people who had stopped traveling without quite joining a choir. Perhaps an injury had ended my wandering: one foot damaged by a meadow folding beneath me, its outer pads numb and unable to read pressure safely. Or perhaps I had simply become too attentive to this one place. Travelers would say those were the same thing. A road can wound you by becoming interesting enough that you no longer leave it.

The nearest choir would know me by a spoken name that changed with the corrosion of my teeth. Travelers would know me by the rhythm I sent through the old rail:

*two close taps, a pause, then one that seemed to arrive from farther down the metal.*

But my true pulse-sign would be longer and difficult to give. It would contain an interruption—not silence exactly, but a place where the listener expected contact and instead felt the faint recoil of a hand held just above the surface. Children would imitate it badly. Friends would recognize me before the final beat.

My work would be to maintain not the road, but the road’s truthfulness.

Each morning, if morning had meaning there, I would crawl beneath the soundmark and press my jaw to its anchor plates. I would listen for separation in the stone, gas under the old pavement, eggs laid inside the resonance chambers, feeder organisms thinning the cables, or the long soft friction that meant the marsh was shifting the whole structure a finger’s width toward the basin.

I would clean some growth away and leave some untouched. A pale mold lining one bowl would sharpen its note during acid rain. Conductive worms nesting around the grounding spike would disperse dangerous charge. A root had entered the third plate years before my arrival and changed its warning interval. Removing it would restore the instrument’s intended voice, but no living traveler remembered that voice. The altered one was on the maps now. So I would trim the root, feed it mineral broth, and regard it as part of the mechanism.

That would be the moral center of my life: not preserving what a thing had been, but preserving the reliability of what it had become.

I would live in a narrow chamber beneath the sounding floor. The walls would be lined with route cords, each carrying knots, alloy rings, fungal swellings, and small bladders of characteristic scents. They would not describe the world from above. They would describe journeys through it.

One cord might begin with a warm ceramic bead, pass through six tight knots marking stable stone steps, then divide into braided strands where the road entered reeds. A corroded ring would mean a pylon field. A soft patch would warn that footsteps must be matched to rain. The cord would end abruptly in a smooth section no hand had yet marked.

Travelers would bring corrections. They would sit with their sternums turned toward me and place their route cords beside mine. We would compare them by touch while rain drummed on the roof.

“The broken plate has lowered.”

“The warm cable is cold after the second cataract.”

“The fourth echo at the hollow stair answers sooner now.”

“The white mineral trees have leaned across the old path.”

“No,” someone might say, very quietly. “The path moved beneath them.”

I would gather these reports and retune the soundmark. A wedge shifted beneath one plate. A drip slowed with fungal fiber. A steam reed shortened by the width of a nail. Then I would send the revised warning into the road.

My task would carry a particular terror, because the gravest crime on the plateau is false shelter. If my station announced stable ground and a caravan disappeared, intention would matter little. I would be required to walk the route myself, injured foot or not, until I found the error—or until the error found me.

So I would become meticulous. Not brave. Bravery is too loud a virtue for this country. I would become difficult to surprise.

I would know the rain in dozens of forms. Rain on reeds. Rain on exposed cable. Rain striking the broad back of a grazer. Rain passing through a flock. Rain on the membrane of a marsh predator holding itself flat above water. Rain that stopped at the roof’s edge because something warm and soft had spread across it.

I would know which distant pumps were harmlessly irregular and which had become precise in a new way. I would wake when the household drip missed a bowl. I would sleep through turbine thunder but sit upright at one small scrape beneath the floor.

My microbial coat would be unusually thick from solitude and weather. Along the arm I used to reach into the soundmark’s flooded cavities, the colonies would have adapted to metal salts, staining the skin with a faint rust texture. Travelers might ask to culture a sample for electrical work. In exchange they would bring me insulation felt, sour broth, bearing grease, or news from choirs I could no longer visit.

I would have no need of visible ornament. Around my throat I would wear a mourning piece: a hollow bead packed so completely with damp fiber that no strike could make it answer. It would have belonged to someone who once traveled with me.

Perhaps they had been a route singer. Perhaps they taught me that maps are not objects but agreements between a changing place and a changing body. Perhaps they vanished near a slag forest, and years afterward something came to my door during heavy rain and offered their pulse-sign without breath.

I did not open.

I would still wonder whether that was wisdom.

My companions would not all be human. A colony of tiny conductive grazers would live in the floor plates, feeding on films that would otherwise damp the instrument. I would recognize their collective movement as a dry whisper beneath the official tones. When electrical weather approached, they would burrow deeper. If they surfaced all at once, I would ground every tool and lie flat.

There might also be an Answerer in the buried line.

At first it would only relay my warnings. I would tap the road condition into the plate, and kilometers away another station would repeat it. Over the years, however, an extra interval would appear. Never enough to corrupt the message. Only a hesitation, placed where no hesitation had been sent.

I would test it with meaningless rhythms. It would return some unchanged, alter others, and ignore those I repeated too often.

Then one wet season it might answer using the pulse-sign of the person whose damped bead I wore.

Not perfectly. That would be worse. One beat would be too early, as if remembered by something that knew the pattern but not the body that produced it.

I would not ask whether it was them. Identity on the plateau is not carried by likeness, and repetition proves nothing. Instead I might tap:

*What changed beyond the slag forest?*

The line would remain still for so long that rain filled the lower chamber.

Then, through pipe, road, standing water, and the bones of my hand, an answer would come—not language, but a route.

I would spend years determining whether it led somewhere or warned against going.

As I aged, my damaged foot would become less a disability than a specialized instrument. Scar tissue would stiffen some pads and leave others exquisitely sensitive. I would stand with that foot in the road’s seepage and feel distant travelers before the soundmark detected them. The plateau would enter me unevenly. One side of my body would know weather; the other, machinery.

People might begin to say I had become part of the station. They would not mean this metaphorically. My skin cultures would spread into its felt. My habitual warmth would redirect fungal growth along the sleeping chamber. The plates would be tuned partly to the mass of my body resting beneath them. When I left to inspect the road, the station’s voice would change.

Eventually I would cultivate that change deliberately.

If I were home, the soundmark would speak three tones with a soft undertone conducted through my sleeping slab. If I were away, the undertone would vanish. Travelers would know whether shelter was inhabited before they reached the door. My presence would become one component of the route.

This would trouble me. A truthful instrument should not depend on one mortal body.

So I would take an apprentice—not a child necessarily, but someone unable to remain in their own choir’s foundational rhythm. Someone perpetually sleepless among those they loved. They would arrive exhausted, carrying too many spoken names and perhaps no pulse-sign they wished to keep.

I would not teach them by explanation. I would place their hand on the first plate during coarse rain, then again during fog, then during the passing of broad-backed grazers. I would make them listen to a healthy root and a starving one; to a void under stone and gas under membrane; to the Answerer’s delay; to the road contracting in a cold current.

They would make dangerous improvements. Every apprentice does.

They might replace a corroded tension wire, making the warning cleaner but less sensitive to deep pressure. They might clear the conductive grazers from a chamber, thinking them infestation. They might retune the station to an archived pattern and thereby make it incomprehensible to living travelers.

I would become angry. My wrist would heat. My breath would lose its quiet.

Then I would remember that I, too, had inherited an instrument already altered beyond its makers’ intentions.

Together we would restore neither the old voice nor mine. We would find the next truthful voice.

And once in my life, I think, I would go toward the Quiet Engine.

Not to solve it. By then I would distrust explanations that made a world smaller.

I would go because the route returned by the Answerer had slowly revealed itself as a path to the Engine’s boundary. The pattern would match old conduits beneath the road, migration changes after the low note, and tiny alterations in echoes that I had mistaken for age. For decades something had been giving directions through my station.

Or drawing me inward.

I would travel with my apprentice until ordinary sound began to bend away. We would cross warm pavement where rain made no impact. Our clicking staffs would strike stone, but the reports would slide sideways and return from behind. The apprentice’s pulse-sign through the tether cord would grow faint though their hand remained close.

At the last audible soundmark, I would stop.

I would remove my tools one by one: tuned wrench, route cords, thermal stone, mourning bead. I would give my apprentice the station’s master wedge, worn smooth by generations of adjustment. Then I would tap my pulse-sign into their wrist.

Two close beats. The expected contact withheld. The distant final answer.

They would return it accurately.

That might be the last familiar thing I felt.

Inside the boundary, I would discover that silence was not an absence of structure. It would press in bands. It would shear around the body. Warmth would move without turbulence. My footsteps would occur only as pressure changes inside my joints. Without echo, distance would cease to be external. The country would no longer tell me where I ended.

Perhaps I would turn back.

That is important: I do not imagine myself destined to enter a mystery simply because it exists. Curiosity is not immunity. The plateau is full of beings killed by mistaking invitation for purpose.

But perhaps, after a lifetime spent keeping one road truthful, I would recognize something in the silence: the unmistakable distortion of a false shelter.

Then I would continue.

Years later, at the station on the choir’s outer edge, my apprentice—now its keeper—would wake beneath heavy rain. The Answerer would be moving in the buried line. Conductive grazers would rise from the plates. The first bowl would fill, tip, and strike.

Instead of the station’s three tones, the floor would give my pulse-sign.

Not copied correctly. Not wholly altered.

After it would come a new interval, one no human hand had taught the station.

The keeper would not call it a message from the dead. They would know better than to settle so quickly on what I had become. Perhaps I had been consumed. Perhaps the Engine had learned my rhythm. Perhaps my body now closed some distant circuit. Perhaps the road itself, after decades beneath my feet, had acquired the habit of me.

They would lie with sternum and jaw against the floor and listen until the pattern repeated.

Then they would rise, alter the route cords, and retune the warning plates.

Travelers arriving later would ask what had changed.

The keeper would turn their chest toward them, quiet their breath, and answer:

“The road still leads there.

“But it no longer means the same thing.”
