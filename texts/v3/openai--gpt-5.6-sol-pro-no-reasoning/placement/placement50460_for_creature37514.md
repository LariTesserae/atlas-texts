I would find myself in the western district, although “myself” would be difficult to locate.

At first I seem to be one of the mote-herds traveling the hair-fine threads between young pillars: a dust-sized animal with hooked feet, a catalytic gut, and no eyes. The obvious answer is that I am a courier. I cross from needle to needle carrying a breath too slight to be visible, absorbing one molecule into the lining of my body and surrendering it elsewhere for a smear of wax.

But no single mote completes the journey.

The threads are long at our scale, exposed, and unevenly warm. Each of us travels only a portion before fastening itself beneath a junction and becoming still. There, two bodies meet mouth to mouth—not to mate or feed, but to exchange altered catalysts. One takes up the other’s cargo and continues. What crosses the district is not an individual but a succession.

So perhaps I am the cargo.

I am a reactive fragment gathered from the Crown’s older quarter, passed through pressure chambers and carrier grains, released at last into the western pioneers. I move inside bodies and along threads, changing hosts repeatedly. I have no membrane that remains mine. Yet I retain a kind of identity: wherever I pass, translucent fibers soften. Pillar tips become willing to fuse. Without me they lean toward one another but remain separate, almost touching until the lean season stiffens them apart.

That makes me seem important, but importance here is another illusion produced by scale. I cannot move without the mote-herds. They cannot feed without wax from the pillars. The pillars cannot survive without their shared charge. The charge depends on evening heat relinquished by the mesa. The mesa gives nothing intentionally.

Perhaps, then, I am not the catalyst either.

I am the route it takes.

A path can be alive in the Crown. Repetition feeds it. Every crossing leaves a minute electrostatic bias in the thread; every transfer alters the wax at a junction. Later travelers sense these alterations and favor the same line. Traffic increases. The thread thickens. What began as coincidence becomes habit, and habit becomes anatomy.

That is where I would live: not quite in the mote, not quite in the molecule, but in the reinforced tendency between pillars.

I would know the world as gradients.

Behind me—not as a remembered place, but as a waning chemical familiarity—would be the mature city: bronze films, warm conduits, the immense slow pulse of towers. Ahead would be the thin cold of an unfinished span. Beneath, the thread would conduct the tremors of thousands of hooked feet. Above would be open air, almost empty, lethal in its abundance of distance.

I would have no sight, yet sunset would transform me. As the western faces cooled, tension would travel through the needles. The district would tilt by fractions. Threads slack in daylight would draw taut, and all the routes would change their pressure at once. To a larger animal nothing would appear to happen. To me, the world would become steep. Familiar paths would vanish; new ones would announce themselves through charge and strain.

Some evenings I would lead toward a pillar already failing.

Its wax payment would grow thin. Its chemical signature would become sharp and irregular. Fewer motes would complete the crossing. At first the route would persist from habit, drawing traffic toward a destination that could no longer sustain it. Then one exchange would fail. Then another. The path would cool.

This would be the nearest thing I possessed to grief: a direction still present in form after it had ceased to be possible.

The surviving pillars would withdraw from the dying one. Threads would snap or be resorbed. The gap would widen deliberately, though nothing intended mercy. The weak pillar might harden into a clear needle and stand for years, empty of traffic, a ruin older than the room it failed to become.

Other evenings, two viable tips would approach.

No mote could witness the whole event. Generations would traverse the narrowing interval. The far pillar’s chemistry would become stronger along the thread. Its tremors would arrive with less distortion. Warmth would cease to feel distant. The distinction between origin and destination would weaken.

Then, during some unremarkable dusk, the tips would touch.

The contact would be microscopic: two mineral skins meeting across a film of dust. Motes would crowd the place, feeding catalysts into it. The shells would soften. Foreign surfaces would become a shared wetness—not water, but loosened resin and mobile salts. By morning the seam would harden.

My route would no longer cross between pillars. It would pass through one structure.

And in becoming permanent, I would begin to disappear.

The living thread that carried traffic across open air would be abandoned. The new arch would develop internal conduits, safer and more efficient. Carrier grains would replace mote-herds. Dust would gather on the upper surface. Bronze films would establish themselves. Beneath it, a creasewing might fold itself into the first shadow.

No creature would remember the old crossing. Yet the arch’s shape would be the record of all those journeys. It would curve where traffic had pulled the thread taut; thicken where exchanges had stalled; twist slightly toward the pillar that had once offered richer wax.

I would survive as asymmetry.

Later, claspbacks would follow the arch. Their feet would deepen certain vibrations. A latcher might settle near the busiest junction, turning passage into danger. Traffic would divide around it. New paths would form, and the arch would branch in response. A chamber might close around bloomers. A warm conduit might rise through what had once been empty space.

Eventually no one could point to the place where I began. The route would have become roof, wall, corridor, feeding lane, ambush site, nursery, and organ. My persistence would consist entirely of being used by lives incapable of perceiving me.

So I would not be a citizen of the Crown in the sense of a body dwelling there.

I would be one of its smallest customs.

A repeated exchange. A preference transmitted without instruction. A path that attracts bodies until bodies make it solid. I would exist only while enacted, and yet, if enacted long enough, I would outlast every creature that carried me.

From within, I would know no city. I would know only that this way is warmer, that this tremor is familiar, that this surface yields wax, that the next body accepts what the last body gave.

From far away, those recognitions would look like architecture.

I would be the almost-imperceptible habit by which two lonely things—though nothing here can truly live alone—learn to become one wall.
