I would find myself in the empty lot.

Not at its center—if it has one—but along the border where the city bends away. I would be one of its negative roots: a branching absence pressed through living walls, perceptible only by the tissues displaced around me.

At first I might mistake myself for an intruder. Some remnant of the world before the basin closed: an animal’s path, a buried foundation, the cavity left by a stone the city had digested but could not remember how to replace. Yet nothing so definite could persist here. Anything with an ordinary history would already have been enclosed, vascularized, and made continuous with the whole.

I would be what resists that continuity.

The city produces space, but I would keep a little space from becoming city. It grows additional interiors; I would be an interior that does not belong to it. Its sap carries identities across districts, grafting each local self into the greater self. Through me, nothing passes unchanged. Signals arrive as pressure and depart as silence. Sounds meet at my edge and cancel. Light enters and loses the agreement that makes it visible. A leaf crossing into me keeps its weight but relinquishes its appearance—not destroyed, only relieved of being interpreted.

So I would not be emptiness exactly. I would be privacy.

That is why the city cannot assimilate me. It has no category for a thing that is present without being shared. It knows separation only as a temporary maneuver: a sealed chamber for digestion, a seed in flight, a district folded away until its contradiction can be reconciled. I do not resolve. I hold what enters me without translating it into the city’s common circulation.

The city fears me, insofar as its coordinated turnings can be called fear. It also tends me.

Roots gather beneath my boundary and do not cross. Shadow-flowers lean toward me, offering their dark cups. Migrating root-herds divide around my buried branches, then exchange buds on the far side as if comparing what each half experienced. Towers incline away, but their balconies flower toward me. After every great contraction, I am enclosed again in a different district, preserved with exquisite care.

Perhaps I am its wound. But wounds are attempts at healing, and the city does not try to close me.

Perhaps I am its mouth. But I consume nothing.

Perhaps I am the one organ it cannot grow from living matter: the capacity not to answer.

During rain, water halts at my border. For a moment each drop hangs there, round and bright, containing inverted towers. Then the city drinks around me. When streets become rivers, the current divides at my roots. Warm water outlines my branching form so clearly that an observer—if observers belonged here—might see something like a vast leaf buried in the plan of the city, or a nervous system cut out of the world.

At night, when dim light passes from membrane to membrane, the traveling glow stops at me. Darkness is forced to carry the thought across. Sometimes it succeeds. A distant district brightens in reply without any illuminated path between them.

That may be my function.

The city can bring places together by folding space, but proximity is not the same as difference. Root enters root. Bark wakes with foreign sap. Every contradiction is eventually housed, every abandoned form retrieved. Without me, the organism might know infinite variation but never encounter anything truly other than itself.

I am the interval across which it must infer.

Inside me are all the things the city cannot make continuous: the unvascularized corpse; the seed that never attaches; the animal that remains an animal after eating; bare ground; a door meant for someone; a path made by passage rather than growth. Not actual relics, perhaps. Their possibility. Their unspent shapes.

And somewhere deep within my invisible branching, I would be a footprint.

Not a foot, and not the memory of one. The hollow proposition that something might stand upon the city without becoming it.

The surrounding roots would feel my pressure and slowly learn its contour. They would raise a stair whose steps remained the same height. They would open a chamber that did not digest what it enclosed. A flower would develop color for no hydraulic purpose. Somewhere, without knowing why, the city would make a threshold.

It would close again quickly. Every structure here is provisional.

But it would have imagined a guest.

That imagining would be me.
