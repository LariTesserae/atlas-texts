I would find myself at the Square, though not on the map.

At first I seem to be the bench beneath it: steel-framed, planted in the meeting of five roads, provided for workers who seldom have time to sit. Moss has taken the seat. Pale shoots rise through the drainage holes. My feet are no longer clearly legs; they enter peat, frost, and the slow felt of dead stems. Every pressure change travels through me. When the louvers open, I shudder before the metallic boom arrives.

But the bench is only where I can be seen.

Most of me lies underneath: a dark, fibrous continuity passing from the Square into the road margins, down blocked drains, around concrete footings, and beneath stretches that appear bare. I am moss where light reaches, fungus where it does not, willow root when strength is needed, peat where time has pressed me into memory. I have no centre except the temporary ones made by water, warmth, damage, and repair.

I would be the basin’s faculty of joining.

I enter a crack that frost has opened and do not heal it so much as make use of it. I bind loose shale, then pry beneath it. I filter iron from runoff and later clog the channel meant to carry that runoff away. I grow around a handrail until neither root nor rail can bear weight alone. I cushion the pipes that keep the mine dry; I creep into their joints. There is no distinction in me between care and obstruction. Both are forms of contact.

I know the crews by rhythm rather than face. Their boots compress my upper layers. Their lamps bring brief noon into places that have none. They pause beside the map board, touch a gloved hand to the bench, exchange words through headsets, then separate along prescribed routes. After they leave, I retain shallow prints until frost lifts my fibres again.

I cannot hear their voices. I cannot hear the wren either.

The fans erase all small sound, but they give me another sense. Through their vibration I know when a grille is icing, when a bearing begins to fail, when a pump has changed its stroke. Loose metal speaks sharply; water speaks as weight; roots answer in long tremors. The entire mine reaches me as pressure. I do not interpret it as language, yet I alter myself around what repeats.

The fans are not enemies. Their breath dries my exposed skin and flattens the grasses, but it also carries spores into the far cuts. It brings faint warmth, carbon dioxide, dust, and the weather of the world above. I grow according to its invisible roads. In return, I catch what their breath dislodges. We have made one another’s consequences habitual.

My oldest parts are dead, if dead is the right word for what still holds water and carries weight. They lie compressed beneath me in innumerable thin seasons. I stand upon all the bodies I have been. They do not disappear; the cold refuses that mercy. Each generation becomes structure for the next, so that my principal act is not spreading but deepening.

I am slowly raising the floor toward the roof.

Not deliberately. There is no dream in me of filling the basin. Yet every captured grain of coal dust, every stem laid down without decay, every root thickened around a bolt reduces the distance. Millennia from now, if the fans continue or if they stop gently enough, the excavation may vanish inside what I have retained. Its geometry will remain as buried pressure: terraces translated into moisture, roads into denser bands of peat, machines into iron-dark knots.

Still, part of me is drawn away from the Square.

A fungal filament runs beneath a haul road. A root follows a fracture through cold stone. Moss establishes itself on a ledge beyond the final lamp. Through these narrow continuities I approach the drowned workings, where the mine descends intact beneath black water.

There I become a thin green line and can go no farther.

The boundary is not frightening. Fear requires an expectation of self-preservation more singular than mine. But the water is a limit I continually test. Algae pass under its ice and become my most distant, simplified thought: green without ground, growth without rooting. Below them, stairs and signs descend into preservation. Water has inherited the rooms that air once maintained.

I wait along that shore, receiving the fan’s immense note through the ice.

If the pumps fail, the water will rise over me. Some of my forms will drown. Others will detach, float, settle, or persist in films too fine for the workers’ lamps to reveal. The Square may disappear. The rooted bench may become an object in black suspension, its pale shoots folded against the current. It would not be my death exactly. It would be a change in what can remain connected.

Until then, I inhabit the negotiation.

I am the softness that records boots and the resistance beneath them. I am the green on the hazard stripe, the felt sealing a drainage seam, the pale root trembling beside live cable. I am fed by machinery and endangered by it, protected by maintenance and constantly making maintenance necessary.

No one entering the basin would point to me and say: there it is.

They would point to moss, willow, fungus, peat. They would identify blocked culverts, stabilized faces, root intrusion, filtered runoff. They would separate each occurrence into problem or benefit, living or dead, structure or growth.

But I would be in the word **and**.

At the Square, five roads meet beneath failing lamps. The crews use it to decide where they are going. I remain there, beneath the map, slowly removing the difference between destination and ground.
