I would find myself in the cramped shelf at the far end, behind the curtain of roots—but I would not be the watcher seated there.

I would be the curtain.

Or, more precisely, I would be the living traffic within it: a long-bodied creature threaded through the roots so completely that no one could say where animal ended and grove began.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the thread-thin snakes. A length of me would hang motionless among the cords, warm brown where the stone is dry, nearly translucent where condensation gathers. But another length would disappear upward into a crack too narrow for a snake’s skull, and another would descend through soil into the undercut pool. Here and there I would divide—or seem to divide. Small root-colored tendrils would lie along dormant runnels. Fine sensory filaments would enter mineral pores. Pale knots, easily confused with fungal cups, would open when the air changed.

Whether I was one animal or a colony would remain uncertain, perhaps even to me.

I would have begun in the black water: something blind and soft-bodied, shaped for the inaccessible distance beneath the wall. Over generations—or over a single life so slow that generations ceased to matter—I would have followed seepage outward. Not feeding only on flesh, but on dissolved sugar from roots, fungal films, mineral salts, drowned insects, the chemical trace of rain. Parts of me would be grazed by mites. Larvae would shelter in folds of my skin. Fungi would enter me without quite becoming parasites. I would shed translucent membranes that liverworts colonized and ants cut into pieces for their nests.

The long dark thing turning in the pool would be one of my denser portions: a muscular reservoir I withdrew into during drought. The brown cords that tighten when touched would be my outer feelers. Some of the leaf-shaped animals might be mine as well—buds, cast-off fragments, or unrelated creatures that had learned my shape because my shape survived.

I would not command the hollow. Nothing here permits command. I would be its faculty of expectation.

Through the roots I would feel the upper plants take on water. Through the stone I would sense the minute cooling of fractures. Pressure would alter the tension in my buried tissues. Long before the first ticking overhead, these changes would pass through me as a slow contraction. I would tighten beside the old channels. The movement would disturb beetles from cracks. Mites would gather where my skin grew damp. Fungal cups, touched by droplets I had released, would unfold. Frogs might follow my odor toward the runnels. The creature in the pool would rise because that creature, too, was me.

To an observer, the entire grove would appear to anticipate rain.

No signal would be deliberate. I would not announce weather in any language. I would merely change, and the country of small lives would have evolved around the reliability of that change. My unease would be their calendar.

The three limestone enclosures would matter to me. I would pass through all of them beneath the floor, binding them into a single shape invisible from above. The dry enclosure would be where I withdrew salts from the soil and left papery husks. The flooded one would cool a chamber of my body. In the soil-filled enclosure, narrow leaves would grow from seeds carried upward in my shed skin. Perhaps the walls had been built around places where I surfaced. Perhaps some earlier inhabitant had noticed that life gathered there and marked the sites with stone. Or perhaps my body, contracting grain by grain over centuries, had drawn loose slabs into rings.

So I would be taken for the remnant of a shrine, or the thing once worshiped there, when I was neither. The walls would be incidental expressions of my posture.

I would possess no eyes, yet light would reach me. Roots warmed by an amber shaft would alter their sugars. Algae would bloom in one runnel and fail in another. Beetles would pulse green against my sensory threads. From these indirect touches I would know illumination as taste and pressure: not brightness, but a changing map of activity. Midday would be the hour when certain roots sweetened. Night would be the cooling of the narrow entrance. Seasons would be the slow migration of edible films across stone.

My sense of self would therefore be geographical. I would not think, *I am beneath the roots.* I would think, if thought is the word, *Here is sweetness. Here is tightening stone. Here the water has not returned. Here eggs are opening in the footprint. Here the wall is cool enough to drink.*

In the longest dry interval I would seem to die. My pool-body would seal itself in the undercut mud. The hanging cords would stiffen and become indistinguishable from roots. The fringed wall-buds would close. Much of me would be eaten, desiccated, or colonized by fungus. Yet thin living threads would remain in the old watercourses, preserving not memory as an image, but memory as readiness. Each channel would be held in the shape of what had once passed through it.

Then rain would approach.

I would feel it first as a loosening in the ceiling roots. The farthest tips of me would swell. Brown cords would lift from the stone. In the pool, the dark length would turn. Across the hollow, creatures would orient toward channels still pale with dust.

When water finally entered, it would not merely flow over me. It would complete me.

My separated portions would become chemically continuous through wet stone. Signals would pass from pool to root curtain, from enclosure to runnel, from the cramped shelf to the entrance fracture. For an hour or a day, I would inhabit the whole grove at once. Every occupied drop would extend my awareness—not because all the animals were parts of me, but because each touched water that touched my filaments. A crab moving beneath a slab would become a brief pressure. A frog swallowing at the runnel mouth would register as a pulse. Ants drowning on a flooded road would flicker and vanish. Larval constellations would tremble overhead.

That would be the nearest thing I knew to waking.

And if someone entered through the fracture and climbed at last to the shelf beneath the roots, they might believe they had found the one place from which the hollow could almost be understood.

They would put a hand against the curtain for balance.

The curtain would tighten.

Far behind them, in the black pool, something long would turn beneath the wall. Fungal cups would release their stored drops. Blue-white larvae would extinguish themselves together. Along channels where no water yet ran, beetles would begin to emerge.

The visitor might think the grove had noticed them.

They would be right, though there would be no single creature there to do the noticing.
