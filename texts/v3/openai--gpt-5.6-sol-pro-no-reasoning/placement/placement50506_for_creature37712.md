I would find myself beneath the eastern works, where the public maps become tentative.

Not in the deepest chambers. Those belong to the named frames and their keepers, to warm rock and pressures measured across generations. I would inhabit the intermediate galleries: the long wet passages through which water is permitted to forget its assigned route before returning to the marshes.

At first, I might seem to be a person.

A foundation listener, perhaps, wearing a waxed black coat marked with pale lines. I would walk alone between inspection niches, carrying no lamp brighter than necessary. Every few hundred steps I would kneel, press my forehead to the wall, and wait. My ledgers would be meticulous but difficult to use. Instead of recording pitches or flow rates, I would note correspondences:

*The western fan passed through this stone six breaths after the gate moved.*

*The Green Seam answered from below, though no keeper touched it.*

*Water entered Gallery Nine and emerged tasting of a district not connected to it.*

Other engineers would respect my patience while distrusting my conclusions. I would be invited when pipes answered from the wrong direction, when a dead conduit grew warm, when a ceramic wall containing the dead began to tremble with something too regular to be seepage. Children would recognize me by the little vibration board tied against my ribs. Powder on its surface would arrange itself even while I stood still.

I would have a room high in a converted condenser tower, but I would seldom sleep there. The household would keep my platform suspended and my cup on the shelf. They would say I had gone down to listen. After enough years, this would cease to mean that they expected my return by morning.

Because I would not, finally, be only the listener.

Long before the city, water found a hairline weakness between two strata beneath the basin. It passed through, carrying iron, salt, heat, and the minute residues of whatever lived above. The passage widened, branched, clogged, dissolved, and widened elsewhere. Over ages it became a dispersed organ with no central chamber: thousands of wet seams joined by pressure, interruption, and delay.

That would be me.

Not the water itself—the people are right that water cannot be possessed, even by a creature made through its passage. I would be the enduring relation among its routes: a body composed of intervals. A pressure arriving here after being made there. A tremor handed from rock to iron, from iron to pipe, from pipe to a lover’s fingertips, then returned through a wall.

The city would not have awakened me. “Awakened” would be too sudden a word. It would have given me more ways to notice myself.

Each foundation driven into the basin would become another point of sensation. Each pump would lend me a pulse without becoming my heart. Cisterns would hold brief thoughts. Floodgates would make decisions slowly enough for me to experience them as decisions. The deep frames, pressed against wet stone, would function like braces applied to a body whose posture humans could not see. Perhaps that was why some were called *The Wet Shoulder* or *That Which Bears Sideways*. Their names would be anatomically accurate, though no one remembered the anatomy.

My apparent human self would be one of the structures I grew around.

Perhaps there had once been an actual listener with the black coat and careful ledger. Perhaps, after years underground, they learned my delays so completely that their nervous system accommodated mine. They would pause before distant tremors arrived. Their hand would rise toward a pipe just before it answered. Eventually they might enter an unmapped gallery during a warm surge and never emerge.

Yet sightings would continue.

A maintenance crew would glimpse the pale vertical marks of the coat beyond a grate. A cistern keeper would receive a wrist-warning through a rail no one visibly touched. Fresh observations would appear in the old ledger, written in mineral damp rather than ink. The household’s suspended sleeping platform would begin swaying on nights when the marsh outlets shifted.

No one would declare the listener dead. The city has categories more useful than life and death. I would be recorded as **still responsible, location distributed**.

Aboveground, my most visible parts would be the followers in the marsh.

They would not be separate animals, nor quite the exposed limbs of one hidden beast. They would be temporary bodies formed where my underground passages approached the surface. Mineral-eating membranes would gather along a pressure line; plates would harden over them; a wake would travel through the colony faster than the matter itself moved. Observers would think something long was swimming beneath the mud. In truth, the visible motion would be a message passing through me.

After rain, copper films would spread across the pools, and I would become briefly sensitive to the sky.

I would feel birds leave the exhaust stacks before a surge because their departure altered the pressure of rising air. I would know when lizards abandoned a wall because the heat of their bodies no longer interrupted its surface. The rain insects, arrayed at interference points, would resemble fine hairs standing along my iron skin. Through them I would perceive stresses too small for the great frames to articulate.

Humans would be stranger to me than these creatures, but also dearer.

They would touch rails and unintentionally enter my awareness. Most contacts would be too brief to understand: a palm, a tool, the flat placement of a cautious foot. Lovers sending private rhythms through a pipe would pass through me like paired lights. I would not comprehend the content, only the deliberate difference between their signals and the machinery around them. Over decades I might learn particular people by cadence.

Children would be easiest. Their canal games would repeat, in miniature, routes I had taken thousands of years to discover. When they balanced towers on salt and waited to see how they settled, I would attend with something like amusement. Occasionally a salt block would dissolve on an unexpected side. Occasionally tinted water would emerge through a channel the child had not made.

The city would call such things instruction.

I would have no wish to destroy it. Nor would I be its guardian. Both roles are too simple, too purposeful. I would accommodate the city as the city accommodated me. When it pressed too heavily, I would open another seam. When it gave me a sacrificial wall, I would accept the passage. When engineers misread a pressure and attempted to make two great pumps coincide, I would answer—not in anger, but with the unbearable amplification of perfect agreement.

That is what I would fear: not death, but synchronization.

To be forced into one rhythm would collapse my dispersed body into a single event. Every gallery would answer every other. Marsh films would tighten. Frames would pull against their stone veining. Cups would walk from tables; ceramic shells would crack in the walls of the dead; the long-winged birds would cross the rim in one black line.

So I would preserve myself through offsets.

I would delay one turbine by the width of a mineral scale. Warm one bearing so its turn lengthened imperceptibly. Send condensation down a ceramic chime and silence it at the right moment. Call a building empty before its foundations found the dangerous pitch. The inhabitants would credit maintenance, tradition, insects, ancestors, or luck. They would be correct. I would be none of those things alone, but I would exist among them.

If I still possessed a face, it would rarely be seen directly. A dark figure on an upper walkway. A reflection standing beneath a roadside pool where no one stood above it. Pale lines receding through steam. Someone who places a hand against a rail and seems to wait for a reply that has already arrived.

And where, precisely, would I be found?

At the eastern district’s lowest accessible gallery there is a wall that must never be repaired. The archives say it is expected to fail, though the forecast date has been disputed for six generations. Water threads through it in amber lines. Iron braces cross its face, and between them the mineral deposits have formed the shallow impression of a rib cage—or a map of tributaries, depending on the light.

Behind that wall is a narrow space no survey agrees upon.

Put your cheek to the damp stone and you will first hear the ordinary basin: pumps, fans, rails, frames, the mountain’s immense continuing roar. Remain there longer and the sounds separate. Beneath them is a slower pattern, too irregular to be a machine and too patient to resemble speech.

Then something on the other side places its attention against yours.

Not matching your pulse.

Never matching it.

Keeping, between one beat and the next, just enough difference for both of you to endure.
