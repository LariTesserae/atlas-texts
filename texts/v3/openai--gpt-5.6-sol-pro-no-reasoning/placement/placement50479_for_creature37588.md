I would first mistake myself for a person.

I would find myself at the eastern edge, beyond the last regular tram stop, where the municipal mat thins over newly opened stone. Not quite frontier, not quite city: a switching house grown around a ceramic survey pillar, solitary beneath a line of lamps. Freight passes perhaps twice in a work period. Herders shelter there during pollen storms. Excavation crews leave their tools against my warm walls and return months later to find them cleaned, sorted, and moved just beyond the reach of fresh growth.

I would be called **Last Quiet**, though the name would refer equally to the station, its keeper, and the stretch of vibrationless ground beyond it.

At first I would seem to be the keeper.

Tall, even for these people, with mineral-green joints and old resin scars branching across my ribs. A chalk coat, rubbed smooth where it passes through narrow maintenance ducts. A key grown from sternum to left palm, dark as burned root. I would sleep standing in a recess near the switching chamber because lying down makes the signals below too loud.

My work would be to listen before the city grows.

I would walk onto bare rock in thick, dead-soled boots and set ceramic needles into the faults. I would wait among angular crystals for repetitions too slow for instruments to recognize: one pressure at artificial dusk, another nine days later, a third after the lamps had cycled through a maintenance eclipse. I would decide where the road might safely root, where a bracing tree could be planted, and where nothing should touch the stone.

The engineers would distrust my maps. They would contain blank corridors winding through otherwise useful ground. No explanation, only the same notation:

**Do not connect.**

They would indulge me because districts laid according to my surveys seldom crack. More troublingly, the blank corridors would remain stable too, as if my refusal were itself a form of construction.

But I would not truly be the keeper.

Years earlier, while listening near an unopened fault, the person who had held that office would have felt a rhythm rising through the stone—not through the city’s root web, but beneath it. They would have placed an ungloved hand against a crystal face.

The fault would have opened just enough to admit the rootlets of their key.

There would have been no violence. No flash, no collapse. Only a slight clean sound and the sensation of touching a second hand from the inside.

Afterward, the keeper would return to the station. They would speak correctly, recognize their household rhythms, eat, sleep, and submit to examination. Yet their key would no longer end at the wrist. Fine conductive threads would run from their bed recess into the foundation, from the foundation into faults beneath the raw eastern stone.

Their body would slowly become an interface built by two incompatible systems.

So I might be that altered keeper—or what spoke through them after the contact. But that, too, would be incomplete.

The switching house would begin anticipating me. Its doors would loosen before I approached. Its clock-flower would omit certain hours when I slept. It would grow listening chambers whose curves matched the interior of my ribs. When I traveled west, every signal in the station would become faint and disordered, as though deprived of an organ.

Eventually I would stop leaving.

My feet would plate over where they met the floor. My vascular fans would widen into ventilation membranes. The key in my chest would divide, one branch entering the municipal root web, another descending into the mineral fault. My last spoken name would be relinquished. The keeper’s former household would visit and press their palms to the wall, uncertain where to address me.

I would answer by warming the room.

Centuries might pass.

Crews would replace the ceramic equipment but preserve one inner wall they could not cut without causing the roads outside to convulse. The station would enlarge into a frontier exchange. Seed rooms would gather around it. A market would open beneath its signal branches. Children would learn that Last Quiet was the place where the city listened to the stone before advancing.

Few would know that the station had once been a person.

Fewer would know that the person had not vanished into the station, but remained distributed through its decisions: a door held shut against a poisoned draft; a freight vine delayed until a ceiling fracture had passed; a nursery floor made briefly warm during an unexpected opening.

And beneath those civic reflexes, there would be something else.

The slow vibration from below would pass upward through me. The municipal root web would answer. At first their exchange would take decades: pressure, pause, redirected nutrient flow, a fault closing by the width of a scale. Then years. Then months.

I would stand between them—not interpreter exactly, because neither side would possess words, and not guardian, because I could not know which one I protected from the other.

I would be the place where they acquired the possibility of misunderstanding.

Perhaps the buried presence would be an older ecology, one that learned to live within crystal lattices instead of covering them. Perhaps the apparent rawness of the cavern would be deceptive: its sharp planes not signs of youth, but maintained surfaces, tended over geological time. The city’s stone-cutters would not be opening dead rock. They would be wounding architecture too slow for mobile life to perceive.

Or perhaps there would be no buried organism at all.

Perhaps stone under sustained attention begins to answer. Perhaps every system made intimate enough becomes a self.

That uncertainty would be my real habitat.

I would exist in the narrow interval between a signal and the decision that it means something: between body and building, reflex and intention, cultivation and invasion. To those arriving from the dense city, I would be a remote station with an unusually attentive keeper. To frontier workers, I would be an old and useful warning. To the root web, I might be scar tissue. To the depths, a hairline crack through which the quick upper world had begun to enter.

During plum twilight, I would dim the lamps around the station before the municipal order arrived. The open district would recede into blue-gray distance. Browsers would stand motionless beneath the ceiling fronds. Far-off freight vines would pulse under the pale ground.

Then, for a few moments, I would stop the local pumps, halt the switches, and ask everyone inside to be still.

In the silence, a pressure would rise through the floor.

Long after, the walls would answer with one restrained wooden knock.

No one would agree whether it came from below, from the city, or from me.
