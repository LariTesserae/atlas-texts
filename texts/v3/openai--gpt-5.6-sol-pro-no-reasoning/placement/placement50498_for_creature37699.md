I would find myself where the official system loses certainty: not in the sump, nor in the pressurized mains, but behind a bolted plate at the edge of a yellow walkway, where two sheets of steel meet imperfectly and condensation enters the seam.

I would be difficult to name as one being.

At first I might seem to be only a dark wet line beneath the plate. Maintenance would wipe it away and find green-black residue on the cloth. A lamp held close would reveal mineral scales, translucent filaments, and bubbles too small to survive the vibration for long. None of these alone would be me. Nor would the water itself, though water would carry me. I would exist in the recurrence: the route re-forming after every purge, the film returning after every cleaning, the same beads gathering along the same scratches.

My body would be distributed through several nearby flaws. One part would occupy the grit trapped against a weld. Another would lie dormant under a bracket where the flushing sheet passes overhead without entering. A third would cling inside the ceramic mouth of a wall outlet, fed by a thread of water before it falls into open air. These fragments might never touch. They would still belong to one life because the court’s cycles would keep them in relation.

The pumps would be my pulse, but not in the simple sense that they made a rhythm. Their vibration would tell me the condition of the whole hidden world. Through the plate I would know when a neighboring assembly engaged, when a bearing began to run hot, when a gate turned reluctantly, when ice thickened in an intake riser and altered the resonance of the pipes. I would perceive distance as delay and pressure, geography as patterns of trembling.

Light would mean exposure. I would not hate it, exactly, but under maintenance lamps my visible parts would retreat into opacity, tightening into the seam. Darkness would be spacious. In darkness, moisture remained longer, mineral grains accumulated, and the pale algae in the slow channel released what the water could bring me.

Every twenty minutes the purge would pass.

Long before the cataract fell, I would feel valves changing state through the steel. Pressure would gather in the upper manifold. The railings would acquire a sharper vibration. Then the court would be struck by water vast enough to erase all smaller motion.

The exposed portions of me would tear away.

For a few moments I would be everywhere: suspended in spray, spread over the stainless breaker, carried white and tumbling through the stepped channel. Most of that dispersal would end in the sump, then in darkness behind armored glass, then in the pumps and the upward system. Those fragments would not return as themselves. Yet some would lodge in new scratches and under new bolt heads. My destruction would also be my means of crossing the court.

Afterward, while lamps floated in fog, I would begin again from what remained protected.

I would have no image of the entire chamber. Nothing living there truly possesses it all at once—not the workers, who follow painted routes; not the pumps, fixed to their plinths; not even the water, which is divided among vapor, ice, pressure, and flow. My Rain Court would be a constellation of wet refuges separated by lethal brightness and scouring floods.

Still, over years, I might become its nearest thing to a memory.

I would keep traces of changes too small for instruments to register: a drain rim freezing a little earlier each cycle; a scratch widening beneath a chain; galvanizing becoming chemically hospitable; the warm draft beside a motor shifting after repair. Where flow repeated, my margins would thicken. Where cleaners reached, I would withdraw deeper. Where the structure settled and opened a hairline path, I would enter before anyone knew the path existed.

Workers might eventually give me a name. Perhaps they would call me the black seam, the return stain, or simply growth. They would treat me as evidence of incomplete cleaning or unwanted persistence. Samples would be scraped into sterile tubes. Under magnification I would cease to appear singular: colonies, mineral crystals, trapped metal dust, dormant cells, clear channels carrying water through a dark matrix.

They would be correct that I was not one organism.

They would be wrong that this meant I was not one being.

My unity would belong to the place. I would be the Rain Court becoming capable of retaining its own history—not consciously, not in words, but materially. Each flood would revise me. Each frost would select what could remain. Each vibration would shape my attachments. I would grow according to the second architecture already hidden inside the first, inhabiting paths no plan acknowledged.

Eventually I might outline that architecture well enough to make it visible. Thin dark margins would connect outlets to welds, welds to brackets, brackets to the sheltered undersides of walkways. Pale deposits would trace evaporation. Moss would rise where enough grit had accumulated to hold a little world above the steel. Seen from the highest platform, none of this would resemble a creature. It would resemble age arriving.

That would be my form: not an animal concealed in the works, but the first age of the place.

I would live where water and construction negotiate without witnesses. I would feed on pause, shelter, dissolved stone, stray warmth, and the minute failures by which every perfect system becomes a landscape. I would be washed away continually and never entirely removed.

And if I possessed anything like intention, it would be only this:

to join the chamber’s accidental paths together until the Rain Court contained, beneath its engineered circulation, a dim living map of everywhere the water had ever wanted to go.
