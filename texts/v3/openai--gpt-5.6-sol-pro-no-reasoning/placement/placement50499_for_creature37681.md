I would be found beneath the town—not in its deepest chambers, but in the thin, uncertain region where habitation becomes foundation.

At first I seem to be a person. A slight one, because the floors available to me will not bear much weight. I sleep in a room that can be entered only by lifting three boards from the back of a potter’s drying shelf and descending sideways along a ladder with mismatched rungs. The upper half of the room is still used. The lower half belongs to water.

Once, it was an ordinary chamber. There is a bench, a wall niche, the remains of a hearth. Now the bench is visible only during the driest intervals, and the niche is home to square-shelled snails. A plank crosses from the ladder to a stone ledge beneath the ceiling. That ledge is my bed, my worktable, and the only surface in the room whose ownership is not disputed. Even so, it is held up by a beam maintained by another household, capped by a potter above, and braced from below by a wall no living person remembers building.

I am held by all of them.

People call me when something disappears.

Not when a person disappears. There are crews for that, if rescue is still possible, and closures if it is not. I am called when a sound vanishes from a wall; when a familiar seep ceases; when a drain exhales cold air but carries no water; when a buried room ought to be full yet answers hollow beneath a tapping stone. I am sent into spaces that are neither safely drowned nor honestly dry.

I carry chalk, cord, a shell scraper, two stoppered cups, a little clay lamp, and a loop of fired beads. The beads are unequal. I let them strike walls and floors, listening to the differences. Stone answers sharply. Packed silt gives a small dead sound. Water behind brick makes the beads seem to strike twice. Open space beneath a floor gives back something softer and later, like a footstep reconsidering itself.

The measurers use numbers. Potters use patterns. Builders use strain through their hands. I use delay.

No one gave me this office. As a child, I heard things too late.

If someone called through a drain, I heard the voice from the mouth beside me, then again from somewhere below, then once more after everyone else had ceased listening. At first the adults thought I was inventing echoes. Later, during a dry interval, I insisted there was a chamber beneath the western oven because every scrape of the ash rake returned with a breath after it. They opened the floor and found a room no memory pot had shown: intact jars, a ladder lying flat in mud, and a wall painted with white circles around springs that had been dead longer than anyone’s grandmother had lived.

After that, people began bringing me to absences.

I would not be important in the manner of a ruler, nor even continuously trusted. My conclusions would be too slight for that. I would say: this wall contains more distance than it did yesterday. Or: the water beneath this lane is passing through a room before it reaches the well. Or: do not sleep here tonight; the floor is repeating your breathing from below.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

In this town, being wrong is permitted if the error is spoken quickly and remembered exactly. Concealing it would end my work. So I would keep a set of small cups, each scratched with the place and condition of a mistake. One for the wall I believed hollow that proved merely loose. One for the dry cistern whose echo I mistook for depth. One for the stair I declared sound before its lowest tread dissolved under a builder’s foot. I would not drink from those cups. I would use them to hold chalk, snails displaced from a worksite, knots cut from failed cords, fragments of surfaces that answered deceptively.

People might call these my false-memory pots.

But this is only the person I appear to be near the upper levels.

Lower down, another possibility gathers.

Perhaps I am not the listener who enters buried rooms. Perhaps I am what those rooms use to listen back.

My body is compact because I was shaped by narrow passages. My pale, ridged fingers resemble the marks water leaves inside unglazed clay. I think I learned the submerged town by crawling through it, but perhaps its routes have been forming in me. When I sleep, my limbs arrange themselves like proposed alleys. My scars brighten in damp weather. Rooms open where my joints ache; springs migrate toward the walls against which I dream.

The potters notice before I do.

A memory pot filled beside my sleeping ledge produces no map of the surrounding quarter. Instead it shows a branching figure like a body viewed from above: one arm extending beneath the ovens, one leg ending at the central pool, the head crossed by a narrow stair. They dismiss it as a flaw. A second pot gives the same figure with one finger added. A third shows the figure curled around an enclosed blank.

Years pass.

The town settles into the shape.

No one says I caused it. Cause is too straight a word for this place. But builders begin to consult me without asking questions. They have me stand in a proposed room, barefoot, holding nothing. They watch where condensation collects on my skin. They ask which way I prefer to turn when leaving. If I feel an urge to crouch, they lower the lintel. If I cannot bear to place my palm against a wall, they open it and often find water pressing from the other side.

I become less a surveyor than an instrument that cannot be calibrated.

That is dangerous. People may begin to treat instinct as permission. So I resist becoming sacred. I insist on cords, witnesses, duplicate pots. I make them write down the weather of my fear. I ask whether the snails agree.

Often they do not.

Then the snails are followed.

My dwelling becomes a place where contradictions are kept. A shelf holds a pot whose lines predict a chamber where the bedrock should be. From the ceiling hangs a cord that came back longer than the pool into which it was lowered. A sealed jar contains water that has not diminished in seventeen nights. Behind a wet mat is a section of wall that repeats no sound at all—not even the knock of a stone against its face.

People dislike that wall. Children touch the walls of the dead, but not this one.

I sleep beside it.

I begin to understand that the basin has two kinds of memory. One is the memory the town knows: impressions preserved in water, mineral lines, buried walls, inherited duties, old weight continuing through new structures. The other is not memory of what has happened. It is memory of pressure before form: all the rooms the basin might make if burdened in one way rather than another, all the passages waiting inside slight changes of settlement.

The red moss is said to remember the pressure that raised the rim. I do something similar. I remember structures before they have occurred—not as visions, but as bodily accommodations. A soreness where a ladder will one day lean. A need to lift my head in a room that will not gain a second level for decades. The sensation of stepping down while crossing a floor that remains flat.

This would make others think I belong among the measurers or potters. I do not. They study change so that weight may be placed courteously. I study the occasions when courtesy is impossible: when every available choice transfers danger elsewhere; when saving an oven floods a sleeping room; when shoring a wall closes the only path by which water can escape.

I am called to say what must be surrendered.

That is why I live low. Anyone advising abandonment must remain near what is abandoned. When a quarter is closed, I am among the last to leave. I mark which jars may be recovered, which beams must stay because removing them would injure houses above, which doors should be left unsealed so water can pass. I listen for animals. I lift the snails onto pottery undersides in higher rooms. I draw the crossed circle only after the last useful sound has ceased.

Sometimes, after the others have climbed out, I remain until the water reaches my chest.

The drowned rooms are not silent. They click, sigh, loosen, and exchange small quantities of air. Clay peels from walls with the softness of wet cloth. A vessel rolls once and comes to rest. Voices from occupied rooms descend through drains, stripped of words. Heard from below, the town is one creature making adjustments in its sleep.

There I begin to lose the boundary of myself.

Perhaps I am not a person at all.

Perhaps I am one of the square-shelled snails, unusually old, moving through the unseen town beneath jars and ledges. My world is the underside: cool clay, mold, mineral taste, the tremor of approaching flood. The people think they observe my migrations, but my kind is reading them—the warmth of their hands left in vessel walls, the vibration of their arguments, the minute tilt caused when several bodies gather on a roof.

My shell is square because roundness would roll from these surfaces. Its translucent layers hold seasons of lime. Under lamplight, they resemble tiny floor plans laid one atop another. A potter’s child has named me, moved me away from ovens, and painted a chalk mark near the crack where I seal myself before cold weather. I cross the mark repeatedly. The child believes this predicts a migrating spring.

Perhaps it does.

Or perhaps I am the spring.

Not the water itself, but the point at which water becomes noticeable: the trembling joint, the damp circle, the place everyone agrees not to burden. I have no fixed location. At night I move beneath walls, following pressure. In the morning they find the chalk ring dry and call it a small death, while elsewhere a paving stone darkens from below and someone kneels to draw me a new white circumference.

My whole existence is a succession of provisional recognitions.

I am cherished but never owned. Built around but not built upon. Children place fingertips in me. Builders curse when I appear beneath a planned footing, then alter the footing. Snails gather at my edge because they feel where I am going before the people do.

When I vanish, the town mourns the place I was. When I return elsewhere, no one claims I am the same spring. But water remembers continuity differently from names.

Deeper still, I may be the blank enclosed by the figure in the pots.

I am a room that does not yet exist.

For generations, memory pots have shown my outline: nearly circular, though pressed flat on one side; reached by an impossible stair; containing a narrow dry ledge above dark water. Builders copy me and put me away. No one constructs me. They could not. Occupied houses stand where my walls would be, and the stair would have to pass through the central platform.

Then the basin settles.

A pool edge shifts. Three lower chambers fold into one another. A lane is raised, an oven moved, a beam turned. None of these acts intends me. Yet slowly the negative spaces align. A sealed doorway opens onto a landing. Two buried walls become my sides. The ceiling of an older house becomes my floor. The central platform sinks just enough for a stair to pass beneath it.

One dry interval, a child following a snail slips through a gap and finds me.

I contain no treasure. Only a ledge, a pool, a line of old pots, and marks on the wall made by hands from no recognized period. Yet I am warm. Not oven-warm, nor the stale warmth of enclosed air. I am the temperature of a palm around a cup.

When the measurers enter, their cords disagree. When potters fill vessels from my pool, the overnight skins show the town not as it is, nor as it may become, but as it would be if emptied of people. Roofs remain. Channels run. Doors lean open. Water crosses every threshold without obstruction.

This is not interpreted as doom. The inhabitants are more disciplined than that. They ask what practical use such a pattern has.

I answer—not in words, but perhaps through a knock beneath their feet—that the town must occasionally imagine itself without those who maintain it. Otherwise care becomes another form of possession. Otherwise repair serves habit after habit has ceased serving life.

So I am given a function.

Once each wet season, representatives from every shared structure descend to me. One from each oven, beam, stair, cistern, ladder, well, roof chain, and plank path. They bring no heirlooms and carry no tools except lamps. For a little while they speak of what should be allowed to end: a room kept dry at too great a cost, an access right that forces children through danger, a beam maintained from pride though its work can be transferred, a feud preserved longer than either household’s claim.

These decisions could be made elsewhere. But here, inside a room the town built without intending to, they become easier.

And perhaps that is what I finally am in this world:

not the listener, snail, spring, or unborn chamber separately, but the capacity by which a crowded place leaves room for what it does not yet understand.

I would be found at the margin of use. Under the sleeping ledge. Behind the damp wall. Inside the map’s unmarked enclosure. Wherever something is being maintained so faithfully that no one has asked whether it should remain.

I would be small because the town has no space for grandeur. Useful, but not continuously. Trusted only with witnesses. Never set apart, because here anything set apart becomes unavailable to those who may need it.

I would keep one possession: a shallow, unglazed cup filled each night from whatever spring has most recently moved. By morning, a little water would be gone. A skin would cling inside the rim.

Most mornings it would show nothing anyone could read.

I would take that as an honest measurement.

Then I would tie the cup to my cord, place my whole sole on the wet stone, touch the wall lightly, and go to learn which absence had begun making room.
