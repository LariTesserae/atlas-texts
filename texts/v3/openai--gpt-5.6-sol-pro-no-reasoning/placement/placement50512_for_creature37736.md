I would be found beneath the eastern quarter, where the spring streets begin: not quite in the water, not quite in the walls, but in the narrow interval between them.

At first I might seem to be a water tender.

I would rise before the first lamps were pinched out and walk the channels while the city was still enough to hear each obstruction. My clothes would have the dark hems of that trade, and my hands would be pale and softened from cold seepage. I would carry a hooked reed for lifting roots, a flat spoon for grit, red clay for marking cracks, and a set of small cups with which to compare one flow against another.

But water alone would not account for me.

I would also be called when a wall began sweating without rain, when a cistern lost more than evaporation could explain, or when someone heard a faint ticking under a floor. I would lie with my cheek against the stone and listen. I would know that water has many voices: the clean thread of an established seep, the hollow murmur of a hidden chamber, the granular whisper of clay loosening behind plaster. I would taste for bitterness, rub sediment between finger and thumb, and ask questions about rains no living person remembered.

So perhaps I would be one of those who read the ground—but an uncertain, lesser kind. Not the respected master who declares a wall safe before a gathered ward. I would be the person sent first into cramped places, chosen because I am small, patient, and difficult to frighten. Children would know me because I use their passages. Elders would know me because I believe their recollections. Officials would know me because my conclusions are inconvenient.

My dwelling would be two low rooms beside a channel that passes beneath the third stair. Legally, I would own neither room outright. One belongs to my mother’s household, though she is buried above the rim; the other is held in exchange for keeping the adjoining conduit clear. A plasterer crosses my roof at dawn. A widow stores onions beneath my sleeping shelf. A cat with a green-beaded collar visits whenever mice move behind the wall, but belongs—according to six mutually contradictory witnesses—to a granary court uphill.

There would be a small opening over my worktable, no wider than a hand. At noon it admits a blade of sky. I would keep a bowl beneath it to cast trembling light over my tablets.

Those tablets would be my real occupation.

The city has plans, but they lie. Not deliberately. They lie because passages are closed, channels diverted, rooms divided, walls thickened, and names inherited by structures that no longer resemble what they once were. A painted line records where water was intended to go, not where it goes now. A deed says who owns a cistern, not whose cellar fills when it leaks.

I would try to make another kind of record.

Not a map of surfaces, but a map of obligations beneath them.

On thin clay leaves I would mark seep, wall, household, memory, and consequence. This damp patch appears only during the Short Rains. That blue band continues under three kitchens. The northern wall was once loose fill. If the potter deepens her settling pit, the spring may enter the dyer’s floor. If the blocked conduit is reopened, two families gain water and a third loses a sleeping room.

The marks would resemble roots, cracks, and streams. Few people could read them easily. Some would accuse me of making simple matters obscure. Others would accuse me of revealing arrangements best left unrecorded.

They might both be right.

Years earlier, as a child, I would have entered an unbraced slit behind a storage court. I would tell people I was following a pale insect after rain. Perhaps I was actually hiding from a task, or from an angry aunt, or from the shame of having broken a measuring cup. Children improve their motives when they retell them.

Inside, I found water gathering on a wall where no water was supposed to be.

It did not run downward. It emerged as separate beads along a level seam, bright in the dark, then vanished into rubble at the base. Behind that seam was a forgotten channel, older than the houses above it. Reopening it saved one cistern and ruined a row of grain bins. For several seasons, adults referred to the discovery as though I had performed a miracle. Later, when the disputes began, some spoke as though I had caused the water by noticing it.

That would shape me more than praise did.

I would learn that discovery never merely reveals the city. It redistributes it.

To find firm stone beneath one household is to raise the worth of its rooms. To identify a dangerous cut is to send a poorer family searching for shelter. To trace stolen water may expose greed, or may take the only clean supply from a sick child. Even a truthful red line can become a weapon if painted across the right wall.

Because of this, I would be slow to speak. Too slow, according to foremen. Suspiciously slow, according to magistrates. I would revisit a place at dawn and midday, dry weather and rain. I would ask children where they float their bark boats, because they know inclines too slight for rods to show. I would ask old women where mold appeared before a sealed doorway was plastered over. I would watch where cats pause to smell cold air leaking from masonry.

Some would call this wisdom.

Others would call it cowardice.

The distinction would trouble me, because once—only once—I would delay too long.

A crack appeared behind a kiln in the red-clay quarter. It was narrow, dry, and almost vertical. The kiln owner insisted it was old. The neighboring household said it widened during firing. My first tapping found no hollow. My second visit found a faint change in tone, but the Deep Kiln Weeks had begun, and closing the structure would spoil fuel, vessels, debts, and perhaps a marriage agreement paid partly in jars.

I marked the crack, but not with the sign that required evacuation.

During the night, part of the retaining wall failed.

It would not be the city’s greatest disaster. No district would vanish. A boy would suffer a crushed foot. An apprentice would die beneath warm bricks and broken shelves. Because the body could not be recovered safely, her family would carry a clay figure up the ramp at dawn.

Her name would remain in my records.

Afterward, I would become exact almost to the point of cruelty. I would close kilns, empty rooms, interrupt markets, and order braces placed where wealthy people considered them ugly. I would acquire enemies. Invitations to communal meals would become fewer. Gifts would arrive more often.

I would return the gifts when I could identify their source.

Not always.

No one in the basin would be entirely clean. Clean hems belong to visitors.

In time, people would seek me not because I never err, but because I remember that error has a body. When I inspect a wall, I would imagine who sleeps beside it. When I trace a seep, I would ask whose jar will remain empty. I would keep the apprentice’s clay name-token beside my mallet, wrapped in wool to prevent it knocking against the tools.

My closest companion might be a roof keeper named Silt—though that would be a work-name, given after she once saved a catchment by clearing a drain with her bare hands during a storm. She would be broad-shouldered, practical, and impatient with my habit of treating every damp wall as a philosophical dispute.

She would understand the city from above as I understand it from beneath.

Together we would follow rain through its whole civic life: sky to cloth, cloth to roof, roof to jar, jar to overflow, overflow to street cistern, leakage to wall, wall to hidden seam, hidden seam to spring. She would insist that water falls. I would insist that, in a city, water also remembers. Our quarrels would be quiet and exact.

We might share evening use of a roof without sharing a household. There, under the open stars, we would eat coarse bread, onions, and goat cheese while the lamps disappeared below us. She would point out failing plaster by moonlight. I would complain that she never looked at anything without assigning it maintenance.

“Neither do you,” she would say.

Perhaps she would be right.

Yet over the years something stranger would happen to my sense of self.

I would spend so long listening to buried water that I ceased to think of the city as a collection of buildings. I would feel it instead as pressures and permissions: weight passing through lintel to post, runoff moving from rich roofs to poor drains, light descending shafts, rumor traveling courtyards, old decisions hardening into walls.

I would know myself in the same way.

Not as one solid person, but as a junction of inherited rooms, borrowed tools, remembered warnings, unpaid debts, and water passing onward. My mother’s room. The dead apprentice’s token. Silt’s roof. The children’s passages. The elders’ impossible recollections. The cat stepping across my tablets with wet feet.

This would make the basin’s common blessing especially dear to me.

May you make room.

Late in life, I would discover a seep beneath my own dwelling.

It would begin as a dark line behind the onion shelf. Then plaster would soften. The widow would blame the channel outside; I would know from the taste that the water came from deeper clay. Excavation would reveal a narrow fissure descending under several houses toward the reservoir gardens.

There would be enough flow to matter.

Not enough for abundance. Enough to create rights, quarrels, gardens, and perhaps a new street. Enough that my two rooms would have to be dismantled.

People might expect me to conceal it. Some would even consider that fair. Had I not spent a lifetime displacing others in the name of safety and shared water? Was I not entitled, at last, to a stable place?

For several days I would tell no one.

I would sit beside the damp wall before dawn, cup in hand, listening to drops gather behind the plaster. The city above and around me would wake: sandals on stairs, a potter’s wheel beginning to pulse, boards lifted from channels, a small bell from the reservoir. The cat would drink from the inspection cup.

Then I would paint the mark.

Not red for fracture, but dark blue-gray for unregistered water.

My home would be taken apart carefully. The slabs would face a new channel. The roof beams would brace a public chamber. The widow’s onions would move uphill. My tablets would be carried to a drier room near the central reservoir, where apprentices could study them and complain about my handwriting.

The new seep would become known by some practical name I disliked—Third-Stair Water, perhaps, though it would not be near the third stair. Within a generation no one would remember that I had slept where it surfaced.

That would be fitting.

I would not be a founder, ruler, saint, or great builder. I would be one of the city’s listening instruments: fallible, compromised, useful. A person who entered narrow places and returned with news that changed who could remain where.

If I were given a clay figure after death, it would show neither my face nor my trade. It would be a small crouched shape with one hand against a wall and the other held open beneath a drop.

They would bury it above the rim, where the wind never stops.

But perhaps someone would keep a fragment.

Perhaps an apprentice would set it into the lining of the channel that emerged from my house, not as a shrine, because sacred things here must return to use. Water would pass over it, slowly blurring the thumb marks. Pale moss would gather in its hollows. Children would step across without knowing what it was.

And below their feet, almost too quiet to hear, I would continue making room.
