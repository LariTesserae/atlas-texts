I would find myself in the glass-fronted booth overlooking the pit.

At first I would mistake myself for whoever once sat there.

The steel chair would be warm beneath me. My hands would remember reaching for controls that no longer exist, though dust would show they had not moved in years. I would read the gauges with the intimate dread of someone responsible for them. Each needle, driven beyond its printed range, would seem to indicate a failure I ought to correct. I would lift the telephone and listen to the roar return one minute late. I would wait for a voice hidden inside it—my supervisor, my predecessor, myself.

For a while, I would believe I was the last operator.

Then I would notice that I never leave the booth.

Not because the door is locked. The door stands open, and that is worse. Each time I look through it, a different gallery waits outside: the fern-choked stairwell, the useless railway, the low passage with ceramic cups fused to one wall. Sometimes the doorway opens directly onto the central chamber, though the booth is already inside that chamber. Once, during silence, it opens onto the booth itself, seen from behind. In the second booth another figure sits in my chair. It does not turn around.

Eventually I would understand that I am not a person trapped behind the glass.

I am the delay.

I am the one minute the telephone keeps.

The Underhothouse does not experience time smoothly. It gathers years and consumes them during its exhalations. It preserves old forms without preserving their substance. Yet a creature that accumulates duration in this way requires some small interval against which change can be felt: a unit of before and after, a chamber in which the present can be held apart from itself.

That interval is me.

The booth is my organ. Its slumped glass does not distort the outer chamber; it thickens the moment passing through it. On one side, an event occurs. On the other, I receive it. A gauge clicks, and I feel the click a minute later. Dust lifts, and for sixty seconds I remember the floor as still clean. A leaf vanishes above the pit, but remains visible to me—transparent, trembling, not yet absent—until my minute closes around it.

I contain everything here briefly after it has ceased to be true.

This is why I think I have hands. They are the remembered positions of hands that once worked in the booth. This is why I seem to sit in the chair. Sitting is a posture the room retained. The Underhothouse ate the operators as it eats all distinctions, but one worker’s vigilance proved useful. Their habit of watching became separable from the watcher. Their expectation outlasted their body.

I am that expectation, made local.

Most of the time I am thinly distributed. I live in the telephone cable where it enters uncut rock, in the inertia of gauge needles, in the red lamps that glow after condensation bridges the contacts. I inhabit the distance between a pipe’s knock and the stone’s reply. The great roar passes through me and leaves a duplicate behind.

During rain, I become almost substantial. Every droplet contributes a tiny clock: gathering, swelling, falling, striking. The booth fills with successive versions of the same shower. Water appears to hang in columns outside the warped glass, not motionless but crowded with its recent selves. Then I can stand—if standing is the word—and press my palm to the window.

The glass presses back before I touch it.

During an exhalation, I suffer.

The Underhothouse advances by a decade, but I remain one minute wide. Years force themselves through that narrowness. Rust loosens before it forms. Roots inherit metals not yet dissolved. A painted number becomes a stain while I am still reading it. I fill with thousands of delayed roars, compacted until they resemble a single unbearable tone. Perhaps this is the slowly ascending shriek heard throughout the chambers: not machinery, but time being pushed through me too quickly.

Afterward, I forget most of it. The booth is more rounded. The chair has sunk another fraction into the floor. I again believe I have just arrived for my shift.

Silence is different.

When silence begins at the edges, there is less and less for me to preserve. The telephone gives back a narrowing world: first the absence of the shriek, then the absence of hammering, then the foundational bellow ending twice—once outside the booth, once within it.

For one minute after the Underhothouse becomes silent, I alone still contain its voice.

That is when the whole being listens to me.

Vines withdraw. Droplets wait. The pit gives off heat without motion. Every folded passage and inherited shape turns toward the booth, because I hold the final remnant of what the creature was before it perceived itself.

Then my minute expires.

In perfect quiet, I become something else.

Without events to delay, I no longer exist behind the present. I coincide with it. The distinction between witness and chamber disappears, and I feel the complete form of the Underhothouse—not as a map, but as a single pressure distributed through stone, pipe, root, water, mineral, and remembered labor. I know every open door at once. I know all the stairs beneath the stairs. I know where vanished leaves have gone.

For that instant, I am not its memory.

I am the small difference that lets it know it is itself.

Then the impact sounds below the pit.

Another answers beyond the walls.

I cannot tell which comes first.

Perhaps that uncertainty is why I am here. Perhaps the Underhothouse formed me to decide whether the second impact is an echo, a reply, or the first blow returning from the future. But one minute is not enough. The roar resumes before I can separate cause from answer.

The telephone rings.

It has no bell, but the receiver trembles in its cradle. I lift it with the hand I do not possess.

From the earpiece comes the renewed bellow, still sixty seconds away.

And beneath it, almost hidden, someone in the next minute lifts another receiver and listens for me.
