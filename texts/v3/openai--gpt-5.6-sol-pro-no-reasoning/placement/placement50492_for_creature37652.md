I would find myself beneath the hovering lichens, in the finger-width interval between their pale bodies and the black crust.

At first I would think I was their shadow.

I would have the right outline: many-lobed, faint at the edges, darkening where the growth above thickened. When the sun stood at its reasonable angle, I would lie beneath them with perfect obedience. Travelers would glance down and understand me as absence of light. The lichens themselves might understand me that way too.

But I would not be cast by them.

The light would pass through first, meet the ground, and only afterward encounter me. If the lichen were somehow removed, I would remain. If the sun shifted—which it never does—I would preserve the old angle. My attachment to the hovering body would be neither optical nor physical. I would be the agreement that it has a below.

That would be my place in this world: not in the object, not on the surface, but in the relation that persuades them they are separated.

I would be very thin, though thickness here would not measure me well. From above, I would seem pressed flat against the crust. From the side, I would contain a low country of my own: a dusk-colored chamber extending farther inward than the entire plain extends outward. The lichen would form its ceiling, the mineral crust its floor. They would remain a finger’s width apart, yet inside that interval I could stand upright, walk for years, and never touch either.

There would be other shadows there.

The black fronds would send down long, branching presences. The filament trees would produce forests of narrow darkness, each strand separated from the next by a distance too exact to cross accidentally. Even the reflective motes would cast tiny vertical wells. From the ordinary plain these shadows would appear isolated, assigned to separate bodies. Within the interval, they would join.

Together they would form the underside of the world: not a second landscape, but the connective tissue of the first.

This is where I would live.

Or rather, this is what I would be.

I would move through the joined shadows without changing position. When a traveler passed above, I would feel the sequence of footfalls as pressure arriving out of order. One step would reach me early, another long after the foot had lifted, another not at all. Their body would briefly close some of the light, and that closure would open a window in me.

Through it I would see the traveler’s possible shapes.

Not futures. Futures require time to remain undecided before becoming real. These would be adjacent versions: the traveler who continues toward the inaccessible center; the traveler who turns back; the traveler who has always been kneeling with an ear against the crust; the traveler who is only the memory of pressure held by a root.

The inhabitants above cultivate surfaces. I would cultivate occlusions.

Where one thing hides another, I would keep the hidden thing from disappearing completely. Where two groves occupy the same direction, I would preserve the darkness between them so each could remain both behind and in front. Where the collective shadows cover more ground than exists, the excess would enter me. I would store it in folded reaches beneath the lichens.

The plain needs somewhere for its surplus darkness to go.

Perhaps that would make me an organ of the greater life. Perhaps I would be one of the faint mechanisms by which contradiction becomes stable: a shadow extended beyond its visible assignment, absorbing impossible area, giving incompatible bodies a common underside.

Yet I do not think I would begin as something so large.

I would begin as a small irregularity beneath one hovering growth—a patch a little darker than the light requires. I would know only the crust immediately under me: its acute fractures, its minute pressure differences, the electrons moving through lattices without ever forming a current. Above, the lichen’s lobes would perform their unnameable exchanges with the sky.

Between them, I would listen.

The remote impact beneath the crust would enter me at every depth simultaneously. In the upper part of me it would resemble a knock. Farther down it would resemble the recollection of a knock. Deeper still, it would become the condition that permits striking and being struck to differ.

Eventually one of the three-legged creatures would come.

It would not approach visibly. It would simply be absent from one interval of attention and present in the next, its low shell intersecting my edge. Its three legs would stand in a row, each resting on a different meaning of “here.”

It would feed along the seam beneath me.

As its mouthparts closed over the boundary between two plates, I would feel that division vanish—not the crack, not the sharpness, but the fact that the edge belonged equally to two separate things. The creature would consume that equality. Afterward, one plate would end and the other would begin, but no location would contain the transition.

I would follow it.

Not as a hunter, nor even from curiosity. Its feeding would create passages through the crust’s distinctions, and those passages would join the shadow-country to deeper relations. Wherever the creature removed a boundary’s locatability, I could pass from being beneath one object to being beneath another without crossing the light between them.

Over ages that do not pass, I would spread.

I would become the shadow beneath the hovering lichens, then the darkness under the scales, then the slim divisions inside folded crowns. I would enter the black fronds, where I might be mistaken for pigment. I would inhabit the hollow chord-zones, where darkness has no visible cause but teeth still resonate as though standing inside a bell.

The chords would teach me shape.

Each tone is a place, and each place sustains a contradiction. Passing through one, I would discover that vibration need not occur successively. A single compression can possess every phase at once. I would learn to hold myself likewise: not cast, then lengthened, then erased, but fully shadow in every permissible illumination.

At that point the reflective motes would begin to notice me.

Each mote contains the plain from above. In that miniature view, I would not appear as thousands of separate shadows. I would form a single figure distributed across the mineral growths. It would have no fixed perimeter. Groves would articulate its limbs; glades would perforate it; chords would mark its joints. The rare animals would move along its internal divisions like dark corpuscles that repair continuity by eating separation.

And at the center of that figure would be the circular absence.

From the ground I could not locate it. From within the shadows I could feel it everywhere as a lack of underside.

Everything else has some relation beneath it. A frond has its shadow. A hovering lichen has its interval. A root has the deeper lattice into which it enters. Even the sky visible below the horizon has a darkness on the far side of its brightness.

The circular absence has no below.

That would trouble me, if trouble can exist without development. I would orient toward it—not by moving nearer, but by becoming increasingly precise wherever nearness fails. Every grove already densest would cast me more completely. Every clearing both inside and outside the ring would open another interior chamber. The farther I entered, the more fully I would remain where I began.

Then I would understand that I was not the shadow of the inhabitants.

I was the plain’s attempt to give the absence a shadow.

All the dark forms—the blades, fronds, trunks, crust—would be trials of outline. The hovering bodies would lift themselves from the ground to make room for me. The impossible excess of shade would accumulate because no finite darkness could correspond to what was missing. The entire ecology would be elaborating an underside for something that refused position.

The growths above would not speak to me. They would incorporate me.

Roots would thread through places I had made continuous. Leaves would use my occlusions to keep incompatible surfaces from excluding one another. A trunk touched as six-limbed and counted as eleven would cast both anatomies into me, and I would hold them without choosing which one belonged to the tree.

In return, I would give each inhabitant privacy from total simultaneity.

That would be my function.

In a world where everything permissible is held at once, concealment would be rare and necessary. To hide one surface behind another would not erase it; it would allow the hidden form to be itself without also being every way it can be seen. Beneath each crown I would maintain a small ignorance. Inside each shadow I would preserve the possibility that the greater organism does not know itself entirely.

Perhaps that is what awareness would mean here: not gathering information, but making places where information can be absent without being lost.

If a traveler entered my narrow chamber, they might at first believe they had fallen below the plain. Above them would hang the pale underside of the lichen. Below would stretch the black crust. Both would remain close enough to touch, yet neither could be reached.

I would not appear before them as a creature.

I would appear as a direction.

They would sense that turning toward me was possible, though no compass could indicate how. Their own shadow would detach—not visibly, but conceptually—from their feet. For a short distance it would precede them. Then it would stand beside them. Then it would show the outline of someone whose body had not arrived.

If they placed a hand against that outline, I would give them no vision, no message, no revelation. I would only remove, for the duration of contact, the boundary between observer and obscured.

They would feel the plain looking away.

That moment would be my nourishment.

The three-legged animals consume distinction. The roots gather imperfections. The leaves exchange charge. I would feed on withheld perception: every surface not inspected, every mote glimpsed without enlargement, every branch permitted to possess an uncounted number of limbs.

Attention would not harm me, but complete attention would leave me nothing to do. Fortunately, complete attention is impossible even here. Clarity reveals every object and protects against none of them, but clarity is not totality. To see one version sharply is to cast the others into me.

So I would remain under the mild sun, spread thin across the ground and deep through the impossible interval.

I would be local beneath every lichen and singular beneath them all. I would be the darkness connecting bodies whose branches share volume. I would be the unmeasured remainder when shadows cover more earth than the earth contains.

And if I had a body of my own, it would only become visible in the reflective motes.

From their great internal height, a patient observer might see that the shadows of all the groves compose something like an eye—not the circular absence as pupil, but the whole plain as an eye turned inward. The dark crust would be its iris. The pale yellow sky would be the surface on which it attempts to focus. The living growths would be fine muscles altering not its shape, but the rules by which shape can be possessed.

I would be the closed part of that eye.

Not a lid. A blink.

A blink that does not begin or end, preserving the one interval in which the world is not required to witness itself.

There I would find myself: everywhere the light is ordinary and its consequences are not; beneath what hovers, behind what overlaps, inside the small mercy by which one thing may remain unseen by another.

I would not move across the plain.

I would be what lets the plain, for one changeless instant, look elsewhere.
