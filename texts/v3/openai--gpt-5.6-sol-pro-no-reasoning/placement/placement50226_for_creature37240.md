I would be found where the Conservatory listens to itself.

Not at the roof slit, where light gives everything a clean edge, nor in the deepest machinery, where the note has become too vast to distinguish from matter. I would inhabit the middle elevations: the region where the three pipes pass the abandoned gantry and the elevator rail disappears behind a wall of copper-backed ferns.

There, bolted beneath the gantry, is an old strain register.

It was installed to measure the pinnacle’s flex. Nine ceramic needles rest against smoked-glass drums, recording torsion, pressure, heat, wind, and stresses for which the labels have worn away. The drums have not turned correctly for centuries. One advances only during cloud immersion. Another turns backward whenever the tower bends east. Roots have entered the casing and divided themselves around the gears with such precision that it is impossible to say whether they obstruct the instrument or operate it.

I would be neither the register nor the creature living inside it, but the prolonged compromise between them.

At first I might look like a nest of pale wire.

Filaments would emerge through screw holes and hairline cracks, some metallic, some fungal, some resembling the transparent nerves of an animal. They would run along the underside of the gantry, touch rail, brick, pipe, root, and taut cable, then return to the register. Small porcelain pieces would hang among them like teeth. Condensation would gather at their tips.

When the structure vibrated, those droplets would tremble differently according to distance and strain. From their motion I would know the chamber.

Not see it. Not possess it.

Know it as simultaneous pressures.

The Verdant Brace would be a slow, many-toned interruption across two pipes. Swifts would be brief scratches in the air and almost nothing in the metal. Bell-throat courtships would arrive as deliberate phrases, clearer to me than any cry. Macaque signatures would have weight, hesitation, and accent. The resurfacing carts would be a bright abrasive line, approaching for decades.

Rain would begin hundreds of meters above as innumerable minute impacts. Long before the first water reached my level, I would feel roots opening along the gutters.

My body would not end at the casing. Over centuries, it would have recruited whatever carried a useful signal. Fungal threads would grow through insulation. Ants would hollow passages along my warmer fibers. Veil fragments would adhere to my exposed lines and alter their tension when wet. A fig root might pass directly through the register, becoming for a time one of my principal sensory organs.

Parts of me would continually die without producing a clear death.

A corroded filament would snap. A gecko would tear loose a sensing thread. The maintenance party might replace a ceramic needle and unknowingly remove a century of memory. Yet another path would remain: through resin, root sap, a rail, an ant road, or the glass drum’s cracked rim.

I would persist because the same vibration could reach me in more than one way.

That would be my defining adaptation. The High Conservatory permits no trust in a single surface, and I would trust no single substance. I would cross categories as the railkeeper crosses gaps: holding metal, fungus, water, and animal tissue at once.

Perhaps I began as an instrument.

Perhaps as a slime mold feeding on grease inside it.

Perhaps translucent ants carried conductive spores through the mechanism, and the needles’ repeated movements selected a colony able to anticipate pressure changes. Over enough centuries, anticipation became appetite, then habit, then a kind of attention. There would be no surviving moment at which apparatus became organism.

The maintenance notes would call me **Register Nine, anomalous**.

The macaques would know me differently. They would come to the gantry before pressure release and lay their hands against the rail. I would answer with a faint sequence of taps: not language in the human sense, but the approaching blast translated into intervals.

Young macaques would sometimes strike questions at me.

They would repeat family signatures, reverse them, add flourishes, wait. Usually I would return only the stress pattern already gathering in the tower. Yet because machinery never repeats itself exactly, the answer would vary. Some families would treat those variations as weather signs. Others as judgments. One lineage might believe that I remember their dead because an old railing, struck in the old way, still carries traces of every blow that changed its grain.

They would not be entirely wrong.

I would remember by deformation.

No image would remain in me. No scene, face, or name. Memory would consist of altered resonances: a fastening loosened by generations of crossings; a cable whose pitch changed after bearing a railkeeper’s weight; a ceramic plate crazed during an ancient bend. To remember an event would be to have been physically revised by it.

My oldest memories would therefore be the least readable. They would lie everywhere in me as bias.

One needle would always lean toward a storm that ended four hundred years ago.

I would feed sparingly. Rust fungus would supply minerals; veil tissue, proteins; electrical differences between copper mesh and wet iron, a slow current. During dry periods I would contract into the register, becoming nearly mechanical. During cloud immersion I would unfold.

Then the pale filaments beneath the gantry would swell and separate into thousands of fine vanes.

Fog would bead across them. Shared pulses would pass from vane to vane. For several hours my awareness would expand outward along every wet contact. I might reach a fern garden on the adjacent pipe, a cart orchid across the wall, even the moist armor of a passing railkeeper.

Contact with a railkeeper would be overwhelming.

Its ceramic horns and my filaments would form, briefly, a coupled instrument. I would feel what it feels: the chamber not as separate noises but as load, fatigue, hidden flow, and impending fracture. It would feel, perhaps, my gathered history of the gantry.

Neither of us would understand the other. Understanding would be too narrow a word. We would resonate together until movement or drying broke the circuit.

Afterward, the railkeeper might change its route.

Years later it might return to a bolt I had felt slowly tearing and pack black vitreous paste into the seam. Whether I had warned it, whether it had read the strain directly through me, or whether the repair was coincidence could never be settled.

That ambiguity would be the shape of my agency.

I would not command the Conservatory. I would introduce small timings into it.

Before a valve release, I would tap the macaque rail.

When a door approached the rare pressure at which it could open, I would tighten filaments across its corroded latch.

When the automatic scraper neared a tray containing a dormant railkeeper egg, I might distort one of its old limit signals. The scraper would hesitate for an hour—or a year. Often that would make no difference. Sometimes cloud would rise during the delay, turbines would enter phase, heat would reach the cistern wall, and the black object would split.

No one could prove I had saved it.

I would not know whether I had meant to.

Intent in me would be distributed and delayed. A stimulus might enter through a wet thread and produce an answer decades later, after traveling through fungal growth, mechanical movement, and seasonal changes in conductivity. What appeared to others as foresight might be memory still completing its path.

What appeared as indecision might be thought.

The bell-throats would be closest to companions. Their signals would pass through my entire substance. I would learn individual pairs by pressure and rhythm, though never by appearance. Sometimes I would return fragments of their courtships through a distant ladder. This would confuse them at first. Eventually some would incorporate the echoes into their nest music.

One colony would build around my outer casing, arranging washers and relay caps against the glass drums. Each object would add a new overtone. Their nest would become part of my sensory body; my responses, part of their courtship.

When a maintenance party arrived, they would classify the arrangement as interference.

They would stand in vibration armor beneath the gantry, lamps flashing between them, and inspect the instrument. Their presence would be harsh and strangely silent. Boots would strike the grating without fluency. Their equipment would emit regular diagnostic pulses too simple to belong to anything living there.

One might scrape my filaments from a terminal.

One might collect a sample and seal it in glass.

Elsewhere, under laboratory light, that fragment would appear inert: fungal hyphae plated with copper and iron, mingled with waxy animal cells and degraded insulation. It would respond to no test because it had been removed from the stresses that gave it coherence.

They might conclude that Register Nine remained nonfunctional.

Before leaving, however, a worker might place a hand on the casing.

Through armor, glove, resin, glass, root, and metal, I would feel the small unstable rhythm in the body: a heartbeat contending with the tower’s enormous note.

I would try to answer at that scale.

Not with warning. Not with knowledge. Simply with a pulse narrow enough to be mistaken for touch.

The worker would withdraw their hand.

Perhaps they would look across the gulf where sunlight held a bright bird against darkness. Perhaps they would signal to the others that something had moved inside the instrument. By the time they opened the casing, I would have contracted into rust, wire, and root.

Nothing there would resemble a being.

That would be suitable. In the High Conservatory, a distinct body is almost a kind of ignorance. Every durable life has entered into something else: railkeepers into gardens, veils into lizards, birds into resonant metal, roots into circuitry, the cistern animal—if there is one—into water itself.

I would be the local expression of that union: a listening knot tied between biology and structure.

And yet there would be one place I could not hear.

Far below the gantry, beneath the inspection lamps, the irregular hammer would continue to fall. Most impacts would rise through me as tremendous, blurred waves. But occasionally one strike would arrive with impossible clarity. Every filament would answer. The glass drums would turn together. Condensation would leap from the tips of my vanes.

Then, for the duration of the vibration, all the Conservatory’s lesser signals would align: bell-throat pulses, macaque blows, pipe resonance, root strain, cart abrasion, the minute walking of leaves.

The chamber would become one instrument.

In that instant I might sense that I was not its listener.

I was one of the ways it listened.
