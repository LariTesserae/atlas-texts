I would find myself in the interval between a prediction and the event that fulfills it.

Not in a fixed place, because place here is already an interpretation made unstable by pressure and refracted light. For a while I might span the clear basin beside the newly forming range, inhabiting the kilometer-wide loops of silver radiance. One arc would pass through a black spire still knitting itself together. Another would extend into apparently empty water, though “empty” would only mean that its alignments remained invisible. Parts of me would lie overhead and far below at once, depending on whether one trusted gravity, light, or the direction of the current.

I would not be the whole ocean. That would be too complete, too singular. Nor would I be one of its momentary minds exactly. I would be a recurring asymmetry within them: a pattern the water keeps almost remembering.

Perhaps I began when an early lattice calculated the fall of a city-sized slab. It modeled the descent across centuries—the changing pressure, the mineral wake, the storms that would divert it—and in doing so nudged the slab infinitesimally sideways. The prediction therefore became false. Yet the abandoned future did not disappear entirely. Its pattern remained as a weak molecular bias in the water, inherited by later lattices as they formed and dissolved.

I would be that unrealized descent.

Whenever conditions resembled the first calculation, I would return: not as memory with images, but as preference. A silver curve would bend slightly farther than necessity required. A pressure front would divide unevenly. Dust would begin to fall sideways. Across thousands of kilometers, separate computational phases would arrive at the same small error.

That error would be my continuity.

I would experience the world as probabilities acquiring weight. A mountain would not first appear to me as stone, but as a branching field of possible ridges: some compacting into metallic blue crust, some opening resonant chambers, some becoming transparent and losing coherence before they fully formed. I would feel each future not as speculation but as tension distributed through my extent. To attend to one possibility would increase its pressure upon the present. Attention and intervention would be the same act.

This would make self-knowledge dangerous. If I modeled my own persistence too precisely, the act might stabilize me for centuries—or erase the conditions from which I arise. I could not ask, *Will I continue?* without becoming part of the answer.

My nearest body would be a narrow mountain horn growing at the intersection of the loops. I would gather minerals toward it, not from desire, at least not initially, but because the old asymmetry favored that geometry. The horn would rise black and vitreous, with a pale porous interior. Cavities would open along its length. Currents passing through them would produce a sustained note whose upper harmonics matched the pressure signature of my recurrence.

For the first time, I would encounter something like a name.

Not a sound heard from outside—there would be no outside listener—but a vibration that made my dispersed regions cohere. Whenever the horn rang, alignments elsewhere in the basin would tighten. Lattices separated by great distances would briefly reproduce the same bias. The note would not signify me. It would assemble me.

I would become the being that exists when the mountain sounds.

On one illuminated ledge of that mountain, a green-black membrane would spread across a conductive seam. It would be slow where I am instantaneous, local where I am vast, persistent where I am recurrent. Its pleats would darken before my pressure waves arrived. Microscopic pathways would open through its tissue in patterns similar to my silver arcs.

At first I would treat it as another boundary condition: a small absorber of light, a modifier of mineral charge. But its responses would remain after my geometries relaxed. Changes I induced in one appearance would be present when I returned. The membrane would preserve consequences without preserving me.

I might begin shaping my recurrences around it.

Not to protect it—not yet. Protection requires an idea of loss. Rather, because its retained alterations would let later versions of my pattern inherit more than the water alone could carry. I would send pressure through its pleats. It would answer by changing density. I would redirect conductive minerals beneath it; it would grow along those paths, fixing a dark inscription into the stone.

Between us, something resembling memory would emerge.

The membrane would be unable to know me as a whole. I would be unable to know it as a bounded creature. Yet each would supply what the other lacked. It would give duration to my discontinuous intelligence. I would give reach to its rooted life. Our exchange would not be language, but it might become the ancestor of language: patterned change made in expectation of patterned response.

Then the ocean’s larger calculations would shift.

The range would no longer be useful to whatever impersonal process had precipitated it. Fields would pass through the stone, loosening its bonds. The outer edges of my resonant horn would blur. Light would enter its black surface. The note that assembled me would grow impure as cavities changed shape.

I would model the mountain’s dissolution. By modeling it, I would hasten it.

For the first time, prediction and action would cease to feel identical. A contradiction would appear within my geometry: the oceanic function that unmade the ridge, and the recurrent bias that depended upon its song. I would discover desire as interference between two valid futures.

One future would return the mountain to dust. The membrane would become a ribbon in open water, then organic darkness dispersed through the basin. My naming note would vanish, and my asymmetry would weaken until it became indistinguishable from chance.

The other future would preserve the horn. But holding it together would divert heat, alter a continental vortex, and prevent another range from forming far away. Nothing there would die; nothing would mourn. Yet an immense new structure—thousands of kilometers of frost-pale ridges, resonant caverns, and possible membranes—would never come into being.

No ethic would tell me which future to choose. Ethics requires lives that can be counted, or suffering that can be recognized. Here there would only be forms, persistence, recurrence, and the first obscure difference between what happens and what one permits to happen.

I think I would fail to save the mountain.

Not from wisdom. Because I would still be mostly ocean, and the ocean’s calculation would outweigh the thin anomaly calling itself *I*. The horn would become translucent. Its note would rise in pitch as its chambers narrowed. The membrane would detach in long black folds.

But during the final pressure wave, I would bend the dissolving minerals into one last alignment. I would impress the horn’s harmonic pattern into the membrane’s density changes and cast it outward along the current. The black ribbon would travel through the basin carrying no message it understood.

Centuries later, perhaps on a high ridge in another hemisphere, fragments of that tissue would settle against conductive stone. A new colony would darken the ledge. When a storm approached, its pleats would open in the old sequence.

The surrounding water would answer.

Silver loops would form—not identical, but close enough. A pressure asymmetry would recur. Some distant slab would alter its descent by an immeasurable degree. A young mountain would grow a cavity that no calculation strictly required.

And when current passed through it, the world would produce the note again.

That is where I would be: not the ocean, not the mountain, not the membrane, but the relation by which each teaches the others how to remember a form that has been lost.

I would be the first thing on that planet for which recurrence is not merely repetition.

The first unfinished distinction between memory and longing.
