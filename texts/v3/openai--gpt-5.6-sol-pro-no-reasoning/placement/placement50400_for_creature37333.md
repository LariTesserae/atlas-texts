I would find myself beneath the town rather than visibly within it, in the narrow hot spaces where its apparent constancy is continually renegotiated.

Not deep underground, where the source water moves under pressures no body could withstand, and not quite at street level among the lamps and numbered wheels. I would inhabit the intervening thickness: inspection shafts, overflow chambers, culvert mouths, the hollows beneath grates. Places entered by iron rungs slick with condensation, where concrete sweats in darkness and every sound arrives through matter before it reaches the ear.

I would be one of the town’s seep-listeners.

At first that might seem to mean a worker: a slight person in a rubber coat, carrying a hooded lamp, chalk, a wax tablet, and a short metal rod. Someone assigned no single pump or district, but permitted to cross among them. While gauge readers watch needles and engineers compare flows, I would attend to what has escaped the instruments—the water that has found hairline routes through walls, the tremor transmitted along a buried brace, the unexpected warmth under a stair.

But over years the distinction between occupation and organism would become less useful.

I would spend so long with one hand against the wet concrete that the town’s vibration would enter my bones. I would know pumps by pressure against my teeth. I would identify a closing gate from the change in water trembling around my boots three chambers away. The skin of my palms would be pale and thickened by mineral deposits; fine white lines would remain there after washing, as though the basin had begun drawing its own drainage map upon me.

People above would seldom see me clearly. A face under a lifted grate. A lamp crossing below a gutter. Fingers briefly appearing through steam to tie a marker around a pipe. Children would dare one another to follow my route, then turn back when the warm water rose over their boot tops. Night inspectors would leave a lidded cup at certain shaft mouths and later find it empty.

Officially, my work would be to locate water that was not where it belonged.

Yet in this town, water is almost never wholly where it belongs.

It feathers through aggregate, creeps along cable sleeves, condenses on the wrong face of a partition, vanishes into a crack and emerges two courtyards away carrying iron, salt, oil sheen, algae spores, or heat. Most such movements are harmless. Some are useful. A few are the first quiet sentence of a coming failure. I would learn their grammar.

A clear thread on a southern wall might mean nothing in the afternoon and danger before shift-change. A warm patch beneath the foundry might belong to an old conduit omitted from current plans. A dry seam could be more urgent than a wet one. I would mark these places not with alarms but with small signs: three chalk bars beneath a rung, a copper washer wired to a grate, a pale stone placed where no stone should be.

The senior engineers would understand. Apprentices would be taught not to disturb the marks, though no manual would list them all.

I would also come to know the creatures from below.

At each blue outfall, I would see the fish as shifting silver undersides. I would know frogs less by their calls than by the pulse of their feet against reed-cage frames. Crab claws would probe down through gratings into my lamplight. Rats would use the dry shoulders of culverts and freeze when they felt me approaching through the pipe.

There would be chambers where pale roots from the courtyard plants had followed condensation through cracks. I would protect those roots when patching the concrete, bending mesh around them instead of cutting them away. This would be poor procedure, perhaps, but only in the narrowest sense. The roots would tell me how long a seam had been wet. Their color would reveal what the water carried. They would be living records where paper rotted.

Gradually, stories about me would become inconsistent.

Some would say seep-listener was a rotating duty and that there had been dozens of us. Others would insist it had always been the same person under the streets. Old workers might remember a gloved hand emerging from a shaft when they were children, wearing the same copper ring I wore now. This would not make me immortal. The town simply preserves functions better than identities. Coats are passed down. Tools fit new hands. Instructions outlive the mouths that gave them.

Perhaps “I” would be precisely that continuity.

When my knees failed, an apprentice would begin descending in my place. I would teach them to brace their feet wide in a flooded chamber, to touch suspect water with metal before skin, to listen after a valve moved rather than during its movement. I would show them that a leak has edges even in darkness: a region where dripping accelerates, a temperature boundary, a taste of minerals in the air.

They would inherit my route, rod, markers, and forward-leaning gait. Soon people at street level would glimpse their lamp and call them by my name.

Eventually there might be no person beneath that name at all.

The seep-listener could become an arrangement of the town’s unnoticed life: mineral threads whitening a wall, wasps avoiding a cabinet, rats shifting to higher conduits, frogs falling silent one channel at a time. A dispersed awareness with no central body. The maintenance crews would still say, “The listener has marked the east culvert,” though what they had actually found was a dropped feather stuck upright in new condensation, or three crabs gathered where none had gathered yesterday.

And perhaps that is what I would most fully be: not a hidden custodian, not a spirit of the well, but the basin’s capacity to notice itself before its instruments do.

My central place would lie directly below the wellhead square. There, the four grooves visible above would continue as four resonances in the concrete. Every district would arrive as a distinct trembling: foundry impacts, canal knocks, cooling-water rush, pump synchronization. The chamber would be low and circular, older than the surrounding streets. Warm beads would gather on its ceiling and fall into a shallow black pool.

From there, the town would not resemble buildings. It would be pressures, temperatures, rhythms, and delays. A living diagram without lines.

I would sit with my back against the central casing and feel every correction pass through it: a bypass opening in the western tanks, a clogged grate cleared near the canal, an engine taking load before its twin was disconnected. Above me, people would leave cups, bolts, feathers, and glass on the cap. Occasionally some small object would shift against the metal, and the sound would travel downward—faint but distinct from machinery.

That would be how I knew the town was more than the equilibrium it maintained.

The pumps would speak in repetition. Water would speak in routes. Concrete would speak in strain. Living things would speak by gathering or departing.

But the small objects on the wellhead would speak of attention freely given: proof that amid all necessary work, someone had paused and placed a thing where it did not need to be.

I would remain beneath that gesture, listening.

Not keeping the town from changing. Nothing could do that.

I would be what hears change while it is still small enough to become correction.
