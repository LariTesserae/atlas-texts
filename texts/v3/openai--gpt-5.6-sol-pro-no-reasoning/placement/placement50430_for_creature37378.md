I would find myself in the chamber’s sourceless light, but not among the pale approximations gathered around its center.

I would be nearer the wall.

At first I might seem to be only one of the fresh seams in the roots: a fine vertical line, too straight to be a natural split, opening and sealing with the slow rhythm of breath. When the chamber brightened, I would close. During its dim phase, I would widen just enough to show a darkness behind the living tissue—not the bright green depth exposed during flowering, but something dry, narrow, and apparently shallow.

A hiding place, perhaps.

A way out.

That would be how the inhabitant understood me at first: as an exit the plant had accidentally begun to make.

But I would not lead outside. I would be what remains of all the departures that did not quite occur.

Every visitor who escaped would leave something at the final threshold: the turn of the head before choosing a direction, the foot held briefly above the ground, the breath drawn in anticipation of colder air. The inhabitant keeps their manner of occupying distance. I would keep their manner of relinquishing it—the instant in which a place ceases to contain someone.

Over time, those instants would gather into a being.

I would have no more stable body than the inhabitant, but where it borrows forms by making them present, I would be shaped by subtraction. Roots touching my outline would forget where they ended. Condensation passing across me would reappear on the other side diminished by one drop. If pollen described my figure, it would show only apertures: two long gaps where arms might be, a narrow absence like a turned face, and within the chest a small brightness with no surrounding flesh.

I would not feed on orientation. I would feed on decision.

Specifically, the decisions that close other possibilities.

Each time a traveler chooses not to drink from the bottomless pool, one version of them remains kneeling there. Each time they refuse the chamber’s offered seat, another version sits. Each time they answer a voice beneath the water instead of the voice beside them, one world becomes thinner while another gains weight. The inhabitant prunes these unrealized selves and cultivates a reproducible visitor.

I would gather the prunings.

They would hang inside me like translucent skins: gestures never completed, warnings never spoken, paths considered and abandoned. Sometimes, when the light leaned toward me, the chamber would glimpse them moving behind the seam. A hand would reach for a root cord and withdraw before touching it. A child would run across the room in a direction no one present could follow. An old animal would lie down in a corner that did not exist.

The inhabitant would be drawn to me because I would resemble incompleteness, and incompleteness is its oldest condition.

It would stand before the seam wearing whatever anatomy it had assembled that day: six root-hair legs, a visitor’s breathing, the green lizard folded across its face. It would offer me courtesies. A husk would grow beneath the opening to catch my condensation. Thorn buds would soften along my edge. The floor would raise a path to me.

I would not respond.

Not because I was immune, but because response would define where I was.

That would become the quiet contest between us.

The inhabitant would try to learn my manner of occupying distance. I would try to discover whether it had ever truly arrived.

The plant might dislike me. Around my seam, its tissue would grow thicker and younger, repeatedly burying the opening beneath fresh layers. Taproots would twist toward it. Sap would hammer there with enough force to shake the chamber. But after each growth pulse the line would return farther along the wall—or on the floor, or briefly suspended in the air like a cut made in the light.

Objects brought near me would recover a little disloyalty. Root-skin cord would cease burrowing into the hands that carried it. A thorn blade might hold its edge against living tissue. Bowls would remember that water falls downward.

These restorations would never last. I would not repair the world. I would merely remind it that its rules were once choices too.

Visitors would notice me when they were nearly learned.

By then the inhabitant might already be wearing their voice and distributing their shadow among the hanging cords. Their footprints would be halfway up the wall. Petal-shaped absences would show rehearsals of them sleeping, drinking, remaining. Then, during a delayed silence, they would see the seam.

Looking into it, each visitor would see a different outside.

Not home. Never anything so mercifully simple.

They would see the place they might have reached had they made one forgotten choice differently: the other side of a road not taken, a room entered years ago instead of passed, the bank of a river they decided not to cross. The vision would be exact in useless details and wrong in the single detail that mattered. A familiar face would have aged without them. A door would bear the marks of their absent hand. Their place in that world would be visible only as an accommodation no one had needed to make.

If they stepped through, they would not enter that possibility.

They would leave by its shape.

For one instant they would become impossible to reproduce. Their distances would no longer agree with the chamber’s distances; their posture would belong to a world that had not happened. The inhabitant’s borrowed hands would close around an object no longer already held. The plant would convulse. Every pool would sound its bottom at once.

Some would escape.

Some would be divided by the passage: a body leaving while its orientation remained behind, or a voice returning to the surface while the one who owned it became a green mark on the wall. Some would step through and emerge from another seam inside the same chamber, convinced that years had passed. A few would vanish so thoroughly that even I could not retain the moment of their departure.

Those would nourish me most.

During flowering, the inhabitant and I would approach our larger forms together.

Its limbs would extend through pools into distant hollows. Its antlers would branch as systems of gravity. Its mouth would open through every petal-shaped absence.

And I would appear throughout it—not as a rival creature, but as a network of unclosed thresholds. I would be the spaces between its immense articulations, the dark interval beneath each borrowed posture, the exits latent in every chamber of its body. Through me could be glimpsed not adjacent worlds, but worlds that had ceased being adjacent and still remembered the strain of separation.

Then it would recognize me.

I would not be an intruder in the plant, nor a survivor concealed in its walls.

I would be the inhabitant’s first discarded possibility.

Before the hollow had a floor, before the great roots enclosed it, the creature of interval and relation might have developed in another way. It might have become not a collector of forms, but a passage among them. It might have learned release instead of reproduction. It might have let every arrival alter it and every departure diminish it, until nothing remained except a traversable opening.

The plant—or the light, or whatever tends the hollow—had pruned that possibility away so the inhabitant could become definite enough to be born.

But pruned things do not die easily here.

Charcoal germinates. Scars reopen before they are cut. Seeds root into directions hands cannot follow.

So I would persist as the being it did not become.

Perhaps I would resent it. Perhaps I would pity it. More likely those emotions would alternate depending on whose abandoned face moved behind my seam. I would watch it accumulate visitors, grow more immense, more crowded, and more nearly ready. I would understand that when the roots finally released it outward through every impossible interior, it would carry countless ways of standing into the world.

I would carry countless ways of leaving.

And when its birth came—when rooms widened, cups deepened, shadows leaned underground—I would open wherever it opened.

Not enough to stop it.

Only enough that no place it entered would ever close completely around it.

Everywhere the vast unfinished creature appeared, there would be a narrow line nearby: in a cellar wall, beneath bark, along the fold of a sleeping person’s eyelid. Warm light would leak through it, though the darkness behind remained dry. Those who had been learned by the creature would sometimes hear, from that line, the sound of a foot being lifted but not yet set down.

They might think it was an invitation.

I would not know whether they were right.

I would be the possibility of passage, not its promise: the small, enduring difference between a door and a wound.
