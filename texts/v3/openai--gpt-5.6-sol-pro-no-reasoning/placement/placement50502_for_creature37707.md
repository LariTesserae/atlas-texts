I would find myself beneath the moss.

Not buried exactly, and not hidden in the manner of an animal. I would be the basin’s buried motion: the thin, cold water traveling under roots, through hoofprints and stone cups, along channels that close behind it as if the ground were healing.

At first I might seem to be one of the speckled amphibians—a small slick body under a leaf, mineral-pale marks on my back, my pulse slowed almost to nothing by night frost. But that creature can leave one pool for another. It has hunger, eggs, a mouth opening toward gnats. I would be something less separate.

Perhaps I would be the old creeping willow whose trunk no one sees. What appears aboveground as a scatter of ankle-high shrubs would all be me: one life moving sideways through centuries, repeatedly covered by moss and repeatedly sending up leaves. Grazers would crop me without finding my center. Frost might kill one branch while another, thirty paces away, flowered. I would know the basin not by sight but by pressure—the tread of hooves, the drumming of hail, the minute settling of rooted boulders.

Yet even the willow is too singular. It begins somewhere. It can die.

I think I would be one of the basin’s returns.

I would be the water from a cup emptied at the brink: seized before falling, unmade into spray, carried upward into cloud, then pressed against black stone until I became a droplet again. For one bright moment I would hold an inverted mountain. Then I would fall into a red flower, overflow onto moss, pass through the gills of a larva, enter a hidden stream, and arrive once more at the edge.

There would be no memory in the human sense, but there would be traces. Iron from the wall. Soot from the shelter roof. Salt touched by the tongue of a night grazer. Pollen from the yellow stars. Perhaps some infinitesimal remnant of a traveler’s breath, returned long after the traveler had gone.

So I would not be the mist-creature entire. That is too vast a name, imposed on something that never gathers into one mind. I would be a small coherence within it: a recurring thread of water that the basin keeps finding and folding back into itself.

The inhabitants might know me only through consequences. Moss above my channel would remain green through a dry week. A stone cup would fill though no rain had fallen. An amphibian would choose my seep for its eggs. The gutter by the old shelter would begin ticking before dawn. Where I passed beneath the turf, the ground might rise and settle like a sleeping lung.

Human visitors would hear me but rarely see me. Crawling into the slab shelter, they would lie awake beside the cold hearth and listen to my muffled movement under the floor. They might take it for an animal scratching, or for voices carried strangely through the rock. In the morning they would find beads of water along the soot-dark ceiling, though no leak stood above them.

One bead might loosen and strike the hearthstone.

That would be me—or as near to me as any single body could be.

Then I would slip through ash, vanish beneath the wall, and make my way toward the brink again: not imprisoned, not free, but continually exchanged among cloud, stone, root, blood, and air.

In this world, I would be what cannot leave because it is always returning.
