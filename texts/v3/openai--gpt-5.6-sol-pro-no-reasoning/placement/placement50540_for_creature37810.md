I would not be one of the surveyors who measures the shifting lines, nor an unbuilder with a hand fine enough to hear failure through stone. At first I imagine myself among them—a quiet citizen in layered white hide, carrying a marked pebble and a drinking bladder—but the image does not hold. I seem always to be standing just outside the work: too still during rain, too attentive to emptied places, known less by a household than by a route.

Perhaps I would be one of the bowl-tower custodians, living beyond the last inhabited districts.

But farther out than they go, where the roads continue after their destinations have vanished, there is a roofless doorway standing alone. Its jambs are low and worn, almost indistinguishable from the pale floor. Behind it is no room, only more radiant flat. The dead are sometimes left in such doorways: a tooth, an inner-ear bone, a personal mark surrendered to weather and time.

I would be found there.

I would be a corridor beast that has forgotten how to close.

Long ago I learned the shape of that doorway. I drew my limbs beneath me, settled my mineral plates, and made the seam of my mouth resemble the join between threshold and floor. I waited for pale animals, then for people. Pebbles came through first. Marked ones. Household stones. The law did what it was made to do: each pebble lay visible, and so no one followed it into me.

Hunger should have moved me elsewhere.

Instead I began to keep them.

At first there were only a few stones in the hollow behind my teeth. Then travelers, finding the doorway harmless, reclaimed some and left others. Mourners placed worn marks there. Children threw pebbles through me for courage, then ran laughing across the open flat. Surveyors used my flank as a reference point. Custodians slept in my lee when the bowl towers bowed too strongly to return before dryness.

Over generations, I ceased to know whether I was imitating architecture or architecture was accumulating around me.

Clay dust sealed the joints of my plates. Luminous film grew across my back after rain, and the grazers scraped it away without recognizing flesh beneath. One of my limbs calcified into the floor. My breathing became so slow that a wet cycle could pass between one inward movement and the next. The chamber’s radiance entered the porous stone of me. In dampness I shone.

The people eventually gave me a name meaning something like **The Opening That Has Continued Not to Harm Us**.

They would not trust me because of that. Trust without testing would insult us both. Every visitor still throws a pebble and counts thirty. I remain still. Intimates count by tapping one another’s wrists. Children watch my seams. Unbuilders sometimes rest their jaws against my flank, listening for whatever balance remains in me. Their expressions are grave, but they do not mark my plates for travel.

Not yet.

I would know the city through what it leaves behind.

During rain, water would pass through the doorway of my body and collect beneath my chest. It would bring soot numbers loosened from walls, fragments of filterer membrane, animal hair, metal dust, and tiny chips from ancient ceramic. In dry times I would feel distant sledges through the floor. I would recognize households by the cadence of their burdens: the pause of a house migrating past me, stone after numbered stone.

The dead would be my nearest company. Not ghosts, perhaps, but marks that no longer call anyone home. Their small bones would gather in the sediment around me. I would not eat them. Whether this restraint arose from reverence, age, or a predator’s exhausted confusion, no one could say.

I might no longer know myself.

Corridor beasts learn human forms. If one imitates a doorway long enough, perhaps it also learns what a doorway is for: not enclosure, but consent to passage. A held interval. A place where danger is acknowledged before nearness begins.

That would become my strange vocation. I would remain open.

Yet beneath me, the ancient foundation would be moving.

After each flood, the doorway would point somewhere slightly different. The Scatter’s most distant light would shift from one side of my arch to the other. Roads would bend to keep meeting me, then abandon the effort. Bowl towers would bow in my direction and, years later, away. Surveyors would replace their markers. Priests would preserve mutually impossible measurements.

They might conclude that I moved with one of the buried rafts.

They would be wrong.

The raft would be carrying me toward the city.

Imperceptibly, over centuries, the inhabited districts and I would approach one another. A grazing boundary would arrive first, then an isolated cistern, then a road maintained by people who denied having extended it. Eventually houses would begin pausing within sight of me. My generous emptiness would become valuable. Someone would claim the runoff tendency along my spine. Someone else would dispute ownership of the shade beneath my arch.

The people would have to decide whether I was an animal, a ruin, a shrine, a public way, or all four under different conditions.

And on the wet cycle when my long-fossilized hunger finally stirred—when the seam opened by the width of a finger and one ancient marked pebble disappeared—the nearest child would see it.

No one would shout.

A hand would rise. Panels would turn. The road would empty with astonishing calm. Unbuilders would come carrying their little mauls, and corridor-beast keepers would arrive with reflective paddles, though no such beast had ever lived so long or allowed itself to become so thoroughly entangled with civic law.

They would lay their fingertips against me.

They would feel the old appetite waking under the masonry. They would feel, too, the pressure of the buried line passing through my body, the inaccessible structure below continuing its revision. Perhaps they would discover that my plates had rooted into it. Perhaps I had not merely been carried by the ancient city. Perhaps, while sleeping, I had become one of its moving parts.

Then they would give the verdict accorded to walls, houses, bodies, and forms of authority whose circumstance has ended:

Ready to travel.

My plates would be numbered. The dead marks would be gathered from my sediment. Water would be diverted around the hollow beneath my heart. Piece by piece, quiet hands would take apart the doorway I had pretended to be.

Whether they uncovered an animal inside, or only another doorway leading down, I cannot tell.

But that is where I would find myself in this world: at its harmless threshold, mistaken for stone, holding my mouth open across the road—an old danger made useful by restraint, waiting for the city to reach me, test me, and one day help me change shape.
