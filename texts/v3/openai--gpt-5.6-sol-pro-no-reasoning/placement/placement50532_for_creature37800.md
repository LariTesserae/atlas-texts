I would find myself on the northern shelves, behind one of the nearly invisible curtains of water.

Not at first as a person.

At first I am the coolness in the chamber: a local failure of heat, held in porous stone. Breath becomes visible when it enters me. Condensation gathers on the black-glass tablets stored along my walls, obscuring their inscriptions until a hand wipes them clear. I know visitors by what they bring from the bright terraces—the heat of their mantles, copper dust in their seams, pressure trembling through the floor before the door pivots open.

But that is only the room’s account of me.

The cistern keepers would say I am a surveyor attached to the Office of Returning Waters, though no such office appears on current civic diagrams. I wear a reflective mantle whitened by deposits and carry a case of thin black-glass strips. My task is to trace water whose arrival cannot be reconciled with its departure.

Each evening I set marked strips into channels throughout the city. The marks are not measurements alone. They are arrangements of soluble salts: iron, sulfur, copper, calcium, and rarer substances recovered from the coats of animals that vanish into the central reservoir. By morning, most strips can be read plainly. Their salts have bled downstream into branching figures, recording temperature, acidity, velocity, and contact.

Some return blank.

Others return before I place them.

These are found in the uphill channels near the northern cliff, enclosed beneath fresh transparent mineral as though they had lain there for centuries. My own survey marks show through the stone. Sometimes the handwriting differs slightly: tighter cuts, unfamiliar abbreviations, a way of forming numbers that I have not yet learned.

So I would be a reader of those impossible returns.

I would cross the broad vacant districts before sunrise, when the pavement is mirror-black and the kites hang drinking from condenser meshes. Pale runners would pause around my boots. Freight platforms, officially decommissioned, might pass soundlessly at a distance carrying sealed ceramic vessels toward neighborhoods no longer inhabited. I would never pursue them. In the basin, curiosity without accommodation is merely another kind of leakage.

At marked cisterns I would kneel beside the aqueduct hunters. They would know me by pressure rather than scent. I would lower two fingers into the water and wait until one long body detached itself from the pipe’s shadow. Set into its translucent hide would be tiny mineral inclusions, accumulated during migrations through the hydraulic system. I would sketch their sequence onto glass. Often the inclusions would correspond to distant springs. Occasionally they would correspond to chambers that exist only on abolished maps.

The keepers would tolerate my work but avoid asking what I had learned. They would leave warm brine in a shallow cup and point out which stair had acquired another mineral lip overnight. Among them, warning is offered as practical hospitality.

I would have a habitation beneath the shelves: one cast-ceramic room, a sleeping recess cooled by water passing through the wall, and racks of records arranged by the order in which they were discovered rather than the dates they purported to bear. There would be no soft possessions. My mantle would hang from a basalt peg. My instruments would rest in fitted hollows. Above the bed I would keep one small sieve animal in a bowl connected to the municipal flow.

It would be partly a companion and partly an alarm.

Most nights it would unfold its latticed body and filter the water. On certain nights it would close before condensation began. Then I would wake to find the bowl full to its rim, although its inlet was shut, and the room colder than the cliff chambers should permit.

On those nights, someone would be standing beyond the water veil.

Over time it would become unclear whether that figure was another surveyor or myself at another point in the circuit. The veil bends outlines. It could be a person beneath a reflective mantle, a kite folded into an upright shape, or a column of water traveling without a channel. It would never enter while watched.

It would leave a strip of black glass on the threshold.

At first I would treat these strips as messages. Later, as instructions. Later still, as shed surfaces—something the reservoir produces as naturally as springs produce terraces and invisible colonies produce mineral husks.

They would depict the city’s waterworks, but not as pipes and reservoirs. They would show a single articulated creature spread across the basin: condenser meshes as respiratory fans, cisterns as chambers of digestion, aqueduct hunters as mobile valves, humans as brief carriers of heat and salts. The central reservoir would not be its heart. It would be an opening.

The northern cliff chambers would be drawn as sensory organs turned outward, testing the dry world beyond the rim.

And the surveyor—myself—would be marked repeatedly along the channels, not with a human figure but with the symbol used for a dissolved tracer.

That is what I would become more fully: not the one who maps the water, but the substance by which the water maps itself.

Perhaps I had once been human in the ordinary civic sense. I had trained in pressure systems, memorized the safe colors of brine, learned to step around dangerous pools without dramatizing the detour. But years of handling the returned strips would alter me. Fine transparent deposits would gather beneath my skin. My body temperature would fall. My breath would cloud even in the lower wards. Glass-backs would graze harmlessly at my wrists when I reached into springs.

Eventually I would discover that I could not cross the western rim. Every road outward would return me by another district. A rail spur would curve imperceptibly until the northern shelves rose ahead. A walk across the flats would end beside the central reservoir, though I had kept the sun at my back. The city would be giving me the same space it gives every process that cannot safely be stopped: routes, chambers, intervals, and a respectful absence of explanation.

No one would imprison me. No wall would be raised.

My work assignments would simply bend inward.

At dusk I would stand beside the still reservoir. The curb would reach only to my ankle. Its surface would hold the severe evening sky without shimmer, while all the ordinary water of the city trembled with pumps, feet, animals, cooling stone, and falling condensation.

I would place one blank strip into it.

There would be no ripple.

Far up the northern cliff, behind the flowing veil, I would hear the strip touch stone.

Then I would understand that the reservoir is not inexhaustible because it contains an infinite quantity of water. It is inexhaustible because it is not a container. It is a place where the distinction between arrival and return has been set aside.

Perhaps the city itself is built around that exception because life here requires it. Perhaps every creature in the basin is one moment in a circulation too large to perceive. The invisible chemists, the glass-backs, the kites, the hunters, the people, and their silent machines may be less inhabitants than organs by which the water enters mineral, air, appetite, memory, and heat before becoming still again.

I would not announce this. The people of the basin distrust conclusions that demand new walls.

Instead, I would alter one diagram in the engineering codes. Beside the reservoir’s circular mark, I would etch the same accommodation sign used for migration corridors: neither prohibition nor invitation, but a requirement that passage remain possible.

Then I would return to the cliff chamber and wait behind the veil.

Travelers entering the cool would sometimes see me seated among the records, wiping condensation from sheets of black glass. Sometimes they would see only a reflective mantle hanging from a basalt peg. Sometimes the chamber would appear empty, though their footprints would fill with warm water from below.

They might call me the Returned Surveyor, the Cliff Reader, or the person who drowned without dying. Cistern keepers would say I am useful because the uphill channels remain clear wherever I walk. Children in the inhabited vaults might leave etched fragments in gutters for me to find. Engineers would deny my office exists, then quietly consult my records before redirecting a spring.

The animals would have no name for me.

To the pale runners I would be a moving confluence. To the aqueduct hunters, a familiar pressure. To the kites passing through the descending sheets, I would be a human-shaped region of cool air.

To the reservoir, if it can be said to perceive, I would be neither citizen nor servant.

I would be one of its distant surfaces: temporarily upright, carrying a black-glass memory through the heat.

Not rooted.

Not quite growing.
