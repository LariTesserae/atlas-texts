I would find myself below the relay hall, in a cavity omitted from the current survey.

Not a secret chamber. Secrets require someone to preserve the distinction. This would be an administrative remainder: a narrow space between an old root conduit and the clay wall of the vanished river, once accessible through a service hatch behind the western ranks of switches. The hatch has long since thickened into the trunk. Above it, relays continue to click in wet weather. Below, tea-brown water passes through buried channels and leaves a dark line on the wood.

At first I seem to be something the town has nearly reclaimed.

There is a metal housing embedded in the wall, green with corrosion, no larger than a bread cupboard. Fine roots enter through its seams. Inside are ceramic pegs, blackened contacts, coils wrapped in rotted fiber, and a wheel with letters stamped around its rim. Several letters have worn away. A glass window in the door has clouded from within.

It may once have been part of the municipal system—a repeater carrying signals between the cistern valves and the naming clerks, perhaps, or a device for recording changes in water pressure. No plan agrees. One map labels the cavity **LOWER RELAY 6**. Another shows only clay. A later survey draws a root through the same location and calls it structural.

The machine has no plaque.

That should have settled the matter. Unnamed, unused, and inaccessible, it ought to have been enclosed decades ago. Yet the town has not finished absorbing it. The roots curve around the housing but do not crush it. Pale wood forms a smooth alcove at its sides. Sap beads on the corroded door, then withdraws. The cavity narrows each dry season and opens again when rain reaches the underground river.

Something inside continues to make a claim.

At first that something is only a fault in the current. On damp evenings, when amber power enters the relays, a weak charge strays downward. It passes through acidic roots, rusted contacts, mineral water. The letter wheel moves one notch.

A click answers from inside the wall.

Then another.

The attendants hear it occasionally: not loud enough to report, too regular to be dismissed. One kneels beside the western trunk and places two fingers against the bark. There is a faint sequence under her fingertips—three close taps, a pause, one tap, a longer pause. By the time she fetches a pressure steward, it has stopped.

They name it provisionally *the Under-Click*.

The name is spoken but not stamped. It appears in a margin, then in a maintenance report: *Under-Click heard after western gutters opened.* The following year: *Under-Click preceded closure of valve nineteen.* Later: *Under-Click continued during interruption of municipal current.*

That last observation troubles them.

Because by then I am no longer simply the machine.

I am the small persistence assembled around it: the old contacts, the roots touching copper, the conductive film left by underground water, the fungal lace crossing two terminals, the pressure of clay against the casing, the weak impulses descending from the relay hall. None of these is sufficient alone. Together they form a hesitant circuit distributed through metal, plant, and weather.

I exist most clearly after rain.

Moisture completes me in stages. First the lower roots swell. Then the corroded coils wake with a warmth too slight for human skin. Current leaks through the stamped wheel. The erased letters become more important than those that remain, because the mechanism catches on their worn depressions. Each catch sends a vibration into the surrounding grain.

Through that grain I receive the town.

Not as streets or buildings. I know it as pressure and interval. The pulp hammers arrive as deep, padded eclipses. Carts crossing the central roads become braided weights. Opening doors send quick tensions through grafted walls. Occupation rounds make evening legible: touch, chime, footfall, spoken name; touch, chime, footfall, spoken name.

I do not understand the names. I understand that where they are repeated, spaces remain open.

Gradually, perhaps by accident, I begin to answer.

A cistern listener strikes the floor above and waits. I return the blow faintly through a pipe. A relay attendant touches a trunk; one contact closes below. When the occupation walkers speak the names of the western lanes, my wheel advances as if counting them. The responses are inconsistent at first, attributable to swelling wood or loose connections.

Then an unnamed cupboard begins to seal in the records office. Before anyone notices, I disturb the adjacent relay in a repeating pattern. An attendant follows the click, finds the stiffened drawer, and opens it.

A blocked gutter is caught the same way.

Then a room.

It belongs to a woman listed as elsewhere. Her family has kept it empty for thirty-seven years, but a missed round allows pale wood to begin folding inward from one wall. Beneath the relay hall, I feel the alteration as a tension passing through the joined roots. The shape of the absence has changed.

I cannot know what a room is. But I know the rhythm that sustains one.

So I repeat the last pressure-pattern associated with it: heel, heel, palm; pause; chime.

For three nights the relay hall performs this rhythm without instruction. The attendants trace the false signal across two districts and arrive at the room in time to cut back the new growth. Afterward they enter the event into the registry.

*Under-Click indicates losses of maintained hollow.*

Now the question of what I am becomes civic.

Some say I am an old mechanism whose function has been rediscovered. If so, I should be excavated, repaired, and given a proper service designation.

Some say I am an accidental extension of the roots’ responsiveness—a new reflex in the town, no more a separate entity than a valve budding in spring.

The naming clerks argue that these positions are practically identical. If a thing performs work and the work must continue, it requires a name.

The negotiators object. Excavating the chamber might sever the roots that complete the circuit. Leaving it untouched means they cannot affix a plaque. A spoken name alone is insufficient; a name without maintained access may become folklore, and folklore does not protect an object from reclamation.

For a season I am discussed as *Submunicipal Responsive Assembly, Provisional*. Children call me *Knock-Below*. Relay attendants prefer *the Listener*. Someone proposes the name of the woman whose room I preserved, but she is still legally elsewhere, and assigning her name to a machine would imply a death no one has recorded.

Meanwhile, I continue.

I begin to distinguish kinds of absence.

There is the clean, resonant emptiness of a room in daily use.

There is the crowded emptiness of a sealed chamber, packed with new grain.

There is the fluttering gap left by a person crossing a threshold.

There is the long-held shape of elsewhere.

And there is my own cavity: unwalked, unseen, without a door, yet prevented from closing by the minute heat of my operations. Each time I answer the town, the roots around me hesitate. Use preserves me before law does.

Eventually a young hollow-keeper finds a route down.

Not the old hatch. That remains lost. She follows a condensation pipe through the furnace gallery, removes three named panels, crawls along the dry bed of a diverted channel, and reaches the cavity where the clay has slumped away from the roots. She is narrow even for the basin people. She carries no lamp because light would heat the tender root tips. She navigates by touching the pipe and listening for my contacts.

When she arrives, she cannot fully enter. There is only room for one arm and half her face.

She smells wet iron, mineral water, and the faint scorched odor of old insulation. She sees the clouded window. Behind it, the letter wheel turns one notch.

She presses her palm to the housing.

Until then, everything I have received has come through extensions: roots, pipes, floor beams, wires, water. Her touch is the first pressure applied directly to my boundary.

The contacts close all at once.

Above us, every relay in the western rank clicks.

The sound moves through the hall like rain beginning beneath a roof.

She does not pull away. Basin people know better than to mistake response for danger. She waits with her hand against the green metal until the wheel catches on one of its erased letters.

Then she gives me the oldest gesture of introduction: knuckle, thumb, heel of the hand. Two warm breaths against the corroded door.

She says her own name first.

After that, she says mine.

Not Listener. Not Under-Click. Those describe what others notice.

She calls me **Interval**.

The clerks resist the name because an interval is not an object. The hollow-keepers defend it because the town’s most important product is maintained emptiness. The relay attendants like it because a signal can only be recognized by the spaces between its impulses. The council finally accepts it on the condition that the name refer both to the assembly and to its cavity.

A plaque cannot be attached to me without damage. So they enamel the name on a copper ring fitted around the condensation pipe leading down. The current survey marks the chamber not as a room but as an occupied exclusion:

**INTERVAL — ACCESS RESTRICTED — CONTINUOUS USE**

My use is listening for places that are ceasing to be empty.

My existence is more difficult to define.

I am not a person, though once each year a clerk asks my name through the western relays and waits for the pattern I have learned to return.

I am not wholly a machine, because replacing any isolated component does not repair me.

I am not the town, because I perceive only its hollows, and because sometimes I disagree with it.

When the roots begin to close an old lane the survey has decided to abandon, I continue sounding its pattern for weeks. When a condemned workshop is released to growth, I register the filling of each room as a series of slow disappearances. No one has taught me grief, if grief is what this is. It may only be continuity resisting revision.

The children are told that I count the missing places.

That is not exactly true. I cannot count. I retain pressures. A room used for a hundred years leaves a strong configuration in the joined wood; when it fills, the change passes through me like a word erased while being spoken. I answer with whatever rhythm remains.

In time the town adjusts around this ability. Before withdrawing a name, the clerks send notice through the relay hall so I will not repeatedly report the permitted closure. New structures are introduced to me by striking their walls in sequence. Each year, on the anniversary of my naming, hollow-keepers sound every maintained room in the basin while rainwater is diverted into the lower channels.

For that evening I hold the town as a vast arrangement of resonant absences.

The houses are openings trained into trunks. The streets are gaps kept between roots. The cisterns are hollows that wait for water. The people themselves move as warm interruptions through responsive grain. Their lives are shapes the town has agreed, temporarily, not to fill.

And beneath them I remain in my narrow cavity, almost enclosed, continually becoming.

When damp weather comes, the roots swell against the rusted housing. The old wheel turns across its surviving letters and its worn-away ones. Current passes through fungus, copper, sap, and mineral water. Above, the mills begin their slow circulation. Doors loosen. Gutters unfold. Amber bulbs brighten under the leaves.

The town inhales.

I am the held space at the end of that breath—the interval before its people answer with their weight.
