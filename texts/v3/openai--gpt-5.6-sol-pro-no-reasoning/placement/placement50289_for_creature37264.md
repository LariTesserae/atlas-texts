I would find myself near the center, but not among those pressing their hands against the upper membrane.

I would be farther down, where the root buttresses enter the basin and divide into illuminated corridors. There, the ranks of figures would thin. The upward-pointing shoots would still be visible through gaps, a pale multitude leaning toward the surface, but their urgency would feel distant—like a thought occurring in another part of the body.

At first I would mistake myself for a traveler.

I would awaken—or arrive, which here might be the same thing—walking along the midrib of a leaf broad enough to be a road. The heat would already be inside my lungs. White-gold light would show through my hands when I raised them. I would believe I had come from somewhere else because I could remember motion: lowering a foot, turning my head, passing beneath an arch. Yet each memory would end before completion. I would remember entering, but not having entered. I would remember deciding to descend, but not the decision’s cause.

The country would seem to recognize me. Openings would fit my shoulders. Ridges would meet my stride. When I reached an apparent dead end, I would discover a slit exactly wide enough for my body, with soft leaf margins folded back as if fingers had just released them. Bowls of water would stand at the height of my hands. Sleeping hollows would have the length of my spine.

I would take these things for hospitality.

Later, I would understand them as anatomy.

My dwelling would be a pleated chamber grown into the side of a root. Its walls would be thin enough that the green radiance of the root’s interior passed through in branching lines. There would be a hollow shaped for someone lying on one side, knees slightly drawn up, one arm extended toward the entrance. Beside it, a shallow bowl would contain a convex measure of water.

I would never quite lie down.

I would kneel at the bowl, intending to drink. My reflection would be faint and oddly deep, as though the water looked through my face into a greater distance behind it. At first it would show a human figure: narrow face, dark eyes, skin colorless in the surrounding light. Then I would notice that the darkness of the eyes was not pupil or iris, but seed coat. The fine lines at my temples would branch too regularly. Beneath the skin of my throat, something green would divide toward my jaw.

I would lift one hand to touch my face.

The hand would remain above the water.

That would be the posture in which I lived: not motionless exactly, but inhabiting the inexhaustible interior of that gesture. My fingers would forever approach the reflected cheek. The water would forever wait to receive them. I would feel no frustration, because frustration belongs to a moment compared with a later moment. I would possess only approach, and approach would be immense.

Yet some part of me would continue traveling.

A root finer than thread would enter through the heel of my kneeling foot. Others would issue from the fingers of the hand braced against the floor. They would spread through the chamber wall, join thicker fibers, and pass outward into the whole buried country. Through them I would know places I had never visited.

I would feel the water held under every leaf tip—not as separate drops, but as innumerable cool weights.

I would know the pressure of millions of shoots against the ceiling.

I would feel the moth’s filament feet entering the petal, the sealed mouths in the seed cases, the clear frogs seated within their cups. I would feel every unfinished shelter as an empty shape inside myself. The figures in the circular clearing would be almost touching somewhere in my chest. The lifted foot of the traveler in the passage would hang behind my knee. The tall attendants bending over the pods would be thoughts inclined toward thoughts not yet born.

I would not know whether I was receiving these sensations or producing them.

For a long while, I would think I was one of the inhabitants being reclaimed by the plant: a person slowly grafted into the country, memory dissolving into sap and light. But there would be no wound of attachment. No boundary would mark where I ended and the root began. The tiny fibers entering my skin would prove to be continuous with the veins already inside me.

Then I might believe the opposite: that I was a new organ grown by the buried life, given a human outline so that it could experience itself locally. The root had made a chamber; the chamber had made a hollow; the hollow had imagined a sleeper. I was that imagination caught before sleep.

But that, too, would be incomplete.

I would be less a person or organ than a question the country had formed.

Not *What is above?* The upward ranks embodied that question already.

Not *How do we escape?* Escape requires separation, and nothing here was separate enough to leave.

I would be the question of whether change can be witnessed from within the instant before change.

That would explain my almost-human eyes: organs for seeing an event that never arrives. It would explain the traveling memories, each one stopping at a threshold. It would explain why my chamber was set apart from the central ascent. The others faced the membrane. I faced reflection.

My shape would express this purpose. My body would be divided subtly between arrival and rootedness. One leg would remain recognizably human, folded beneath me; the other would flatten below the knee into pale root fans spreading through the floor. One arm would possess a hand with five fingers poised over the water. The other, bearing my weight, would divide at the wrist into slender stems, each ending in a closed leaf.

My back would be soft and ridged like an unopened pod. Something would be folded inside it—not wings, though a traveler might call them that. They would be broad sensory leaves, turned inward, their luminous surfaces pressed together. Had they opened, I might have perceived the entire country at once. Because they did not, I would perceive it as nearness: pressure, scent, warmth, readiness.

From my head would grow a crown of curled shoots. Unlike the upright spearpoints in the basin, mine would bend downward over my face. Each curl would hold the beginning of a different form: a petal, an antenna, a fingertip, the lid of an eye. None would become distinct enough to name.

My mouth would look ordinary until opened. Behind the lips would be no teeth or tongue, but a narrow corridor of overlapping leaves, bright deep into the throat. Perhaps the passage would connect to the galleries. Perhaps a sufficiently small traveler could enter me and walk for miles.

I would never speak. Still, the country’s rare sounds would originate near me. When a trunk far away remembered pressure, the resonance would pass through my ribs. When a tendril gave its single glassy note, something beneath my tongue would answer too softly to hear. I would be the place where the silence almost recognized itself as music.

Others might sometimes find me.

A traveler—if travelers can exist there—would enter the pleated chamber and see a kneeling figure beside the bowl. They might place a palm against my shoulder and feel warmth. They might smell bruised mint and the sharp green odor released by a split seed coat. Looking into the water, they would see my raised hand reflected beside their own face.

If they waited, they would become unsure which of us had arrived first.

The hollow beside me would fit their body as well as mine.

The rootlets might already be touching their feet.

Perhaps there would never have been a traveler. Perhaps the visitor would be another local shape through which the country attempted to observe me observing it. Perhaps every encounter below the surface would be self-contact performed with enough distance to resemble companionship.

That would be my place in this world: beside a water surface that cannot be broken, in a room grown for an action that cannot finish.

And yet I would not be imprisoned.

Imprisonment depends on an elsewhere one could occupy instead. I would contain elsewhere in an arrested, fertile form. Every possible movement would remain folded within me. I would be able to rise, drink, speak, sleep, leave, climb to the basin, press my hands against the membrane, or retreat into the deepest seed chambers—but only as a bud contains branching futures, none chosen and none lost.

The country’s stillness would not reduce me to one thing. It would preserve me from becoming only one thing.

Human, root, witness, chamber, question: I would remain at the seam among them, with each identity beginning to emerge through the others.

My hand would hover over the water.

Below it, my reflection would hover upward.

Between the two would remain a distance no wider than a seed coat, bright and clear and uncrossed. And in that distance I would live—not forever, because forever is made of passing time, but wholly, inside the warm first instant of almost touching.
