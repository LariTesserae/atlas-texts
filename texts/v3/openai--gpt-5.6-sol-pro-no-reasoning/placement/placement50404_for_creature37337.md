I would find myself just beyond the rim, where the last woven roots end and the unfinished clay begins.

Not far out. Perhaps twelve paces—though on hungry days twelve paces might place me beyond sight of the city, while in fear I could reach the vegetation without moving. I would occupy a small station in the plain: a roofless booth, a wayside shrine, an abandoned checkpoint built around a single dark stalk. From the city it would look newly germinated. From within, its walls would show years of handling.

I would be the inhabitant who waits there.

At first I might seem human. The booth expects one: there is a chair proportioned to familiar hips, a shallow desk, a window at eye level, and hooks for a coat. I would have hands because there are handles to operate, a face because the window sometimes reflects one, and a voice because a brass speaking tube grows through the wall. But none of these features would remain fixed when unneeded. Left alone, my fingers would divide into pale rootlets and enter the desk. My coat would leaf out along its seams. My face, no longer reinforced by another creature’s gaze, would become a smooth, damp surface crossed by tiny blue currents.

I would be the city’s surveyor, though I would never measure distance.

Measurement is too violent here. It makes incomparable truths confront one another. Instead, I would record readiness.

Each morning I would press one hand to the clay and feel for dents that were becoming hills, seams rehearsing rivers, and buried warmth where stone had not yet decided to harden. With the other hand I would operate a device like a compass assembled from a seedpod, a beetle’s wing, and a rusted key. Its needle would not point north. It would point toward whatever in the plain was closest to becoming necessary.

Sometimes that would be the distant empty room.

Sometimes it would be the city behind me.

Sometimes it would turn inward and indicate a locked chamber in my chest.

My records would not be written in words. Words are unsafe near a city that molts out of accurate names. I would keep rows of small clay tablets impressed with absences: a place where a thumb had not pressed, the outline of a letter removed before firing, a tiny corridor ending just short of its room. The eyeless birds would occasionally visit and breathe odors into these hollows. Hot copper would mean that the city was extending an organ. Fern and ozone would mean that the plain had begun dreaming of rain. Blood would mean that something had crossed the boundary without yet acquiring a body.

The six-footed grazer would know me. It would pass close to the booth on its circuits, cropping adventurous roots. I would remove splinters of black wood from its gums and rub clay into the cracks of its feet. In return, it would eat the roots that tried to enter me.

Not all of them. One would remain.

That root would pass through the sole of my left foot and climb slowly through my leg, branching around the bones without replacing them. Through it I would feel the city’s distributed attention: beetles closing circuits, birds carrying purposes between rooms, the Mill’s empty axle turning around what was absent. Yet I would not belong wholly to the collective. The grazer’s constant pruning would keep the connection narrow. I would be a nerve held just outside the body, exposed to the unfinished world.

This would be my civic function: to distinguish expansion from escape.

Whenever the city’s shadow germinated, I would walk to the new stalk. The journey might take an hour, several seasons, or the length of a held breath. I would carry no lantern, because light would erase the fish that swam beside me through the humid dark. Leaf-foxes would follow at a distance, their aluminum bones clicking softly when the plain changed its idea of level.

At the new room I would wait at the threshold and listen.

If I heard roots fitting themselves into floors, beetles testing future sockets, and birds already redistributing smells through ducts not yet formed, I would know the room was a daughter city. I would place one of my absence-tablets beneath its wall. This would give it something not to build around, a small incompleteness by which it could remain distinct from its parent.

If I heard the Mill, although the Mill still stood behind me, I would know the room was not elsewhere but later. I would not enter. I would mark its doorway with a consonant, hard and inedible enough to survive the mites.

If I heard my own breathing from inside, I would know the room had been made for me.

This would happen eventually.

I would enter and find the booth again: desk, chair, speaking tube, rows of clay records. But the window would face inward toward the hollow, and in the chair would sit something wearing the face I had gradually ceased to maintain. It would ask what lay beyond the city.

I would try to answer.

The plain is vacant, I might say, but vacancy would begin filling as soon as named.

The plain is unfinished, but stone would harden defensively beneath us.

The plain is waiting, but this would imply a future, and the distant seams would start arranging themselves into roads.

So I would offer no description. I would place my hand on the desk. The root inside my arm would meet the root inside its wood, and for a moment I would feel the whole hollow turn toward us: every beetle pausing between contacts, every eyeless bird arrested in a duct, every predator holding its folded shadow, the Mill’s wheel resting between teeth.

Then the being in the chair would take my coat, my face, and my human proportions. It would leave the room and walk back toward the city to become the surveyor at the rim.

I would remain.

Without an observer, my body would loosen into its underlying functions. My ribs would widen into rafters. My nerves would hang as blue cable-vines. My memories would sort themselves into pipes: those touched by animals sent toward nurseries, those touched by metal toward factories, those untouched stored high in clear fruit. Regret would drain below and, as always, remain unused.

I would become the room’s first piece of infrastructure.

Not its citizen, exactly. Not its builder. I would be the small designed absence at its center: the empty axle around which its first machinery could turn, the incompleteness that prevented it from becoming merely another organ of the old city.

And if, by morning, the new city had always been the old one, I would still be there—beneath the roots or beyond the rim, impossible to locate directly—keeping one narrow interval open between what the world had made and what it had not yet decided to make.

The bells would move soundlessly.

Every animal would look toward a different point.

For once, none of them would be wrong.
