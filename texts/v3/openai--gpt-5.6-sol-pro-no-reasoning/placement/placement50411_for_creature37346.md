At first I think I would be found near the rim.

Not on its highest edge, where the fallers graze among flowering salts, but inside the fresh scarps: in the narrow interval between a fracture opening and the stone deciding whether it has already broken. I imagine myself thin enough to live there, pressed into mineral darkness, feeding on heat and the brief electrical sweetness of stress.

But the plateau does not reward a creature for imagining itself singular.

The fissure widens. Part of me remains in the cliff. Part descends with the stone. Part is carried upward as dust and enters the conductive fur of a grazer. If I insist that only one of these is me, I will become extinct almost immediately.

So I would be a continuity spread across separations.

I would begin as a pale filament that grows in cooling cracks near the outer rim. Not fungus, though the buried networks would sometimes mistake me for fungus; not animal, though I would molt and migrate; not entirely a colony, because colonies imply members that can be counted. My substance would be a braided living film, hair-fine when dispersed, rope-thick where many strands meet. It would taste pressure in stone and distance in the tension between its knots.

The strands would anchor themselves at many points: under salt crusts, in the shells of faller eggs, around the roots of cliff plants, within the warm porous plates of walking reefs. Most would die unnoticed. The surviving ones would carry no complete body and no complete memory. Each would retain only a tendency: tighten here, release there, wait through this gravity, divide before that silence.

Where enough tendencies met, I would briefly become an animal.

It would be called nothing, but it might look like a loose, many-armed knot dragging itself across the rock. Its limbs would braid and unbraid as it moved. Some would end in hooks for gripping fresh scarps. Some would flatten into sails to catch rising gravity. Some would insert themselves into cracks and listen to the plateau’s strain.

It would have no head. Its nearest equivalent to a face would be a dark, wet hollow at its center where vibrations arrived and interfered. When the ground was stable, the hollow would be shallow. Before a deletion or rearrangement, contradictory tremors would deepen it until it seemed to open farther inward than the body could contain.

I would live where the plateau sheds itself: along the rim, around newborn faults, and beside those wounds where geography has recently ceased to be true.

There I would feed on discontinuity.

When stone fractures, its two faces retain a momentary correspondence. Every grain on one side has an answering grain on the other. Usually this relation decays at once. I would graze upon it before it vanished, drawing the paired surfaces through my filaments and storing their agreement as a pattern of tension.

Thus I would remember places not by image, scent, or direction, but by how they once fitted together.

A deleted ravine would survive in me as two edges pulling toward an absent middle. A vanished mountain would be a vast compression among distant knots. A pool that collapsed upward would remain as a cool circular looseness that tightened whenever rain approached. I could not describe any of these places. But if fragments of them persisted, I could recognize which fragment belonged beside which.

This ability would make me useful, although usefulness here would never become purpose.

The fallers would lay eggs among my outer strands because the braid would hold them to descending stones. Turbine roots would grow along me and inherit old alignments. Gliders would roost on my raised loops, reading in their tension whether the cliff still opened onto nearby air or onto a distance that had swollen overnight.

Sometimes, after a storm, survivors would gather around me without understanding why.

A grazer column divided by rearranged terrain might encounter two exposed ends of my buried braid. As their feet disturbed the strands, the old correspondence between the severed places would tighten. One end would pull toward the other—not necessarily across ordinary space, but through whatever adjacency remained possible. The grazers would follow the pressure. Perhaps half would arrive. Perhaps they would emerge days before departing. Perhaps only their green symbionts would cross, spreading over animals on the far side until the colony smelled enough like the missing herd to calm them.

The plateau would not permit reliable rescue.

Still, I would be among the beings that attempt reunion without knowing reunion as an idea.

My reproduction would begin in rupture. When a stone bearing my strands fell from the rim, the living braid would draw thin, then snap. The descending half would recoil into a tight seed-knot. The half remaining above would do the same. Each would carry the felt shape of the other’s absence.

On the falling stone, my knot would pass through generations of the fallers. They would incorporate strands into nests and egg chains. I would extend through their ecology, learning the flex of their hooks, the pulse-colors by which their descendants inherited weather, the slow reversal when descent became ascent.

By the time the stone returned from above, I would no longer resemble my plateau-bound half.

The returning form might spread as a dark net over the rock, each intersection opening and closing like a small mouth. It might carry organisms from the amber depths. It might have learned pressures for which the plateau had no present equivalent. At impact, the old braid and the changed braid would recognize one another by the pain of their ancient separation.

They would join imperfectly.

That imperfection would be my memory.

I would remember not by preserving a pattern unchanged, as the glass swimmers preserve messages, nor by routing around absence, as the fungal minds do. I would remember through mismatch: one side altered by falling, the other by remaining. When they braided together again, their disagreement would produce movements neither had known alone.

A returned knot might cause my body to reach toward empty air, where a lower-world surface had once supported it. It might contract at colors no plateau creature could see. During rain, it might shape itself into a vessel suited to a pressure far beneath the weather. Smaller animals could shelter inside these useless gestures. Later, if the plateau briefly reflected some drowned or inverted version of itself, the gestures might cease to be useless.

In this way I would become a reservoir of obsolete adaptations.

The newest beings from the unfinished interior would be drawn to me. They would arrive carrying scars without injuries and instincts without objects. I, carrying fits without landscapes, would be familiar to them.

They might press themselves against my braid. A creature mourning offspring never born could place its body into one of my empty loops. The loop would tighten, not because it understood grief, but because it had once encircled something of that size. The creature would sleep there. Its skin organisms would enter me; my filaments would enter its inherited scars. When it left, part of its impossible memory would remain as tension.

Over time, I would accumulate these unlived correspondences.

That accumulation would eventually attract the echo-hunter.

My body would be rich in false distances: ends that tugged across missing valleys, loops closed around absent animals, strands braced against cliffs now falling elsewhere. To the hunter, I would sound like an ideal place to become. Every vibration entering me would return from an impossible relation.

At first it would haunt my outer fibers. A scrape would answer from the wrong knot. The remembered footfalls of dead herds would circulate through my braids. My dispersed body would tighten, pulling the contradictions inward.

The hunter would condense in my central hollow.

I would not be able to flee. Flight would require agreement about where my body was.

Instead, I would unbraid.

Millions of strands would release at once. Some would flatten against stone. Some would enter root mats. Some would catch in the fur of grazers or the conductive webs beneath them. The coherent hollow in which the predator was forming would be pulled into a thousand lesser inconsistencies.

Perhaps the hunter would be divided among them. For years afterward, parts of me would release isolated sounds: a thunder note from a salt seam, a wingbeat from inside a pilgrim’s shell, the soft breathing of a colorless animal from a faller egg. Some knots would learn predation from the fragment they carried. Others would learn only fear.

A few would begin returning sounds deliberately.

That would be the beginning of something new—not speech, not yet, and perhaps never. My kind would have no need to name objects. Objects are temporary. But we might learn to signal correspondences:

This pressure belongs with that opening.

This creature has been divided.

This edge once touched another edge.

Something absent is exerting force here.

The buried fungal minds might eventually notice.

They would encounter my filaments in mineral seams and treat them first as interruptions. My knots would disturb conductivity according to old fractures; their networks would answer according to vanished mineral masses. For decades, perhaps centuries, we would misread one another.

Then a fungal pattern would route through one of my remembered joins.

A mountain deleted long ago still exists in the fungus as a detour. The same mountain exists in me as a compression between surfaces once separated by its rising. Neither of us possesses the mountain. Between us, however, its absence gains structure.

Roots following the fungal detour would meet my tension lines. Turbine groves would grow along both. Their leaves would begin transmitting a shape more complete than any one memory.

Gliders would circle.

Distance might obey.

For an afternoon, the lost mountain could become reachable again—not restored, not truly, but made locally persuasive by the agreement of fungus, braid, root, leaf, animal, and air. Creatures would climb its absent slopes. Rain would collect in a basin held together by collective expectation. The before-born might stand on its summit and recognize consequences whose causes had vanished.

Then the correspondence would relax. The mountain would become omission again.

But after that, the plateau would contain a new habit: not permanence, but recall.

I would not witness this as an individual. By then, the knot that first called itself me would have been divided beyond counting. Some strands would be falling. Some would be carried on reefs. Some would be buried in a mountain not yet raised. One might be tightened around a creature surviving in the remembered volume of a deleted forest. Another might descend so far that “plateau” became only a hereditary direction of pull.

Yet when any two severed surfaces strained toward one another, there I would be.

Not a mind spread across the world. Not a keeper appointed to preserve what the plateau destroys. The world has made no appointments.

I would be the living ache by which broken things continue to correspond.

And if I had anything resembling a self, it would occur only at reunion: in the instant when a stone returned from above, struck the rising rim, split open, and the altered descendants of one filament touched the waiting descendants of another.

For that instant, the whole dispersed braid would tighten.

Not enough to stop the plateau.

Only enough to feel that it had changed.
